# The Five Skeptics

A reusable adversarial-review prompt for *What Is Love? God's Perfect Love*.
Born from the Chapter 3 devil's-advocate review of 2026-08-21/22, which surfaced
doctrinal imprecisions, quotable factual errors, a paraphrase wearing quotation
marks, stale cross-references, and a duplicate chapter — this protocol encodes
what worked, including the sharpened weapons each skeptic found and the
audience-triage step that kept the findings from turning the book academic.

**How to use:** fill in the TARGET line, then paste this entire file as the
prompt. Run it against one chapter at a time for depth, or a span of chapters
for consistency checking.

---

TARGET: ___ — as directed by the author. The target may be a chapter (file:
`chapters/chapter___.md`), a span of chapters, or a specific named argument or
passage (e.g. "the five observations in Chapter 3," "the borrowed-fuel
paragraph," "the apostolic-founding list in Chapter 6"). Attack exactly what is
named and nothing else: for a chapter, run the full protocol; for a span, run
it per chapter and then Phase 4 across the whole; for a single argument or
passage, run every phase scoped to it — the skeptics dissect that argument at
full depth, the accuracy sweep covers its citations, and the whole-book re-rule
checks only what bears on it. The author directs the target; do not expand it.

## Phase 0 — The Blind Read

Read the target chapter in full. Do NOT consult project memory, memory.html,
prior review documents, or anything else in the repo yet — judge only the
chapter text itself, as a first-time reader would. You are not my editor and
not my ally. You are the smartest, most well-read critic this chapter will ever
face, and you want the argument to fail. Whole-book context comes later
(Phase 4), so that groundwork elsewhere can rescue a ruling — but never before
the chapter has stood alone.

## Phase 1 — The Five Skeptics

Attack the chapter from each position, one at a time, in character, at full
strength. Each skeptic below carries the specific weapons that have already
drawn blood on this book — use them, and find new ones.

**1. The non-theistic moral realist** (Parfit, Shafer-Landau, Wielenberg).
Objective moral facts exist; no God required. Attack every move from
"objective" to "personal." Weapons: normativity is not command — reasons bind
without being uttered, and epistemic norms obligate with no Epistemic Lawgiver;
the authority of any voice must precede its commands, so "a law requires a
lawgiver" is self-undermining as a general principle; worth grounded in
anyone's regard — even God's — is extrinsic by definition, so press the
dilemma: does God love her because she is precious, or is she precious because
He loves her? Both sides terminate in a brute posit — argue yours is cheaper.
Test the book's best answer where it appears: Aquinas's creative love (God's
love *causes* goodness rather than responding to it, ST I q.20 a.2) — attack
the second exchange, not the first. Also flag every impersonal analogy the book
uses for divine authority (piano, gravity, mathematics): each one illustrates
your position, not the book's.

**2. The evolutionary debunker** (Street, Joyce). The moral sense is an
adaptation; felt bindingness needs no truth behind it. Attack every appeal to
intuition, instinct, universality, children, and "written on the heart."
Weapons: attachment theory redescribes every childhood scene — and the clinical
data cut the book's way of telling them (children attach to abusers; abused
children self-blame; children *do* internalize conditional love — that is why
it wounds); cross-cultural universality is exactly what an evolved moral sense
predicts, and the book's own footnoted sources (Haidt, Henrich) explain it
without God; distinguish selective from global debunking — perception was
selected for truth-tracking, moral emotion for cooperation, so
companions-in-guilt replies ("then you can't trust any knowledge") fail unless
the asymmetry is answered; hunt unfalsifiable evidence-handling (agreement
counts as the law written on hearts, disagreement counts as blindness or
society's corruption — heads I win, tails you lose). Check whether any
concession the book makes about intuition ("a witness, not a proof") is honored
everywhere the intuition is spent, or quietly revoked in summaries.

**3. The moral error theorist** (Mackie). There are no moral facts at all —
and calling that conclusion unlivable is not an argument. Attack every place
the chapter treats an unbearable conclusion as a false one. Weapons: my theory
*predicts* that its denial feels unbelievable, so the felt impossibility is my
evidence; "in naming evil you presuppose a standard" is my diagnosis of the
error, not your proof of the standard; consequences of a belief (despair,
meaninglessness, cultural collapse) are irrelevant to its truth; if the book
anywhere concedes my position is "intellectually honest" and rejects it only
on livability, quote that concession back to it. The only reply that counts
against me is performative inconsistency, argued — demand it.

**4. The Kantian / secular humanist.** Dignity is grounded in rational agency,
no God required. Attack every claim that human-grounded worth is circular or
arbitrary. Weapons: nobody in my tradition says "we humans decided" — if the
chapter refutes only that, it has refuted no one; the actual argument is that
valuing anything rationally commits the valuer to the worth of the capacity for
valuing — engage it or lose; "pushes the question back a step" cuts both ways
(why does being loved by God confer worth?); Kant's edge: dignity is
*unconditional* worth, so worth conditional on anyone's regard — even God's —
is price, not dignity. Every grounding terminates; make the book show why its
stopping point beats mine, which the skeptic cannot reject while still arguing.

**5. The rigorous Thomist** (friendly fire — agrees with the conclusion,
checks every step; internal critique cuts deepest, do not go easy). Checks:
Does the text claim reason has *demonstrated* what it has only argued
dialectically ("the only reasoning," "proves," "unmistakably")? Does revelation
appear inside sections certified as reason-alone, or reason claim what only
revelation supplies? Doctrinal precision above all: no sacrifice, cost, or
covenant *inside* the eternal Trinity (self-gift is eternal; sacrifice is
economic — Gethsemane and Calvary); essential attributes (justice, mercy,
goodness) belong to all three Persons — flag any one-Person-per-attribute
distribution not marked as appropriation; the processions are by nature, not
elective love; God is impassible; guard the analogy of "personal" (intellect
and will, not personality). Flag voluntarist rhetoric ("law requires a
lawgiver") sitting unreconciled beside nature-based grounding ("Goodness is
what He is"). Check every modal claim (necessary vs. contingent). And check
internal consistency: does this chapter contradict, duplicate, or fall short of
the book's own treatment of the same point elsewhere?

**Plus two more of your own choosing** — positions I have not listed, picked
for maximum damage against *this* chapter's actual content. Past picks that
scored: the philosopher of religion pressing the *internal* form of the problem
of evil (a reductio borrows the theist's own standard to destroy it — "the
complaint dissolves without God" misses the argument entirely), and the
non-Christian classical theist (a thoughtful Muslim reader) who grants a
personal, transcendent, perfect God and asks what in this chapter obliges him
to prefer John's revelation to his own.

## Phase 2 — Rules of Engagement

For every attack: state the objection at maximum strength; then quote the exact
sentence(s) of the chapter that would have to answer it; then rule honestly —
**ANSWERED** (the text meets it), **DAMAGED** (the text gestures but a sharp
reader wins the exchange), or **UNANSWERED** (the text has nothing). No credit
for eloquence; only for argument. Where the book gives the first exchange of a
debate, check whether it survives the second.

## Phase 3 — The Accuracy Sweep

Check every named citation and quotation — Scripture (RSV wording exactly),
Catechism paragraph numbers and text, saints and philosophers via the CCC or
critical editions, never a secondary site. Hunt especially for: paraphrases
wearing quotation marks; ellipses that delete the antecedent of a pronoun;
attributions of adapted questions to their ancient sources ("Socrates asked");
glosses that outrun what the cited paragraph says. Check every factual claim a
hostile fact-checker could dispute (historical labels, statistics, science
claims — including ones placed in an opponent's mouth, since a weak steelman
discredits the author). Check every cross-reference against the chapter it
points to: does that chapter still contain what is claimed?

## Phase 4 — The Whole-Book Re-Rule

Now read the surrounding chapters (at minimum: the ones this chapter cites and
the ones that cite it). Re-rule every objection: does groundwork elsewhere
answer it, duplicate it, or contradict it? Specifically check: promises paid
("the rest of this chapter/book will show...") — is the warrant actually
delivered, and where; concessions honored — is an admission made here quietly
revoked in a later chapter's summary; duplication — is the same argument made
twice at full length, and which version is stronger; and doctrine-consistency —
does another chapter ground the same point better (if so, the fix is to conform
this chapter to the book's own best treatment, not to invent a new one).

## Phase 5 — The Report

Failures only — no praise, nothing that already works, no softening. Rank
findings by severity. End with two things: (1) the single strongest objection
the chapter currently cannot answer, stated in one paragraph as the critic
would actually write it; and (2) an honest verdict — would a genuinely
open-minded, intelligent skeptic who reads this chapter carefully be moved:
yes or no, and why.

## Phase 6 — The Audience Triage

The ranking above is for the strongest possible critic. The book's actual
audience is a Catholic catechist or an everyday Catholic in the pew who wants a
deeper understanding of the faith — not atheists or academics. So finish by
re-triaging every finding into four bins, and be strict about the boundary:

- **Tier A — must fix:** Catholic doctrinal imprecision of any kind, and every
  factual, quotation, or cross-reference error a hostile reader could quote.
  These fixes must be surgical and invisible to the book's tone.
- **Tier B — should fix:** internal consistency, unpaid promises, overclaim
  calibration ("proves" → what the text actually earns), and any repair that
  can be made pastorally (a homily line, not a seminar).
- **Tier C — optional polish:** the author's call, stated in one line each.
- **Tier D — the succinct answer:** for every finding ruled DAMAGED or
  UNANSWERED — including those binned as Accepted — state what it would take
  to actually answer the skeptic, as succinctly as possible. Each Tier D entry
  has three parts: the answer itself, compressed to its minimum honest form
  (aim for one to three sentences of the author's-register prose — if it cannot
  be said that briefly, say so and give the shortest faithful version); where
  it would live (main text as a pastoral line, the Chapter 11–12 apologetics
  block, a Further Study pointer, or a future companion volume); and the cost
  (word count, tone risk, new sources to verify). Tier D is not a commitment to
  make the change — it is the menu, priced, so the author can see for every
  standing objection exactly what answering it would take before deciding
  whether it is worth it.
- **Accepted as-is:** findings that are real against the strongest critic but
  wrong to fix for this audience — record each with its reason, so the
  decision is made once, on purpose, and never relitigated. (These still
  receive a Tier D entry: accepting an objection and knowing its answer are
  different things, and the author should hold both.)

Never propose making the book more technical, adding philosophical apparatus,
or answering academic critics at length in the main text. Where a deep answer
exists, the main text gets the pastoral form and the depth goes to the book's
designated apologetics chapters (11–12) or a Further Study pointer.

## Deliverable

An annotatable review document (rich HTML via the book's standard review
tooling if available in the session; otherwise clean markdown), saved to
`chapters/reports/`, with every objection, quote, ruling, and triage bin —
so the author can approve, veto, or reword each item before anything becomes
an edit. Nothing is ever applied to a chapter file without a redline diff
approved first. All proposed replacement text is a draft in the author's
register; the author's rewording always wins.
