## The Question That Won't Go Away

We've spent eleven chapters unpacking what God's perfect love is: the sacred gift of covenant which binds truth, justice, mercy and sacrifice into life-giving communion. We've seen how each facet combats specific forms of evil. We've explored how this love creates authentic relationships and transforms human hearts, and, in Chapter 11, how prudence puts it into practice in the hardest cases.

Before we go further, we need to pause and check the ground beneath our feet.

Everything we've explored—that love is real for everyone, that its demands bind us, that it is sacred—only holds if God actually exists. This is not a detour. This is the foundation check. We've been building on the claim that love is a law as real and universal as gravity. But laws require lawgivers. If that claim is wrong, the whole building falls. Everything else depends on this question.

*How do we know God exists?* And how do we know the law of love is not just evolutionary programming or cultural convention?

It's a fair question. And here is the remarkable answer: the very definition of love we've been exploring *provides the argument*. This chapter presents the **moral argument**: if love is an objective law—true and binding whether anyone agrees or not—it points to a Lawgiver. We don't have to assume God to begin—we can reason our way to God from the reality of love itself. The Church calls such reasoning "converging and convincing arguments," which "allow us to attain certainty about the truth," though they are not proofs of the kind the natural sciences give (CCC 31). They do not replace faith, but they "can predispose one to faith and help one to see that faith is not opposed to reason" (CCC 35).

## The Classical Moral Argument

The moral argument belongs to a long classical tradition of reasoning from the world and from the human heart to God (CCC 31-36). In its simplest form, it goes like this:

1. **If God does not exist, objective moral values and duties do not exist.**
2. **Objective moral values and duties do exist.**
3. **Therefore, God exists.**

The logic holds: if both premises are true, the conclusion must be true. So everything turns on the premises. Nearly everyone lives by the second, even many who deny it in theory. The first is where the real debate lies. We take the second premise first.

### Premise 2: Objective Moral Values and Duties Do Exist

Our definition of love provides powerful evidence. Love requires:

**Truth:** When a spouse hides an affair with lies, you don't treat honesty as “personal preference.” You recognize a real obligation was violated—betrayal only makes sense if truth is objectively required. And truth binds the mind as well as the tongue. We may not believe a comforting falsehood, even if no one would ever know, and we may not stop looking because we fear what we will find. "Follow the evidence" is not advice for people who happen to like the truth. It is something every reasoning mind ought to do (cf. CCC 2104).

**Justice:** When someone murders your child, your outrage appeals to more than emotion—you recognize an objective wrong. Every “That’s not fair!” assumes a real standard. And some things are wrong for everyone: torturing a child for fun, betraying a friend for gain, enslaving the weak. They would be wrong even if every culture on earth approved them, and no one's consent, given before or after, could make them right.

**Mercy:** Forgiveness only makes sense if there was something objectively wrong to forgive. And notice whom forgiveness faces. Suppose I promise my dying friend to keep a secret she trusted to me. After her death I tell it for a laugh to a stranger in a far city, who never knew her and will never repeat it. No one on earth is any the worse for it. Still, she was wronged. A wrong sum needs correcting; a wrong done to a person needs forgiving. Done knowingly and freely, it leaves me guilty, not merely mistaken. Shame looks only at itself and says *I am less*. Guilt looks at her and says *I wronged her*, and only guilt asks her pardon.

**Sacrifice:** We call the firefighter who dies saving a child a hero—because we recognize self-gift for another is objectively good. And the same love that gives itself away draws lines it will never cross, not to save itself and not to buy a better outcome for many. A surgeon may not kill one healthy patient to give his organs to five, even if no one would ever know. A city may not hang an innocent man to calm a riot. Almost everyone sees this at once: side effects may be weighed, but persons may not be spent. The Church holds that line at every number, and even against a person's own request to die. She teaches that it belongs to the natural law: the right and wrong God wrote into human nature itself, which reason can know on its own (*Evangelium Vitae* 57, 65–66). Not everyone holds it that far; many who would never hang the innocent man would grant a dying man's request to die. But the line is not a private rule for Catholics. It belongs to the same law as the rest.

**Covenant, Life-giving, and Communion:** Broken vows and broken promises aren’t just broken words. They wound because we know we were meant to be bound in faithful, life-giving communion.

Authentic love—the sacred gift of covenant which binds truth, justice, mercy and sacrifice into life-giving communion—cannot be reduced to evolutionary programming, cultural convention, or personal preference. These are objective requirements that point to an objective moral order.
</div>

Every facet of love reveals objective moral truth.

And beneath all of these lies the truth we may be surest of. Every human being has the same dignity, whatever he can or cannot do: the newborn, the dying, the severely disabled, even the enemy. Not the same gifts, and not the same holiness, but the same worth. Our instincts do not teach us this. They favor our own, the healthy and the beautiful, and whatever is before our eyes. The ancient world left weak and deformed newborns on hillsides to die. So the test comes where instinct fails: the infant no one is drawn to hold. There we learn whether what we meant was worth or only attraction. The Church teaches that this dignity can be known by reason alone (*Dignitas Infinita* 1), though history shows how late it was seen and how easily that sight can be lost again (cf. *Dignitas Infinita* 10, 13, 32).

Some deny this premise outright. Nothing, they say, is right or wrong for everyone; there are only the values a person or a people happens to hold. Then nothing is left to say that cruelty is worse than kindness, except that more of us happen to prefer kindness. This is relativism, and it is the full weight behind what we called the *dictatorship of relativism* in Chapter 5, on how love binds. Chapters 6–9 traced it through truth, justice, mercy and sacrifice. The phrase is Cardinal Joseph Ratzinger's: on the eve of his election as Pope Benedict XVI, he warned of a relativism "that does not recognize anything as definitive and whose ultimate goal consists solely of one's own ego and desires."<sup>1</sup> His point is not a mood but a logical claim: once objective moral truth is denied, no principled line can be drawn against tyranny. Sentiment, consensus, and usefulness can all *feel* like standards, but when they conflict — and they always do — nothing settles it but force. Relativism is not the absence of a ruler; it is the ruler's disguise. St. John Paul II traced the same line a decade earlier: "when the sense of God is lost, the sense of man is also threatened and poisoned" (*Evangelium Vitae* 22). And he named its cost:

<div class="blockquote">
"In this way, any reference to common values and to a truth absolutely binding on everyone is lost, and social life ventures on to the shifting sands of complete relativism. At that point, everything is negotiable, everything is open to bargaining: even the first of the fundamental rights, the right to life."

\hfill\small — St. John Paul II, *Evangelium Vitae* §20 (1995)
</div>

Notice what both Popes are arguing. They are not saying relativists are wicked; most are not. They are saying relativism is *unstable* — it cannot bear the weight of the moral claims its own adherents continue to make. The relativist who insists "nothing is objectively wrong" still protests injustice, still mourns betrayal, still weeps at cruelty toward children. Those reactions are not preferences dressed up; they are the voice of a moral law that his theory denies and his heart cannot silence. Premise 2 is simply the refusal to pretend that voice is not real.

### Premise 1: If God Does Not Exist, Objective Moral Values Do Not Exist

Here is where the real debate lies, and we should say so plainly. Many thoughtful people who do not believe in God agree with almost everything in the last section. They hold that torturing a child is truly wrong, that the infant no one holds is worth as much as anyone, and that a person may never be spent for others. Some of them live it more faithfully than many believers do. So we will not defend this premise by announcing that every attempt to ground morality without God fails. We will defend it the way a careful judge weighs a case: by comparing the accounts, asking which one best explains what we actually find, and noticing exactly where each one stops.

And let us be honest, as truth asks us to be: every account of morality stops somewhere. Each rival below stops at something it takes as given. Ours stops at God, who is not one more thing needing an explanation but the One who simply is, and needs nothing else in order to be (cf. Exodus 3:14; CCC 213). The question is which stopping point explains more. Here are the rivals a Catholic is most likely to meet, each described as its best defenders would describe it.

**The materialist.** Many who hold that matter is all there is take one of two roads. Some say plainly that nothing is really right or wrong. Our sense of being bound, guilty and watched, they say, was built into us because it helped our ancestors survive together, and that one cause explains it all. This view is consistent, and its ablest defenders do not tell us to stop caring; they would have us keep morality as a useful human tool. But it comes at a price. On this view, torturing a child for fun is not really wrong. The newborn has no worth except what we are built and taught to give her, and we are least built to give it to the infant no one is drawn to hold. Its ablest defenders accept that price, or argue that it is none.

Others take a sharper road. Evolution, they say, explains why we hold the moral beliefs we do whether or not those beliefs are true, so we have no reason to trust them. And this debunker has an answer to the obvious reply, that evolution shaped our logic too: logic helped our ancestors survive because it was true, while a belief that kin must be cared for would have helped them either way. That is a serious argument. But two things still stand against it, though neither settles the matter alone. First, his argument asks us to give up our moral beliefs because the evidence requires it. Yet the rules of his own thinking — *follow the evidence, don't contradict yourself* — are not things he sees; they are things he *ought* to do. If a duty binds only those who happen to want what it gets them, then *follow the evidence* does not bind the man who fears where the evidence leads. Second, he asks us to give up a belief because of an argument about our ancestors. So look at the infant left on the hillside, and ask which you are surer of: that she was wronged, or any premise of his argument. The trust he keeps for his reasoning and withdraws from his conscience is a weighing, not a finding, and each of us must make it.

**The view that we make our own values.** A second family of views says that moral words do not report facts at all. Some say they voice our deepest attitudes, refined by sympathy, knowledge and consistency; some, that we create our values by our freedom; some, that they are simply our best words for living together, slowly widened to take in the slave, the stranger and the child. Their best defenders will still say, in their own voice, that cruelty is wrong whatever anyone thinks and that the slave was wronged whether or not anyone said so. Some of them stood up against torture when their own country approved it. Where we part is quieter. They decline to say that anything makes those words true beyond what was done to the one who suffered, and they call that question idle. Asked why our circle of concern should widen rather than shrink, they answer from within: because we are people who count cruelty the worst thing we do. To a man who is not, they have nothing more to say. No argument can compel him, ours included; the difference is what he is missing. On the simplest of these views, only a feeling. On the subtlest, a good that is there whether he knows it or not. On ours, Someone too. And on the view that freedom makes our values, the one who will never again be conscious has only the worth our freedom gives her.

**The utilitarian.** Another view says that what matters is well-being, counted impartially. Every pain counts the same, and the stranger counts as much as my own child. It is right that consequences matter; the Church weighs them too, though never first: they can never make an act that is evil in itself good or right (CCC 1754). This view has often spoken up for those whom others passed by: the slave, women, the distant poor. Its best defenders can explain why we should keep firm rules against killing the innocent, and even live by them day to day as if they had no exceptions. But it explains a wrong only as a loss in the total. The betrayal of the dead friend counts, at most, as one frustrated wish among many, not as a wrong done to her that no total can outweigh. And in principle it lets any person be deliberately killed once the numbers grow large enough. It can say that it is good that your life goes well. It values your good, not you.

**Respect for persons.** The noblest of these views begins with the dignity of persons themselves. Every rational being, it says, has a worth beyond price, which admits no equivalent and so can never be traded, not even for many; no one may ever be used merely as a means. A close cousin says that a wrong is whatever could not be justified to each person it touches, above all to the one it is done to. So no total can justify spending one person for others, because each must be answered one by one. These views stand with us on nearly everything in Premise 2. One of them argues that whoever values anything must also value our human power to value. And it proves something real—that we cannot help *treating* ourselves as precious. But what we cannot help doing is not yet a truth about the world. These views must still say why there are beings at all whose reason gives them this law. And they must say why the newborn, who cannot yet reason or answer anyone, stands among those to whom we must answer. Their best defenders give real answers: she has our nature, or she is born of us and is one of our kind. But they take as given why that nature or that bond, and not her powers, should bring her in. We face that question too, and answer it below. And when the one we wronged is gone, they can say she is still owed. But most who hold these views today also hold that nothing of her remains, though the philosopher who first set out this view held that reason must assume the soul lives on. So they cannot say she can ever forgive.

**The godless realist.** The strongest rival is the one we met in Chapter 3, on love's sacred source. He agrees that some things are wrong for everyone, as surely as two and two make four, and that no vote and no feeling makes them so. He stands with us against relativism and against the claim that matter is all there is. And he holds that these truths need nothing behind them: their necessity lies in what pain, persons and promises are, the way the truths of logic need no one to hold them. We owe him the plainest honesty. Every account has a starting point, ours included, and on several points he stands level with us. We part wherever persons are at stake. We part over what we owe the dead: on his account we can honor it, but no one can ever release us from it. We part over why worth follows what a human being is rather than what she can do; many of his ablest allies hold that some human beings, lacking certain powers, are worth less. And we part over minds. On most such accounts, natural selection shaped our minds and no one designed them. We ask why such minds can read truths that hold for every mind, in logic and number as well as in right and wrong. He says the law holds of itself. We say it holds first in a Mind, the same Mind that made the natures it is about and the minds that read it. Against him, this argument offers no refutation, only an account that reaches further and fits together better.

Some of the great traditions of the East, above all those of China, deserve a word of their own, and a charitable one. The Second Vatican Council teaches that the Church "rejects nothing that is true and holy in these religions" (*Nostra Aetate* 2), and that whatever good or truth is found in them she looks upon "as a preparation for the Gospel" (*Lumen Gentium* 16). Some of their teachers saw parts of Premise 2 more clearly than many in the West. Every person, they taught, however weak in body or mind, carries the whole of human nature, as one moon shines whole in ten thousand rivers; the sage abandons no one; and we must ask pardon of the one we wronged. Other traditions of the East, the Council notes, seek freedom through "a flight to God with love and trust" (*Nostra Aetate* 2). From many of their teachers we part over whether anyone at the root of things knows and loves each of us. On their accounts, Heaven and Earth give life to things. On ours, God wanted *you*.

So the comparison is not between a God who explains everything and rivals who explain nothing. Each serious rival explains a great deal, and each stops at something it takes as given; ours stops at God, who alone needs nothing outside Himself to explain Him (cf. CCC 213). The question is which explains more. A personal Good explains at one stroke what the best accounts without Him must explain piece by piece or simply take as given. It explains why the infant no one holds is worth as much as anyone, why the law calls each of us by name, and why the law and the minds that read it fit together. And He answers two things they leave open: the guilt that asks to be forgiven by the one we wronged, even after she is gone, and the longing to be known and loved wholly. Those five are where the roads part. After one famous objection, we will walk each of them.

## The Divine Command Theory Problem

There's a famous objection to this argument, adapted from a question Socrates asks in Plato's *Euthyphro* dialogue: is something good because God commands it, or does God command it because it's good? The first answer is called divine command theory: an act is right or wrong only because God commands or forbids it, so that His command alone makes it so. The second seems to set a standard above God. Chapter 3, on love's sacred source, already answered the dilemma: goodness is neither a standard above God nor an invention of His will. It is His own unchanging nature. God's commands do not create the good; they reveal the Good that He eternally is. And the moral law in our hearts is a share in that same Good: St. Thomas Aquinas calls the natural law "the rational creature's participation of the eternal law" (*Summa Theologiae* I-II, q.91, a.2), that is, of God's own wisdom ordering all things, imprinted on the minds He made.

Love flows from God's nature, not from arbitrary commands; it is the blueprint of reality itself. So right and wrong rest on God's unchanging nature, and human freedom stays whole, because we freely recognize and accept what is true, good, and beautiful.

## Where the Roads Part

Here, then, are the five places where the accounts part. None of them is a proof that compels. In three, one Source explains what the rivals must take as given. The other two, the guilt and the longing, are clues: they show what answer would fit, without proving it.

### The Infant No One Holds

Go back to the infant no one is drawn to hold. Being human explains why she belongs among us. The best accounts without God also explain much of why she may not be killed for others: the wrong would be done to *her*, and no total answers her. But if her worth lies in what she is, and not in what she can do or in what anyone feels for her, then her worth is as much a gift as her very existence is. Here the moral question and the question of creation meet.

And here St. Thomas Aquinas hands us one more treasure, in a single sentence: our love for anything "is not the cause of its goodness," whereas "the love of God infuses and creates goodness" (*Summa Theologiae* I, q.20, a.2). Read that again. Your love responds to goodness—you love your child because she is precious. God's love *creates* goodness—your child is precious because His love called her into being and holds her in being right now. His love is not an opinion about her that might be mistaken; it is the free act by which there is a *her* at all. Our love answers to the worth it finds; the Creator's love is why there is anything to find. Her dignity is truly her own, woven into what she is: rational, free, made for communion with Him. And it is truly a gift, because He gave her all that she is. This is why the Church can teach that every person's dignity "is not conferred subsequently... it is prior to any recognition, and it cannot be lost" (*Dignitas Infinita*, 15). No one else bestowed it on her—not her parents, not the state, not even her own achievements—and so no one can revoke it.

The Council puts it plainly: the human person is "the only creature on earth which God willed for itself" (*Gaudium et Spes* 24). Not for the species, not for the total, not for us. That is why her life is not ours to spend, not even for many. It is held in trust from the One who is "the Lord of life from its beginning until its end" (CCC 2258; cf. 2280). Not every child is wanted by those who conceived her. Every child was wanted by the One who made her, before anyone saw her (cf. Jeremiah 1:5; CCC 2270). As He says through the prophet:

<div class="blockquote">
"Can a woman forget her sucking child, that she should have no compassion on the son of her womb? Even these may forget, yet I will not forget you."

\hfill\small — Isaiah 49:15
</div>

### A Law That Calls Each of Us by Name

The moral law does not reach us the way the tides do. It addresses each of us. The Council describes conscience as the place where a person is "alone with God, Whose voice echoes in his depths" (*Gaudium et Spes* 16). The impersonal accounts can say that each person carries the whole of human nature, and that whoever sees her rightly must answer to her. What they cannot say is that *this* one is wanted for her own sake by One who sees her when no one else does.

And notice: we don't recognize this authority only by argument, the way we might infer some distant lawgiver to explain a law. We live inside it. Chapter 1, on the problem of evil, already named the pattern: sin is "choosing a lesser good over the greatest Good," never a choice of no good at all. The addict, the adulterer, the man who cheats his neighbor: each is reaching for something he believes, in that moment, is worth having. We have been living this pull our whole lives, pursuing what looks like good in every act, faithful or fallen — following a light without yet knowing its name. So when we recognize that the source of that pull has a name, we are not only concluding an argument, though reason can reach it (CCC 36). We are finally learning the name of the light we have been following all along.

This Lawgiver is not a commander who picks rules. He made creatures who can be wronged, and set in their very natures what they are for. So He fixed what love must answer to, not by decree but by what He is, for He is Himself the Good He asks of us (cf. *Veritatis Splendor* 9, 40). The law binds because He is good. The offense against Him can be forgiven because He is Someone. And the one we wronged is kept, because He forgets no one.

### The Law and the Mind That Reads It

If the facets of love are built into us—if even atheists cannot deny their pull—how did they get there? We did not invent them. We discovered them—the way we discovered the laws that hold the stars together and the mathematics our minds are uncannily fitted to read: already there, already binding, waiting to be found.

Evolution explains well why our senses mostly get the world right: a creature that misreads cliffs and lions does not last long. But the heart of the moral law is not fitted to our kind alone. Cruelty for its own sake, betrayal, and treating a person as a thing would be wrong for any creature that can reason and suffer. If so, there are truths about what is good and what we owe that hold for every possible mind, as the truths of logic and number do, and our minds can know them. The Church places the moral law in God's own wisdom, the eternal law (cf. *Veritatis Splendor* 43), and St. Thomas holds that every possible nature is first known there (cf. *Summa Theologiae* I, q.15, aa.2–3). Most of the best rivals answer that such truths need no mind behind them, and that survival and reason together explain why we can read them. A few look instead to an order in nature that tends toward mind. That is a real answer. But it still takes as given the fact it rests on: that minds no one designed can grasp truths that could not be otherwise, and that the law, the minds that read it and the persons it is about all fit together. We hold that one Source of law and mind explains that fit more fully. That is a judgment about where explanation may stop, and the rivals do not share it.

And the moral law does not stand alone. It is one layer in a universe full of law. The one who refuses a Lawgiver must take each layer of that order as given on its own; to recognize Him is to find that all of it, together, finally makes sense. Then notice what this last layer adds that the others cannot. Gravity binds bodies, and mathematics binds quantities—and neither ever asked our consent. But look at what *this* law holds out to us: truth spoken, justice rendered, mercy extended, sacrifice offered. It is not imposed the way gravity is imposed; it is a gift, a law that is fulfilled in us only when it is freely and humbly received. Every clause of it is something only a person can do, and only for a person. And a gift can only come from a Giver. An impersonal source might explain a law for things; a law whose entire content is love points to Someone who loves.

### Guilt That Asks to Be Forgiven

Go back, too, to the friend whose secret I betrayed after her death. The pardon that heals a wrong between persons is hers to give, and she is gone. There are three ways to take that. Some say that what I owed her died with her, and what is left is remorse to be quieted. Others say, nobly, that it outlives her, and the living give back what they can: they keep her memory, honor her wishes and care for those she loved. The Church walks much of that second road. But on that road she is not there to receive what the living give, and many of the dead have no one left to remember them by name. Guilt does not only mourn her. It asks to be forgiven *by her*, and only someone can forgive. The third way is that she is still someone, and can one day receive what I owe her.

That is the Catholic answer, and it begins with God. Every such wrong is also, and before all else, an offense against Him, a rupture of communion with Him (CCC 1440), not because it harms Him, but because His love is refused in her. And God alone forgives sins (CCC 1441), through the Cross of His Son (CCC 613). He forgives first in Baptism, and for grave sins after Baptism ordinarily in the Sacrament of Reconciliation (CCC 977, 1484, 1497; cf. 1452, 1458). When He forgives the repentant sinner, He takes away the whole guilt of the sin, without waiting on her pardon. What remains is not guilt left unforgiven. There remains reparation owed to her, the duty to set right what I can, for every offense against justice and truth "entails the duty of reparation, even if its author has been forgiven" (CCC 2487; cf. 1459). And there remains the reconciliation between us, which I must seek and which God does not grant in her place: that pardon is hers to give (cf. Matthew 5:23–24; 6:12). For sins after Baptism I also still owe satisfaction: the penance, such as prayer, self-denial or works of mercy, by which I make amends for my sin and expiate it, recover my full spiritual health, and help repair the disorder it caused in me and around me. Even after forgiveness, sin leaves an unhealthy attachment to creatures that must still be purified, here or in purgatory, and that purifying frees us from what the Church calls the temporal punishment of sin (CCC 1459, 1472–1473). That satisfaction is made through Christ (CCC 1460). Nor has she ceased to be. Her soul did not perish at death (CCC 366). She lives to God (cf. Luke 20:38), who forgets no one, and on the last day He will raise her body too (CCC 1001). Reason can discern "signs of [the] spiritual soul" (CCC 33), but it cannot prove the hope that she and the one who wronged her will one day be reconciled. So this is a clue, not a proof. Its weight depends on whether you find that what is truly owed to her is something she must one day be able to receive. A truth can be true; only a person can forgive.

### The Longing

Last, the longing. Only a person can love us; a law, a force, an order or an idea cannot. We long to be known and loved wholly, and no one on earth can do it, because no one knows the whole of us, not even ourselves. When you are isolated, you don't just think "I'm lonely"—you *ache*. Science can trace the paths that longing travels in us, but that tells us how we long, not whether there is Someone to long for. What love says to another, as the Catholic philosopher Josef Pieper put it, is "It's good that you exist."<sup>2</sup> And love does not say that for a while only. A longing does not prove its answer, but it shows what answer would fit: a Person who said that word to each of us before anyone else, who knows us wholly, and who loves us first. "We love, because he first loved us" (1 John 4:19). That is not a proof, for we may not believe a thing because it comforts us; yet the Church counts this longing for the infinite among the signs by which we come to know God (CCC 27, 33). And it is where the other accounts stop. If the highest good knows and loves no one, then love, the best thing in us, is answered in the end by no one besides ourselves.

None of these five compels. Taken together, they converge. One Source explains what the best rivals must explain piece by piece or take as given. And that Source is Someone, which is what forgiveness and communion ask for. It does not remove all mystery. It is one mystery, and a mystery that explains.

## The Weight on the Other Side

Honesty cuts both ways. We have asked each rival where its account stops. Now we must show where ours carries its heaviest weight: evil.

Children suffer horribly. Animals suffered for ages before any human chose anything, and pain in nature falls roughly where survival would put it, not where justice would. The wolf lives by the fawn, and the parasite by the child. Many who do not resist God never come to know Him by name in this life, and whether a person does depends far more on where he was born than on how honestly he sought. These weigh on us, not on the impersonal rivals. If no one made the world, there is no one whose goodness her suffering calls into question. We say that Someone willed her for her own sake and could have spared her, and so we must give what account of her we can (cf. 1 Peter 3:15), though His ways are not ours to measure as we would measure a man's (cf. Isaiah 55:8–9; CCC 314).

The Church does not claim a formula that dissolves this: "Only Christian faith as a whole constitutes the answer to this question" (CCC 309). But she says three things, and none of them makes the weight light. First, the evil we do to one another is ours. God is "in no way, directly or indirectly, the cause of moral evil"; He permits it, because He respects the freedom He gave (CCC 311), as Chapter 1 showed. For the suffering no human choice explains, she gives a setting rather than an explanation: God freely willed a world "in a state of journeying" toward its perfection. Until creation reaches that perfection, physical evil exists alongside physical good (CCC 310). Second, His permission is never a trade in which she is spent for someone else's gain. Faith is certain that God "would not permit an evil if he did not cause a good to come from that very evil" (CCC 324), and yet "evil never becomes a good" (CCC 312). Whatever good He draws from it, she is not used as a mere means (cf. *Gaudium et Spes* 24), and the good hoped for is offered to her as well (cf. *Gaudium et Spes* 22). When the first man fell, Pope St. Leo the Great says, Christ's grace gave us more than the devil's envy took away (CCC 412). And we will know God's ways fully only when we see Him "face to face" (CCC 314). Third, the God weighed here is not a designer watching from a distance. He entered suffering Himself: the eternal Son became man and suffered in His own flesh on the Cross. And He will raise the dead (CCC 998). That is not one more argument. It is who the God being weighed actually is. And if He is, the last word over the suffering child is not the suffering.

The hardest form of this weight is personal: if such a Person loves us, why do so many honest seekers not find Him? Some of the most disciplined seekers in history looked inward for a lifetime and found a deep peace, or a principle, but not a Person. The Church does not presume that such seekers are hiding from God (CCC 847), though she knows that any of us can (CCC 29). And the Council teaches that divine providence does not "deny the helps necessary for salvation to those who, without blame on their part, have not yet arrived at an explicit knowledge of God and with His grace strive to live a good life" (*Lumen Gentium* 16). He makes Himself known through the world and the conscience, fully in Christ, and for most of us first through persons: a mother's love before a creed, a neighbor's mercy before a name. But for the infant no one holds, there is no mother's love to carry His love to her. The Church holds that Baptism is necessary for salvation, and that God, who bound salvation to it, is not Himself bound by His sacraments (CCC 1257). So for children who die unbaptized, "the Church can only entrust them to the mercy of God," and she hopes "that there is a way of salvation for children who have died without Baptism" (CCC 1261). All the more urgently, she asks that nothing keep a child from coming to Christ through Baptism. That is part of an answer, not a full answer.

And the faith adds a weight of its own. The Church teaches that hell is real and eternal, that those who die in mortal sin suffer its punishments, "eternal fire," and that its chief punishment is eternal separation from God (CCC 1035). A mortal sin is a choice of something gravely wrong, made with full knowledge and deliberate consent, that destroys charity in the heart and turns a person away from God, his last end (CCC 1855, 1857, 1861). A person can refuse God forever by his own free choice (CCC 1033). God desires all to be saved (cf. 1 Timothy 2:4), and He holds no one to account beyond what that person knew, or could and should have known, and freely chose (CCC 1735, 1791, 1859–1860). "God predestines no one to go to hell; for this, a willful turning away from God (a mortal sin) is necessary, and persistence in it until the end" (CCC 1037). His grace does not take away the will's power to refuse it, and He commands nothing impossible (Council of Trent, Session VI, ch. 5, ch. 11 and can. 4). Yet freedom does not finish the answer, for His grace brought Mary to her yes without forcing it (CCC 488, 490–494). The Church does not explain why one will is won and another refuses to the end. The one who is lost is not spent for anyone's gain; he excludes himself. But reason cannot show how God's permitting that loss fits with His having willed that man for his own sake. The Church teaches that He permits it out of respect for the freedom He gave (CCC 311; cf. 1033, 1037); beyond that, she has not explained it. She prays that no one be lost (CCC 1058), and she does not declare of any human being that he is lost, for without special divine revelation it is not granted to us to know who is (St. John Paul II, General Audience, 28 July 1999). For the one who refuses to the end, this book rests its claim on the Church's word that God predestines no one to hell (CCC 1037), prays with her that none be lost, and bears this weight.

There is one more weight, and a Catholic book must not hide it. Scripture tells of wars in which Israel was told to destroy whole peoples, children too:

<div class="blockquote">
"Now go and smite Amalek, and utterly destroy all that they have; do not spare them, but kill both man and woman, infant and suckling, ox and sheep, camel and ass."

\hfill\small — 1 Samuel 15:3
</div>

How can that stand beside a line that may never be crossed? St. Thomas answers that the precepts of the Ten Commandments "admit of no dispensation whatever" (*Summa Theologiae* I-II, q.100, a.8): no one can grant an exception to them. And this is not an exception. By God's sentence all die, the righteous with the sinners, on account of the sin of our first parent. So a death He decrees is not the undue killing the commandment forbids. One who carried out such a command would be "the executor of that sentence by Divine authority," acting by God's right and never by any right of his own (I-II, q.100, a.8, ad 3; q.94, a.5, ad 2; II-II, q.64, a.6, ad 1). This book holds with him, as Catholic theologians may, though the Church has not defined it. God does not make a wrong right by willing it, and He, who willed each of us for our own sake, uses no one merely as a means (cf. *Gaudium et Spes* 24). The right is never ours: "no one can under any circumstance claim for himself the right directly to destroy an innocent human being" (CCC 2258). And no one may now claim such a command, whatever voice he says he heard, for a private revelation, that is, a message claimed from God after Christ, never belongs to the deposit of faith, and the faith cannot accept one that claims to surpass or correct the Revelation of which Christ is the fulfillment (CCC 67). Christ laid down His life and took no one's.

The Church holds those pages inspired: God is their author, and the human writers He chose are true authors too, who wrote everything and only what He wanted. Since everything the sacred writers assert is asserted by the Holy Spirit, those books teach "solidly, faithfully and without error that truth which God wanted put into sacred writings for the sake of salvation" (*Dei Verbum* 11). She asks us to seek what their human authors meant through the "literary forms" of their age, the ways of writing their first readers knew (*Dei Verbum* 12). She also teaches that the books of the Old Testament, though inspired, "contain some things which are incomplete and temporary" (*Dei Verbum* 15), and that in such pages "revelation is suited to the cultural and moral level of distant times"; they are to be read in their historical and literary context and in the light of Christ's Paschal Mystery (Benedict XVI, *Verbum Domini* 42). Even so, reason can see only that the Lord of life does no wrong in calling a life home, not why He would call these lives home by these hands. Many who stand with us on the dignity of persons part from us here. Some hold that a holy God could never give such a command, so that whoever heard it must have mistaken another voice for His; others, that such a death would still be the killing the line forbids. To the first, St. Thomas points to Abraham, who would never have prepared to sacrifice his son had he not been "most certain" the command was God's (II-II, q.171, a.5). A reader weighing by reason alone may be surer that those children were wronged than of any argument that they were not; for him it is a cost against the case, not only a mystery. For the believer, faith is the more certain (CCC 157), and no real conflict between faith and reason is possible (CCC 159): these pages are inspired, and what their sacred authors assert they teach without error, and, whatever the right reading of their literary form, God does no injustice.

One thing more belongs here, as an invitation, not a reply. Whoever protests against God on the suffering child's behalf does not refute himself; his protest is this weight at its strongest. But ask him, gently, why. Why is what was done to her really wrong, whoever approves it? Why could no one make it right? Why was she worth as much as anyone, though she could give nothing back? His indignation stands on the very ground this argument starts from. Follow it to its source. What rises in him at injustice is a hunger for truth told, justice done, mercy given, and a love that gives itself for the one who suffers. That is what the Author of the law is. And in His truth, justice, mercy and self-giving lies the only way to fight injustice rightly: not by becoming cruel against the cruel, but by loving as He loves. The invitation is to come and see where that hunger leads.

## Conclusion: Therefore, God Exists

If objective moral values exist (premise 2), and if they can only exist if God exists (premise 1), then God must exist. The logic is not in doubt; the weight rests on the first premise. So, weighing both sides, here is this book's judgment: the best explanation of love as we actually find it is a Lawgiver who is Love. Evil weighs against that conclusion, heavily; it does not overturn it. Against the godless realist the case is closest. Evil weighs nothing against him, for on his account no one permitted it, and this chapter alone does not settle the question between us. An honest reader may weigh this argument differently. The Church does not presume that he is at fault, and she leaves the judgment of every heart to God (*Lumen Gentium* 16; CCC 847, 1861).

She also teaches that God can be known with certainty from the things He has made, by the natural light of human reason (CCC 36, citing Vatican I, *Dei Filius* ch. 2), and that arguments of this kind can bring us to certainty (CCC 31). That certainty is not the kind the natural sciences give (CCC 31), and no argument forces itself on a mind that will not receive it. For many it comes, as St. John Henry Newman saw, from many signs converging in one mind (*An Essay in Aid of a Grammar of Assent*, ch. 8–9). Each of us must reach it with his own reason, and many of us find that hard (CCC 37). That is why God has revealed even truths that reason can reach, so that all may know them "with ease, with firm certainty and with no admixture of error" (CCC 38; cf. Vatican I, *Dei Filius* ch. 2). The believer does not wait on this weighing. He believes because of the authority of God who reveals; faith is more certain than all human knowledge (CCC 156–157), and there can never be any real discrepancy between faith and reason (CCC 159).

To the one who gives up the infant's worth, the argument says only this. Look at the newborn the ancient world left on the hillside, weak and not yet able to know herself. Ask which you are surer of: that she was wronged when she was left there, before anyone said so, and wronged no less than a strong child left beside her; or any argument that says she was not. If she was, her worth never followed her strength, or her mind, or how much any of us cared for her, for none of us did. It follows what she is and what she is made for, and she is that as fully as you are. What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it.

The very love we've been exploring—with its objective requirements for truth, justice, mercy and sacrifice—points to a Source beyond this world. More than that: these requirements are built into us, written on our hearts, part of our very nature. And that nature didn't create itself.

## Natural Law: God's Moral Order Written on Hearts

St. Paul explains how this works: "When Gentiles who have not the law do by nature what the law requires, they are a law to themselves, even though they do not have the law. They show that what the law requires is written on their hearts, while their conscience also bears witness and their conflicting thoughts accuse or perhaps excuse them" (Romans 2:14-15).

This is **natural law**—the moral order God has written on every human heart. It's why people across all cultures recognize basic moral truths: murder is wrong, promises should be kept, children should be protected.

This explains why atheists can be moral people—they're discovering the moral order God has written on their hearts. But knowing a moral truth and explaining why it is true are different things. Atheists can recognize that murder is wrong, and the best of them hold that it binds all people in all circumstances. But, as we have seen, their accounts stop short of anyone behind that bond: no one before whom we are guilty, no one who can forgive, no one who wanted each of us. A standard with no one behind it can be enforced, and it can even win devotion, but it can never love anyone back. Communion happens only between persons, and the Truth that draws us is a Person (cf. John 14:6).

This raises a question worth facing directly: if the law of love is written on every heart, can someone who follows that light — a Buddhist, an atheist, a person who never heard the Gospel — be saved? The Church's answer is careful and honest. All salvation comes from Christ the Head through the Church which is His Body, and the Church "is necessary for salvation: the one Christ is the mediator and the way of salvation; he is present to us in his body which is the Church" (CCC 846). Truth, justice, mercy and sacrifice are not tasks that save us once we finish them; they are the shape of love as God designed it. And the Catholic Church teaches most fully and most clearly what love requires, down to what breaks love and how it is restored, though the judgment of each heart belongs to God (CCC 1861).

But God's grace is not imprisoned by human ignorance. "God has bound salvation to the sacrament of Baptism, but he himself is not bound by his sacraments" (CCC 1257). And so the Church teaches that "those who, through no fault of their own, do not know the Gospel of Christ or his Church, but who nevertheless seek God with a sincere heart, and, moved by grace, try in their actions to do his will as they know it through the dictates of their conscience — those too may achieve eternal salvation" (CCC 847, quoting *Lumen Gentium* 16). Such people are, in a real sense, doing the will of the Father, even without yet knowing the Father's name, and whatever grace reaches them is Christ's grace. Why does the Church hold this hope? Because "since Christ died for all, and since all men are in fact called to one and the same destiny, which is divine, we must hold that the Holy Spirit offers to all the possibility of being made partakers, in a way known to God, of the Paschal mystery" (CCC 1260), that is, of Christ's dying and rising. Every person bears the same God-given dignity. God's justice does not hold against anyone what he could not know. And His mercy can be at work in the love a person pours out for God, neighbor and self before he has the words to say why. That love does not save by itself; it is grace already at work, and in ways known to Himself God can lead such a person "to that faith without which it is impossible to please him" (CCC 848; cf. Hebrews 11:6).

The Council is just as honest that "often men, deceived by the Evil One, have become vain in their reasonings" (*Lumen Gentium* 16), and the Church "still has the obligation and also the sacred right to evangelize all men" (CCC 848). None of this cancels the necessity of the Church. It only makes it more urgent to bring the fullness of the truth to those who are already following its first faint light.

## The Integration Points to Intelligence

Love holds together what is sacred, covenant, truth, justice, mercy, sacrifice and life-giving communion as one coherent whole, and that points to intelligence. These facets could conflict—truth could oppose mercy, justice could crush it—but in God's perfect love they are not parts kept in balance; they are one. And the reverse is just as telling: every philosophy or ideology that tears one facet away from the others, truth without mercy or mercy without truth, breaks down when it is lived. Chapters 6–9 showed this one facet at a time. This kind of integration doesn't happen by accident. The very coherence of authentic love points to the coherent mind of the Creator.

## From Moral Argument to Personal Encounter

For those who live by love, who feel its demands and see how it holds together as one, God stops looking like a hypothesis and starts looking like the ground they have been standing on all along. As the verse that opens this chapter says, God's eternal power and deity have been clearly perceived in the things He has made (cf. Romans 1:20).

Yet some still don't believe—not because the evidence is insufficient. Sometimes the reason is a wound or an ignorance that is no fault of their own; sometimes it is what St. Paul warns of, holding down a truth we would rather not face (cf. Romans 1:18). Atheism, in its usual materialist form, asks us to believe that mind, meaning and the moral law are, at bottom, matter in motion. But notice how it makes its case: it appeals to our reason, asks us to weigh the evidence and to follow the truth, and none of those is made of matter. The materialist must use what is not material to convince us that nothing is real but matter.

This isn't an intellectual exercise—it's an invitation to encounter the God we've been exploring throughout this book. Every time you recognize authentic love, you're meeting a gift that comes from God, for love is of God (cf. 1 John 4:7). Every time conscience rightly calls you to the good, you're hearing the echo of God's voice (*Gaudium et Spes* 16). Every time you give yourself for another, you're sharing in the self-giving love that God is.

The question isn't just "Does God exist?" but "Does love exist at all?" Love is a law. It flows from God's nature, and we freely recognize it as true. The One who made our hearts wrote it on them, and it frees us to become who we were created to be. As Jesus promised, those who continue in His word will know the truth, and the truth will make them free (cf. John 8:31-32).

<div class="callout" style="background-color: #073642; padding: 26px 34px; margin: 34px 0; border-radius: 14px; font-size: 18px; line-height: 1.45; color: #2aa198; border-top: 4px solid #2aa198; border-bottom: 4px solid #2aa198; box-shadow: 0 0 18px rgba(42, 161, 152, 0.12);">
Weighed honestly, love as we actually find it—binding on everyone, owed to persons, calling each of us by name—is best explained by a Lawgiver who is Love. Every genuine act of love is an encounter with the God who is Love, the Author of the law written on our hearts.
</div>

## The Law of the Gospel: Freedom Through Love

And in Christ, God brings that law to its fullness. St. Paul calls it the law of Christ: "Bear one another's burdens, and so fulfil the law of Christ" (Galatians 6:2). The Church calls it **the New Law**, or **the Law of the Gospel**: "the perfection here on earth of the divine law, natural and revealed" (CCC 1965). It is not a burden imposed from outside, but the interior law of love that the Holy Spirit writes on the hearts of those who believe (CCC 1965-1966). As St. Paul tells us:

<div class="blockquote">
"For the law of the Spirit of life in Christ Jesus has set me free from the law of sin and death."

\hfill\small — Romans 8:2
</div>

This is the New Law that Jesus proclaimed—a perfection and fulfillment of the Old Law. It's the law of freedom, the law of grace, the law of love. And here's what makes it revolutionary: **moved by the grace that is this law, we freely submit to it because we recognize it as true, good, and beautiful.** It's not imposed by force but offered as a gift that we freely accept. It is not followed out of fear, but out of love (CCC 1972), for perfect love casts out fear (cf. 1 John 4:18).

When we say "yes" to this law, we're not enslaved—we're liberated. We become who we were always meant to be.

Think of it this way: A fish is most free when it swims in water, conforming to its nature. Try to "liberate" it onto land, and you've destroyed its freedom entirely. The same is true for us—we're most free when we conform to the law of love written on our hearts. It's not a restriction on our freedom but the very condition for it.

The Catechism teaches that the Law of the Gospel is "the grace of the Holy Spirit received by faith in Christ, operating through charity" (CCC 1983). It works through love and expresses itself most perfectly in the Sermon on the Mount.

The Law of the Gospel calls us to an interior transformation that enables us to love as God loves—with truth, justice, mercy and sacrifice perfectly integrated and for perfect communion with God and each other.

## The Beatitudes: The Heart of the New Law

But what does this interior transformation actually look like? Jesus did not leave us guessing. He climbed a mountain, sat down as a teacher in Israel, and painted the portrait Himself. The Beatitudes are not eight unrelated slogans for nice people; the Catechism places them "at the heart of Jesus' preaching" (CCC 1716), the charter of the New Law that the whole Sermon on the Mount unfolds.

<div class="blockquote">
"Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven. Blessed are those who mourn, for they shall be comforted. Blessed are the meek, for they shall inherit the earth. Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, for they shall be satisfied. Blessed are the merciful, for they shall obtain mercy. Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God. Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called sons of God. Blessed are those who are persecuted for righteousness' sake, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven."

\hfill\small — Matthew 5:3-10
</div>

Read them slowly and something becomes clear: this is not a list of separate virtues to accumulate. It is one face seen from eight angles. As Pope Benedict XVI observed, the Beatitudes are "a sort of veiled interior biography of Jesus, a kind of portrait of his figure" (*Jesus of Nazareth*, 2007, ch. 4, p. 74)—they describe Christ before they describe us, because they are simply what perfect love looks like when it walks. To live the Beatitudes is not to complete eight tasks but to be conformed to one Person.

And when we look closely, truth, justice, mercy and sacrifice, which we have been tracing all along, are all here—not bolted together, but radiating from a single life:

**Truth** shows itself in *"Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven"* and *"Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God."* Poverty of spirit is the truth about ourselves spoken without flinching: we are creatures, not gods; everything we have is gift. Purity of heart is the undivided gaze that wants one thing only—and so finally sees what is real. Truth begins in humility and ends in vision.

**Justice** shows itself in *"Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, for they shall be satisfied."* The word Matthew uses, *dikaiosynē*, means both righteousness and justice. Notice the verbs Jesus chose: *hunger* and *thirst*. He doesn't bless those who merely think justice is a good idea; He blesses those who crave it the way a starving person craves bread—who *ache* to see every person given their due and every wrong set right, as God Himself wills.

**Mercy** shows itself in *"Blessed are the merciful, for they shall obtain mercy"*; *"Blessed are those who mourn, for they shall be comforted."* Here love bends toward the wounded—grieving with those who suffer, forgiving those who fail, refusing to leave anyone in their brokenness. And mercy given is mercy received: the measure we use is measured back to us (cf. Matthew 7:2).

And **Sacrifice** shows itself in *"Blessed are the meek, for they shall inherit the earth"* and *"Blessed are those who are persecuted for righteousness' sake, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven."* Meekness is not weakness; it is strength laid down, the refusal to grasp and dominate. The willingness to be persecuted for what is right is sacrifice in its starkest form—love that costs everything and gives it anyway. This is the road that ends at the Cross.

The peacemaker—*"Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called sons of God"*—is the one in whom all four have become one, for peace is not the absence of conflict but the fruit of truth, justice, mercy and sacrifice held together. This is *shalom*, the Bible's word for peace as wholeness: the communion the whole book—and the entire Christian faith and life—has been pointing toward.

Then Jesus presses further still. He shifts from speaking *about* the blessed to speaking directly *to* them: *"Blessed are you when men revile you and persecute you and utter all kinds of evil against you falsely on my account. Rejoice and be glad, for your reward is great in heaven, for so men persecuted the prophets who were before you"* (Matthew 5:11-12). The persecution beatitude widens into something deeply personal—no longer only suffering *for righteousness* but suffering *for Christ Himself*—and the response He commands is not grim endurance but joy. This is love so certain of its reward that it can sing in the fire.

And here is what makes the whole sermon so startling: the Beatitudes are the exact inverse of what our culture calls blessed. The world says *blessed are the rich, for they can buy whatever they want; blessed are the powerful, for they bend others to their will; blessed are the comfortable, the admired, the self-sufficient*—the ones who never have to mourn, never have to hunger, never have to forgive. Jesus turns the whole scoreboard upside down. Every counterfeit love this book will go on to diagnose runs on the world's version of blessedness—and every one of them leaves the heart emptier than it found it. The Beatitudes are not a harder road to the same happiness the world promises; they are the only road to a happiness the world can neither give nor take away.

Notice, too, what every Beatitude promises. *Theirs is the kingdom. They shall see God. They shall be called sons of God.* The reward is not a prize stacked on top of the virtue; the reward *is* communion with God Himself. This is why the Church teaches that the Beatitudes answer "the natural desire for happiness": God "has placed it in the human heart in order to draw man to the One who alone can fulfill it" (CCC 1718). The very word *blessed*—*beatus*, the root of *beatitude*—means the deep happiness we were made for. Jesus is not loading burdens onto us. He is telling us where joy actually lives, and the path runs straight through the kind of love this book has been describing all along. This is the call to be perfect as our heavenly Father is perfect (cf. Matthew 5:48; Chapter 10, on communion), now given flesh: not a flawless record, but a heart so conformed to Christ that His own blessedness—His own love—cleanses us, completes us and radiates through us.

## Your Mission: Help Others Recognize the Law Written on Their Hearts

If you've recognized the Law of the Gospel in your own life—if you've experienced the freedom that comes from conforming to God's love—you have a mission: **help others recognize what's already written on their hearts.**

Every person you meet already lives, imperfectly, by the natural law written on their heart—it's the water they swim in. When they speak truth, demand justice, show mercy, or sacrifice for their children, they're encountering the God who is Love.

**Our role as good Catholics isn't to present this law as something the Church imposes from outside, but to show people that this law, which the Church authentically teaches and interprets (CCC 2036), is already written on their hearts, and that it is a gift that is freely received from the Author of the law who is the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.** It's to gently help them recognize what they already partially know deep within and to help them submit to the fullness of this law.

**With non-believers:** Don't start by arguing theology. Start by affirming what they already recognize. "You believe that's wrong—why? What makes it objectively wrong?" Help them see that their moral convictions point to something beyond themselves. The law is already written on their hearts; you're just helping them read it.

**With struggling Christians:** Many know the rules but haven't encountered the freedom. They see Christianity as burden, not liberation. Show them—through your own life—that conforming to God's law isn't slavery but the condition for flourishing—the fish set free in the water it was made for.

**Your witness matters more than your words.** When you live authentically—integrating truth, justice, mercy and sacrifice in your daily life—others see the Law of the Gospel made visible. To live authentically is to live in communion with God, the Author and Authority of all that is good, so that His perfect love flows through you to help others live in that same communion. Your self-giving love becomes evidence that God exists. Your joy in obedience becomes an invitation to freedom.

That perfection is not a burden but the fulfillment of your nature—and your deepest freedom, for to be free is to be rightly ordered toward love. You were created to love as God loves—perfectly integrating all the facets we've explored. The same Christ who came not to abolish the law but to fulfill it (cf. Matthew 5:17) came also that we might have life—and so love—in abundance (cf. John 10:10). And when you pursue this perfection through grace, **others will come to know God through your sacrifice.**

This is how the Gospel spreads: not through coercion, but through witness. Not by imposing law from outside, but by helping others recognize the law already written within. Not by argument alone, but by lives that demonstrate the freedom and flourishing that come from conforming to God's love.

## The Bridge to Faith Working Through Love

If God's nature is love, then growing in virtue means conforming our lives to that love—and vices are whatever distorts or denies it. In Chapter 13, "Faith Works Through Love," we turn to the virtues, the steady habits through which faith works through love (cf. Galatians 5:6), and see how each one holds truth, justice, mercy and sacrifice together.

- **Acts 17:22-31:** Paul's sermon to the Athenians on the unknown God whom they worship in ignorance
- **Psalm 19:1-4:** Creation's silent witness to the Creator, opening with "The heavens are telling the glory of God; and the firmament proclaims his handiwork" (v. 1)
- **1 John 4:7-8, 16:** Love comes from God, for God is love, and whoever abides in love abides in God
- **1 John 4:18:** Perfect love casts out fear - the New Law is a law of love, not of fear (CCC 1972)
- **John 10:10:** Christ came that we may have life, and have it abundantly
- **John 14:6:** Christ is the way, and the truth, and the life - the Truth that draws us is a Person
- **Matthew 5:23-24:** Before offering a gift at the altar, first go and be reconciled to the brother who has something against you - the reconciliation we must seek with the one we wronged
- **Matthew 6:12:** In the Lord's Prayer we ask to be forgiven as we forgive those who have wronged us - the pardon we are asked to give one another
- **Jeremiah 1:5:** God knew Jeremiah before He formed him in the womb - read by the Church as true of every human life (CCC 2270)
- **Isaiah 49:15:** Though a mother forget her nursing child, God will not forget us
- **Isaiah 55:8-9:** God's thoughts are not our thoughts, and His ways are not our ways
- **Exodus 3:14:** God reveals His name to Moses: He is the One who is (CCC 213)

- **Luke 20:38:** God is not God of the dead but of the living, for all live to Him
- **1 Timothy 2:4:** God desires all to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth
- **1 Peter 3:15:** Always be ready to answer anyone who asks the reason for the hope that is in us
- **1 John 4:19:** "We love, because he first loved us" - the longing of the heart answered by the One who loved us first

### Catechism of the Catholic Church
- **CCC 27-43:** The desire for God and humanity's capacity to know God through reason and creation; arguments that are "converging and convincing" and can bring us to certainty (31, 36)
- **CCC 1965-1974:** The Law of the Gospel as the New Law; the grace of the Holy Spirit received by faith in Christ
- **CCC 1716-1729:** The Beatitudes - at the heart of Jesus' preaching, depicting the countenance of Christ and answering the desire for happiness God placed in the human heart
- **CCC 1966, 1972, 1983:** The New Law is the grace of the Holy Spirit received by faith in Christ, operating through charity (1983); a law of love, of grace and of freedom (1972)
- **CCC 846-848:** All salvation comes from Christ through His Church; those who, through no fault of their own, do not know Him; the Church's duty to evangelize all
- **CCC 1257-1261:** The necessity of Baptism, God who is not bound by His sacraments, and the hope for children who die without Baptism
- **CCC 1954-1960:** Natural law as participation in God's wisdom; moral law written on human hearts
- **CCC 1776-1794:** Conscience as the voice of God within; moral judgment and formation of conscience
- **CCC 1950-1953:** The moral law as God's fatherly instruction; various expressions of moral law
- **CCC 67:** No private revelation can surpass or correct the Revelation of which Christ is the fulfillment
- **CCC 156-159:** Faith is more certain than all human knowledge, and faith and reason can never truly conflict
- **CCC 213:** God alone is: the revelation of the divine name
- **CCC 309-314, 324:** The scandal of evil - only Christian faith as a whole answers it; God is in no way the cause of moral evil; He would not permit an evil unless He drew a good from that very evil
- **CCC 412:** Why God did not prevent the first sin: Christ's grace gave us more than the devil's envy took away (Pope St. Leo the Great)
- **CCC 366, 998, 1001:** The soul does not perish at death; God will raise the dead on the last day
- **CCC 488, 490-494:** Mary's free yes, brought about by grace without force
- **CCC 613, 977, 1440-1442, 1452, 1458, 1484, 1497:** God alone forgives sins, through the Cross of His Son; sin as an offense against God; Baptism and the Sacrament of Reconciliation, the ordinary way of forgiveness for grave sins; contrition, and the confession of everyday faults
- **CCC 1459-1460, 1472-1473, 2487:** Reparation and satisfaction still owed after forgiveness, satisfaction made through Christ; the temporal punishment that remains after forgiveness
- **CCC 1033-1037, 1058:** Hell as definitive self-exclusion from God; God predestines no one to hell; the Church prays that no one be lost
- **CCC 1735, 1791, 1855, 1857, 1859-1861:** What makes a sin mortal; ignorance and freedom in moral responsibility; the judgment of persons left to God
- **CCC 1754:** Consequences are secondary elements that can never make right an act evil in itself
- **CCC 2036:** The Church's teaching authority extends to the precepts of the natural law
- **CCC 2104:** Every person is bound to seek the truth and to hold to it
- **CCC 2258, 2270, 2280:** God alone is the Lord of life; no one may claim the right directly to destroy an innocent human being; every life known by God before birth; we are stewards of life, not its owners

### Christian Thinkers
- **St. Thomas Aquinas, Doctor of the Church (c. 1225-1274):** His *Summa Theologiae* presents the classical arguments for God's existence (I, q.2, a.3), explains natural law as participation in God's eternal law (I-II, q.91, a.2), teaches that God's love creates the goodness it loves (I, q.20, a.2) and that every possible nature is first known in God's wisdom (I, q.15, aa.2–3), and holds that the Ten Commandments admit no dispensation, so that one who carries out a death God decrees acts by God's right and never his own (I-II, q.100, a.8 and ad 3; q.94, a.5, ad 2; II-II, q.64, a.6, ad 1), and that Abraham was "most certain" the command was God's (II-II, q.171, a.5); his treatment of the virtues shows how they align us with God's nature
- **Pope Benedict XVI (1927-2022):** *Jesus of Nazareth* (2007), ch. 4, reads the Sermon on the Mount and the Beatitudes as a portrait of Christ Himself
- **Peter Kreeft (1937-present), Catholic philosopher at Boston College:** His *A Refutation of Moral Relativism* presents the moral argument through Socratic dialogue, making it accessible to modern readers
- **Pope St. Leo the Great, Doctor of the Church (c. 400-461):** Taught that Christ's grace gave us greater blessings than the devil's envy had taken away (quoted in CCC 412)
- **St. John Henry Newman (1801-1890):** *An Essay in Aid of a Grammar of Assent* (1870), ch. 8-9, on how certainty grows from many signs converging in one mind
- **Josef Pieper (1904-1997), Catholic philosopher:** *Faith, Hope, Love*, on what love says to another: that it is good that you exist

### Encyclicals & Church Documents
- **Second Vatican Council, *Lumen Gentium* 16 (1964):** How those who have not yet received the Gospel are related to the People of God; whatever good or truth is found among them is a preparation for the Gospel
- **St. John Paul II, *Evangelium Vitae* 20-22, 57, 65-66 (1995):** How losing the sense of God threatens the sense of man and leaves even the right to life open to bargaining; the direct killing of the innocent, euthanasia and assisted suicide, as contrary to the natural law that reason can know
- **Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith, *Dignitas Infinita* 1, 10, 13, 15, 32 (2024):** Human dignity can be known by reason alone; how slowly it was recognized, and the risk of regression; it is not conferred by others, is prior to any recognition and cannot be lost
- **Cardinal Joseph Ratzinger, Homily at the Mass *Pro Eligendo Romano Pontifice* (April 18, 2005):** The "dictatorship of relativism"
- **Council of Trent, Session VI (1547):** On justification - grace does not take away the will's power to refuse it, and God commands nothing impossible
- **First Vatican Council, *Dei Filius* ch. 2 (1870):** God can be known with certainty from created things by the natural light of human reason; He has revealed even truths reason can reach, so that all may know them with ease, with firm certainty and with no admixture of error
- **Second Vatican Council, *Dei Verbum* 11-12 (1965):** Scripture teaches without error the truth God willed for our salvation; the sacred authors' meaning is sought through the literary forms of their age
- **Second Vatican Council, *Dei Verbum* 15 (1965); Pope Benedict XVI, *Verbum Domini* 42 (2010):** The Old Testament contains things incomplete and temporary; how to read the Bible's "dark" passages
- **Second Vatican Council, *Nostra Aetate* 2 (1965):** The Church rejects nothing that is true and holy in other religions
- **Second Vatican Council, *Gaudium et Spes* 16, 22, 24 (1965):** Conscience, where we are alone with God; the call offered to every person; the human person as the only creature on earth God willed for itself
- **St. John Paul II, *Veritatis Splendor* 9, 40, 43 (1993):** God alone is the Good; the moral law has its origin in God, and the eternal law is His own wisdom
- **St. John Paul II, General Audience of 28 July 1999:** On hell; without special divine revelation it is not granted to us to know which human beings are lost
