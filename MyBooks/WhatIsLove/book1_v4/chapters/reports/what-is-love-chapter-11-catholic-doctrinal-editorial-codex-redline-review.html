    Chapter 11 · Prudence: How to Love

    Chapter assessment

    Chapter 11 succeeds in moving the book from principles to acts. It defines prudence from CCC 1806, treats it as command rather than timidity, affirms intrinsically evil acts, introduces the order of charity, urges conscience formation and counsel, distinguishes euthanasia from refusing disproportionate treatment, and repeatedly warns that case studies cannot replace prayerful attention to particulars.

    Its strongest caveat, however, is contradicted by the chapter’s method. The father in the Prodigal Son is made a complete icon of every prudent act; the four facets must occur “all at once”; every case is reduced to a choice among three parabolic roles. Catholic prudence also examines moral object, intention and circumstances; absolute norms; vocation and role; acquired and infused virtues; the gifts of the Spirit; competence; counsel; likely consequences; and concrete duties. The order-of-love list is too rigid, God is again assigned “infinite sacrifice,” providence is said to cover every need through assigned relationships, and natural reason outside explicit theism is dismissed. Several hard-case claims confuse soul, truth and bodily life, while the gender/sexuality, end-of-life, marriage and abortion cases need safer and more exact pastoral language. The chapter’s final claim that the reader’s life-written book “saves” household and world must be removed.

    CH11-01 · Distinguish acquired prudence, infused prudence and the gift of counsel

    Severity: Major / virtue and grace · Control: DC-06, DC-08 · Location: chapter_11.md:49–59

    Issue. Prudence can be an acquired moral virtue strengthened through repeated good acts; in the Christian life infused prudence accompanies sanctifying grace and is elevated by charity; counsel is a distinct gift of the Holy Spirit. “A gift before a skill” without these distinctions blurs acquired habit, infused virtue and charismatic prompting.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1804–1811, 1830–1831; Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I-II, q.63; II-II, qq.47–52.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    Prudence is a gift of God before it is ever a skill of ours both a moral virtue that can be acquired through truthful deliberation and repeated good acts, and—within the life of grace—an infused virtue elevated by charity. The Holy Spirit’s gift of counsel further perfects practical judgment. We learn, practice and grow in prudence, always dependent on God’s creating and sustaining grace.

    Growing in love and wisdom are deeply related, but every act of real love makes us a little wiser, and every ounce of wisdom is poured back out in love no isolated act automatically increases prudence; grace-enabled good acts, reflection, correction and experience can form the stable habit.

    Editorial consequence. The chapter can teach divine dependence without denying ordinary virtue formation or collapsing distinct gifts.

    CH11-02 · Define sanctifying grace as created participation, not simply God’s own life slowly working

    Severity: Major / grace · Control: DC-06, DC-08 · Location: chapter_11.md:67–69

    Issue. Sanctifying grace is a stable supernatural disposition and created participation in divine life, not the divine essence or Holy Spirit simply identified with an interior process. Growth in holiness follows an initial real gift and includes actual graces and cooperation.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1996–2005, 2010–2011.

    Source sentence with proposed inline redline:

    Scripture calls this becoming “a new creation,” Christ living within us—what the Church names sanctifying grace: God's own life at work inside us, slowly making us holy the stable, created gift by which God makes us participants in His divine life, accompanied by the indwelling Holy Spirit and actual graces that move our free cooperation and growth in holiness.

    Editorial consequence. Created grace, the uncreated Spirit, initial justification and growth are no longer collapsed.

    CH11-03 · Mark cultural details and inferred actions in the Prodigal Son as interpretation

    Severity: Major / exegesis · Control: DC-20, DC-26, DC-28 · Location: chapter_11.md:73–85

    Issue. “I wish you were dead,” the father watching every day, public loss of dignity and the calf being his “costliest thing” are common homiletic amplifications, not statements in Luke. They may illuminate the parable if explicitly marked as cultural interpretation or imaginative reading.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    The younger one demanded his inheritance early—which, in that culture, was as much as to say “I wish you were dead” a shocking rupture that many interpreters compare to treating the father as though he were already dead. ... Scripture says the father saw him while distant; which means he had been watching that road every single day the detail invites us to imagine watchful longing, though the text does not specify a daily vigil. He runs—an action culturally striking for a patriarch—throwing away his dignity in front of the whole village accepting public self-abasement in the homiletic reading. He kills the fattened calf, the costliest thing he owns a costly sign of festal restoration.

    Editorial consequence. A vivid meditation stops presenting reconstruction as inspired narrative fact.

    CH11-04 · Do not make the father’s conduct a complete map of every prudent decision

    Severity: Stop-ship / method · Control: DC-01, DC-27 · Location: chapter_11.md:77–103

    Issue. The father chiefly reveals divine mercy toward repentant sinners. Assigning every detail to one of four facets and claiming his running simultaneously is truth, justice, mercy and sacrifice turns the parable into proof of the private framework. It does not answer cases involving competence, conflicting obligations, public authority, uncertainty or technical knowledge.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    He is showing us exactly how Perfect Love acts when it is tested—and every facet ... is right there above all the Father’s merciful joy over a sinner who returns. This book’s four terms can illuminate aspects of the scene, but they do not exhaust the parable or provide a decision procedure.

    The father does not choose among the four facets. He does all four at once, and they are not four acts but one. His running is his truth-telling is his justice is his sacrifice. His single response contains several morally distinguishable acts—seeing, running, embracing, restoring and feasting—held together by love. Prudence may integrate several goods, but not every prudent act visibly contains all four categories at once.

    Editorial consequence. The parable remains the chapter’s spiritual icon without becoming an exhaustive moral algorithm.

    CH11-05 · Remove the false claim that culture offers only two choices

    Severity: Major / reasoning and tone · Control: DC-22, DC-26 · Location: chapter_11.md:89–101

    Issue. “Our whole culture” and “only these two sons” erase many thoughtful approaches and frame disagreement before examining it. The three-role image can be offered as a temptation pattern, not a universal description of everyone outside the framework.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    Now meet the two counterfeits ... our whole culture is built out of them two recurring distortions the parable can help us notice. Public debates often pressure families toward a binary: uncritical affirmation or total rejection. The culture offers us only these two sons That pressure is real, but it is not the only position people actually hold. Prudence seeks a response grounded in truth and charity rather than merely assigning everyone one of three characters.

    The rest of this chapter is simply ... the only question is which son ... and will I be the father a set of limited case studies. The parable can examine our posture, while the actual moral judgment must consider the full concrete case.

    Editorial consequence. The method becomes invitational and analytical rather than totalizing.

    CH11-06 · Expand prudence beyond finding one act containing all four facets

    Severity: Stop-ship / Catholic moral method · Control: DC-01, DC-10 · Location: chapter_11.md:101–136

    Issue. Prudence does not invent moral truth, but it cannot be reduced to finding an act where four selected categories are simultaneous. Moral evaluation requires object, intention and circumstances; absolute precepts; virtues and gifts; role and vocation; competence and counsel; and proportionate attention to consequences.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1749–1761, 1783–1789, 1806; Veritatis Splendor 71–83.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    The entire work of prudence is finding the single integrated act in which truth, justice, mercy and sacrifice are all present at once discerning and commanding the good act: respecting absolute moral norms, identifying the moral object, purifying intention, weighing circumstances and consequences, honoring duties of state and competence, seeking counsel, and exercising the virtues under grace. The four terms may prompt useful questions within that fuller process.

    It judges—to find the single act in which God's truth, justice and mercy converge to identify morally good and practicable means. It commands—it acts, and the acting will cost something. That cost is sacrifice faithful action may carry cost, though not every act’s defining feature is sacrifice.

    Editorial consequence. The chapter finally gives prudence the robust Catholic structure its stated caution requires.

    CH11-07 · Correct the rigid order-of-love ladder

    Severity: Stop-ship / charity and obligation · Control: DC-07, DC-23, DC-26 · Location: chapter_11.md:107–120

    Issue. Aquinas offers principles of ordered charity, not a universal six-rung allocation list. One loves God above all, oneself and neighbor in God, and may owe different goods according to spiritual need, common good, kinship, vocation and urgency. A stranger in mortal danger can outrank a relative’s convenience; an enemy is not simply last in the duty of benevolence.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1822–1829; Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II, q.26.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    The order runs like this: 1. God ... 2. Our own soul ... 3. Spouse and children ... 4. Extended family ... 5. Neighbors ... 6. Enemies from God as the supreme good to love of self and neighbor in God. Concrete priority then depends on the kind and urgency of the good, duties of vocation and office, spiritual or bodily danger, kinship, common good and available means. Spouse and children ordinarily have grave proximate claims, yet emergency duties to a stranger or the common good can take precedence over a lesser family preference. Enemies remain neighbors whom charity commands us to love, not a class simply placed last.

    Editorial consequence. Ordered charity guides prudence without becoming a mechanical hierarchy.

    CH11-08 · Remove “God’s infinite sacrifice” from the finitude section

    Severity: Stop-ship / doctrine of God · Control: DC-03, DC-04 · Location: chapter_11.md:122

    Issue. Truth, justice and mercy are infinite divine perfections; sacrifice in the strict sense belongs to Christ’s created human offering. God does not lose, suffer or expend a finite resource by loving creatures.

    Source sentence with proposed inline redline:

    God's truth, justice, mercy and sacrifice are infinite; He can pour Himself out for all without running dry are infinite; His goodness is not depleted by His free gifts. The incarnate Son’s sacrifice is of infinite worth because the divine Person offers Himself in His humanity. Our created attention, time and strength are finite.

    Editorial consequence. The chapter’s doctrine of God becomes consistent with the Chapter 9 control.

    CH11-09 · Remove the unsupported claim that providence assigned relationships so every need is covered

    Severity: Major / providence and social responsibility · Control: DC-15, DC-26 · Location: chapter_11.md:122

    Issue. God places people in real relationships and vocations, but the manuscript invents a closed distributive system in which each person loves “the ones God gave them” and the whole Body is covered. Many people lack safe kin, and structural injustice leaves needs outside family networks. The claim can excuse the stranger or systemic reform.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    God made us finite and placed us in particular relationships and communities precisely so that if each person loves the ones God gave them, then together, the whole Body is covered in which we have real duties. Yet no promise guarantees that every need will be met through kinship networks; charity also creates new bonds, serves abandoned persons and reforms institutions that leave people uncovered. Ordered love helps us spend finite gifts faithfully; it does not identify every generous priority outside the home as betrayal.

    Editorial consequence. The chapter honors family duties without spiritualizing social abandonment.

    CH11-10 · Acknowledge natural reason and real virtue outside explicit theism

    Severity: Major / natural law and nonbelievers · Control: DC-07, DC-22, DC-26 · Location: chapter_11.md:128–130

    Issue. Without God no created good has its ultimate explanation, but secular moral realists and nonbelievers can know genuine moral truths, affirm intrinsic dignity and perform real natural goods through reason, conscience and actual grace. “Strip God out” does not logically make every account collapse into usefulness.

    Controlling sources: CCC 31–38, 1954–1960; Gaudium et Spes 16.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    Strip God out of the picture and “good” slowly shrinks to usefulness ... all such standards are ultimately set by human beings If human dignity is treated as a revisable social convention or reduced to usefulness, persons can be weighed and spent. Catholic faith supplies its deepest ground in creation and redemption. Natural reason can nevertheless recognize much of this dignity, and nonbelievers can defend real moral truth and act virtuously; such knowledge is incomplete without implying it is unreal. Revelation confirms, purifies and elevates what reason can glimpse.

    Editorial consequence. The positive Catholic foundation is made without a demonstrably false account of every secular ethic.

    CH11-11 · Do not equate a person’s infinite dignity with being the greatest possible good

    Severity: Stop-ship / dignity and moral object · Control: DC-24 · Location: chapter_11.md:136, 150

    Issue. Human dignity is intrinsic and, in Dignitas Infinita, called infinite, but God is the supreme good. One may also accept risk to bodily life for proportionate reasons. The prohibition of intrinsically evil acts rests on moral object and God’s law, not the claim that no good exceeds one created soul.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    The innocent person can never be reduced to a means for someone else's good, because no good is greater than the single soul that bears God's likeness God alone is the supreme good, and no proportion of intended good can transform an intrinsically evil moral object into a good act. Love cannot save twenty persons by intentionally killing one innocent person, not because arithmetic proves the one a greater good, but because direct killing of the innocent is never a licit means.

    Editorial consequence. The inviolable norm is defended by Catholic moral analysis rather than a false ranking of goods.

    CH11-12 · Say murder ends bodily life, not the immortal soul

    Severity: Stop-ship / anthropology · Control: DC-24 · Location: chapter_11.md:144–152

    Issue. The cellar example says smothering the infant destroys his soul. Catholic doctrine teaches that the human soul is spiritual and immortal; murder kills the body and gravely violates the person but cannot annihilate the soul.

    Controlling sources: CCC 362–368, 2258–2262.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    Love cannot save twenty images of God by desecrating one, because there is no good greater than the soul you would destroy the proposed outcome that could make intentionally killing this innocent child a licit means. The act would end his bodily life and gravely violate his person; it would not destroy his immortal soul.

    Editorial consequence. The case preserves the absolute norm without teaching annihilation of the soul.

    CH11-13 · Present the cellar case with moral precision and without “strongest possible” rhetoric

    Severity: Major / moral analysis and pastoral proportion · Control: DC-10, DC-26 · Location: chapter_11.md:140–154

    Issue. Hypothetical certainty about consequences is rarely available, and “strongest case there is” is an unsupported superlative. The act must be specified: deliberately suffocating the infant is intrinsically evil; attempting a nonlethal means of muffling sound with death as an unintended risk would require a different analysis.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    Take the cruelest scenario ... the strongest case there is an extreme hypothetical designed to test the norm. You know with the certainty stipulated by the scenario ... or you can press your hand over the child's mouth until he is silent, and dead deliberately suffocate the child as the means of silencing him. That specified intention matters: a nonlethal attempt to quiet or shield the child that carried unintended risk would be a different moral object requiring separate analysis. The refusal does not cause the soldiers’ murders; it refuses to make an intended killing the means of rescue.

    Editorial consequence. The example teaches object and intention rather than relying on horror and certainty.

    CH11-14 · Use the Catechism’s full definition of sin

    Severity: Stop-ship / moral theology · Control: DC-01, DC-10 · Location: chapter_11.md:160–164

    Issue. Sin is not always choosing one’s good at another’s expense, nor is every sin neatly denial of one selected facet. A person can knowingly choose a recognized evil from weakness, passion or malice. The Church defines sin as offense against reason, truth and right conscience and failure in genuine love because of perverse attachment.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1849–1869.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    Sin is a failure to love—choosing a perceived good for ourselves at the expense of the true good of another; it is the heart that denies truth, violates justice, refuses mercy, or rejects sacrifice an offense against reason, truth and right conscience, and a failure in genuine love for God and neighbor caused by perverse attachment to created goods. The four terms may disclose aspects of particular sins but do not exhaust their nature, object or culpability.

    Editorial consequence. The case studies begin from Catholic moral doctrine rather than the framework’s compressed definition.

    CH11-15 · Separate persons, experiences, inclinations, acts and proposed celebrations in the first case

    Severity: Stop-ship / gender, sexuality and pastoral care · Control: DC-22, DC-24, DC-25 · Location: chapter_11.md:166–176

    Issue. Gender distress, a same-sex civil wedding and cohabitation are distinct situations requiring different facts and counsel. Calling all three “the master lie” and “pigsty” risks contempt for persons and forecloses listening. Catholic teaching requires respect, compassion, sensitivity and avoidance of unjust discrimination while retaining moral truth.

    Controlling sources: CCC 2331–2336, 2357–2359; Dignitas Infinita 55–60.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    Your adult child may disclose gender distress or a changed identity, introduce a same-sex partner and proposed civil wedding, or disclose cohabitation. This is the master lie in its purest form These cases are not morally or pastorally interchangeable. Before deciding words, attendance, housing, titles or boundaries, listen carefully; distinguish the person from inclinations, identity claims, acts and events; learn the concrete facts; protect minors or dependents where relevant; and seek competent pastoral counsel. Catholic love neither lies about the body or marriage nor treats a person with contempt. It requires respect, compassion and sensitivity and forbids unjust discrimination. The door should remain open and always will be unless safety requires boundaries; love and prayer continue even where contact must be limited.

    Editorial consequence. The case becomes usable in real families rather than a single culture-war script.

    CH11-16 · Correct “your own soul, which is to say, the truth”

    Severity: Stop-ship / category error · Control: DC-24 · Location: chapter_11.md:176

    Issue. A soul is the spiritual form of the body, not “the truth.” The order of charity places God above self, and fidelity to truth is one obligation. The sentence also risks making parental disagreement a choice between preserving oneself and loving one’s child.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    There is one thing above even your child in the order: your own soul, which is to say, the truth God, whose truth and moral law no relationship authorizes us to violate. You must also guard your own soul by refusing sin, but your soul is not identical with “the truth”. You may sacrifice comfort and approval, but not choose a lie or other intrinsically evil act as the means of helping.

    Editorial consequence. God, truth and the human soul return to their proper categories.

    CH11-17 · State end-of-life decisions through intention, proportionality and ordinary care

    Severity: Stop-ship / life and end-of-life ethics · Control: DC-10, DC-24, DC-25 · Location: chapter_11.md:178–188

    Issue. “Never permitted to end a life” should say never intentionally kill an innocent person. Withdrawing disproportionate treatment permits foreseen death from the underlying condition; ordinary care, including nutrition and hydration when beneficial and not excessively burdensome, remains due. Analgesia need not “hasten” death as a factual rule, and palliative sedation requires clinical and moral precision.

    Controlling sources: CCC 2276–2279; Evangelium Vitae 65; Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith, Samaritanus bonus.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    The Church distinguishes forgoing medical procedures that are burdensome, dangerous, extraordinary or disproportionate from euthanasia. We are simply never permitted to end a life never permitted intentionally to kill an innocent person, whether death is sought as end or means. Basic care remains due when it achieves its purpose and is not excessively burdensome; death may be accepted when caused by the underlying condition after disproportionate treatment is forgone.

    A clinician may administer proportionate analgesia knowing it might hasten the end even when a possible shortening of life is foreseen but not intended, provided ordinary moral and clinical conditions are met. Exact treatment decisions require the patient, family, clinicians, chaplain and applicable directives—not this vignette alone.

    Editorial consequence. The chapter accurately distinguishes killing, allowing natural death and proportionate relief.

    CH11-18 · Do not prescribe “always stay” or exhaustive caregiving to exhausted children

    Severity: Major / caregivers and limits · Control: DC-23, DC-25 · Location: chapter_11.md:184–188

    Issue. Presence is a profound good, but a child may be ill, distant, caring for minors, without leave or unable to provide technical care. “The shape is always the same: stay” and “patiently, exhaustingly, to the end” can shame faithful caregivers and conflict with the chapter’s own teaching on finitude.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    What this costs is real, but its shape is always the same: to stay faithful accompaniment within one’s actual vocation, capacity and circumstances—to remain relationally present, arrange competent care, advocate, visit, pray and share burdens rather than abandon. ... Answering a parent’s claim patiently, exhaustingly, to the end with prudent fidelity, while accepting respite and help and honoring duties to spouse, children and health, can be a beautiful form of covenant love.

    Editorial consequence. Accompaniment is preserved without contradicting finite duties or encouraging burnout.

    CH11-19 · Distinguish temporal happiness from beatitude

    Severity: Major / human final end · Control: DC-07, DC-26 · Location: chapter_11.md:190–198

    Issue. Happiness is not simply a lie about life’s purpose. The human person is made for beatitude in God; passing satisfaction cannot govern marital vows. The vignette also presumes all relevant facts from one spouse’s report and promises joy on the far side of sacrifice.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1716–1729, 1601, 1646–1651.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    The lie at work is that happiness is the purpose of life immediate emotional satisfaction is the supreme criterion of a good life. True beatitude in God is our final end, and marriage’s seasons of desolation do not by themselves dissolve a covenant. Before offering counsel, a friend should not assume one report exhausts the marriage; hidden abuse, coercion, addiction, betrayal, illness or questions of validity may require competent pastoral, clinical or legal help. The joy she seeks is on the far side of a sacrifice cannot be guaranteed as an emotional result of staying, although grace can sustain fidelity and open paths of healing.

    Editorial consequence. The defense of permanence becomes Catholic rather than anti-happiness or overconfident about an unseen marriage.

    CH11-20 · Remove “the worst thing a person can do” from the abortion case

    Severity: Stop-ship / abortion and pastoral care · Control: DC-10, DC-25, DC-26 · Location: chapter_11.md:200–208

    Issue. Abortion is gravely evil, but the Church does not rank it in every circumstance as the worst personal act. Culpability can be diminished by coercion, fear, ignorance, pressure and other factors. The physician and woman also require distinct pastoral approaches.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1735, 1857–1860, 2270–2275; Evangelium Vitae 99.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    This is the hardest case of all ... the worst thing a person can do a grave case in which objective evil, personal culpability, grief and mercy must all be distinguished. Abortion directly takes innocent human life; individual responsibility may be affected by fear, coercion, pressure, ignorance or psychological factors, none of which should be presumed from a vignette. A person involved repeatedly as a clinician and a woman who underwent an abortion may share guilt and grief in different forms and need individualized sacramental, pastoral and clinical care.

    Editorial consequence. Objective gravity is taught without an invented ranking or motive assignment.

    CH11-21 · Replace payment-only atonement language and guaranteed healing

    Severity: Stop-ship / atonement and trauma · Control: DC-05, DC-18, DC-25 · Location: chapter_11.md:208–210

    Issue. “Justice paid in full” repeats Chapter 9’s narrowing of the Cross. “The only way that actually heals” and walking “the whole road” promise a process and impose an indefinite accompaniment duty. Absolution forgives; grief and trauma may remain and can need clinical care.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    The father’s way is the only one that actually heals an image of truth joined to mercy. On the Cross, mercy did not bypass justice; it flowed through a justice that had been paid in full Christ freely offered loving obedience, sacrifice and superabundant satisfaction, reconciling sinners and conquering death. Sacramental absolution truly forgives the contrite penitent. Healing may be gradual and include grief, restitution where possible, penance, pastoral accompaniment, community and trauma-informed clinical care. A friend should not promise to walk “the whole road” alone; faithful help also connects the person with competent care and respects healthy limits.

    Editorial consequence. The passage offers real sacramental hope without narrowing Calvary or promising total felt recovery.

    CH11-22 · Correct the formation section’s claims about prudence and Confession

    Severity: Major / virtue and sacrament · Control: DC-08, DC-18 · Location: chapter_11.md:216–232

    Issue. People have natural inclinations and developing practical reason; “no one is born with it” is serviceable rhetoric but too absolute. Confession is not a lens-cleaning technique: it forgives sins, reconciles and gives grace. The Eucharist does not automatically remake every receiver irrespective of disposition.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    No one is born with prudence begins with the mature habit of prudence; natural capacities develop through formation, experience and grace.

    The Sacraments. Confession scrubs the lens, clearing away self-deception forgives sins through sacramental absolution, reconciles us with God and the Church, and gives grace that can heal habits and aid clearer judgment; it is not guaranteed psychological insight.

    In the Eucharist Christ gives Himself, and we are slowly remade into people who can love—and therefore see—as He does worthy reception nourishes charity and can form prudent disciples as they cooperate with grace.

    Editorial consequence. Formation is neither purely acquired nor sacramentally automatic.

    CH11-23 · Do not reduce the gift of counsel to an interior feeling

    Severity: Stop-ship / discernment · Control: DC-06, DC-20, DC-25 · Location: chapter_11.md:234–250

    Issue. Counsel perfects prudence by making us docile to the Spirit, but “quiet prompting” and “which way Love is leaning” can teach readers to canonize impressions. Peace and agitation are not infallible signs; discernment remains under Scripture, Church teaching, objective moral norms, reason and competent counsel.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1783–1789, 1830–1831; 1 John 4:1.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    The gift of counsel is simply prudence lifted up and perfected by God Himself. It is the quiet prompting ... the sense ... of which way Love is leaning the Holy Spirit’s gift perfecting practical reason and making the soul docile to divine guidance. It never dispenses with revealed truth, moral law, facts, duties or sound counsel, and no interior impression is self-authenticating. Ignatian consolation and desolation require disciplined rules and spiritual discernment; peace is not automatic proof of God’s will, nor agitation proof of its opposite.

    Editorial consequence. Readers are protected from mistaking preference, trauma or impulse for divine direction.

    CH11-24 · Correct the authorship and polemical description of Wisdom

    Severity: Major / Scripture and ecumenism · Control: DC-22, DC-28 · Location: chapter_11.md:238–248

    Issue. Wisdom of Solomon speaks in Solomon’s literary voice but is generally dated much later; “Solomon set the reason down” is historical overclaim. “Volumes later cut from some Bibles” is polemical shorthand for differing canons and should be stated accurately.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    Solomon, who received that gift ... set the reason down The Book of Wisdom, writing in the Solomonic wisdom tradition, gives voice to the prayerful posture ... and once again it is one of the volumes later cut from some Bibles one of the deuterocanonical books received as inspired in the Catholic canon and not included in the Protestant Old Testament canon.

    Editorial consequence. The chapter is historically careful and ecumenically factual without weakening Catholic canonicity.

    CH11-25 · Do not say sinful mistakes themselves deepen virtue

    Severity: Major / sin and sanctification · Control: DC-06, DC-08, DC-10 · Location: chapter_11.md:252–268

    Issue. Errors can become occasions of humility and growth through repentance and grace, but sin as such never builds virtue. “He will not let you fall” can also sound like a guarantee against future sin or calamity, contrary to the entire preceding section.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    The very mistakes deepen the virtue, if we bring them home can, through honest repentance, reparation, sacramental grace and reflection, become occasions from which prudence grows; the wrongdoing itself never causes virtue. ... Trust Him: He will not abandon you, though He will not let you fall He may permit trials and you may still err; His grace always calls you to rise and return.

    Editorial consequence. Redemption of failure is distinguished from treating sin as an instrument of formation.

    CH11-26 · Do not call the reader’s life-written “book” salvific

    Severity: Stop-ship / soteriology · Control: DC-06, DC-19 · Location: chapter_11.md:270–276

    Issue. “The book that saves—you, your household, and the world” makes lived praxis sound salvific in itself, even with a later grace qualifier. Christ alone saves through His Paschal mystery; our prudence and charity cooperate in His saving mission and can serve others without guaranteeing household salvation.

    Controlling sources: CCC 430–451, 849–856, 1987–2029; Acts 4:12.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    That book is yours: the one you will write with your life, in daily practice. Practiced faithfully, it is the book that saves—you, and your household, and through your household, the world—not by your own strength, but by His love alive in you a life through which Christ’s grace can sanctify you and serve your household and world. Jesus Christ alone saves; no pattern of prudential decisions guarantees another person’s conversion or salvation, but grace-enabled fidelity truly cooperates in His mission.

    Editorial consequence. The stirring close no longer transfers the title and work of Savior to the reader’s praxis.

    CH11-27 · Present the framework and examen as aids rather than sufficient equipment

    Severity: Major / scope and structure · Control: DC-01, DC-29 · Location: chapter_11.md:270–289

    Issue. After admitting one chapter cannot teach every case, the text says its three-part framework “is enough.” The examen asks only four selected questions and one parabolic identity question. It should send readers back to the fuller discernment controls and begin with grace, gratitude and objective duties.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    What this chapter has done is give you the framework: the four facets seen as one, the order of love, and the father on the road ... That is enough to begin several aids: a four-question lens, principles of ordered charity and the father as an image of merciful posture. They are not sufficient by themselves; prudent discernment also requires moral norms, object, intention, circumstances, vocation, counsel, competence, consequences, virtue, prayer and grace.

    Each evening, begin by thanking God and asking for light. Use the four questions, then ask: What was the moral object? What did I intend? Whom did my vocation bind me to serve? What circumstances and consequences mattered? Did I seek counsel where I lacked competence? The father-and-sons question can examine posture, but it should not replace an objective examination of conscience.

    Editorial consequence. The chapter closes with a genuinely usable Catholic practice rather than a self-contained private system.

    Chapter 11 disposition

    Not ready for episcopal review in its present form. Its teaching on prudence, absolute moral norms, conscience formation and prayer is worth retaining. Before submission, however, the author’s four-part/parabolic heuristic must be subordinated to the Church’s full moral method; ordered charity, providence, natural reason, soul/body language, the difficult cases, counsel, sacramental formation and soteriology require the corrections above. The chapter is robust, but recurring father/far-country and “all four at once” language should be consolidated after the governing method is fixed.
