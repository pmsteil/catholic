    Chapter 17 · The Gospel — Perfect Love Revealed in Christ

    Chapter assessment

    Chapter 17 is the manuscript’s theological climax and contains much to preserve: the eternal Son truly became man; Christ fulfills the Law and gives the New Law of the Holy Spirit; grace precedes human response; the New Covenant is established in His blood; the Cross is a free, once-for-all and all-sufficient sacrifice; the Resurrection is bodily and decisive; Christ is really, truly and substantially present in the Eucharist; the Church is visible, apostolic and missionary; and Christian discipleship requires faith, repentance, sacramental life, charity and mission.

    The chapter is not yet ready for episcopal review because it repeatedly identifies the Gospel, grace, Christ and the Church with the book’s private definition more tightly than Catholic doctrine permits. The Gospel is the apostolic proclamation of Jesus Christ—His identity, Kingdom, saving death, burial, Resurrection, exaltation, gift of the Spirit and call to repentance, faith and Baptism—not the author’s synthesis elevated into revelation. Grace is not simply “Perfect Love”; the Holy Spirit is uncreated, while sanctifying grace is a created participation in divine life. Faith remains a real theological virtue even when dead through lack of charity. God did not become love; the Son, who eternally is God, assumed a human nature without change in His divinity. The Incarnation and adoption are free gifts, not justice owed to humanity. Covenant cannot be defined by negotiated adult acceptance because infants truly enter it in Baptism. The Church is the seed and beginning of the Kingdom, not simply identical with the Kingdom in its consummation. Several Eucharistic claims need sacramental precision; the “fourth cup” and Psalm 85 readings must be labeled theological interpretations, not established doctrine. The chapter also denies ordered concern for one’s salvation, dismisses every other religion as receiving nothing of love’s truth, confuses state of grace with “perfect love,” and tells every OCIA participant to be baptized even though baptized candidates must not be rebaptized.

    CH17-01 · Open with Catholic confidence rather than an unverifiable claim about “everyone”

    Severity: Moderate / rhetoric and grammar · Control: DC-19, DC-22, DC-26 · Location: chapter_17.md:17–23

    Issue. “Everyone talks” and “few can define” are unsupported. In American punctuation, the period belongs inside the closing quotation mark. The chapter should begin by stating the apostolic proclamation rather than claiming superior comprehensiveness over other Christians.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    What IS the Gospel?

    Everyone talks about "the Gospel". Few can define it fully. Christians use the word “Gospel” often, sometimes to emphasize one part of the apostolic proclamation. The Church receives its fullness in Jesus Christ.

    Chapter 16 ended by asking where God's Perfect Love comes from. This chapter gives the full answer a Christ-centered answer under the Church’s rule of faith.

    Ask ten Christians "What is the Gospel?" and you'll get ten different answers Common answers often emphasize different true elements—“Jesus died for your sins.” “God loves you.” “Believe and be saved.” But The apostolic Gospel is more comprehensive than any single slogan.

    Editorial consequence. The opening invites readers into Catholic fullness without caricaturing Christians.

    CH17-02 · State the objective kerygma before discussing reductions

    Severity: Stop-ship / definition of the Gospel · Control: DC-19, DC-20 · Location: chapter_17.md:23–27

    Issue. The chapter never gives the apostolic proclamation priority over its love framework. The Gospel announces who Jesus is and what God has done in Him: Kingdom, death for sins, burial, Resurrection, appearances, lordship, Spirit, repentance, faith, Baptism, forgiveness, Church and promised consummation.

    Controlling sources: Mark 1:14–15; Acts 2:22–41; 1 Corinthians 15:1–8; Romans 1:1–6; CCC 422–429, 571–682, 763–769.

    Source paragraphs with proposed inline redline:

    ~~Consider the common reductions in modern Christianity. Some say "the Gospel is going to heaven when you die." This ignores transformation—Christ doesn't just save us from something but for something. Others say "the Gospel is social justice." This ignores sin and salvation—we cannot build the Kingdom by human effort alone; we need grace. Still others say "the Gospel is believing the right things." This ignores relationship and sacraments—even the demons believe (James 2:19).

    Each reduction captures a fragment while losing the whole.~~ Before considering reductions, state the proclamation itself: Jesus Christ, the eternal Son and promised Messiah, announced and inaugurated God’s Kingdom; died for our sins; was buried; rose bodily on the third day; appeared to His witnesses; is exalted as Lord; pours out the Holy Spirit; gathers His Church; and calls every person to repentance, faith, Baptism and new life while we await His return. Going to heaven, transformation, justice, doctrinal faith and sacramental communion belong within that Gospel but do not replace this saving event and Person.

    Editorial consequence. The chapter receives its definition of Gospel from Scripture and Tradition.

    CH17-03 · Do not identify grace with the book’s definition of Perfect Love

    Severity: Stop-ship / grace and the Holy Spirit · Control: DC-01, DC-06, DC-07, DC-08 · Location: chapter_17.md:29

    Issue. Grace is God’s free favor and help; sanctifying grace is a created participation in divine life; actual graces are divine helps; the Holy Spirit given to us is an uncreated divine Person. “Grace ... is nothing other than His Perfect Love” collapses these distinctions into the author’s formula.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1996–2005, 2023–2024.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    And notice what every one of them fails to begin with. None begins where Scripture itself begins ... None begins with grace, which is nothing other than His Perfect Love given away to us ... Start anywhere but there and you have already lost the reason He came at all—to establish a New Covenant that fulfills the Old, so that we would be saved by love and for love, by Him who is Love. The Gospel begins with God’s initiative: “God is love” (1 John 4:8), and He first loved us (1 John 4:19). Grace is His free favor and help; sanctifying grace gives created participation in divine life, and the Holy Spirit is the uncreated Gift. Christ establishes the New Covenant in His blood so that, saved by grace through faith, we may live in the charity poured into our hearts. The commandments to love God and neighbor and Christ’s new commandment reveal the form of that new life.

    Editorial consequence. Grace is no longer absorbed into a private definition.

    CH17-04 · Retire the diagnostic matrix as a test of the Gospel

    Severity: Stop-ship / framework authority and circularity · Control: DC-01, DC-19, DC-26 · Location: chapter_17.md:31

    Issue. A framework developed in the book cannot test and authenticate the Gospel that judges the framework. “Heaven,” “social justice” and belief are not components to grade; they are claims whose true relation to the kerygma must be explained.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    You already know how to test this. In Chapter 15 we ran twelve counterfeits through the components of God's Perfect Love ... Do it here. "Heaven when you die" keeps communion ... "social justice" keeps justice ... "believing the right things" keeps truth and drops the covenant and the cost. The Gospel tests this book, not the reverse. Christian hope includes heaven and bodily resurrection; the Gospel generates justice but is not reducible to a social program; revealed truth requires assent but living faith bears fruit in charity and sacramental communion. Each partial slogan should therefore be restored to the whole apostolic proclamation rather than scored by the matrix.

    Editorial consequence. Revelation regains authority over the author’s conceptual tool.

    CH17-05 · Replace the “faith alone” caricature with Catholic distinctions

    Severity: Stop-ship / justification, faith and ecumenism · Control: DC-08, DC-09, DC-22 · Location: chapter_17.md:33–37

    Issue. Scripture does present faith conceptually apart from charity, and Catholic theology calls faith without charity unformed or dead faith: still real faith, but not living or salvific. “Faith alone” has different Protestant meanings. Pope Benedict XVI could say Luther’s formula is true if faith is not opposed to charity. The passage presently says faith ceases to be faith and implies separated Christians teach a faith that need not love.

    Controlling sources: James 2:14–26; Galatians 5:6; Council of Trent, Decree on Justification 7–10; CCC 1814–1816; Benedict XVI, General Audience of November 19, 2008.

    Source paragraphs with proposed inline redline:

    The most consequential reduction of all is the one that sets the word alone beside faith ... It takes faith—which Scripture never once presents by itself—and cuts it loose from the love ... the moment you can hold the faith apart from the love, what you are holding is no longer the faith Paul meant. The relation of faith, grace and works requires careful terms. We cannot merit initial justification or purchase salvation; it is wholly God’s gift. Catholic doctrine nevertheless distinguishes faith from charity: faith without charity can remain real but “dead” and does not unite us fully to Christ. Living faith works through love (Galatians 5:6). Some Protestant uses of “faith alone” intend precisely a grace-received faith that necessarily bears fruit, while other formulations differ from Catholic teaching. Critique the defined proposition, not the word alone in isolation.

    And when faith is cut from love, the rest unravels behind it. A faith that need not love also need not tell the truth in love, need not render justice, need not forgive, need not cost anything ... a real component of God's Perfect Love, held alone. A profession of faith deliberately severed from charity is dead and cannot save, yet separated Christians must not be presumed indifferent to truth, justice, forgiveness or costly discipleship. Catholic fullness holds faith, hope and charity together while preserving their real distinctions.

    Editorial consequence. The Catholic position is taught without condemning a slogan whose meaning varies.

    CH17-06 · Make the Gospel statement subordinate to the Church’s kerygma

    Severity: Stop-ship / private definition elevated to revelation · Control: DC-01, DC-19, DC-21 · Location: chapter_17.md:39–45

    Issue. “The Gospel IS God’s Perfect Love” makes the author’s exact wording the identity of the Gospel. Jesus embodies divine love in His humanity, but the Gospel is not an abstract quality incarnate. The seven-item list also differs from the book’s inviolable core sentence.

    Source paragraphs with proposed inline redline:

    When we misunderstand the Law of the Gospel, we misunderstand Christ ... A fragmented Gospel produces fragmented Christians—unable to integrate truth, justice, mercy and sacrifice into the whole love Christ actually offers. When the Gospel is reduced, Christian understanding and witness can be distorted. Its fullness is restored by receiving Christ’s entire saving mystery in the faith of His Church. The book’s recurring terms may illuminate aspects of that mystery but do not define its total content.

    Here is what brings it all together: The Gospel IS God's Perfect Love revealed in Christ. ... The Gospel is not just a message about love; it IS love incarnate. Here is what brings it all together: The Gospel proclaims Jesus Christ, in whom the Father’s saving love is fully revealed and given. Truth, justice, mercy, sacrifice, covenant and communion can serve as themes under which we contemplate His saving work. They remain a fallible synthesis submitted to the Gospel and the Church’s faith.

    Editorial consequence. The chapter no longer canonizes the manuscript’s formula.

    CH17-07 · Correct “the highest good made flesh” and the Aquinas attribution

    Severity: Major / Christology and historical claim · Control: DC-02, DC-04, DC-28 · Location: chapter_17.md:43–45

    Issue. The eternal Son—not an abstraction called “the highest good”—became flesh. God is the supreme good and our final beatitude, but attributing the exact philosophical sequence to Aquinas requires a precise source. Christ fulfills every covenant promise, not every human desire without qualification.

    Source paragraphs with proposed inline redline:

    Christ is the summum bonum—the highest good—made flesh. Jesus Christ is the eternal Son made flesh, and in the Triune God we find the supreme Good and our final beatitude. Throughout history ... But St. Thomas Aquinas demonstrated what the Incarnation reveals: the highest good is not an abstract principle or philosophical concept—it's a Person. He is the fulfillment of every covenant, every human longing, the answer to every philosophical quest, the satisfaction of every desire Philosophers have sought the human final end in different ways. Aquinas teaches that complete beatitude consists in the vision of the divine essence. In Christ, God personally enters history and fulfills His covenant promises, purifies human longing and draws us toward the truth, goodness and beauty for which we were created.

    John 3:16 is not merely information about God's love—it IS God's Perfect Love made flesh, the highest Good entering human history it announces the Father’s gift of His incarnate Son, crucified and risen, to draw us into eternal communion with Himself.

    Editorial consequence. The Incarnation has a divine subject and the philosophical attribution becomes defensible.

    CH17-08 · Qualify adult cooperation and include infant Baptism

    Severity: Stop-ship / grace and sacramental initiation · Control: DC-06, DC-08, DC-16 · Location: chapter_17.md:47–51

    Issue. Grace precedes and enables every response. Adults must freely cooperate, but infants are truly justified and reborn in Baptism without an elicited personal act. “He does not save us without our cooperation” is therefore not universal as written.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1250–1252, 1262–1274, 1987–2002.

    Source paragraphs with proposed inline redline:

    But the verses often left out The following verses also belong to the passage: “And this is the judgment ...” (John 3:19–21).

    Verse 16 is not separated from conversion. The Light has come, and we must come adults are called to come to the Light—so that our deeds may be clearly seen as “wrought in God” (John 3:21).

    This is crucial: Christ doesn't save us while we remain passive. He saves us by transforming us—by giving us His grace (His Perfect Love) so we can cooperate with Him in living authentic love. We cannot save ourselves, but neither does He save us without our free cooperation with His gift of grace and even our cooperation is preceded and enabled by grace. Adults are called to a free response; infants, who cannot yet make such an act, are truly reborn by the freely given grace of Baptism (CCC 1250–1252, 1993, 2002).

    Editorial consequence. Synergy is taught without excluding baptized infants or making cooperation self-originating.

    CH17-09 · Call Christ the Lawgiver and fulfillment, not “the Law incarnate”

    Severity: Major / New Law · Control: DC-06, DC-19 · Location: chapter_17.md:55–69

    Issue. The New Law is principally the grace of the Holy Spirit; Christ gives, fulfills and perfectly embodies it in His humanity. Calling Him “the Law incarnate” is not the Catechism’s formulation. A person who despises his brother has not obeyed every commandment, and the Church’s moral teaching is more than the Sermon on the Mount preserved unchanged.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1965–1974.

    Source passages with proposed inline redline:

    Jesus doesn't just teach us about love—He is the Law of the Gospel incarnate is the divine Lawgiver who fulfills the Old Law and gives the New Law of the Holy Spirit. In the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5–7), He reveals what this law looks like in human flesh.

    This is how Christ's law combats evil: Rule-keeping without love leaves the heart exactly where it found it—a man can obey every commandment on the tablet and still despise his brother Merely external observance is incomplete, and contempt for one’s brother already violates God’s commands—but Christ changes what the heart wants. The claim that a Christian is simply free of all law ... And the Church's moral teaching ... is the Sermon on the Mount kept intact Christ frees us from sin, not from the moral law; by the Spirit, freedom receives the form of charity. The Church hands on the Decalogue, the Sermon on the Mount and the whole apostolic moral teaching, applying them authoritatively to new circumstances.

    Editorial consequence. The passage agrees with the Catechism it quotes.

    CH17-10 · Say that the Son became man, not that God became love

    Severity: Stop-ship / Trinity and Incarnation · Control: DC-02, DC-04 · Location: chapter_17.md:73–79

    Issue. God eternally is Love; He does not become love. In the Incarnation, the eternal Son assumes a complete human nature without divine change. “The infinite God entered His creation” needs the same qualification so it does not imply local movement or abandonment of divine transcendence.

    Controlling sources: Council of Chalcedon; CCC 456–483.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    God doesn't just send love—He BECOMES love incarnate the eternal Son, who is one in being with the Father and the Holy Spirit, becomes man without ceasing to be God. This is one reason we bow at the Creed's words, to pay honor and express gratitude to our Lord who “was incarnate of the Virgin Mary, and became man.” The infinite God entered His creation to dwell among us. The Son assumed our human nature and dwelt among us while remaining eternally divine.

    Editorial consequence. The chapter avoids divine change and modal confusion.

    CH17-11 · Source the John Paul II quotation and preserve Christ’s full humanity

    Severity: Major / source accuracy and anthropology · Control: DC-24, DC-28 · Location: chapter_17.md:79

    Issue. The quotation needs its exact source and translation: John Paul II, General Audience of February 20, 1980, commonly numbered Theology of the Body 19:4. “Christ didn’t just have a body—He IS the Word made flesh” is true devotionally only if it does not eclipse His rational soul; the Word assumed a complete human nature, body and soul.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    Pope Saint John Paul II taught that “the body, and it alone, is capable of making visible what is invisible: the spiritual and the divine” (General Audience, February 20, 1980, 4; verify the edition’s wording). This is why the Incarnation matters so profoundly: God chose to reveal Himself through a human body. Christ didn't just have a body—He IS the Word made flesh the Son’s complete human nature. Christ is the Word made flesh, true God and true man, with a human body and rational soul. Every action, every touch, every wound on the Cross speaks the language of perfect love through His body. And His bodily Resurrection proves that redemption isn't just spiritual—our bodies participate in divine life forever we await the resurrection of the body and life everlasting. We're not souls trapped in bodies; we're embodied persons made for embodied love.

    Editorial consequence. The source is auditable and Christ’s humanity remains complete.

    CH17-12 · Remove the claim that humanity was owed the Incarnation

    Severity: Stop-ship / gratuitous salvation and sacrifice · Control: DC-03, DC-04, DC-13 · Location: chapter_17.md:81–83

    Issue. Creation, Incarnation, forgiveness and adoption are free gifts, not strict justice owed to humanity. The Incarnation itself is not divine “emptying” understood as loss; the Son assumes a servant’s human condition, and His redemptive sacrifice culminates in the Paschal mystery.

    Source paragraphs with proposed inline redline:

    When Jesus declares “I and the Father are one” (John 10:30), He establishes that authentic love has its source in the divine nature itself. The Incarnation is God's ultimate act of truth ... of justice, giving humanity what we're owed; of mercy ... and of sacrifice, emptying Himself to become our Paschal Lamb reveals His unity with the Father. The Incarnation reveals divine truth and gratuitous mercy; it is not something humanity was owed. In His assumed humanity the Son humbles Himself in obedience, and in the Paschal mystery freely offers His life as our sacrifice. The sacred source means love is not what we decide it is, but what God reveals it to be.

    God sent His Son so we could ground our knowledge of His love on something that is physically real and relatable to us know the Father through the incarnate Son, who is true God and true man and whose saving deeds occurred in history.

    Editorial consequence. Grace remains grace, and kenosis does not become divine diminution.

    CH17-13 · Do not say God’s love “stops” without our response

    Severity: Major / divine love and mission · Control: DC-02, DC-06 · Location: chapter_17.md:89–91

    Issue. Human failure can obstruct love’s fruit and witness, but cannot halt or diminish God’s infinite love. “Without our love being freely given, God’s love stops with us” gives creatures control over a divine perfection.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    Gift: Christ is freely given, not something we deserve or achieve ... He gives to us so that we can give sacrificial love to others ... Without our love being freely given, God's love stops with us. If we refuse charity, we fail to bear the fruit and witness His gift intends; God’s love itself does not diminish or cease.

    Editorial consequence. The missionary duty remains without limiting God.

    CH17-14 · Define covenant from God’s initiative and include infant entry

    Severity: Stop-ship / covenant and Baptism · Control: DC-06, DC-16 · Location: chapter_17.md:93

    Issue. A covenant with God is not simply “established through voluntary acceptance.” God initiates and establishes it; adults respond by grace, while infants truly enter the New Covenant through Baptism before personal consent.

    Controlling sources: Genesis 17:1–14; Acts 2:38–39; CCC 1213, 1250–1252, 1267.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    Covenant: Permanent, binding relationship established through voluntary acceptance. Jesus invites us to willingly accept the Father's design for love. This is not coercion but invitation. freely initiated and established by God. Adults are called and enabled by grace to respond in faith and conversion; baptized infants truly receive covenant life through the faith of the Church and later must make that gift their own. The marriage imagery throughout Scripture—Christ as Bridegroom and the Church as His Bride (Ephesians 5:25–32)—reveals covenant's intimate, permanent nature.

    Editorial consequence. The chapter’s covenant theology no longer contradicts Catholic infant Baptism.

    CH17-15 · Label the covenant “expansion” and New Noah claims as typology

    Severity: Major / biblical theology and source status · Control: DC-16, DC-27, DC-28 · Location: chapter_17.md:95–97

    Issue. New Adam and Davidic fulfillment are explicit New Testament themes; “New Noah” and the sequence couple–family–tribe–nation–kingdom–world are popular covenantal syntheses, not dogmatic stages stated by Scripture or Magisterium. Abraham’s household is more than a tribe, and Adam/Eve are not explicitly called a covenant in Genesis.

    Source paragraphs with proposed inline redline:

    Covenant Fulfillment: Christ fulfills every covenant God’s covenant promises in salvation history. As the New Adam, He restores what the first Adam lost (Romans 5:12–21). As the New Noah, He carries a new humanity through the waters—the flood that once destroyed now saving us in the water of Baptism First Peter presents the flood as a type of Baptism, permitting a theological comparison between Noah and Christ (1 Peter 3:20–21). As seed of Abraham, He blesses all nations (Galatians 3:16). He fulfills the Mosaic Law perfectly (Matthew 5:17). As eternal King from David's line (Luke 1:32–33), He establishes His Kingdom that will never end.

    Notice how God's covenant grew with each stage: from two people with Adam and Eve, to a family with Noah, to a tribe with Abraham, to a nation with Moses, to a kingdom with David. One useful modern biblical synthesis traces an expanding social horizon from the primeval narratives through Noah, Abraham, Israel and David; present this as a theological model and cite its source, not as the Church’s definitive covenant taxonomy. Jesus' New Covenant is opened offered to the entire world as the Kingdom of God—His Body, the Church and gathers the messianic people who are the seed and beginning of His Kingdom, the Church.

    Editorial consequence. Helpful typology is not mistaken for defined doctrine.

    CH17-16 · Distinguish Christ’s fidelity, our covenant-breaking and sacramental restoration

    Severity: Major / covenant and sacraments · Control: DC-16, DC-18, DC-20 · Location: chapter_17.md:99–101

    Issue. Christ is faithful and the New Covenant cannot fail, but individuals can repudiate baptismal grace through mortal sin. The Church is not simply an ark that automatically keeps every person in; covenant life begins in Baptism, is nourished especially by Eucharist and is restored after grave post-baptismal sin in Penance. The Church is not identical to a political “kingdom on the march.”

    Source paragraphs with proposed inline redline:

    Christ's covenant is unbreakable ... He will not leave us or forsake us; and where insecurity paralyzes, a bond that cannot be revoked frees a person to love without guarding himself yet we remain free to reject or gravely violate covenant life. His fidelity grounds repentance and hope rather than guaranteeing our perseverance without cooperation.

    He gave us a visible household—an ark He carries us aboard and keeps us in. There we are washed ... fed ... forgiven ... The Church is where the covenant is entered, where it is renewed week after week, and where it is repaired when we damage it the Church, the sign and instrument of communion with God and human unity. We enter the covenant sacramentally in Baptism, are nourished above all in the Eucharist, and after grave sin are reconciled through Penance. ... Having gathered us in, He sends us back out as His kingdom on the march as witnesses and servants of the Kingdom to overcome evil with good.

    Editorial consequence. Ecclesiology and sacramental causality become exact.

    CH17-17 · Present the Cross through complementary Catholic images

    Severity: Major / atonement and justification · Control: DC-05, DC-13 · Location: chapter_17.md:105–107

    Issue. Satisfaction and debt language is Catholic when properly framed, but “the debt ... is paid (justice), the guilty are forgiven and restored (mercy)” can imply a single penal transaction and automatic subjective restoration of all. Scripture and Tradition also speak of sacrifice, covenant, obedience, victory, ransom, recapitulation and reconciliation.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    The Cross is the moment where everything converges ... In that single act of perfect love, sin is exposed in its full horror (truth), the debt we could never pay is paid (justice), the guilty are forgiven and restored (mercy) Christ offers the obedient satisfaction and sacrifice we could not offer, vindicating God’s holiness and opening justification (justice and mercy), and the Son lays down His life freely for those who rejected Him (sacrifice). You cannot pull these apart ... The Cross does not balance competing demands—it reveals that in God, they were never in competition. No one image exhausts the mystery: sacrifice, covenant, satisfaction, ransom, victory, reconciliation and recapitulation illuminate the one Paschal mystery in which justice and mercy are perfectly one. Its fruits must be received by grace; not every person is thereby automatically restored to friendship with God.

    Editorial consequence. A Catholic atonement theology replaces an easily misread transaction.

    CH17-18 · Remove what humanity is “owed” and state justification accurately

    Severity: Stop-ship / justice and justification · Control: DC-04, DC-06, DC-08, DC-13 · Location: chapter_17.md:129–137

    Issue. Christ gives the Father the perfect human obedience and worship creatures owe; humanity is not strictly owed Incarnation, grace or adoption. Justification is not merely “Christ’s righteousness” voluntarily accepted but God’s gracious act forgiving sins and making us inwardly just through sanctifying grace because of Christ’s merits.

    Controlling sources: Council of Trent, Decree on Justification 7; CCC 1987–1995.

    Source paragraphs with proposed inline redline:

    Jesus gives God His due, gives humanity what we're owed offers the Father perfect filial obedience in His humanity and freely gives sinners what they could never claim as owed, and restores right relationships.
