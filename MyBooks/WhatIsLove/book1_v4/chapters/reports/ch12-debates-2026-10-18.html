Chapter 12 — Four Debates on the Moral Argument

Notes from a previous pass whose passage changed

These were written against text that's since changed — most often because the suggested edit was applied. Compare each note against the new prose, then clear it.

    Four separate debates, one per option, each against the strongest opponent for that argument, with Patrick's side bound by every rule of the book. Nothing in the chapter was changed. The synthesis comes first; the four full reports follow, each ending in its verdict and suggested wording. Questions are at the very end.

    The headline

    No option survives on its own as a deduction, and all four debates arrived at the same shape. Each argument held only when recast as what the Church calls "converging and convincing arguments" (CCC 31): an obligation witness that reaches a Lawgiver, a communion witness that shows the Lawgiver is Love, and an honest comparison of where each account of morality stops (what line 107 already does). Option 4 is that shape; Options 2 and 3 turned out to be its two strongest parts; Option 1 is a weaker version of Option 2.

    What all four debates found

    1. Premise 1 was never the real fight. Narrowing it (Option 1) makes it something even an atheist grants: no human has authority over every person. The fight moves to a step the chapter never writes down, "if everyone is bound, then Someone binds." The strongest opponent, a realist who says duties are claims our neighbor lays on us, accepts "only persons lay claims" and names the neighbor. Nothing in a three- or four-line syllogism forces the step to God. That is why every option had to become a comparison.

    2. Five new arguments the book does not yet make, and should.

    • The release test (Options 1 and 2). Your victim cannot release you from the gravest duties; your nation cannot vote them away; the whole human race together could not. A claim no one on earth can cancel was not laid by anyone on earth. This answers the strongest secular view better than anything now in the chapter, and Dignitas Infinita 15 (already at line 99) is its bridge.
    • Guilt and forgiveness (Options 1 and 2). Breaking love's law leaves us guilty, not merely mistaken (line 107 names this but never unpacks it). Guilt is answerability, and answerability has a "to whom." Only the one wronged can forgive, so who forgives the sin no one else saw? This makes it an argument from love through the facet of mercy (CCC 1440, 1850).
    • How we know (Options 1, 2 and 4). Turn the evolutionary debunker on the godless realist: if moral facts are just there and conscience was tuned only for survival, it is luck that they ever met. If the same Love wrote the law and the heart that reads it, it is no accident (ST I-II q.91 a.2). Survival explains kindness to kin; it pulls against mercy to enemies and self-gift for strangers, which is exactly where the book's love is most itself. Line 43 has this argument but aims it only at materialism.
    • Owed, not imposed (Options 1 and 2). "Authority to bind" and "laws require lawgivers" sound like a commander-God whose will makes the rules, which reopens the Euthyphro dilemma Chapter 3 closed. Recast: God's first claim on us was not an order; it was our life. We owe everything to the One from whom we received everything, who loved first (1 John 4:19). This keeps grace first and joins the two witnesses.
    • Received whole (Option 3). Your insight, sharpened. No human can receive the whole of us, because no one knows the whole of us, not even we do. Only the Author can, and He gives us back to ourselves whole. And love's own evidence: we grieve not because love failed, but because love was meant to last. Even the marriage vow is "all the days of my life."

    3. Existing lines the debates flagged. Line 33–35's syllogism is the modern popular formula nearly word for word (a Protestant apologist's). Line 41 and 133 claim more than the Catholic tradition holds (see 4 below). Line 81 ("God must exist"), 109, 131 ("far more faith") and 135 ("you're hearing God's voice") overclaim against line 107's honest weighing. Line 103 calls the godless realist "incoherent" and says denial "refutes itself in the speaking"; both are too quick. Line 77's mathematics analogy helps the realist (laws that bind without persons). Line 111 jumps from the body's ache to "designed."

    On line 23: you kept "a law as real and universal as gravity… laws require lawgivers," and that ruling stands. The debates found that the gravity comparison is where a skeptic will answer, "gravity describes what bodies do; it needs no lawgiver." Your premise survives; the wording invites the reply. One option, if you want it: "Gravity describes what bodies do. Love tells us what we owe, and to whom."

    4. An honest map of our own tradition. Catholic thinkers have argued over whether "this is bad" would survive without God (Gregory of Rimini and Gabriel Vázquez said yes; Francisco Suárez said law in the full sense needs God). They agree that law, obligation and sin as offense against God need Him (CCC 1849–1850). The book should claim the second, not "without God nothing would be wrong." Saying so makes it harder to refute.

    5. Risks to the book's rules the debates caught, some of them in my own options message.

    • "Five marks" sounds like the "four marks" you rejected on 2026-10-06; use "five features" if anything.
    • "Strand" (which I used) is too close to the banned "threads," and so is St. John Henry Newman's famous image of a cable of threads. Use his principle, not his image.
    • The best-known forms of all three arguments come from Protestant writers. Catholic sources are available for each: St. Thomas (ST I-II qq.90–94; II-II q.23 a.1), Suárez, Newman (the argument from conscience; converging probabilities), G. E. M. Anscombe, Gabriel Marcel, St. John Paul II (Veritatis Splendor 41, "participated theonomy"), Benedict XVI (Deus Caritas Est), Gaudium et Spes 16, and Peter Kreeft.
    • Natural desire pressed too hard makes heaven owed to us; the Church says beatitude is free gift (CCC 1722).
    • Option 3 invites the "why is God hidden?" objection and needs one honest paragraph on it, without making every unbeliever culpable (CCC 847).

    Where this points

    A converging case with three witnesses, in plain words, replacing lines 29–81 and absorbing lines 101–111:

    • Love binds, and no one on earth can release us (obligation, guilt, forgiveness): a Lawgiver.
    • Love is one, and we can read it (the facets never truly contradict; conscience tracks even the costliest claims): one Source who made both the law and the mind.
    • Love is made for communion (only a person can love us back; no one but the Author can receive us whole; He loved first): the Lawgiver is Love.
    • Then the honest comparison of stopping points, with evil put on the scale and a pointer to Chapter 1, and the Church's frame that this is reason's witness, not a proof that replaces faith (CCC 35).

    Option 1 — Classical Form, Premise 1 Narrowed

    Opponent

    There are two voices, and they argue against each other as well as against the book.

    Voice A: a non-naturalist secular moral realist (the "robust realism" or "godless normative realism" school: Enoch, Wielenberg, Shafer-Landau, Parfit in his later work). This voice says moral facts are objective, necessary and brute. They are not made of matter, they need no author, and they bind every rational agent the way logical truths hold in every possible world. This is the strongest opponent for Option 1 because the option was built to answer it. Narrowing Premise 1 to duties assumes the realist will concede "no God, no one with authority." The realist concedes that happily and then denies that duties need anyone with authority. The whole debate is fought on that ground.

    Voice B: a moral error theorist (Mackie's queerness argument, as developed by Joyce, plus evolutionary debunking in the line of Street). Voice B is the realist's natural enemy and an awkward ally of Patrick's. It agrees that a categorical, inescapable "ought" would need something extraordinary behind it. Mackie himself granted, in his later book on theism, that objective prescriptive values would lend some support to theism. But Voice B denies Premise 2: nothing really binds. It is brought in because Option 1's Premise 2 ("every person is bound") must survive someone who denies it outright, not only someone who grants it.

    The argument as tested

    As submitted:

    1. If there is no God, there is no one with the authority to bind every person to love.
    2. Every person is bound to love: to truth, justice, mercy and sacrifice toward others.
    3. Therefore God exists.

    Best-defended form after repair:

    1. Every person is bound to love. We must never betray the truth, deny another what is his due, or harm the innocent, and we must give of ourselves when another's real good requires it. Some of these demands hold even at the cost of our lives.
    2. No human being, and not even the whole human race acting together, can release anyone from these demands.
    3. A demand that binds every person and that no human can release is owed to Someone above every human being. A duty is something owed, and what is owed is owed to someone. Here the argument turns on the difference between guilt and error.
    4. If there is no God, there is no such Someone.
    5. Therefore God exists: the One who loved us first, whose claim on us is love.

    Three changes were needed. The hidden premise (3) is now stated and argued. "Authority to bind" (which sounds like a commander) became "Someone to whom it is owed" (which sounds like a person in communion). And Premise 2 now rests on the clearest, least contested duties, with the facets serving as illustration.

    Debate highlights

    1. The validity gap. (Voice A, opening) Objection: "As written, the argument is invalid. Premise 1 says no God means no one with authority to bind. Premise 2 says everyone is bound. The conclusion needs a third premise: if everyone is bound, then someone binds them. I deny that premise. I grant your Premise 1 completely, since no human person has that authority. Duties need no binder. Narrowing Premise 1 hasn't removed the load. It has moved the load to a premise you didn't write down." Best reply: Grant it, and write the premise down. The binding the book means is answerability, and answerability is always answerability to someone (line 105: "an ought is a claim laid on a person"). Held? Failed as submitted. Held with repair. This is the central finding. The narrowed Premise 1 is now almost uncontested, which is what Patrick wanted. But the argument's real weight sits in the unstated premise, and that premise is Option 2's Premise 2. Option 1 does not avoid the dispute. It hides it.

    2. Brute duties, like mathematics. (Voice A) Objection: "'Gratuitous cruelty is wrong' is as necessary as '7 + 5 = 12', and no one authored arithmetic. Your own chapter says so at line 77: mathematics binds quantities without asking our consent. Necessary truths need no lawgiver." Best reply: The two kinds of law bind in different ways. Arithmetic binds quantities. It has never asked anything of anyone. A duty addresses a person, and breaking it leaves you guilty, not merely mistaken (line 107). Getting a sum wrong makes you wrong. Betraying a friend makes you wrong toward someone. Guilt has the form of answerability, and answerability has a "to whom." Counter: "Guilt is a feeling. Its fitting object is the victim. You've shown duties are owed to persons, not that they're owed to God." Held? Held with repair, partly. The address/guilt point is real and the book should make it. But it pushes the debate to the next question (exchange 3), and line 77's mathematics analogy works against this option. A careless reader will hear "laws bind without consent and without persons," which is the realist's point.

    3. Authority is shared out among us. (Voice A, the second-personal / contractualist move associated with Darwall and Scanlon) Objection: "Premise 1 is false after all. Every person has the standing to make demands on every other person, and to hold them accountable. That shared standing is all the 'authority' morality needs. Duties are owed to the neighbor, and the neighbor is enough." Best reply, in three parts: (a) Where does the standing come from? It is either one more brute moral fact, which takes us back to exchange 2, or a presupposition built into the practice of making claims. The chapter already answers the second version at line 103: a necessity of our practice is not yet a truth about the world. (b) Unwaivability. If the duty were owed only to the neighbor, the neighbor could release me from it. He cannot, at least not for the gravest duties. A people cannot vote away the wrong of enslaving a minority, and the whole human race could not do it together. Some claims also bind with no one present to make them: the newborn who cannot yet demand anything, the dead whose wishes I promised to keep, the wrong done in secret that no one will ever discover. If no one on earth can cancel a claim, no one on earth laid it. (c) The book's own ground. The standing each person has is exactly the dignity Dignitas Infinita 15 calls prior to any recognition, the dignity the chapter grounds in the Creator's love (line 99; ST I q.20 a.2). The second-personalist is right that the neighbor has a claim on me. He has not explained why that claim cannot be bought, sold or voted away. Held? Held with repair. This exchange forced the wording change from "authority to bind" to "Someone to whom it is owed, and whom no human can overrule." Unwaivability turned out to be the option's strongest support. The chapter does not yet use it.

    4. A Euthyphro dilemma about authority. (Voice A) Objection: "Why must anyone obey God? If you answer 'because He commands it,' you are going in a circle. If you answer 'because He is good,' then goodness is doing the binding, God's authority adds nothing, and we are back to a brute moral fact. Your Premise 1 rests either on voluntarism (the view that duties exist because God wills them) or on nothing." Best reply: The Church is not voluntarist. Chapter 3 already holds that goodness is neither above God nor invented by Him. It is His own nature. The natural law is our share in His eternal wisdom (ST I-II q.91 a.2), and its first principle, that good is to be done and pursued, is self-evident to reason (q.94 a.2). It is not an extra decree added on top. So the binding does come from the Good, as the realist says. The difference is that this Good is a Person. That is the only place where "what binds" and "Someone to whom it is owed" are the same thing, because in God goodness, wisdom and personhood are not three parts but one (simplicity). Counter: "So your 'authority' language was voluntarist after all." Held? Held with repair. Phrases like "authority to bind" and "laws require lawgivers" (lines 23, 133, 137), set beside the gravity analogy, sound like a commander-God whose will makes the rules. That invites exactly this attack. Reframe as owing and being owed, not commanding and obeying.

    5. "Your own scholastics disagree." (Voice A, citing the Catholic tradition against itself) Objection: "A late-medieval scholastic line, often traced to Gregory of Rimini, held that sinning against right reason would still be wrong even if, impossibly, God did not exist. Gabriel Vázquez, SJ, held that natural law binds by its own rationality, not by command. Modern Catholic natural-law theorists (Grisez, Finnis) say the basic goods and first practical principles are self-evident without reference to God. Your Premise 1 is not even the Catholic consensus." Best reply: Francisco Suárez, SJ, answered this in his own day (De legibus II, ch. 6; verify the chapter). The goodness or badness of an act is intrinsic to it, but law in the strict sense, with obligation, needs the will of a superior. That is why the natural law is truly a law and not just good advice. Aquinas defines law as reason's ordering for the common good, given by the one who has care of the community and made known (ST I-II q.90 a.4). And the Catechism holds both sides: sin is an offense against reason and right conscience (CCC 1849) and an offense against God (CCC 1850). Knowing the law without God is possible (line 119). The law existing without God is a different claim. Held? Held with repair, and this is the most important caution for the book. The tradition is divided about whether the thin sense of wrongness ("this act is bad") would survive without God. It is united that law, obligation and guilt as offense against Someone require God. The argument must claim only the thick sense. The book should never suggest the Church teaches that without God nothing would be bad. Line 41 ("Without God, morality becomes subjective") and line 133 ("they collapse into nothing more than human opinion") both overreach on this point.

    6. Nothing binds at all. (Voice B, the error theorist) Objection: "Voice A has just admitted that inescapable authority is the strange part. I agree with your Premise 1: such authority would be queer, the kind of thing only a god could supply. So I deny Premise 2. Moral thought presupposes a categorical 'must' that the world does not contain. Evolution explains why we feel it." Best reply: (a) The Moorean answer: I am more sure that torturing a child for fun is wrong for everyone, whatever they want than I am of any premise in the queerness argument. A clever argument against something more certain than its own premises refutes itself. (b) The "companions in guilt" answer, already at lines 43 and 105: the error theorist asks us to believe him because the evidence requires it, a categorical "ought" of reasoning. If such oughts are too queer to exist, his case asks nothing of us. If they exist, queerness is no objection. Counter: "Epistemic 'oughts' are only hypothetical: if you want truth, follow the evidence. And my Moorean certainty is just what evolution would install." Reply: If debunking by evolution cancels conscience, it cancels the reasoning faculty that runs the debunking (line 43). And "if you want truth" does not capture what we mean when we call someone who believes against the evidence irrational. We mean he has failed at something every mind owes. Held? Held. Premise 2 survives the error theorist at full strength. But line 103's claim that the denial "refutes itself in the speaking" is too quick. A careful error theorist denies moral oughts, not every ought, so the self-refutation has to be argued through the parity with reasoning, not asserted.

    7. Debunking turned on the realist. (Patrick's side going on offense, Voice A defending) Patrick's side: The error theorist's debunking hits the godless realist harder than it hits the theist. If moral facts are causally inert and brute, why would minds shaped by survival track them? The theist has an answer: the same Love that grounds the law wrote it on the heart (Romans 2:14-15; CCC 1954-1956). Voice A's reply: The "third-factor" answer associated with Enoch. Survival and flourishing are themselves good, so selection pressures pointed us roughly toward the good. Patrick's side: That works for caring for kin and returning favors. It works worst exactly where the book's love is most demanding: mercy to enemies, giving yourself for a stranger, telling the truth when a lie would pay. Those are the claims the book puts at the center (Matthew 5:44; the Good Samaritan, Luke 10:29-37), and they are what selection would have filtered out. Held? Held. This is a real gain the chapter does not yet make. Line 43 uses debunking only against materialism. It works against the godless realist too.

    8. Love cannot be commanded. (Voice A, as an internal critique of the book) Objection: "Chapter 4 says a gift that is forced is no gift, and line 77 says this law 'is not imposed the way gravity is.' Option 1 says every person is bound to love by an authority. Which is it? Love done under orders is not love." Best reply: The tradition holds both. Charity falls under a precept (ST II-II q.44 a.1). Benedict XVI gave the reason in a single line: love can be "commanded" because it has first been given (Deus Caritas Est 14; verify through the quote cache). He loved us first (1 John 4:19). The New Law is a law of love, not fear (CCC 1972). The claim on us is the claim of a Lover who gave first, not a ruler who imposes. Freedom is not cancelled. A claim is something you can refuse, and only a free creature can be bound by one. Held? Held with repair. The option has to show grace first inside the argument itself, by naming the Lawgiver as the One who loved first. Otherwise the syllogism contradicts Chapters 4 and 12's own "law as gift" passage.

    9. Bound by Someone they have never heard of? (Voice A) Objection: "If every person is bound by God's claim, then the honest atheist and the person who has never heard the Gospel are bound by an authority they do not know. Either they are guilty for not knowing, which contradicts your CCC 847, or God's claim does nothing and the duties stand on their own." Best reply: The natural law is made known through nature itself: God puts it in the mind (ST I-II q.90 a.4 ad 1; Romans 2:14-15). One can be bound by a law without knowing the name of the One who wrote it, which is exactly line 95's "light without yet knowing its name." How guilty someone is depends on knowledge and consent (CCC 1857-1860). Ignorance that is no one's fault reduces or removes guilt, and grace can be at work in a person who does not yet know the Father's name (CCC 847; LG 16). Knowing and grounding are different (line 119): God's claim is the ground, not a fact everyone has to be told before they are bound. Held? Held. This exchange fits the book's rules well. The option treats unbelievers as bound and known by God, not as guilty.

    10. Are mercy and sacrifice really duties for everyone? (Voice A) Objection: "Even many realists hold that forgiveness and self-sacrifice go beyond duty: admirable, not required. Your own Church separates what is commanded from the counsels, which are invitations. Premise 2 claims too much." Best reply: Narrow the premise to its hardest core. Some acts must never be done, by anyone, in any circumstance (Veritatis Splendor 52). In those cases the duty of sacrifice is the duty to suffer rather than do evil, which is the witness of the martyrs (VS 90-94). And the duty to give of oneself when another's real need requires it binds everyone (Luke 10:29-37). Forgiveness is commanded for Christians (Matthew 6:14-15; CCC 2843). The argument does not need the opponent to grant it, though. One duty that binds every person is enough for the premise. Held? Held with repair. As submitted, Premise 2 is hostage to its most debatable clause. Use the facets as illustration and rest the premise on the duties no serious realist rejects.

    11. Stopping points on equal terms. (Voice A, closing) Objection: "In the end, your God's goodness is as brute as my moral facts. You stop where I stop, and you add a very large extra entity. By parsimony, and by any honest weighing of probabilities, my view is the simpler one." Best reply: God is not one more brute item on the list. He is the One whose essence is to exist (ST I q.3 a.4). One stopping point explains being, mind, persons, our knowledge of the moral law (exchange 7), why the law cannot be waived (exchange 3), why breaking it is guilt toward Someone (exchange 2), and why it calls us to communion. The realist's facts explain only what is right and wrong, and they leave open why they apply to us, how we know them, and to whom we answer. That is line 107's honest comparison of stopping points. Held? Held only as a comparison. This is the second central finding. At the decisive point, Option 1's deductive form becomes an inference to the best explanation. Against a well-armed realist, the syllogism alone does not force the conclusion. It wins only when paired with line 107's comparison. A rational realist can remain unpersuaded, which is consistent with CCC 35: reason's witness, not a replacement for faith.

    Faults found (ranked by severity)

    1. Hidden premise. Critical; fixable. The argument is invalid without "if every person is bound, someone binds (or is owed)." That premise is exactly what the strongest opponent denies. Narrowing Premise 1 makes Premise 1 nearly uncontested but moves the whole burden into a premise that is never stated. Fix: state it and argue it through guilt as answerability, unwaivability, and address. At that point Option 1 contains Option 2.
    2. Thin and thick "bound." Severe; fixable. Premise 2 is most obviously true in the thin sense ("this is wrong"). Premise 1 and the hidden premise are true only in the thick sense ("I am answerable to Someone"). If the two senses blur, the argument equivocates. Fix: Premise 2 must claim, and show, the thick sense (unwaivable, answerable, guilt rather than error).
    3. Voluntarist overtones. Severe; fixable. "Authority to bind," "laws require lawgivers" and the gravity analogy (lines 23, 133, 137) suggest a commander-God whose will makes the rules. That invites a Euthyphro dilemma about authority, and it sits badly with Chapter 3 and with ST I-II q.91 a.2 / q.94 a.2. Fix: "One to whom love is owed, who loved us first."
    4. Overreach against the tradition. Moderate to severe; fixable. Lines 41 and 133 ("morality becomes subjective," "collapse into nothing more than human opinion") claim more than the Catholic tradition holds (Gregory of Rimini's line, Vázquez, new natural law). Claim only that law, obligation and guilt as offense against Someone need God (Suárez; CCC 1849-1850).
    5. Premise 2 hostage to mercy and sacrifice. Moderate; fixable. If these are read as universal positive duties, opponents (and the precept/counsel distinction) push back. Rest the premise on exceptionless negative duties and "suffer rather than do evil" (VS 52, 90-94).
    6. Grace first not visible. Moderate; fixable. A bare "every person is bound" reads as law before gift and seems to contradict Chapter 4 and line 77. Name the Lawgiver as the One who loved first (1 John 4:19; DCE 14).
    7. The mathematics analogy cuts the wrong way. Moderate; fixable. Line 77 says mathematics binds without consent, which is the realist's best point. Keep the contrast it goes on to draw (this law addresses persons) and drop or sharpen the parallel.
    8. The deductive form cannot win alone. Structural; only partly fixable. At the bottom, the dispute is which stopping point explains more. Option 1 needs line 107's comparison to finish the job. Fix: present it as one strand of a converging case (CCC 31), not as a proof that stands by itself.
    9. "The denial refutes itself" is too quick. Minor; fixable. Line 103 should argue the parity with reasoning (as lines 43 and 105 do), not assert self-refutation.
    10. "Toward others" leaves out duties to God and to oneself. Minor; fixable. Either drop "toward others" or note that love's demands also run toward God and oneself.

    New insights

    • Unwaivability is the strongest evidence for a personal ground, and the book doesn't use it. No victim, nation or human race can release anyone from the gravest duties. A claim no one on earth can cancel was not laid by anyone on earth. This answers the most sophisticated secular view (that obligation is shared among persons) better than anything now in the chapter.
    • Guilt as answerability. Line 107 names "guilty rather than merely mistaken" but doesn't unpack it. Guilt has a "to whom" built into it. That is the bridge from "bound" to "Someone."
    • Debunking hits the godless realist too, at love's most demanding points. Mercy to enemies and self-gift for strangers are what selection would filter out. A realist who relies on "evolution tracked the good" has his worst case where the book's love is most itself.
    • Owed, not imposed. Recasting the Lawgiver as the One to whom love is owed, who gave it first, fits the book's covenant and communion themes better than the language of legislation. It also defuses the Euthyphro dilemma about authority.
    • An honest map of the Catholic tradition. The Church's thinkers have argued over whether "bad" would survive without God. They agree that law and sin as offense against God would not (CCC 1849-1850). Saying so makes the book harder to refute, not easier.
    • A claim can be refused; a force cannot. Being bound to love is evidence of freedom, not a threat to it. That ties Option 1 to Chapter 4.

    Risks to the book's rules

    • Overclaiming (CCC 35). The classical syllogism looks like a proof. Present it as reason's witness and pair it with line 107's honest comparison. "Therefore God exists" should be framed as what the argument points to, not a checkmate.
    • Doctrinal drift toward voluntarism. "Laws require lawgivers" and "authority to bind" can read as duties existing because God wills them. That contradicts Chapter 3's Euthyphro answer and Thomistic natural law. Keep Chapter 3's "His own nature" language in view.
    • Misstating the tradition. Saying "without God nothing would be wrong" goes beyond what Catholic thinkers agree on. Claim law and guilt, not bare badness.
    • Divine simplicity. If Premise 2 lists sacrifice as a demand of the law and the law is God's nature, avoid saying God's essence is sacrifice. Keep the Trinitarian clarification: total self-gift, no loss.
    • Culpability. "Every person is bound" must not slide into "every unbeliever is guilty." Keep CCC 847 / LG 16 and CCC 1857-1860 nearby (exchange 9).
    • Protestant authorities. This argument's modern popular form is strongly tied to Protestant apologists. Don't cite them, and don't echo their exact syllogism (the current lines 33-35 are that formula nearly word for word). Ground it in Catholic sources instead: Aquinas (ST I-II q.90-94), Suárez, Veritatis Splendor, Deus Caritas Est, CCC 1849-1850, 1954-1960.
    • Grotius. The opponent's best historical citation ("even if God did not exist") is often credited to Grotius, a Protestant. If the book addresses the objection, attribute it to the earlier Catholic scholastic line, or to no one. Never cite Grotius as an authority.

    Option 2 — The Argument from Obligation

    Opponent

    Two voices working as a team. Neither is a straw man.

    • The Secular Realist (R). R is a robust non-naturalist moral realist of the "godless normative realism" school (Erik Wielenberg; also David Enoch, Derek Parfit and Russ Shafer-Landau). For R, moral facts are objective, necessary and brute: they are just there, and nothing grounds them. R adds the strongest secular account of obligation in print, the "second-personal" view (Stephen Darwall's school). On that view an obligation really is a demand that persons can address to one another. But the person who makes the demand is the one I would wrong, my neighbor, and the authority behind it is the moral community of free and equal persons. No God is needed.
    • The Debunker (D). D is an evolutionary debunker. D uses the "Darwinian dilemma" (Sharon Street's school) and the evolutionary case for moral error theory (Richard Joyce's school), with cognitive science of religion added: we are built to over-detect agents, and that bias explains the feeling that "Someone" is making a claim on us.

    Why they are the strongest pair against this option. R grants premises 1 and 3 and puts all of the pressure on premise 2 and the step to premise 4. That is exactly where Option 2 carries its load. R's second-personal view is especially dangerous because it agrees with the letter of premise 2 ("an obligation is a claim one person lays on another") and then fills the "person" slot with the neighbor. D attacks premises 1 and 3 and the reliability of the felt "claim." Between the two of them, every premise is under fire. As it turns out, D can also be turned against R, which is the most useful thing the debate found.

    The argument as tested (final, best-defended form)

    1. Love binds us. We really owe one another the truth, owe each person his due, owe our children our very selves, and owe mercy, at least an end to revenge, to anyone who sincerely asks for it. Breaking these leaves us guilty, not merely mistaken. (Repaired: mercy and self-gift are given in the minimal forms every culture recognizes. See Exchange 9.)
    2. Whatever we owe, we owe to someone. An obligation has a shape that only persons can fill. It can be wronged, it gives someone standing to hold us to account, and it can be forgiven. No rock, rule or bare fact can do any of these. (Repaired: argued from these three marks, not stipulated as a definition. See Exchange 1.)
    3. No human being can release us from love's claim. No society can, and neither can I. The neighbor's claim is real, but it is not his to cancel: he cannot sign away his own dignity (Dignitas Infinita 15), and some duties bind where no human claimant can press them. (Repaired: "no one invented them" became "no one can release us." See Exchanges 3 and 8.)
    4. So the best explanation is that love's claim reaches us from a Person beyond every human being, Someone to whom we owe everything because from Him we have received everything. (Repaired: abductive, not deductive. The argument shows a personal source of claim above every human claim. That He is one and that He is Love are handed on to the integration section, Ch. 3, and the communion strand. See Exchanges 2, 7 and 10.)

    Debate highlights

    Exchange 1: "Premise 2 is a stipulation."

    R: "An obligation is a claim one person lays on another" is a definition chosen to win. Moral facts are neither rocks, nor rules, nor equations. They are a fourth kind of thing: normative facts. The fact that lying would hurt her counts against lying, full stop. Your list is a false menu. Reply: We are not stipulating. We are reading the grammar of obligation, which shows three marks. You can wrong someone, which is more than making an error. Someone gains standing to blame you. And the wrong can be forgiven. A fact that merely "counts in favor" yields the "should" of advice ("you should take the faster road"). It does not yield the "owe" of duty. The chapter already relies on this difference: breaking the law leaves us "guilty rather than merely mistaken" (line 107). A Catholic philosopher, G. E. M. Anscombe, argued that the special binding sense of the moral "ought" belongs to a "law conception of ethics" and makes no sense without a lawgiver. Did it hold? Held with repair. As worded, premise 2 asserts what it needs to prove. It has to be argued from the three marks. "Rocks, rules and equations" also misses R's actual candidate, so it should become "no rock, rule or bare fact." One honest caution: the Catholic tradition itself has debated this point. The Spanish scholastics argued over whether natural law obliges by rational nature alone (Gabriel Vázquez) or needs God's command to be law in the full sense (Francisco Suárez). Premise 2 is a reasoned position, not a self-evident definition.

    Exchange 2: The second-personal move (the most serious attack)

    R: I grant premise 2 completely. Obligations are claims persons lay on persons. But the person who lays the claim is the one I would wrong: my neighbor. When I lie to her, she is wronged, she may blame me, she may forgive me. That is the whole structure, and it needs a community of persons, not a God. Your premise 4 does not follow from 2 and 3. Reply (first attempt): But who gives your neighbor the authority to lay that claim? R: Her dignity does. It is a brute fact about persons, and your own chapter says dignity is "prior to any recognition." Did it hold? Failed as stated. This is the decisive fault in Option 2. The neighbor fits the "person" slot in premise 2 perfectly well. Moving from "a person lays the claim" to "a Person above every human being lays it" needs a hidden premise, and the syllogism leaves it out. The repair comes in Exchange 3.

    Exchange 3: Claims the holder cannot cancel

    Reply (escalated): Test whether the claim is simply hers. A claim that belongs to its holder can be waived by its holder, the way I can forgive a debt you owe me. Some claims cannot be waived, though. She cannot validly consent to be enslaved, or to be tortured for someone's amusement, and the Church teaches that her dignity "cannot be lost" (Dignitas Infinita 15). A claim its holder cannot cancel is not merely hers. Some duties also bind where no human claimant can press them: a promise to the dying, the child not yet born, the patient who will never wake, the wrong done in secret against oneself. In each case love still binds, and no human voice is there to make the claim. R: Inalienability is just part of the brute fact of dignity. Duties to the dead are a puzzle for everyone, and realists say we owe them to the person as he was in life. Did it hold? Held with repair. This breaks the pure "the neighbor is the source" view. R has to retreat to "dignity is brute," and the debate becomes a contest between stopping points, which is line 107's ground. The cost is that Option 2 stops being a deduction and becomes an inference to the best explanation. That change is the right one.

    Exchange 4: "The felt claim is an adaptation."

    D: Your premise 1 is a strong feeling, and the "Someone" behind it is a projection. Your ancestors who felt the tribe's eyes on them left more descendants. Our agency-detection is set to hair-trigger, so we hear a speaker behind every "ought," much as we hear a stalker in rustling grass. The feeling would exist whether or not anyone stands behind it, so it is no evidence. Reply: The chapter has already shown the mirror (line 43). The same blind process built the reasoning D trusts, and "follow the evidence" is itself an "ought." If "built to get along" cancels conscience, it cancels thinking too. The argument also does not rest on a feeling of presence. It rests on the logic of being wronged and being forgiven (Exchange 1). D: Epistemic norms are only instrumental: "if you want truth, weigh evidence." Truth-tracking was selected for because true beliefs help survival. Moral beliefs only had to be useful, not true. Reply: Then D's own conclusion, "you ought to give up moral belief," shrinks to "if you happen to want accuracy...". Meanwhile D still blames, still protests, and still says "that's not fair" (line 103). Did it hold? Held with repair, partially. Against a global debunker it holds. Against an instrumentalist about epistemic norms it ends in a stalemate, and honesty requires saying so. The firm repair is this: the chapter must not let "you feel the pull, so you hear God's voice" (line 135) do any argumentative work. Keep it as devotion after the argument.

    Exchange 5: Turning the Debunker against the Realist (the strongest new move)

    Patrick's side: D's best weapon actually hits R hardest. If moral facts are brute and causally inert (they cause nothing), and our conscience came from a blind process aimed only at survival, then the two meeting would be pure luck. Nothing would explain why a survival-tuned conscience tracks facts that never touched it. Street's dilemma was aimed at realists like R for exactly this reason. Theism has an answer the godless realist lacks: the same Wisdom that is the source of the law also made the mind that reads it. Natural law is the rational creature's share in the eternal law (ST I-II q.91 a.2), and line 77's "mathematics our minds are uncannily fitted to read" hints at this. R: I have a "third-factor" reply. Survival and the real good overlap. Pain really is bad, so evolution teaching us to avoid pain also teaches us something true. Reply: That works for welfare goods. It fails exactly where love's claims are hardest and most distinctive: truth told at great cost, mercy to an enemy, self-gift for people who are not our kin, protection of the unborn and the dying who can never repay us. Evolution pulls against these, and these are the ones the conscience of the saints tracks most surely. Did it hold? Held. This is the best move the debate produced, and the chapter does not yet make it.

    Exchange 6: Euthyphro aimed at the Lawgiver, and "Why obey God?"

    R: Option 2 brings back the divine command theory that your Ch. 3 rejected. Does God lay the claim because the act is good? Then the good does the work and the Person adds nothing. Or is the act good because God lays the claim? Then it is arbitrary. Also, what obliges you to obey God's claim? It can't be another claim of God's, because that would be bootstrapping. Reply: In God, the claim and the good are not two things (divine simplicity, ST I q.3). Law, for St. Thomas, is a work of reason, not bare will (ST I-II q.90 a.1), and the eternal law is God's own wisdom ordering all things (ST I-II q.93). As for "why obey?": not because He is strong, but because He gave. We owe God what is due to Him in justice and gratitude, and in St. Thomas religion is part of justice (ST II-II q.81). The first claim God lays on us is not an order barked at us. It is the gift of our very being, and a creature who has received everything owes everything (Ch. 4: covenant and gift). Grace comes first (1 John 4:19). Pope Benedict XVI made the same point in Deus Caritas Est: love can be commanded because it has first been given (paraphrased; verify the paragraph, which is likely §14, against vatican.va before use). R: Gratitude is itself an obligation, so you are relying on a norm that comes before God's claim. Reply: Gratitude is the clearest case of a person-to-person claim there is. Where the Giver is the source of everything, the gift is the claim. Did it hold? Held with repair. Unless the chapter says outright that God's claim is His wisdom and love, not raw will, Option 2 contradicts Ch. 3. With that sentence added, this becomes one of the strongest "from love" points in the debate.

    Exchange 7: "Your stopping point is as brute as mine."

    R: You stop at "God's nature just is good." I stop at "these moral facts just are." At least my stopping point posits no infinite mind. Reply: Theism stops at one necessary Being whose existence and goodness are not two (ST I q.3 a.4), not at a scattered set of brute principles. That single stopping point also explains things R's cannot: why there are persons to be bound at all, why conscience tracks the law (Exchange 5), why the law's whole content is personal (line 77), and why the facets of love never truly contradict (line 125). R: God is a far larger posit, divine simplicity is disputed, and Ch. 1's problem of evil counts against your hypothesis. Did it hold? Held, but not decisively. The comparison is a weighing of explanations, and line 107 is honest about that. Lines 81 ("God must exist"), 133 ("Take God away, and they collapse") and 131 ("far more faith") overclaim against it.

    Exchange 8: The Kantian rival, "We are the lawgivers."

    R (borrowing Kant): Premise 3 says no human invented the obligations. Agreed: reason legislates them. Every rational being gives the moral law to himself. That makes it universal and uninvented, and it needs no God. Reply: Someone who binds himself can always untie the knot. Anscombe judged the idea of legislating for oneself incoherent for this reason. The Church gives the true account of what Kant saw: reason does carry its own law within it, but as a share in God's wisdom. St. John Paul II called this "participated theonomy" (Veritatis Splendor 41). Did it hold? Held with repair. Premise 3 should be about release, not invention. The test of authority is who can dispense from the law. No human, no society, and not even I can release me from the duty not to torture a child.

    Exchange 9: "Mercy and self-gift are not duties."

    R: I accept truth-telling and justice as duties. Forgiveness, though, is a gift, and an obligatory forgiveness is no forgiveness. Self-sacrifice goes beyond duty: your own firefighter (line 67) is a hero precisely because he did more than duty asked. Your Ch. 4 says a gift forced is no gift. Reply: Every culture recognizes the minimal forms. Parents owe their children their very selves at real cost. One owes the sincerely sorry an end to endless revenge. Heroism is the fullness of a self-gift that everyone owes in some measure. Christ's command to love even enemies (Matt 5:44) belongs to the Gospel's fuller law, and it cannot be a premise for an unbeliever. Did it hold? Held with repair. Premise 1 must state the minimal universal forms, or R simply drops mercy and sacrifice and the argument loses its "from love" character.

    Exchange 10: "Why one Person above every human?"

    R: Even if a non-human claimant is needed, why one? Why not many gods, or the idealized community of all rational beings? Reply: Love's claims never truly contradict one another (line 125; Ch. 5). Human claims constantly collide and need something above them to settle them, and otherwise "nothing arbitrates except force" (line 47). The inalienable core is identical in every human being, which points to one source rather than a committee. R: That coherence could itself be a brute feature of the moral facts. Did it hold? Held with repair. The obligation strand earns a personal source of claim above every human claim. That this source is one, simple and Love itself comes from Ch. 3, the integration section, and the communion strand. Premise 4 as written ("a Person above every human being") claims too much for this step alone.

    Exchange 11: The forgiveness test (the closing exchange)

    Patrick's side: Only the one you have wronged can forgive you. So consider the guilt no human being can lift: the sin nobody saw, the wrong done to someone now dead, the conscience that keeps accusing after the victim has said "I forgive you." The Church names what is going on. Sin is first of all an offense against God and a rupture of communion with Him (CCC 1440, 1850; cf. Ps 51:4), and that is why the Sacrament of Reconciliation exists. St. John Henry Newman built his whole argument from conscience on this point: the shame and responsibility of conscience have the shape of answering to a Person. He called conscience the "aboriginal Vicar of Christ" (Letter to the Duke of Norfolk §5). The Council says the same: conscience is the "most secret core and sanctuary," where a person is alone with God (Gaudium et Spes 16). R: Lingering guilt is psychology, or the self is the one wronged. D: Guilt feelings are adaptive. Reply: Granted, this is not proof. But a wrong with no one wronged is exactly what R cannot make sense of, and it is exactly what the hypothesis of a personal Lawgiver predicts. Did it hold? Held as evidence, not proof. This is the most distinctively "from love" datum in Option 2, because it runs through the facet of mercy.

    Faults found (ranked by severity)

    1. Critical: premise 2 does not lead to premise 4 (the neighbor as claimant). The second-personal realist accepts premise 2 and names the neighbor as the claimant, so the syllogism is invalid without a hidden premise. Partly fixable: add the cannot-release and no-human-claimant data (Exchange 3) and recast the conclusion as inference to the best explanation. Option 2 cannot be made a valid deduction from premises an atheist realist must accept.
    2. Major: premise 2 is asserted as a definition. That begs the question against R, and Catholic scholastics themselves disputed it (Vázquez and Suárez). Fixable: argue it from the three marks of being wronged, standing to blame, and being forgiven.
    3. Major: tension with Ch. 3's Euthyphro answer. "A claim laid by a person" reads as divine will, which brings back the divine command theory Ch. 3 rejected. Fixable: one sentence saying God's claim is His wisdom and love, law as a work of reason (ST I-II q.90 a.1; q.93), grounded in gift.
    4. Major: premise 4 overreaches to "one Person above every human being." Oneness and supremacy are not established by obligation alone. Fixable: give a modest conclusion and hand off to the integration section, Ch. 3 and the communion strand.

    New insights the book does not yet state

    1. Use the debunker against the brute realist. If moral facts are inert and conscience was tuned only for survival, their meeting is a lucky accident. If the same Love wrote both the law and the heart that reads it, the match is no accident (ST I-II q.91 a.2). Love's hardest claims, such as mercy to enemies and self-gift for those who cannot repay, are where evolution pulls against them and conscience still tracks them.
    2. Forgiveness is the decisive datum. Only the wronged can forgive. Guilt with no human victim, or guilt that outlives human forgiveness, points to the One against whom all sin is first committed (CCC 1440, 1850; Ps 51:4). This makes the moral argument specifically an argument from love, through the facet of mercy, rather than a generic moral argument.
    3. Test authority by release, not by origin. A claim its own holder cannot waive is not merely his. Inalienable dignity (Dignitas Infinita 15) is the bridge from the neighbor's claim to the Lawgiver's.
    4. Obligation grounded in gift. God's claim on us is the claim of the Giver of our being, which answers "Why obey God?" without voluntarism and keeps grace first. A law of love can be commanded only by someone who has loved first (cf. Deus Caritas Est; 1 John 4:19). This joins the obligation strand to the communion strand.
    5. A sharper version of the "atheist's dilemma" (line 103), following Anscombe. One can keep moral obligation in its full sense of guilt, blame and forgiveness and look for the Lawgiver. Or one can drop it and keep only "flourishing and defect" (the neo-Aristotelian route of Philippa Foot's school), and then a liar is defective, like a wolf that will not hunt, but never guilty. That fork is cleaner and fairer than the current option (2), which is labeled "incoherent."
    6. Answer autonomy with "participated theonomy" (Veritatis Splendor 41). The chapter never meets the Kantian rival. The Church's account keeps everything true in it: reason really does carry its law within.
    7. Be honest about the Catholic tradition's own debate. Natural law is knowable by reason alone (CCC 1954 to 1960), and the goodness or evil of acts lies in their object (Veritatis Splendor 79 to 80). So a realist who says "lying is wrong because of what lying is" is right as far as he goes. The dispute is about the ultimate ground, not the immediate one. Saying so wins the realist's trust.

    4. So love's claim reaches us from beyond every human being: from Someone to whom we owe everything, because from Him we have received everything.

Line 107's honest comparison of stopping points would follow directly, and the integration section and communion strand would carry the argument from "a personal Lawgiver" to "the One who is Love."

    Option 3 — The Argument from Communion

    Opponent

    Two voices, taking turns.

    1. A Buddhist teacher trained in the Theravada texts and familiar with Madhyamaka. She holds three things. Craving (taṇhā), and above all the craving to go on existing as a self, is the root of suffering. There is no enduring self that could be "loved wholly and forever." The cure for the ache is to end the craving, not to find something that fills it.
    2. A philosophical naturalist who works on evolutionary psychology and philosophy of mind. Her fallback position is the absurdist one: the gap between what we long for and what the world gives is simply the human condition.

    Why these two. Option 3 rests on a longing, so the strongest opponents are the ones who can explain that longing without God. The Buddhist accepts the premise that no finite thing satisfies us (premise 3), then turns it against the argument. On her reading the restlessness is the disease, not a signpost. The naturalist attacks premise 4 (longings correspond to real goods) at its root. Natural selection explains why hunger is paired with food, and that same mechanism predicts an open-ended desire with no matching object. Together they cover both ways the argument can fail. One says the longing is a disorder. The other says it is a by-product of evolution.

    The argument as tested

    This is the final, best-defended form after the repairs made during the debate. Changes from the brief's version are marked [R].

    1. We are made for communion. Beneath every lesser want, we long to love and to be loved, wholly and without end. The evidence is experience: loneliness, grief, and love's refusal to accept the death of the beloved. [R: P1 now rests on experience. CCC 27 is cited only after the conclusion.]
    2. Only a person can love us back. A law, a force or an idea can bind us or move us. It cannot know us, and it cannot receive or return a gift of self. (The chapter already says this at line 119.)
    3. No finite person can love us wholly and forever. No creature knows us all the way down, so none can receive the whole of us. None can hold us past death. Even the marriage vow is for "all the days of my life." [R: "no human" became "no finite person". "Wholly" now means knows and receives the whole of us, without limit, not "gives everything it has", which a spouse truly does.]
    4. The deepest longings built into our nature are not the kind of thing that has no object. Every other natural hunger has its bread. The will reaches for good as such, which is why every finite good shows up to us as finite (ST I-II q.2 a.8, read as an account of the will, not as a premise). [R: P4 is no longer stated as a universal law of correspondence. It is now a weighted clue inside an explanatory comparison.]
    5. So the best explanation is a Person without limit, who knows us wholly and can hold us forever, and who loved us first (1 John 4:19). Revelation gives that Person His name: God is Love (1 John 4:8). [R: framed as a best explanation under CCC 35, not as a proof. Grace comes first: God starts the love and does not merely return ours.]
    6. Patrick's insight, repaired. The most perfect love is one given whole and received whole in turn. No one else can receive the whole of us, because no one else knows the whole of us; not even we do. The Author can. When we give ourselves to Him, we are given back to ourselves whole (GS 24; Mt 16:25). [R: "who could return our love" became "who could receive it whole, having first given His".]

    Debate highlights

    Exchange 1: Counterexamples to premise 4

    Objection (naturalist). People long to fly, to be eighteen again, to be adored by a stranger they have never met. Those longings have no object. "Longings correspond to real goods" is false as stated. Reply. The argument concerns natural desires: universal, present before any culture shapes them, and not made up out of fantasy. Hunger, thirst, the desire to know, and the desire for companionship are natural. The wish to fly is a picture we form, not a need we have. Aquinas reasons from natural desire in exactly this sense (ST I q.12 a.1; I q.75 a.6). Did it hold? Held with repair. P4 must say "natural" and define it. The repair opens the next exchange, though, because the naturalist now asks what makes a desire natural and why natural ones should come paired with an object.

    Exchange 2: Selection explains the pairing, and the explanation does not reach this case (the hardest blow)

    Objection (naturalist). Hunger matches food because animals that hungered for things that do not exist died out. Selection explains the pairing only where the object exists and affects survival. A longing for infinite, everlasting love could not have been selected for its object. It is what you would expect as a by-product of the open-ended dissatisfaction that keeps a primate striving, the hedonic treadmill. Your examples of hunger and food are explained by a mechanism that predicts the opposite in your key case. The teleological version ("a natural desire cannot be in vain") assumes that nature is ordered by a mind, which is the conclusion you are trying to reach. Reply. First, a parity argument from line 43. The desire to know also outruns survival: pure mathematics, the age of galaxies. The naturalist still trusts it. Second, a shift in what P4 claims. Aquinas's point in ST I-II q.2 a.8 is not "I feel a longing, therefore its object exists." It is structural: the will's object is good as such, so every finite good registers as not enough. Treadmill dissatisfaction is a craving for more of the same. What the lonely or grieving person aches for is a someone: to be known and kept. The question then becomes which account explains a longing with that content. Counter. The naturalist trusts reason where its predictions are checked. The longing for infinite love makes no checkable prediction. Did it hold? Failed as a deductive premise. Held with repair as a clue in a best-explanation argument. No reply forces the naturalist to grant P4 as a law. P4 can survive only as one weight in a comparison of explanations, the way line 107 compares stopping points.

    Exchange 3: Citing God to prove God

    Objection (naturalist, who has read Catholic theology). Your premise 1 is credited to CCC 27, which states that man is "created by God and for God". ST I-II q.2 a.8 and CCC 1718 are about where happiness lies, given God. All three assume the conclusion. A second point: if a natural desire must be fulfilled, then God owes you heaven, and your own Church denies that (Pius XII, Humani Generis 26). Reply. Both points are conceded. The Church's texts name what reason finds; they are not premises for someone outside the faith. They should follow the conclusion ("the Church has always said this"). The gratuity point is serious. The argument may conclude only that the Good is real, not that our nature is owed Him or can reach Him on its own. Beatitude is the "entirely free gift of God" (CCC 1722). Did it hold? Held with repair. Move the citations after the conclusion. State the conclusion as "the Good exists", not "we are owed it". This exchange also supplied a useful clarification: hunger shows that food exists, not that every hungry person eats. The argument leaves room for freedom.

    Exchange 4: The longing is the disease (Buddhist, on premise 1)

    Objection (Buddhist). You have described craving perfectly and called it a signpost. A desire to be held wholly and forever is craving for existence in its purest form. Your premise 3 is our First Noble Truth, and we agree on the diagnosis. The cure is to let the craving go, not to posit an infinite object for it. Reply. The Church also respects this diagnosis: Buddhism recognizes the "radical insufficiency" of this changing world (Nostra Aetate 2). Catholic teaching likewise separates disordered craving (concupiscence, CCC 2515) from the natural desire for the good, and it calls for detachment from creatures. But the Buddhist path keeps one desire: the wholesome aspiration to be free. The Pali texts themselves speak of abandoning desire by means of desire (SN 51.15), and of nirvana as the highest happiness (Dhammapada 204). So even the Buddhist does not end all longing. She keeps the longing for the Unconditioned. Counter. That aspiration is a raft. It falls away when the shore is reached, and the Unconditioned is not a someone. Did it hold? Held in part. The exchange narrows the dispute to a real fork. Both sides agree that the conditioned cannot satisfy. They disagree on whether the Unconditioned can love. This is the honest place to stand, and it is not polemical.

    Exchange 5: Wanting to be loved back is attachment (Buddhist, on Patrick's insight)

    Objection (Buddhist). Patrick says the most perfect love is "given back whole in turn." That is love with strings attached. Mettā and compassion flow out and ask nothing. Your own Gospel says to love enemies who will never love you back. Reply. Love gives without conditions, but its fulfilment is communion. Aquinas calls charity a friendship with God, and friendship requires love on both sides (ST II-II q.23 a.1). Wanting the beloved to love you back is not grasping. It is wanting the beloved's own good, because to love is that person's deepest good. A love that never hoped for return would not want the beloved fully alive. The highest form is the Trinity: the Father gives Himself wholly, and the Son gives Himself wholly back, with total self-gift and no loss. The order matters, though. With us, God gives first (1 John 4:19). He does not "return" our love; our love is the answer to His. Did it hold? Held with repair. The insight survives, and it is the best thing in the option, but it needs wording that puts grace first.

    Exchange 6: No one is there to be loved forever (Buddhist, on no-self)

    Objection (Buddhist). Premise 1 needs a lasting self, and there isn't one. "You" are a changing stream of form, feeling, perception, mental formations and consciousness. The "whole you" you want received is a construct. Reply. Augustine agrees that we are a mystery to ourselves (mihi quaestio factus sum, Conf. IV.4; X.33). That is exactly why only the Author can receive the whole of us. Every vow and covenant (Ch. 4) also assumes someone who persists to keep it. Counter. Persons are real only conventionally, and a conventional truth is enough for vows. Did it hold? Failed against this opponent on her own ground. The argument assumes that persons are real. Settling that question takes a separate metaphysical case, which the book makes elsewhere (Ch. 3 on dignity; Dignitas Infinita 15). For readers who grant that persons are real, which is nearly all of Patrick's readers, it holds.

    Exchange 7: A good marriage does return love wholly (naturalist, on premise 3)

    Objection (naturalist). Your Church calls marriage a total gift of self, and your Chapter 10 praises it. Happily married people report deep fulfilment. Either premise 3 is false or your theology of marriage is. The "infinite" longing is a theologian's inflation. Reply. A spouse can truly give everything they have. What no spouse can do is know and receive everything we are, or hold us past death. The Church's own marriage vow is for "all the days of my life," and the bond ends at death (Rom 7:2; Mt 22:30). The evidence that love wants more than that is grief. Augustine's account of the friend he lost (Conf. IV) shows a man who loved a mortal as though he would never die. The Catholic philosopher Gabriel Marcel held (paraphrased) that to love someone is somehow to refuse their death. We do not grieve because love failed. We grieve because love was meant to last. Did it hold? Held with repair. P3 must say "no finite person" (which also covers angels and any other created minds) and spell out "wholly". It must also honour human love as a real communion and an image of God's love, never treat it as a failure.

    Exchange 8: Projection (naturalist)

    Objection (naturalist). Attachment research explains the longing. The infant needs a caregiver who seems all-powerful, and the adult projects that need onto the sky. This is wish-fulfilment. Reply. Where a belief comes from does not settle whether it is true, and the parity argument of line 43 applies again. The God this longing meets also judges, commands the Cross and asks for sacrifice (Ch. 9), which is not what a wish would invent. The longing also persists in adults who had good parents, and they still grieve. Did it hold? Held weakly. The reply neutralizes the objection but gains no ground.

    Exchange 9: The argument makes hiddenness worse (naturalist)

    Objection (naturalist, using the divine hiddenness argument). The more you insist that a perfect Lover made us for communion, the worse His silence looks. A perfectly loving person would make Himself available to every sincere seeker, yet many seek and hear nothing. Reply. Love woos; it does not overpower. A gift forced is no gift (Ch. 4). God "never ceases to draw" (CCC 27); the Church does not say He compels. Communion can be real before it is recognized: line 95 speaks of following a light without yet knowing its name, and CCC 847 of grace at work in those who do not know Him. St. Teresa of Calcutta lived for decades in felt darkness while in deep union. Feeling God's absence is not the same as His being absent. Did it hold? Held with repair, at a cost. The chapter must name this objection if it adopts Option 3. Otherwise the argument reads as naive. The reply must not suggest that every non-believer is resisting God (CCC 847).

    Exchange 10: The absurdist fallback (naturalist)

    Objection (naturalist). Grant everything. The longing could still simply be tragic. We are the creature whose hunger outruns its world. Honesty means living with that, not inventing bread. Reply. Apply line 107: every account stops somewhere. The absurdist stops at a nature that gave one animal, alone, a hunger the world has no food for, and calls the deepest thing in us a mistake. The absurdist also goes on living as though love keeps its promises: keeping vows, grieving, refusing to betray. The communion strand does not stand alone. The same love that longs also binds (the obligation strand). The hunger and the law point to one Person, and that convergence is what the chapter's title says: the Lawgiver is Love. Did it hold? Held as a best explanation inside a converging case. It does not hold alone.

    Faults found (ranked by severity)

    1. Premise 4 as a universal correspondence law (critical). Natural selection explains the hunger-and-food pairing and predicts no such pairing for an infinite object. The teleological version assumes a designer. Fixable only by changing its role: turn it into a weighted clue in an explanatory comparison and ground it in Aquinas's account of the will's object (good as such), not in the hunger analogy alone.
    2. Circular citations (serious, easy fix). P1 is credited to CCC 27, and ST I-II q.2 a.8 and CCC 1718 support the conclusion. All three assume God. Move them after the conclusion as the Church naming what reason found. Note also that CCC 27 does not say "love and be loved wholly". CCC 1604 (love as the "fundamental and innate vocation" of every human being) and GS 19 (dignity rooted in the call to communion with God) fit P1 better.
    3. Logical gaps (serious, fixable). "Corresponds to a real good" does not yet mean "the object exists". "No human" leaves room for other finite persons. "The One who is Love" goes beyond what reason reaches, which is an unlimited personal Good. Fixes: write "no finite person"; conclude "a Person without limit"; then give the name from Revelation (1 John 4:8).
    4. "Wholly" collides with the theology of marriage (serious, fixable). Spouses do give themselves totally (Ch. 10). Split "wholly" into knows the whole of us / can receive the whole of us / forever.
    5. Gratuity of grace (doctrinally serious, fixable). If natural longing guarantees fulfilment, beatitude becomes owed (Humani Generis 26). Conclude only that the Good is real. Fulfilment is free gift (CCC 1722) and depends on our free yes.
    6. Patrick's insight puts grace second (moderate, fixable). "Who could return our love" makes God the responder. Reword so that God gives first and receives our gift whole.
    7. Exposure to the hiddenness objection (moderate, fixable). Option 3 invites this objection more than Options 1 and 2 do. It needs one paragraph that names it (see Exchange 9).
    8. Assumes that persons are real (moderate, not fixable inside this argument). Against a convinced Buddhist the argument does not reach the fork without a separate case. It is mitigated by Ch. 3 and by the shared-diagnosis approach (Nostra Aetate 2).
    9. Projection (minor). Neutralized, not answered.
    10. The hunger-and-food analogy is widely associated with a Protestant apologist (minor, fixable). Root it in Augustine (Conf. I.1) and Aquinas, cite Kreeft (Catholic) if a modern source is wanted, and keep the wording Patrick's own.
    11. Existing line 111 overclaims (minor). It moves from the body's reactions to "evidence that we were designed for love". If Option 3 goes in, line 111 should be absorbed into it and toned down to "a clue".

    New insights

    • The title needs both strands. "The Lawgiver Is Love" makes two claims. The obligation strand shows a Lawgiver; the communion strand shows that the Lawgiver is Love. Option 3 is the missing second half, not a replacement for the moral argument.
    • The real lack is in receiving, not just giving. No human can receive the whole of us, because no one knows the whole of us; we are a question even to ourselves (Augustine). Only the Author knows the work, so only He can receive the gift whole and give us back to ourselves (GS 24; Mt 16:25). This sharpens Patrick's insight from "return" to "receive and give back whole".
    • Love itself refuses death. The evidence for P1 and P3 is not only desire but the shape of love: grief, Augustine's Book IV, Marcel, and the Church's own vow, "all the days of my life." This evidence comes from inside love, which is the book's own ground.
    • Wanting to be loved back is not selfish. It is wanting the beloved's good, because to love is the beloved's highest good (ST II-II q.23 a.1). This answers both the Buddhist and the "pure, disinterested love" objection.
    • Hunger shows that food exists, not that everyone eats. The argument leaves freedom intact (Ch. 4). The Good is real, and receiving Him is our yes.
    • The chapter already holds this strand in pieces. Line 95 (the pull toward the Good, which is really the argument of ST I-II q.2 a.8), line 111 (the ache), line 119 ("can never love anyone back; communion happens only between persons"), line 179 (hunger and thirst), and line 191 (CCC 1718). Option 3 gathers what is already there; little new text is needed.
    • A respectful way to address the Buddhist named at line 121. Agree on the diagnosis (Nostra Aetate 2), then name the fork: can the Unconditioned love?

    Risks to the book's rules

    • Overclaiming. The brief's form ends "So there is a Person", which reads as a proof. It must be framed as a best explanation that predisposes the reader to faith (CCC 35) and sits within a converging case (CCC 31).
    • Protestant authority. This argument is best known in a Protestant apologist's version. Do not name him, echo his wording, or rely on his formulation. Source it to Augustine, Aquinas, CCC 1718, Benedict XVI (Spe Salvi 11–12, on not knowing exactly what we long for), and Kreeft.
    • Gratuity of grace. "Natural desire cannot be in vain", pressed too hard, makes heaven owed (Humani Generis 26; CCC 1722).
    • Grace first. God loves first (1 John 4:19). He never merely "returns" our love.
    • Polemic. Describe Buddhism accurately and with respect (Nostra Aetate 2). Do not caricature it as a "religion of craving".
    • Marriage. P3 must not belittle human love (Ch. 10). Human love is real communion and an icon, not a failure.
    • Culpability. The answer to hiddenness must not imply that every unbeliever resists God (CCC 847; LG 16).
    • Simplicity and sacrifice. When invoking the Trinity as love "given and returned whole", speak of total self-gift without loss, not of sacrifice in the divine essence. The Persons are not parts.

    Option 4 — The Converging Case

    Opponent

    Two voices working together.

    1. The lead voice is a Bayesian naturalist in the Humean line, the strongest professional critic of cumulative arguments for God. He accepts that inference to the best explanation can be rational. He then insists on three things: priors must be stated honestly, evidence counts only where it is more probable on one hypothesis than on its rivals, and evidence that is not independent cannot be multiplied. He brings in two rival schools when he needs them:
    2. non-naturalist moral realism: moral facts are objective, necessary and irreducible, and no God is needed (the school of Parfit, Enoch, Wielenberg and Huemer);
    3. second-personal ethics: obligations are claims that persons, as equal members of a moral community, lay on one another (the school of Darwall).

    These are the two positions that do the most damage to the "obligation" and "personal" marks.

    1. The second voice presses the problem of evil in its evidential form (Rowe's school), together with divine hiddenness (Schellenberg's school).

    Option 4 earns this pairing in a way the other options do not. It openly invites a comparison of explanations, and its conclusion is the most loaded hypothesis available: not just "a God" but "a God who is Love." A Bayesian will say that the more you build into a hypothesis, the more all the evidence counts against it, and evil is evidence.

    All positions below are paraphrased by school. No direct quotations are attributed to real thinkers.

    The argument as tested (final, best-defended form)

    The five features of love, each argued from ordinary experience (Chs. 3–10; the examples at lines 61–69). Use "features," not "marks" (see Risks).

    • (a) Not ours to invent. Betrayal and cruelty are wrong whether or not anyone approves. Taken alone, this rules out only the views that reduce morality to something else. It does not tell God apart from brute moral fact.
    • (b) Binding. Breaking love's demands leaves us guilty, not merely mistaken.
    • (c) Addressed. (Repaired. It was "personal in content.") Every demand is something one person owes another. It comes to each of us in the second person, as a "you," and asks for an answer, not just for conformity.
    • (d) One. (Repaired. It was "the facets never contradict.") There is never a situation where love requires us to sin (Veritatis Splendor 52; CCC 1789). Followed to its depth, any one demand turns out to require the others: truth asks for mercy, and justice asks for sacrifice.
    • (e) Made for communion. Love is complete only when it is given and given back. Every finite return falls short of what love asks.

    The independent witnesses, counted honestly. There are three, not five:

    1. The obligation witness (features b and c). A claim that binds every person needs a claimant whose standing is above every person. This points to the Lawgiver.
    2. The coherence witness (feature d). The unity of love is best explained by one Good, not by many separate facts.
    3. The communion witness (feature e). Love's shape is reciprocal, and our hunger is for a love no creature can return wholly. This points to One who is Love and who loves first (1 John 4:19).

    The added discriminator (new, surfaced in debate): how we know. Two of the views make our moral knowledge hard to explain:

    • Brute moral facts are causally idle, and evolved minds would know them only by coincidence.
    • A God who is Love is the source of both the moral law and the minds that read it.

    The honest comparison of four stopping points (the fourth was added in debate):

    1. Love as a by-product of matter. It explains why we feel moral pressure. It cannot explain features (a) or (b).
    2. Love as only what our kind of animal needs to flourish (Aristotelian naturalism, the rival closest to natural law). It explains the content of love's demands. It does not explain their authority, why there is a nature at all, or why that nature is fitted to communion.
    3. Love as brute moral fact. It affirms (a) and (b). It leaves (c), (d), (e) and our knowledge of the facts as separate, unexplained givens.
    4. A God who is Love. It explains all five features, and our knowledge of them, from one source. The price is that the problem of evil must still be faced. Ch. 1 and CCC 309 carry that weight, and this chapter says so.

    The conclusion, stated at its proper strength. This is reason's witness (CCC 35), a set of "converging and convincing arguments" (CCC 31). Converging probabilities can ground real certitude, as St. John Henry Newman argued in Grammar of Assent ch. 8, so the conclusion is not "merely probable." No single step is a demonstration that forces assent.

    Debate highlights

    1. "Inference to the best explanation is cheap. 'God' explains anything, so it explains nothing."

    • Objection. The Bayesian argues that a hypothesis which can be fitted to any data predicts no data. Unless theism risks something, "explains more" is only a slogan.
    • Best reply. "A God who is Love" is not shapeless. It predicts specific things:
    • morality will address persons, not just describe them;
    • its demands will cohere rather than conflict at the root;
    • it will be knowable by the minds it binds;
    • it will be ordered to communion;
    • since love presupposes freedom (Ch. 4), evil will be possible.

    An impersonal moral order predicts none of these in particular. The claim is also deliberately modest (CCC 35), with no "proof" and no "therefore God must exist."

    • Did it hold? Held with repair. The chapter's current conclusion (line 81, "then God must exist") and line 133 ("Theism is simpler… a law requires a lawgiver") talk like a deductive proof. Option 4 has to drop that register entirely, or the Bayesian simply points out that a cumulative case cannot deliver a "must."

    2. "Your first feature does not tell God apart from my realist friend."

    • Objection. Objectivity is exactly what the non-naturalist realist affirms. Its likelihood is the same on theism and on realism, so feature (a) has no weight in the comparison that matters.
    • Best reply. Conceded. Feature (a) is the entry ticket. It rules out the views that reduce morality to something else (line 43) and sets the realist and the theist on the same side against them. The comparison with the realist is decided by (c), (d), (e) and the knowledge problem, not by (a).
    • Did it hold? Held with repair. The chapter currently treats objectivity as if it pointed straight to God (Premise 1, lines 33–45). Option 4 fixes that by giving (a) its true, smaller job.

    3. "Your features are theism smuggled in. 'Gift,' 'for communion,' 'calls us by name.'"

    • Objection. Describe morality as a "gift" and you have already posited a giver. Say love is "for" communion and you have assumed a purpose. Say the law "calls each of us by name" (line 107) and you have described a speaker. Restate the data in neutral words and the argument evaporates.
    • On "personal in content": a law about persons is not thereby a law from a person. The laws of geometry are about triangles, and no triangle wrote them.
    • Best reply. That last point is a fair hit, and the chapter's sentence at line 77 ("a gift can only come from a Giver… An impersonal ground… cannot explain a law whose entire content is love") commits exactly this fallacy. The repair is to move from content to form:
    • the moral demand is in the second person ("you shall not");
    • it waits for a free answer;
    • it can be refused, and refusing it is a betrayal and not an error.

    Geometry never addresses anyone. "By name" overstates the case, but "addresses each of us" is a neutral description of moral experience that a realist will recognize.

    • Did it hold? Failed as currently worded. It held with repair. Feature (c) must be stated as "addressed," with "gift" and "by name" saved for after the conclusion.

    4. "Obligation is second-personal, and that is my point, not yours." (The obligation witness.)

    • Objection. The second-personal school agrees that an obligation is a claim one person lays on another (Option 2's premise 2). The persons are us: every human being, with equal standing to demand respect from every other. No God is needed. The "Someone" behind the ought (line 105) is everyone.
    • Best reply.
    • The claims of the moral community cannot reach the case where the whole community is wrong. The persecuted minority, the unborn and the dead lie outside the circle that does the demanding.
    • More deeply, the second-personal account presupposes a standing it does not create. Each person's authority to make claims rests on a dignity that is "prior to any recognition" (Dignitas Infinita 15, already at line 99). No member of the community conferred it, so the community cannot be its source.
    • Elizabeth Anscombe, a Catholic philosopher, argued in "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958) that the specifically legal sense of "moral obligation" is a survival from a law-conception of ethics that presupposed a divine lawgiver. Without one, the notion loses its footing. (Paraphrase her; do not quote.)
    • Did it hold? Held with repair. The realist can answer that equal dignity is itself a brute value, which sends the argument back to comparing stopping points (exchange 5). The obligation witness is strong against the reductive naturalist and only moderate against the realist. It should be presented that way.

    5. "Parity: my moral facts are necessary and need no explanation. So is your God." (The fairness test.)

    • Objection. Necessary truths do not need causes, any more than 2+2=4 does. The theist also stops at an unexplained necessary being, so line 107's "hanging in mid-air" is rhetoric. Worse, line 103 calls my view "incoherent." It is not. It is one of the most developed positions in contemporary ethics.
    • Best reply.
    • Concede the unfairness. Line 103 is wrong and contradicts line 107. The realist's view is coherent. The claim should be that it leaves more unexplained.
    • Then press the real asymmetry, which is knowledge. On the realist view, moral facts are causally idle. Our moral beliefs were shaped by evolution, which selects for survival, not for truth about value. Either the facts and our beliefs line up by coincidence, or the realist needs a further story about why survival and goodness overlap. That is the "Darwinian dilemma" that evolutionary debunkers (Street's school) press against realists. The realist's "third-factor" replies help, but they still leave the overlap unexplained.
    • On theism there is no coincidence. The same God is the source of the moral law and the maker of the minds that read it. Natural law is the rational creature's "participation" in the eternal law (ST I-II q.91 a.2, already at line 85).
    • Economy. The theist's stopping point is one Being in whom being and good are the same (ST I q.5 a.1) and who is utterly simple (ST I q.3 a.7). The realist's stopping point is a plurality: many moral facts, the links that tie them to natural facts, and the coincidence.
    • Did it hold? Held. This is the strongest thing Option 4 has. Note that line 43 already makes the evolutionary-mirror argument, but aims it at the naturalist's reasoning. Aimed at the realist's moral knowledge, it does far more work. The realist's last move is to call the theist's "simple" God a brute stopping point too. That is true, and it is why the honest conclusion is "explains more, from fewer givens," not "explains everything."

    6. "Unity? Moral dilemmas are real." (The coherence witness.)

    • Objection. Value pluralists (Berlin's school) hold that real goods sometimes conflict without remainder: tragic choices, lying to the murderer at the door. Chapter 11 exists because hard cases exist. Neo-Aristotelian virtue ethicists also teach the unity of the virtues without God, so unity does not tell the views apart. And line 125 ("integration doesn't happen by accident") is circular: you defined love as integrated and are now impressed that it is.
    • Best reply.
    • The Catholic claim is precise. One may never do evil that good may come (CCC 1789). The negative precepts bind always and in every circumstance (Veritatis Splendor 52). So there is always a path that violates no facet, even when it costs everything, and the martyrs walked it.
    • This is a claim about experience, not about definition. Chs. 6–9 showed one facet at a time that truth torn from mercy stops being fully truthful, and justice torn from mercy becomes cruelty.
    • On the Aristotelian point, unity of the virtues within a nature is explained by the nature. It does not explain why the nature's goods form one ordered whole aimed at communion.
    • Did it hold? Held with repair, but with low evidential weight. The circularity charge sticks against line 125's current wording. Feature (d) must be argued from the experience of Chs. 6–9, never from the definition. Even so, against a skilled pluralist it is a modest consideration.

    7. "Longing proves nothing." (The communion witness.)

    • Objection. Hunger shows that food exists only because evolution built the hunger from food. A longing for perfect, permanent love is an extrapolation by a mind that can imagine "more" of anything. Many longings have no object, such as the wish never to die. The Buddhist adds that craving is the root of suffering, not a signpost. And the Thomist principle that a natural desire cannot be in vain presupposes design, so in an argument for a designer it begs the question.
    • Best reply. The communion witness should not be run as "longing proves its object." It runs on the shape of the moral law itself.
    • Love's demands are fulfilled in reciprocity.
    • An impersonal moral fact can be obeyed but never loved back (line 119 already says this well).
    • Patrick's own insight sharpens it: the most perfect love is one given and given back whole, and who could return it more perfectly than its Author? With God the order runs the other way, because He loves first (1 John 4:19). So the hypothesis predicts what we find: a love that reaches past every finite return (CCC 27; CCC 1718).

    In Bayesian terms, this needs only that the evidence be more expected on theism than on its rivals. It does not need the evidence to be impossible without God.

    • Did it hold? Held with repair, as confirmation only. Do not cite the "not in vain" principle as a premise in this chapter. On the Buddhist point, the book's distinction matters: disordered craving is choosing a lesser good (Ch. 1). The longing for the greatest Good is a different thing, but that is not something this argument can prove.

    8. "Ten leaky buckets hold no water. And your buckets are one bucket counted five times."

    • Objection. A cumulative case works only if its pieces are independent. Your five features are one datum, moral experience, described five ways. Doubt objectivity and all five collapse together. Correlated evidence does not multiply.
    • Best reply. Partly conceded. Features (a) through (c) are one cluster. But the coherence of the facets (d) and the reciprocal shape and hunger of love (e) are distinct observations. A thoroughgoing realist could accept (a)–(c) and still deny (d) and (e). So there are three witnesses, not five.

    The strength of the case lies in convergence on one specific profile. Three witnesses who never compared notes describe the same face: a personal Good, one and undivided, who wants communion. This is what CCC 31 means by "converging," and what Newman meant by certitude reached through converging probabilities.

    • Did it hold? Held with repair. The chapter must not say "five arguments." It should present five features and three witnesses. Counted honestly, the case is cumulative. Counted dishonestly, it is stacking.

    9. "Total evidence: you cannot weigh love and leave out evil." (The second voice.)

    • Objection. Bayesian rules require all the evidence. "A God who is Love" makes gratuitous suffering, such as animal pain before any human sin or a child's slow death, much less expected than a thinner hypothesis would. Hiddenness adds that a God who wants communion with everyone should not leave sincere seekers without awareness of Him. You cannot run a comparison of explanations on the moral data while keeping evil off the scale.
    • Best reply.
    • The usual reply, that the outrage against evil itself presupposes the moral law, works against the relativist. It fails against the evidential presser, who runs his argument on the theist's own values as a reductio and needs no realism of his own.
    • The honest reply is twofold:
    • On evil: the Church does not claim a quick answer. Only the Christian faith as a whole answers it (CCC 309), and Ch. 1 carries that weight in this book. Love presupposes freedom (Ch. 4), which covers moral evil but not all natural evil.
    • On hiddenness: the book's own material helps. Not knowing God's name is not the same as being outside His grace (CCC 847; LG 16; line 95, "following a light without yet knowing its name").
    • Did it hold? Failed as a refutation, and it must not pretend otherwise. It held only as candor. The comparison needs one sentence acknowledging that evil sits on the other side of the scale, with a pointer to Ch. 1. Without it, the comparison looks rigged to exactly the reader Option 4 is trying to win.

    10. "You left out the rival closest to your own tradition."

    • Objection. Your three stopping points are matter, brute fact and God. Aristotelian naturalism (Foot's school) grounds moral norms in what a human being needs to flourish as the kind of creature it is. That is objective and not reductive. It needs no God and resembles your natural law. Your own tradition says the moral law follows from human nature, so why go beyond the nature?
    • Best reply. Natural law is the nature's participation in the eternal law (line 85).
    • A nature explains what is good for us. It does not explain why that good obliges rather than just describes health, or why breaking it leaves us guilty and not merely unwell.
    • Nor does it explain why such a nature exists, or why it is aimed at communion with another rather than at the thriving of the species.
    • Did it hold? Held with repair. The comparison must include this fourth stopping point, phrased plainly: "love is just what's good for our kind of animal." Leaving it out is the most serious fairness failure in the recommended structure.

    11. "This is a seminar, not a chapter." (The lay-reader test.)

    • Objection. Five features, two or three witnesses, a knowledge problem and four stopping points. The lay reader is lost by the third paragraph, and the chapter is already long.
    • Best reply. Option 4 works only if it replaces lines 29–81, the classical syllogism and its premises, and condenses lines 101–107, rather than being added to them.
    • The features need one plain sentence each, plus the concrete examples already at lines 61–69 (the hidden affair, the murdered child, the firefighter).
    • The comparison fits in four short paragraphs. The book's diagnostic-table convention could also hold it, with honest entries such as "affirms, but leaves unexplained" rather than ticks and crosses.
    • Did it hold? Held with repair. Estimated at 700–900 words for the whole section, against roughly 1,100 for what it replaces. Added on top, it fails.
