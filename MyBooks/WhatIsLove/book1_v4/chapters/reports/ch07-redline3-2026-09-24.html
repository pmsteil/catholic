Gratitude: the debt we can never repay

line 117

… This is why justice toward God can never be settled. St. Thomas observes that "whatever man renders to God is due, yet it cannot be equal, as though man rendered to God as much as he owes Him" (ST II-II, q.80, a.1) The Church says it plainly: "Between God and us there is an immeasurable inequality, for we have received everything from him, our Creator" (CCC 2007). The psalmist asked the question every grateful heart comes to:¶ "What shall I render to the LORD for all his bounty to me? I will lift up the cup of salvation and call on the name of the LORD, I will pay my vows to the LORD in the presence of all his people."¶— Psalm 116:12-14 ¶St. Bernard of Clairvaux saw that the Cross doubled the debt: "In the first creation He gave me myself; but in His new creation He gave me Himself, and by that gift restored to me the self that I had lost. Created first and then restored, I owe Him myself twice over in return for myself. But what have I to offer Him for the gift of Himself? Could I multiply myself a thousand-fold and then give Him all, what would that be in comparison with God?"1 This is a debt of gratitude that can never be repaid, and God never asks us to repay it. He does not hold it over us, as the parent does who says, "After everything I've done for you." He asks only that we answer it, freely, with our whole lives. Here is the deepest ought in this book: we give Him truth, justice, mercy and sacrifice not to settle an account, but because we have been given everything, twice over. What can never be repaid in equal measure can only be answered, and gratitude answers it by becoming worship. …

We cannot understand how we are "endowed by our Creator with certain unalienable rights" unless we first understand that rights flow from duties. Consider the rights most often named in this tradition — life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness.1 2 Each is a debt of justice owed to every person: life, because every person is willed into being by Love itself; liberty, because love requires freedom (a coerced gift is no gift); and the pursuit of happiness, because every person is made for the good and cannot be prevented from seeking it. …
