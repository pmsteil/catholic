## Chapter 14 · Sin and Vice: The Failure to Love

### Chapter assessment

Chapter 14 contains important Catholic teaching: sin is not merely arbitrary rule-breaking; mortal and venial sin differ; all three conditions are required for mortal sin; circumstances and diminished freedom matter; capital vices generate other sins; temptation is not itself sin; confession restores sanctifying grace; restitution belongs to conversion; imperfect and perfect contrition are distinct; sacramental mercy opens a real road home.

The chapter’s matrix nonetheless repeats Chapter 13’s circular method and then raises the stakes by implying that the number or type of violated facets helps determine gravity. Catholic morality determines the act from moral object, intention and circumstances, then distinguishes grave matter and personal culpability through full knowledge and deliberate consent. Not every sin includes believing or telling a lie; pride and disobedience are root dynamics, and a person can knowingly choose recognized evil. Unbelief, infidelity, despair, presumption, anger, violence, disobedience and mercy are conflated or misdefined. Private revenge is forbidden, but legitimate public authority may punish and proportionate force can be just. If voluntariness is absent there may be no personal sin, not automatically venial sin; deliberately cultivated habits can increase responsibility. Most urgently, the chapter labels an immediately rejected impure thought and glance venial sins: they are temptations without consent and therefore not sins. Mortal sin destroys charity and sanctifying grace but not baptismal character or all incorporation into the Church. The examination of conscience should begin with God’s grace and objective commandments, not pain, while scrupulosity needs compassionate pastoral and sometimes clinical help rather than being labeled mistrust.

### CH14-01 · Withdraw Chapter 13’s universal-virtue premise before applying it to vice

**Severity:** Stop-ship / foundation and circularity · **Control:** DC-01, DC-10 · **Location:** `chapter_14.md:23–29`

**Issue.** Chapter 13 has not established that every virtue flows from all four pillars, so Chapter 14 cannot infer that every vice denies the same four. Sin is a failure in love, but the Church’s definition and classification are broader than the private matrix.

**Controlling sources:** CCC 1849–1876.

**Source passage with proposed inline redline:**

In the previous chapter, ~~every virtue upholds and flows from the four pillars ... every vice denies or distorts these same four pillars~~ ++we used four terms as a tentative devotional lens on virtue. Here the same terms may help expose aspects of vice, but they do not define every sin or replace the Church’s moral categories++. Sin is an offense against reason, truth and right conscience and a failure in genuine love for God and neighbor because of perverse attachment to created goods.

**Editorial consequence.** The chapter begins from Catholic doctrine rather than a conclusion the preceding matrix manufactured.

### CH14-02 · Make the vice matrix an optional heuristic, not a classification of sin

**Severity:** Stop-ship / moral method · **Control:** DC-01, DC-10, DC-26 · **Location:** `chapter_14.md:33–85`

**Issue.** Stars and crosses are not a Catholic classification and have no established relation to moral object, gravity or culpability. Brief author-written “defining offenses” are selected to make the boxes fit, then the fit is called explanatory.

**Source passage with proposed inline redline:**

The following matrix ~~shows which pillars each vice offends ... each row states the vice’s defining offense ... a primary target~~ ++offers provisional associations for reflection. It is neither an examination of conscience nor a doctrinal taxonomy, its descriptions are incomplete, and the marks cannot determine the moral object or gravity of any act++. Because vice is a stable bad disposition and sin is an act, individual acts must still be judged by object, intention, circumstances, grave matter, knowledge and consent.

**Editorial consequence.** The matrix can be retained only as a subordinate illustration.

### CH14-03 · Do not say every sin necessarily believes or speaks a lie

**Severity:** Stop-ship / definition of sin · **Control:** DC-10 · **Location:** `chapter_14.md:77–85`

**Issue.** A sinner can know an act is evil and still choose it from weakness or malice. Every voluntary act seeks some apprehended good, and self-deception often accompanies sin, but neither proves false belief is necessary. Scripture and Tradition commonly identify pride and disobedience—not lying alone—as root dynamics.

**Source callout with proposed inline redline:**

~~Every vice violates truth ... every sin necessarily involves believing or speaking a lie ... lying is the foundational sin.~~ ++Every sin contradicts the truth of the good in an objective sense, and many sins involve falsehood or self-deception. Yet a person may knowingly choose a recognized evil through weakness or malice. The tradition names pride and disobedience among sin’s deepest roots; John 8:44 condemns Satan’s lying but does not define every human sin as an act of false belief or speech.++

**Editorial consequence.** Objective contradiction of truth is distinguished from the sinner’s subjective belief.

### CH14-04 · Separate unbelief from infidelity and preserve diminished culpability

**Severity:** Major / faith and culpability · **Control:** DC-09, DC-10, DC-22 · **Location:** `chapter_14.md:45–48`

**Issue.** Infidelity in the theological sense is culpable refusal of faith; simple nonbelief can involve ignorance, lack of proclamation or diminished responsibility. The matrix’s “unbelief/infidelity” row makes all absence of assent one vice and assigns multiple violations without examining knowledge or freedom.

**Source table row with proposed inline redline:**

| ~~Unbelief/Infidelity | Refusing assent to what God has revealed~~ ++Infidelity | Culpable refusal or neglect of revealed faith; distinguish this from involuntary ignorance, doubt, difficulty and the situation of one who has never received adequate proclamation++ |

**Editorial consequence.** Nonbelief is not automatically classified as a personally culpable vice.

### CH14-05 · Separate despair and presumption

**Severity:** Stop-ship / hope · **Control:** DC-09, DC-10 · **Location:** `chapter_14.md:47, 83`

**Issue.** Despair and presumption are opposite sins against hope, not one combined vice with identical targets. Mercy is not “owed” to the self in strict justice. Depression, trauma and intrusive hopeless feelings are not automatically the sin of despair without deliberate consent.

**Controlling sources:** CCC 2090–2092, 1735.

**Source table row with proposed inline redline:**

| ~~Despair/Presumption | Refusing the mercy God extends ... mercy it is owed ... or presuming ...~~ ++Despair | Deliberately ceasing to hope for salvation, help or forgiveness from God; distinguish the sin from unwanted feelings, depression and psychological impairment++ |
| ++Presumption | Either trusting in one’s own powers without grace or expecting divine pardon and glory without conversion and merit as God’s gifts++ |

**Editorial consequence.** Two formally distinct sins against hope are taught accurately and pastorally.

### CH14-06 · Do not equate indifference with hatred or “willing nothing at all”

**Severity:** Major / charity · **Control:** DC-10, DC-26 · **Location:** `chapter_14.md:48`

**Issue.** Hatred wills evil; indifference can be negligent failure of concern, emotional limitation or nonculpable lack of awareness. They should not share one definition or automatic four-star severity.

**Source table row with proposed inline redline:**

| ~~Hatred/Indifference | Love itself refused—willing evil to another, or willing nothing at all~~ ++Hatred | Deliberately willing evil to another as evil++ |
| ++Culpable indifference | Failing to attend or respond to another’s true good when charity or justice actually requires it; distinguish this from limited affect and involuntary unawareness++ |

**Editorial consequence.** Acts with different objects and culpability are no longer fused.

### CH14-07 · Correct anger, wrath, vengeance and legitimate punishment

**Severity:** Stop-ship / anger and justice · **Control:** DC-10, DC-13, DC-14 · **Location:** `chapter_14.md:59, 85, 226–230`

**Issue.** Anger can be righteous when governed by reason and ordered to correction. Wrath is disordered anger or desire for revenge. Romans 12 forbids private vengeance; public authority may impose proportionate punishment with retributive redress, defense and correction. It is false that no creature can wield punitive authority without being consumed.

**Controlling sources:** CCC 1765, 1866, 2266; Romans 13:1–4.

**Source passage with proposed inline redline:**

| ~~Wrath/Anger | ... seizing the vengeance owed to God alone~~ ++Wrath | Disordered anger and desire for revenge beyond reason and justice; anger itself can be morally good when it responds proportionately to evil and seeks correction++ |

Wrath can lead to hatred and violence. ~~Vengeance is God's due ... no creature can wield it~~ ++Private vengeance is forbidden. Legitimate public authority may impose proportionate punishment for redress, defense, correction and order; individuals may also use proportionate force in lawful defense. The vice lies in disordered passion or retaliation, not every punitive act++.

**Editorial consequence.** Mercy no longer erases public justice or righteous anger.

### CH14-08 · Distinguish violence from legitimate force

**Severity:** Stop-ship / defense and authority · **Control:** DC-14 · **Location:** `chapter_14.md:70`

**Issue.** “Force against the person” is not inherently vice: parents, police, clinicians and defenders can use proportionate force legitimately. Violence should be defined as unjust force or harm.

**Controlling sources:** CCC 2263–2267, 2307–2317.

**Source table row with proposed inline redline:**

| Violence | ~~Force against the person—seizing by harm what was never ours~~ ++Unjust or disproportionate use of force against persons or communities. Legitimate defense and morally bounded public force are not thereby violence in the culpable sense++ |

**Editorial consequence.** The chapter does not condemn every physical restraint, defense or lawful use of force.

### CH14-09 · Qualify disobedience and illegitimate authority

**Severity:** Stop-ship / authority · **Control:** DC-14 · **Location:** `chapter_14.md:64`

**Issue.** Obedience is owed only within legitimate competence and never to commands contrary to moral order. Refusal of an unjust command may be obligatory.

**Controlling sources:** CCC 1897–1904, 2242–2243.

**Source table row with proposed inline redline:**

| Disobedience | ~~Refusing the submission owed to God's order~~ ++Culpable refusal of a legitimate command issued by rightful authority within its competence. One must not obey commands contrary to God’s law, human dignity or the common good++ |

**Editorial consequence.** The virtue of obedience cannot be used to sanctify abuse of power.

### CH14-10 · Do not infer gravity from the number of offended pillars

**Severity:** Stop-ship / mortal sin · **Control:** DC-10 · **Location:** `chapter_14.md:87–113, 153`

**Issue.** Counting stars or crosses does not determine grave matter. A single intrinsically grave object can be mortal with knowledge and consent, while an act that can be rhetorically described under four headings may be venial.

**Source passage with proposed inline redline:**

~~The degree to which we offend against these pillars helps us understand the nature and seriousness of each vice.~~ ++The matrix may suggest dimensions of harm, but neither the number of marks nor a “primary target” determines seriousness. Gravity comes from the moral object and relevant circumstances; mortal guilt additionally requires full knowledge and deliberate consent.++

A sin grave enough, known and chosen enough severs charity; ~~this alignment confirms the definition~~ ++that doctrine comes from Scripture and Tradition, not from how many categories the author can attach to the act++.

**Editorial consequence.** The manuscript will not misform readers about mortal sin.

### CH14-11 · Remove the four “vice families” as a confessional diagnostic

**Severity:** Major / pastoral method · **Control:** DC-01, DC-11 · **Location:** `chapter_14.md:87–107`

**Issue.** The families repeat entries, derive from disputed stars and then tell readers which “pillar they stopped loving” based on prior confessions. This can feed scrupulosity and misdiagnose complex patterns.

**Source passage with proposed inline redline:**

~~The same stars ... sort the vices into four families ... Bring your last several confessions to it: the column where sins cluster names the pillar you stopped loving.~~ ++These columns are only a thematic index and not a safe diagnostic for past confessions. Patterns of vice should be discerned through the commandments, one’s state of life, objective acts, a stable confessor or spiritual director and—when relevant—competent clinical assessment. Repeated entries show overlap, not four natural families.++

**Editorial consequence.** A speculative chart is removed from sacramental self-diagnosis.

### CH14-12 · Avoid overgeneralizing other Christians’ teaching on “all sin is equal”

**Severity:** Major / ecumenism · **Control:** DC-22 · **Location:** `chapter_14.md:115–121`

**Issue.** Some Christians use that slogan, but many Protestant and Orthodox traditions distinguish grave effects, discipline and degrees of punishment even when they categorize mortal/venial sin differently. State the Catholic distinction positively rather than constructing a generic opponent.

**Source passage with proposed inline redline:**

~~Some Christians claim “all sin is equal” and “we're always equally guilty.”~~ ++The phrase “all sin is equal” is sometimes used imprecisely. Christian traditions vary, and many distinguish gravity even without the Catholic terminology. The Catholic Church teaches explicitly that sins differ in object, gravity and effect, while every sinner needs grace++. James condemns selective obedience; Romans teaches universal need for redemption; 1 John distinguishes mortal and nonmortal sin.

**Editorial consequence.** The Catholic case is made without caricaturing separated Christians.

### CH14-13 · Do not make mercy the reason venial sin exists

**Severity:** Major / sin and divine judgment · **Control:** DC-10, DC-13 · **Location:** `chapter_14.md:135–151`

**Issue.** Mortal and venial sin differ because of object, knowledge, consent and effect on charity—not because God mercifully declines to count impatience as murder. Mercy forgives; truth and justice identify what the act is. “No soul need live in confusion” promises certainty about hidden culpability.

**Source passage with proposed inline redline:**

God’s justice judges proportionately. ~~God's Mercy does not weigh every fault the same ... if impatience carried murder’s weight, that would be no mercy~~ ++The distinction itself reflects the truth of different moral objects and degrees of consent. Mercy does not make a lesser act lesser; it offers forgiveness and healing for both venial and mortal sin++. The Church states objective criteria clearly, but ~~no soul need live in confusion about the state of its own heart~~ ++personal culpability can remain partly hidden, so humility and prudent sacramental counsel are necessary++.

**Editorial consequence.** The doctrine is not derived from an inaccurate account of mercy or introspective certainty.

### CH14-14 · Do not label every listed biblical act a mortal sin without the three conditions

**Severity:** Major / Scripture and culpability · **Control:** DC-10 · **Location:** `chapter_14.md:143–149`

**Issue.** Paul identifies acts incompatible with inheriting the Kingdom when embraced without repentance; the objective acts may be grave, but personal mortal guilt still requires knowledge and consent. The verse’s RSV wording should not become an occasion for condemning persons by label.

**Source passage with proposed inline redline:**

These are ~~mortal sins—they kill our relationship~~ ++serious kinds of conduct that Scripture warns exclude from the Kingdom when freely embraced without repentance. Their grave matter must still be distinguished from an individual’s full knowledge, deliberate consent, repentance and circumstances before concluding mortal guilt++. Mortal sin destroys charity in the heart; not every person described by an external label can be judged from the label alone.

**Editorial consequence.** Scripture’s warning and Catholic culpability doctrine remain together.

### CH14-15 · Correct the explanation when a condition for mortal sin is absent

**Severity:** Stop-ship / personal sin and voluntariness · **Control:** DC-10 · **Location:** `chapter_14.md:157–172`

**Issue.** If all voluntariness is absent, there may be no imputable personal sin—not automatically venial sin. “Significantly diminished” needs careful application; lesser matter is venial, while grave matter without full knowledge or complete consent is generally not mortal but the exact culpability cannot be mechanically calculated.

**Controlling sources:** CCC 1734–1736, 1857–1862.

**Source passage with proposed inline redline:**

If any condition is ~~absent or significantly diminished, the sin is venial rather than mortal instead~~ ++not fully present, the act is not imputable as mortal sin. Lesser matter is venial when knowingly and freely chosen. Grave matter without full knowledge or deliberate consent may be venial; if voluntariness is wholly absent, there may be no personal sin at all++. Culpability is not a mathematical slider, and only God judges the heart perfectly.

**Editorial consequence.** The chapter no longer calls an involuntary occurrence a venial sin.

### CH14-16 · Explain habits and addictions as potentially mitigating or aggravating

**Severity:** Major / culpability · **Control:** DC-10, DC-25 · **Location:** `chapter_14.md:164–180`

**Issue.** Addiction, compulsion and habit can diminish freedom, but responsibility can be greater when a person freely cultivated the habit, refuses available help or deliberately places themselves in near occasions. Mental disorders require individualized assessment and should not be casually moralized.

**Source passage with proposed inline redline:**

Freedom can be reduced by grave fear, coercion, strong passion, addiction, compulsion, habit or psychological factors. ~~Addiction or compulsion reduces but does not eliminate; habit makes resistance harder~~ ++Their effect varies and must not be presumed. Antecedent impairment may diminish or remove culpability; a habit deliberately cultivated, freely reinforced or protected from help can increase responsibility. Clinical impairment and moral guilt require competent individualized discernment++.

**Editorial consequence.** Readers receive neither automatic excuse nor automatic blame.

### CH14-17 · Judge aggravating circumstances by Catholic moral objects, not the four columns

**Severity:** Major / moral analysis · **Control:** DC-10 · **Location:** `chapter_14.md:182–188`

**Issue.** Circumstances can increase or diminish goodness or evil but cannot make an intrinsically evil act good. “Against sacrifice: premeditation” is an invented classification. Clerical adultery may include scandal and violation of sacred obligations, but every case should be specified rather than assigned mechanically.

**Controlling sources:** CCC 1750–1756, 2284–2287.

**Source passage with proposed inline redline:**

Aggravating factors deepen guilt through the object, intention and circumstances: harm to the vulnerable, abuse of office, scandal, sacrilege, malice, premeditation or violation of trust. ~~Against truth ... against justice ... against mercy ... against sacrifice~~ ++The four terms may describe effects but do not classify aggravation++. Circumstances can increase or diminish responsibility but never turn an intrinsically evil object into a good one. A cleric’s adultery can add scandal and violation of consecrated obligations; the exact additional offenses should not be assumed without facts.

**Editorial consequence.** The chapter uses the Church’s sources of morality.

### CH14-18 · Qualify “I’m fine,” exaggeration and breach of secrets

**Severity:** Major / lying examples · **Control:** DC-10, DC-28 · **Location:** `chapter_14.md:196–204`

**Issue.** “I’m fine” can be conventional social speech, a refusal to disclose what another has no right to know, or a lie depending on intention and context. “A million times” is often obvious hyperbole rather than assertion. Breaching the sacramental seal is a specific offense by a confessor; malicious outing and disclosure of an ordinary confidence differ.

**Controlling sources:** CCC 2464–2499; Code of Canon Law 983, 1386.

**Source passage with proposed inline redline:**

Saying “I’m fine” ~~is a small social lie ... venial~~ ++may be a conventional way of declining disclosure when the listener has no right to details; it becomes lying if one intends to assert a falsehood. Obvious hyperbole such as “a million times” is not necessarily deception++. Betraying a grave confidence can be serious according to rights and harm. ~~Revealing what was told in confession~~ ++A confessor’s direct violation of the sacramental seal is an especially grave canonical delict; it should be treated separately from a layperson’s breach of ordinary confidence or public disclosure of private facts++.

**Editorial consequence.** The examples teach truthfulness rather than making every nonliteral phrase sinful.

### CH14-19 · State plainly that rejected impure thoughts and glances are not sins

**Severity:** Stop-ship / scrupulosity and consent · **Control:** DC-11 · **Location:** `chapter_14.md:208–216`

**Issue.** This is the chapter’s most pastorally urgent error. A fleeting thought immediately rejected and an involuntary glance promptly turned away lack consent and are temptations, not venial sins. Calling them “minor” or “partial” offenses can severely harm scrupulous readers.

**Controlling sources:** CCC 2846–2849, 1859–1860; James 1:14–15.

**Source passage with proposed inline redline:**

At the venial end is ~~the brief impure thought—a fleeting temptation quickly dismissed, a minor offense never actually consented to, venial for lack of full consent. An immodest glance, quickly turned away, belongs here too: a partial, undeliberate offense~~ ++not an immediately rejected temptation: a fleeting impure thought that is not consented to is **not sin**, and an involuntary glance promptly redirected is **not sin**. Venial lust involves some real but incomplete or non-grave consent—for example, deliberately lingering for a time without the full deliberation required for mortal guilt. Temptation should be resisted peacefully, not confessed as though mere occurrence were culpable++.

**Editorial consequence.** The manuscript stops feeding scrupulosity and teaches the indispensable role of consent.

### CH14-20 · Qualify pornography, adultery and fornication by objective gravity and personal culpability

**Severity:** Major / sexual morality · **Control:** DC-10, DC-25 · **Location:** `chapter_14.md:214–216`

**Issue.** These acts involve grave matter, but the text moves from “objectively grave” to “it is mortal” inconsistently. Habits and addiction can diminish culpability in pornography without making the act good. Adultery and fornication should not be said always to “treat a person as an object” as a claim about subjective intention.

**Source passage with proposed inline redline:**

Deliberate pornography use is objectively grave because it removes sexual acts from intimacy and displays persons for others’ pleasure; habit or other factors may affect culpability. Adultery and fornication are objectively grave matter because they contradict marital fidelity or reserve of sexual union to marriage. ~~It treats a person as an object ... it, too, is mortal~~ ++Objectification and exploitation can be present but should not be assigned as every participant’s conscious motive. Each act is mortal sin only when grave matter, full knowledge and deliberate consent converge++.

**Editorial consequence.** Objective teaching remains firm while internal motives and culpability are not presumed.

### CH14-21 · Clarify what “capital” and “deadly” mean

**Severity:** Major / capital vices · **Control:** DC-10, DC-28 · **Location:** `chapter_14.md:220–238`

**Issue.** Capital vices are “heads” that engender other sins; they are not automatically mortal or guaranteed to progress to spiritual death. Pride has a root role, but the causal catalogues are tendencies, not inevitable outcomes.

**Controlling sources:** CCC 1866.

**Source passage with proposed inline redline:**

The seven capital vices are called “capital” because they engender other sins. ~~“Deadly” ... progressively destroy ... when fully embraced become idolatry ... each leads somewhere specific~~ ++The common title “deadly sins” does not mean every movement or act of a capital vice is mortal. Repeated consent can form habits and generate related sins, but outcomes vary with freedom, grace, circumstance and repentance++. The following catalogues name common fruits, not deterministic chains.

**Editorial consequence.** Readers are not taught that every experience of a capital vice is already spiritual death.

### CH14-22 · Present contrary virtues as remedies without claiming a fixed one-to-one cure

**Severity:** Major / virtue and healing · **Control:** DC-06, DC-28 · **Location:** `chapter_14.md:228–232`

**Issue.** Traditional lists pair vices with contrary virtues, but lists vary and healing involves grace, sacraments, several virtues, concrete restitution and sometimes clinical help. The chapter’s selected “kindness” for envy and “diligence” for sloth should not be called the Church’s settled one-to-one treatment.

**Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:**

The Church has long commended contrary virtues as remedies, though ~~named the virtue that stands opposite each~~ ++traditional lists and emphases vary++. Humility can oppose pride, generosity avarice, chastity lust, patience or meekness wrath, diligence sloth, kindness or gratitude envy, and temperance gluttony. Healing comes first from Christ’s grace and may require Confession, Eucharist, prayer, ascetic practice, restitution, community and competent care—not the fourfold binding or one virtue alone.

**Editorial consequence.** The practical remedies remain useful without claiming a false official taxonomy.

### CH14-23 · Make the temptation–habit progression nonmechanical

**Severity:** Major / sin and habit · **Control:** DC-10, DC-11 · **Location:** `chapter_14.md:234–238`

**Issue.** Temptation is not sin; consent is sin according to object and degree; habit can diminish or increase culpability; repentance and grace can interrupt any stage. “Same pattern” and inevitable hardening oversimplify moral psychology.

**Source passage with proposed inline redline:**

Capital vices ~~typically follow the same pattern ... temptation, consent, habit, hardening, spiritual death~~ ++often grow when repeated consent forms a habit, dulls conscience and makes future resistance harder. Temptation itself is not sin; the gravity of consent depends on object, knowledge and freedom. Habits may diminish immediate freedom or aggravate responsibility according to how they were formed and maintained. Grace, repentance, treatment and changed circumstances can interrupt the pattern at every stage++.

**Editorial consequence.** The warning remains serious without fatalism.

### CH14-24 · Preserve Christian hope for one’s own salvation and Christ’s unique agency

**Severity:** Stop-ship / soteriology and Church · **Control:** DC-19, DC-22 · **Location:** `chapter_14.md:242–260`

**Issue.** Concern for “my salvation” is not selfish by nature; it is an act of hope. Christ saves through His Paschal mystery; the Church is His sacrament and instrument, not the independent savior “by” whom we are redeemed. Baptism incorporates, but not every sin is first an act done to another human.

**Controlling sources:** CCC 1817–1821, 846–856, 1267–1271.

**Source passage with proposed inline redline:**

Asking whether my soul is safe ~~plays into selfishness~~ ++can be a proper exercise of the theological virtue of hope, though it becomes distorted if detached from love of neighbor and ecclesial responsibility++. God does not save isolated individuals; Christ saves persons and incorporates them into His Body. ~~We are saved by the Body of Christ, for the Body of Christ~~ ++We are saved by Jesus Christ through His Paschal mystery; His Church is the sacrament and instrument through which He ordinarily gives faith, Baptism, teaching and sacramental life++. 

~~Most sins are things we do to other people~~ ++Many sins directly harm neighbors; others directly concern God or one’s own good, while every sin wounds ecclesial communion in some way++.

**Editorial consequence.** Personal hope, ecclesial salvation and Christ’s unique role are ordered correctly.

### CH14-25 · Make interpersonal and generational effects probabilistic

**Severity:** Major / families and evidence · **Control:** DC-25, DC-26 · **Location:** `chapter_14.md:246–258`

**Issue.** Sin causes scandal and wounds others, but anger does not automatically harden every room, despair does not “give permission” to stop hoping, and adultery does not determine children’s future families. “Most common reason people leave” and “strongest argument” need evidence and probabilistic wording.

**Source passage with proposed inline redline:**

Sin can travel through relationships: anger may change a household’s climate; cultivated lust can train perception; expressed despair can burden others. ~~Our actions are always teaching ... children absorb ... adultery teaches children ... fracture they carry into families~~ ++Our example influences others, especially children, but it does not determine their identity, virtue or future. Grace, freedom, resilience and other relationships intervene++. Christian scandal is ~~one of the most common reasons people leave ... often the strongest argument~~ ++a serious factor in some departures and doubts; exact frequency and motive require evidence and should not be universalized++.

**Editorial consequence.** The communal nature of sin is taught without psychological determinism.

### CH14-26 · Distinguish sanctifying grace, charity, baptismal character and ecclesial incorporation

**Severity:** Stop-ship / Baptism and mortal sin · **Control:** DC-06, DC-08, DC-16 · **Location:** `chapter_14.md:264–272, 332–338`

**Issue.** Sanctifying grace is not identical to “God’s perfect love.” Mortal sin destroys charity and sanctifying grace in the heart but does not erase baptismal character or all incorporation into the Church. The relationship is gravely ruptured, not metaphysically annihilated, and Confession restores grace rather than rebaptizing or recreating covenant character.

**Controlling sources:** CCC 1262–1274, 1446, 1468–1470, 1855–1861.

**Source passage with proposed inline redline:**

Mortal sin destroys sanctifying grace ~~—God's perfect love poured into our hearts ... stops that heart ... breaks communion with Trinity and Church~~ ++and charity in the sinner’s heart. Sanctifying grace is the created participation in divine life; charity is the infused theological virtue; the Holy Spirit is the uncreated Person. Mortal sin gravely ruptures communion with God and wounds ecclesial communion, but the indelible baptismal character and a real, though wounded, incorporation remain++. Reconciliation restores sanctifying grace, charity and sacramental communion; it does not establish a new Baptism or covenant character.

**Editorial consequence.** The sacramental ontology is Catholic rather than an all-or-nothing severance metaphor.

### CH14-27 · Give the full structure of the Sacrament of Penance

**Severity:** Major / sacrament and canon law · **Control:** DC-18 · **Location:** `chapter_14.md:272–275`

**Issue.** “Tell sins, priest absolves, do penance” omits contrition, purpose of amendment and integral confession of grave sins in kind and number. Penance does not itself make absolution happen. Direct violation of the seal by a confessor has a precise canonical penalty; other persons with knowledge are also bound. OCIA is mainly for initiation, not the ordinary first resource for an already initiated Catholic returning to confession.

**Controlling sources:** CCC 1450–1460; Code of Canon Law 983–984, 988–989, 1386.

**Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:**

Confession is accessible in practice: approach with contrition and a firm purpose of amendment; confess all remembered grave sins in kind and number after a diligent examination; receive priestly absolution; perform the assigned satisfaction, which repairs harm and medicinally aids conversion but does not purchase forgiveness. The sacramental seal is absolute. ~~The priest is bound ... under pain of excommunication~~ ++A confessor who directly violates it incurs the Church’s gravest canonical penalty; interpreters and others who acquire knowledge are also strictly bound under applicable penalties++. If it has been a long time, tell the priest and ask him to guide you; a parish priest or confessor is the ordinary resource, while OCIA serves those preparing for Catholic initiation or reception.

**Editorial consequence.** The sacrament is simple without being incomplete or canonically imprecise.

### CH14-28 · Rebuild the examination of conscience around grace and objective norms, not pain

**Severity:** Stop-ship / conscience and scrupulosity · **Control:** DC-10, DC-11, DC-25 · **Location:** `chapter_14.md:278–316`

**Issue.** Pain, guilt, resentment, shame and relational distance can arise from being victimized, mental illness, false guilt or another’s manipulation; grave sin can also feel painless. Beginning with pain and “follow it to the cause” is unsafe. The rigid order-of-love ladder repeats Chapter 11’s error, and all-four analysis can magnify minor acts.

**Controlling sources:** CCC 1776–1794, 1846, 2052–2557.

### CH14-29 · Complete perfect contrition, treat scrupulosity compassionately and remove grace/framework conflation

**Severity:** Stop-ship / contrition, scrupulosity and grace · **Control:** DC-06, DC-11, DC-18 · **Location:** `chapter_14.md:298–338`

**Issue.** Perfect contrition reconciles before confession only when it includes the firm resolution to confess as soon as possible. The displayed Act of Contrition is one approved/common form, not the universal text. Scrupulosity is not simply mistrust disguised as rigor; it can involve OCD or anxiety and should be guided by a stable confessor and clinician. “God’s perfect love—Grace” collapses created grace into the definition.

**Controlling sources:** CCC 1451–1453, 1492, 1996–2005.

**Source passage with proposed inline redline:**

Perfect contrition arises from love of God above all and obtains forgiveness of venial sins; it also reconciles one with God before sacramental confession ~~by itself~~ ++when it includes the firm resolution to confess grave sins as soon as possible++. The following Act of Contrition is ~~the very prayer you speak~~ ++one common form; approved forms vary, and contrition is an act of the will rather than a required emotional intensity++.

Scrupulosity is ~~mistrust of God's mercy dressed up as rigor~~ ++an anxious distortion of conscience that may involve spiritual habits, OCD, trauma or other clinical factors; it is not itself proof of bad faith. A scrupulous person should ordinarily obey one stable confessor’s guidance, avoid repeated self-reassessment and seek qualified mental-health care when appropriate++.

By learning and submitting to God's perfect love—~~Grace~~ ++with the help of sanctifying and actual grace++—we can avoid mortal sin and grow in holiness. Working out salvation is cooperation with God who first works in us, not progressively installing four facets.
