    Chapter 7 · Justice: God Is Justice

    Chapter assessment

    Chapter 7 has a strong governing insight: justice is the stable will to render God and neighbor what is due, and Catholic social teaching carries that obligation from personal conduct into institutions and the common good. The edited chapter is particularly effective when it joins the virtue of religion, the imago Dei, care for the vulnerable, duties as well as rights, and the social mission announced by Christ in Luke 4.

    Its principal doctrinal problem is the repeated claim that justice is not fully justice until it restores a person or relationship. Catholic punishment also includes proportionate redress of disorder, protection, deterrence and medicinal aims; restitution restores an equality of justice but does not automatically reconcile. The chapter also treats dulia as generic mutual honor, blurs moral right with civil immunity, groups abortion, euthanasia and capital punishment too tightly, makes grace identical with God’s Perfect Love, weakens the enduring marriage bond after betrayal, and gives the Sacrament of Penance the wrong internal structure. Several cultural applications are stated through broad labels rather than precise acts or policies. These revisions preserve the chapter’s urgency while making its justice genuinely Catholic rather than definitionally restorative or politically elastic.

    CH7-01 · Present the natural-law recognition of justice without universalizing immediate judgment

    Severity: Major / anthropology and reasoning · Control: DC-27, DC-29 · Location: chapter_07.md:31–43

    Issue. Natural law gives every person a basic orientation to good and justice, but particular judgments can be obscured by ignorance, formation, passion and sin. “Immediately, instinctively, universally” exceeds what the examples prove.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1954–1960; Romans 2:14–16.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    Every child knows can recognize it. Every human heart feels is ordered toward it. The instinctive recognition that some things are objectively wrong—not just unpleasant or inconvenient, but actually, truly unjust.

    The bully taking lunch money. The boss stealing credit. The judge taking bribes. We know—immediately, instinctively, universally often with striking immediacy—that these things are wrong. Not just socially disapproved. Wrong.

    This universal cry reveals something profound: justice is written on every human heart — part of the natural law built into creation by the God whose image we bear, the God who IS just. That law is real even when our perception or application of it is clouded and needs formation.

    Editorial consequence. The opening retains its experiential force without claiming infallible moral intuition.

    CH7-02 · Do not say nonbelievers cannot rationally justify any authority for justice

    Severity: Stop-ship / natural reason and logic · Control: DC-07, DC-27, DC-29 · Location: chapter_07.md:71–83

    Issue. The chapter rightly argues that objective moral obligation finds its ultimate ground in God, but nonbelievers can reason from human nature, goods, dignity and the common good. Non-theistic moral realism is not refuted merely by saying humans disagree; Chapter 3 already carries the book’s fuller grounding argument.

    Controlling sources: CCC 31–38; CCC 1954–1960; Romans 2:14–16.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    The standard must be eternal, unchanging, and absolute. It must be the author of Justice - God Himself have an ultimate ground beyond fluctuating preference; this book has argued that its personal source is God Himself.

    Every time you've said "That's not fair," you've appealed to God's standard, whether you knew it or not. Every time you've demanded justice, you've leaned on the only ground ultimate ground that can hold you up — because without God (the source of what is good and just), justice is only a word, and words alone cannot bind anyone lacks the transcendent personal source and final end this book defended in Chapter 3.

    Human attempts to author justice without God inevitably fail—not because non-believers can't recognize justice (natural law allows this), but because they can't justify why their sense of justice should be authoritative. They replace the only source of objective justice with human preference serving self-interest can still recognize and defend genuine goods through natural reason; a nonbeliever may offer serious arguments from nature, dignity or the common good. The deeper question is whether any impersonal account can finally explain why the good obliges every person even at the cost of self-interest. As we explored in Chapters 3 and 4, morality authored by humans must be imposed through force because it has no inherent authority reduced to will or preference eventually depends on force.

    Editorial consequence. The theistic conclusion becomes an argument rather than a dismissal of every secular moral philosopher.

    CH7-03 · Label the two-direction failure scheme as an authorial diagnostic

    Severity: Stop-ship / moral taxonomy · Control: DC-01, DC-10, DC-27 · Location: chapter_07.md:85–97

    Issue. Idolatry is contrary to the virtue of religion, and exploitation is a grave neighbor-directed injustice, but the Church does not teach that these are the two exhaustive “opposing sins” of justice. Injustice also includes failures toward self, family, community, authority and equals.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    And when justice is corrupted, it never collapses from nowhere. It always fails in one of two specific directions can be examined through two primary directions emphasized in this chapter: what is owed to God and what is owed to neighbor. This is the author’s diagnostic frame, not an exhaustive Catholic taxonomy of every sin against justice.

    Against God, the opposing sin is its clearest corruption is idolatry.

    Against neighbor, the contrary sin is one especially grave corruption is the exploitation of the vulnerable — above all, the deliberate taking of innocent life in the womb.

    This chapter will show why justice is what love owes — to God and to neighbor — and why its denial, in either direction, is at its root a corruption of love's structure. Other injustices will not always fit these two labels, even though they remain failures to render a real due.

    Editorial consequence. The framework serves the chapter without masquerading as the received classification of justice’s contrary vices.

    CH7-04 · Avoid deterministic effects and an unqualified “unconditional” parental duty

    Severity: Major / family and pastoral safety · Control: DC-24, DC-25, DC-26 · Location: chapter_07.md:101–121

    Issue. Parents owe steadfast love and care, but “unconditional” can be heard as approval without boundaries. The passage also states that weaponized care will shape a child’s understanding of all relationships rather than describing a risk.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    The parent's sacrifices are counted; the child's rightful claim to be loved unconditionally steadfastly, without making basic care a tool of coercion, is erased.

    This is what happens can happen when justice is violated within the family: the child learns may learn that love comes with a bill, that nothing is given freely, that even the most basic care will be used as leverage. This shapes can wound how they understand all later love and relationships. Grace, stable care and competent help can heal these learned expectations.

    Editorial consequence. The vignette remains morally clear without predicting a child’s future or confusing love with boundaryless indulgence.

    CH7-05 · Do not use dulia as the generic name for reciprocal honor

    Severity: Stop-ship / theological terminology · Control: DC-27, DC-28 · Location: chapter_07.md:123

    Issue. In Catholic theology dulia ordinarily names the veneration given to saints, distinguished from latria and from the Blessed Virgin’s special hyperdulia. It is not the general virtue-name for all honor exchanged among equals.

    Controlling sources: CCC 956–957; Second Council of Nicaea; Summa Theologiae II–II, q.103.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    This same justice runs sideways, not just up and down a family tree. St. Paul tells the Romans that mutual honor is a mark of authentic love in any community: "Let love be genuine; hate what is evil, hold fast to what is good; love one another with brotherly affection; outdo one another in showing honor" (Romans 12:9-10). Honor isn't only owed upward, from child to parent—rendering it at all, in any direction, is itself an act of justice: giving another their due dignity. Among equals, love competes to give it away. The Church has a name for this virtue: Dulia, honor rendered to another's real excellence or dignity, apart from parents or country (see Chapter 13 for a fuller discussion). Aquinas treats observance and honor among the virtues annexed to justice; dulia in standard Catholic usage should be reserved for the veneration of saints, not made a generic label for reciprocal courtesy.

    Editorial consequence. The chapter keeps the horizontal-honor insight without teaching a nonstandard devotional taxonomy.

    CH7-06 · Qualify the Hebrew gloss and do not make all biblical justice restorative

    Severity: Stop-ship / Scripture and justice · Control: DC-13, DC-27, DC-28 · Location: chapter_07.md:129–131

    Issue. Mishpat and tsedaqah have broad contextual ranges. Restoration is a major biblical aim, but biblical justice also judges, redresses wrongdoing, vindicates, protects and punishes. Amos 5:24 does not establish that justice is incomplete without restored relationship.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    The Hebrew Scriptures use two main words important word families for justice: Mishpat and Tsedeqah tsedaqah. Mishpat appears over 400 times frequently in the Old Testament, meaning according to context right judgment, legal decision, and vindication of the oppressed—it's the justice of the courtroom, ensuring fair treatment. Tsedeqah Tsedaqah means righteousness, right relationships, and covenant faithfulness—it's the justice of restored rightly ordered communion. When the prophets cry "Let justice (mishpat) roll down like waters, and righteousness (tsedeqah) like an ever-flowing stream" (Amos 5:24), they're calling for both: fair treatment AND restored right relationships. Together, they reveal that biblical justice always aims at healing and restoration, not mere avoidance of punishment cannot be reduced to punishment, even though proportionate judgment and redress remain genuine dimensions of justice.

    Editorial consequence. The Hebrew material illuminates Scripture without forcing every usage into a restorative-justice theory.

    CH7-07 · Correct Aquinas on restitution, punishment and the “parts” of justice

    Severity: Stop-ship / moral theology · Control: DC-13, DC-27, DC-28 · Location: chapter_07.md:133–137

    Issue. Restitution restores equality by returning what was unjustly taken or repairing harm; it does not necessarily restore the relationship. Reconciliation is not simply one of Aquinas’s named virtues annexed to justice. Punishment can be just through redress, defense, deterrence and correction even when reconciliation does not occur.

    Controlling sources: CCC 2266; CCC 2411–2412; Summa Theologiae II–II, qq.58–62, 80–120.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    Commutative justice is fairness between individuals—keeping contracts, paying debts, honoring agreements. And crucially, when this justice is violated, Aquinas insists it is not satisfied by mere apology or punishment: it demands restitution—the active restoring of what was taken, whether goods, reputation, or the relationship itself or reputation, insofar as repair is possible (Summa Theologiae II-II, q.62); justice between persons is inherently restorative ordered to restoring the equality violated by the unjust act, not automatically to relational reconciliation. Distributive justice is the fair distribution of common goods according to need and contribution—which necessarily includes lifting up those who have been deprived can require correcting deprivation and ensuring participation, not merely preventing future deprivation.

    Aquinas also treats restitution, truth-telling, gratitude, honor, and reconciliation religion, piety, observance, gratitude, vindication, truthfulness, friendliness and liberality — acts or virtues belonging to justice's own household — as essential to understanding what the virtue requires (ST II-II, qq.58–122). Justice, rightly understood, is never content with bare equilibrium; it presses toward the restored communion an abstract equality detached from persons and the common good; charity and mercy press beyond strict due toward the healed communion that tsedaqah names. Punishment without restoration is not yet justice; it is only its first half. Proportionate punishment can itself be just when it redresses disorder, protects the community, deters wrongdoing and, when possible, fosters correction; restoration is not always achievable and reconciliation requires more than justice alone.

    Editorial consequence. The account becomes recognizably Thomistic and leaves mercy real as a gift beyond strict due.

    CH7-08 · Add the moral limits to obedience under Romans 13

    Severity: Stop-ship / political authority · Control: DC-14, DC-25 · Location: chapter_07.md:139–141

    Issue. Governing authority is owed obedience only within legitimate competence and the moral order. Unjust laws do not bind conscience; sometimes resistance is required.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1897–1904; CCC 2238–2243; Acts 5:29.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    Legal justice—what we owe the common good—has a concrete shape in Scripture. St. Paul spells it out directly: "Let every person be subject to the governing authorities. For there is no authority except from God, and those that exist have been instituted by God... Pay all of them their dues, taxes to whom taxes are due, revenue to whom revenue is due, respect to whom respect is due, honor to whom honor is due" (Romans 13:1-7). This is legal justice in one breath: authority is owed obedience, and the state is owed its due, because governing authority itself borrows its standing from God, not from itself. That debt is morally bounded: laws contrary to right reason, divine law or human dignity do not bind conscience, and no ruler may demand what belongs to God alone.

    Editorial consequence. The passage cannot be misused to sanctify tyranny or silence conscientious resistance.

    CH7-09 · Label the “every commandment is the First Commandment” reading as a meditation

    Severity: Major / private synthesis and exegesis · Control: DC-01, DC-20, DC-28 · Location: chapter_07.md:149–151

    Issue. All sin involves disordered love, and idols can be found beneath many sins, but adultery, theft, murder, false witness and coveting have their own moral objects. They are not formally just versions of idolatry.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    This reframes the Decalogue itself. Read in order, the Ten Commandments are not a random list of prohibitions; they are justice applied first to God and then to neighbor. "You shall have no other gods before me..." (Exodus 20:3–5). Every commandment that follows is the First Commandment applied to a specific idol As an examination of conscience—not as an exhaustive exegesis—we can also ask what idol a violation of each later commandment serves: do not commit adultery — do not make lust your god; do not steal — do not make possessions your god; do not murder — do not make rage or power your god; do not bear false witness — do not make reputation your god; do not covet — do not make desire itself your god. When God is truly first, nothing else can usurp His place. When He is not, something always will.

    Editorial consequence. The meditation remains illuminating without replacing the distinct content of the commandments.

    CH7-10 · Distinguish moral right from civil immunity and close the malformed callout

    Severity: Stop-ship / rights, religious freedom and production · Control: DC-14, DC-22, DC-30 · Location: chapter_07.md:167–175

    Issue. No one has a moral right to evil, but civil religious freedom is immunity from coercion within just limits even when a person is in error. “Unmercifully” is also too broad because mercy exceeds strict due. The HTML callout’s <p> tag is not closed.

    Controlling sources: CCC 2104–2109; Dignitatis Humanae 2, 7.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    But here's a crucial distinction our culture has lost: A right moral right is always ordered toward the good. There is no "right" moral right to do evil. We don't have a "right" to murder the innocent, a "right" to lie, or a "right" to exploit our neighbor. We may have the freedom to choose evil—free will is real—but freedom to choose is not the same as a moral right to choose wrongly. Civil immunity is a different question: religious freedom protects persons from coercion within just limits and is not a moral license to error.

    <p><strong>We never have the moral "right" to act falsely or unjustly. or unmercifully.</strong> Mercy, because it exceeds strict due, cannot be inserted into this sentence as though every failure to extend it violated another’s strict right.</p>

    Editorial consequence. The chapter distinguishes moral entitlement, free choice and civil liberty, and the callout becomes valid HTML.

    CH7-11 · Name distinct bodily injustices rather than grouping unlike moral objects

    Severity: Major / anthropology and pastoral clarity · Control: DC-23, DC-27 · Location: chapter_07.md:181–189

    Issue. “A body can be rented, photographed, or bought” collapses surrogacy, pornography, prostitution, trafficking and ordinary morally neutral photography into one slogan. The moral object and the harmed person should be named.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    When our culture invites us to believe the body lies — that maleness and femaleness are arbitrary, that fruitfulness can be cleanly severed from sexual union, that a body can be rented, photographed, or bought a woman’s reproductive capacity may be contracted through surrogacy, a person’s sexual intimacy commodified through pornography or prostitution, or a human being trafficked and sold — it commits injustice against the very person the body was given to reveal. To dishonor the body is to dishonor the person. To lie about what the body reveals is to commit injustice against reality itself.

    Editorial consequence. The moral critique becomes precise enough to be pastorally and analytically useful.

    CH7-12 · Replace elastic culture-war labels with act- and policy-specific analysis

    Severity: Stop-ship / social ethics and evidence · Control: DC-15, DC-22, DC-27 · Location: chapter_07.md:207–219

    Issue. “Inclusion,” “equity” and “anti-racism” name many different practices. Some may be unjust; others may redress genuine exclusion or racial discrimination. The paragraph attributes motives and effects to entire categories without identifying the program, policy, criterion or victim.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    But there is a crucial difference between biblical advocacy for the vulnerable and ideological partiality. Biblical justice serves the actual good of the person, aiming at restoration to full dignity and flourishing. Ideological partiality uses groups as political tools, creates permanent victim classes, and judges people by external characteristics rather than individual dignity. A policy becomes partial when it assigns guilt, worth, opportunity or penalty principally by group membership rather than by relevant need, conduct or a proportionate remedy for a demonstrated injustice.

    This is why much of what is sold today as "inclusion" is actually its opposite. When a program called inclusion excludes those who hold to the faith, when equity creates new hierarchies based on race or sex, when anti-racism judges persons by the color of their skin rather than the content of their character, justice has not been advanced — it has been traded for a counterfeit each proposal called “inclusion,” “equity” or “anti-racism” must be judged by its concrete moral object, means and effects. Excluding religious believers because of their faith, assigning collective guilt by race, or imposing sex-based preferences unrelated to a proportionate remedy would be unjust; removing an unjust barrier or repairing documented discrimination can be required by justice. True justice is impartial but not blind to relevant need, vulnerability or past injury.

    Editorial consequence. The chapter can criticize real partiality without condemning every measure bearing a contested label.

    CH7-13 · Preserve the valid marriage bond while recognizing separation and safety

    Severity: Stop-ship / marriage and pastoral safety · Control: DC-16, DC-24, DC-25 · Location: chapter_07.md:241–249

    Issue. Betrayal can radically change duties of common life, trust and contact and can justify separation, but it does not make a valid matrimonial bond bind the innocent spouse “less.” The bond endures unless death or a declaration of nullity establishes that no valid bond arose.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1644–1651; CCC 2382–2386; Code of Canon Law 1151–1155.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    The ideal stands because God defines it, not because the people in our lives have lived it. Honor is owed for the gift of life and for the office of parent, spouse or child , not for harm done within it — and it has never required the wounded to stay within reach of further harm. Where covenant has been broken, the broken vow does not bind the innocent the same way it once did a valid matrimonial bond remains, but grave fault can justify separation, boundaries and the suspension of common life; trust and reconciliation are not owed without repentance, repair and safety. The truth about what happened to you is itself part of what justice owes — to you first.

    Editorial consequence. The paragraph protects victims without implying that adultery dissolves the sacramental bond or permits remarriage.

    CH7-14 · Distinguish grace from God and from the author’s definition

    Severity: Stop-ship / grace · Control: DC-06, DC-07 · Location: chapter_07.md:253

    Issue. Sanctifying grace is a created habitual gift and participation in divine life; actual grace is divine help; the Holy Spirit is the uncreated Person given to us. Grace is not simply “nothing other than God’s Perfect Love itself.”

    Controlling sources: CCC 1996–2005.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    And then the deepest turn: by grace — which is nothing other than God's Perfect Love itself at work in us God’s free gift and help, by which the Holy Spirit heals and elevates us and sanctifying grace gives us a created participation in divine life — what our wounds alone could never produce becomes possible.

    Editorial consequence. The devotional sentence no longer collapses created grace, divine action and the divine essence.

    CH7-15 · Do not promise that trauma becomes fuel or teacher once grace “touches” it

    Severity: Major / trauma and pastoral safety · Control: DC-24, DC-25, DC-26 · Location: chapter_07.md:251–253

    Issue. Grace can bring profound healing and fruit from suffering, but trauma does not automatically become fuel, a school or a teacher. Some wounds remain disabling and require professional care; the victim is not morally deficient if no redemptive meaning is felt.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    Through the Blood of Jesus there is real reconciliation, real forgiveness, real healing — actual salvation from these wounds, not a polite covering of them. This promise concerns Christ’s saving power; it is not a guarantee of immediate psychological relief or restored relationship. Trauma may also require skilled clinical care and long-term protection.

    The pain we were shown becomes the very fuel God uses can, without making the evil good, become one place where God gives wisdom and strength to call us into faithful love now. The child who was not honored becomes may become the parent who honors. The spouse who was betrayed becomes may, by grace, remain the spouse who keeps faith. The wrong done to us, redeemed by Christ, becomes the school of need not dictate the right we now do. The wound itself, once grace has touched it, becomes a teacher Sometimes the wound can teach: it shows may show us with piercing clarity what justice owes every person made in God's image. None of this excuses the offender or burdens the wounded person with producing visible fruit on a timetable.

    Editorial consequence. The passage offers hope without spiritualizing trauma or making recovery a test of grace.

    CH7-16 · Preserve punishment, distinguish abandonment and avoid declaring every defective love unreal

    Severity: Stop-ship / justice and pastoral scope · Control: DC-07, DC-13, DC-25 · Location: chapter_07.md:257–263

    Issue. Justice can combat evil through proportionate punishment as well as restoration. “Elderly parent left alone to die” could include legitimate professional care or unavoidable absence. A grave failure of love does not prove no natural love exists at all.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    Justice combats evil not by abstract proclamation but by rendering concretely what is owed: protecting the vulnerable, exposing oppression, refusing to enable what harms, and seeking restoration rather than revenge while allowing proportionate punishment to redress disorder, protect others, deter wrongdoing and encourage reform.

    The unborn child crushed in the womb, the elderly parent left alone to die the dependent elder deliberately abandoned without necessary care, the trafficked woman sold from city to city, the worker robbed of his wages, the prisoner forgotten behind iron — each is a person owed what Scripture calls their due, and each has been abandoned to harm.

    A love that will not defend the defenseless is not love; it is gravely defective and may be counterfeit; it can become a comfort that has priced the vulnerable out of its circle.

    Editorial consequence. The paragraph remains urgent without erasing natural love or condemning legitimate care arrangements.

    CH7-17 · State religious freedom and life ethics through their distinct Catholic principles

    Severity: Stop-ship / religious liberty and human life · Control: DC-14, DC-22, DC-27 · Location: chapter_07.md:279–285

    Issue. Religious freedom is civil immunity from coercion within just limits, not an unlimited right to act on conscience. Abortion and euthanasia are intrinsically evil direct killings; capital punishment has a distinct doctrinal history, and the present Catechism calls it inadmissible. A shared dignity principle does not make the moral objects identical.

    Controlling sources: CCC 2104–2109; CCC 2258–2269; CCC 2270–2279; Dignitatis Humanae 2, 7.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    And it requires safeguarding religious freedom — the right to worship according to conscience civil immunity, within just limits, from coercion in religious matters — because no society is just that forbids its people to render God what is owed Him. This civil right is not a moral right to error and does not license acts contrary to the objective moral order or the rights of others.

    And the Church has not left these principles abstract. For two millennia she has applied them concretely: ... in her unwavering defense of innocent human life from conception to natural death — so that abortion, euthanasia, and the death penalty are answered by the same principle direct abortion and euthanasia are condemned as intrinsically evil killings of innocent life, while the death penalty has a distinct history and is now taught to be inadmissible because it attacks the person’s inviolability and dignity (CCC 2258–2330; 2267 on capital punishment).

    Editorial consequence. The passage reaches the Church’s present conclusions without flattening unlike moral objects or overstating civil liberty.

    CH7-18 · Remove the unsourced superlative about Catholic social thought

    Severity: Major / evidence and tone · Control: DC-27, DC-29 · Location: chapter_07.md:285

    Issue. “No other body of thought in human history” is an unprovable civilizational superlative and invites an unnecessary challenge. The Catholic social tradition’s breadth can be stated positively.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    No other body of thought in human history has worked out Across centuries, Catholic social teaching has developed the demands of justice across so many domains with such concrete guidance. To be Catholic is to inherit a treasury of moral reasoning no individual conscience, however well-meaning, could ever construct alone — and a humility about that treasury: the diligent layman is right to read the encyclicals and the Compendium before forming his own opinion on a question the Church has already labored over for centuries.

    Editorial consequence. Confidence replaces triumphalism and the reader’s attention remains on the actual sources.

    CH7-19 · Give the Sacrament of Penance its received structure

    Severity: Stop-ship / sacramental theology · Control: DC-18 · Location: chapter_07.md:291–293

    Issue. Contrition, confession and satisfaction are the penitent’s acts; priestly absolution is the other essential element. “Restoration” is an effect, not a second act that completes the sacrament alongside absolution. Sacramental confession is not required by strict justice for every venial sin.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1422–1498, especially 1450–1460 and 1491–1497.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    Justice and mercy meet sacramentally in the Sacrament of Reconciliation. Justice requires it because sin is a real violation that must be named, owned, and confessed rather than ignored — confession names what is owed, contrition acknowledges the debt, and penance offers to repair what was broken. Absolution and restoration, which complete the sacrament, are acts of mercy Christ instituted it so the baptized sinner may receive God’s forgiveness and reconciliation with the Church. Under the Spirit’s grace, the penitent’s acts are contrition, confession and satisfaction; the priest’s sacramental absolution forgives in Christ’s name. Satisfaction seeks to repair harm but does not purchase pardon, and restoration is among the sacrament’s effects rather than a fifth component. Confession is mercy's natural home, and we will see its full shape, facet by facet, in the next chapter.

    Editorial consequence. The preview prepares Chapter 8 without giving the sacrament an invented four- or five-part form.

    CH7-20 · Do not make “human justice” and “God’s justice” rival species

    Severity: Stop-ship / grace, justice and restoration · Control: DC-06, DC-07, DC-13 · Location: chapter_07.md:295–311

    Issue. Every genuinely just human act participates in the moral order from God. Human institutions can protect, repair, reconcile and support rehabilitation, though they cannot confer supernatural grace or raise the spiritually dead. Divine justice does not differ by ceasing to render due and becoming mercy.
