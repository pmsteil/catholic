    1 · Payment language: the Cross as satisfaction, and our sacrifice as gift (item 1)

    Why. Aligned with Ch. 8: Christ's work is satisfaction that love gives its power (CCC 615–616), not a price paid to a creditor. Ch. 8 is cross-referenced, not repeated. On our side, “pay” becomes give, bear or offer. Kept on purpose: the lines that say what sacrifice is not (“not waiting to be paid,” “not the price we pay to keep God interested,” “not a transaction at a distance”), and “transaction” as the Part C counterfeit. “Proves” becomes “shows” (the Ch. 2 ruling). Line 131 drops “body, blood, soul, and divinity” poured out on the Cross: that is the Eucharistic formula, and the divine nature does not suffer; His human soul does. Line 133's truth, justice and mercy “paid for” said something no one means. Line 334's “inevitable” and “guarantees” read as determinism, against the free-will rule.

    When Love Stops Being Theory · line 49

    Sacrifice is the costly self-gift that makes love real; sloth is the refusal to pay give it. Not the inability, but the refusal. The Christian who loves God "in his heart" but will not rise early to pray, will not give to the poor, will not tell an inconvenient truth, will not miss a game for Mass — this is not love, however much it feels like love. It is love theorized and seldom lived. Christ's verdict is fierce: "So, because you are lukewarm, and neither cold nor hot, I will spew you out of my mouth" (Revelation 3:16). Church tradition names this sin The Catechism names lukewarmness as "hesitation or negligence in responding to divine love," and beyond it acedia (ah-SEE-dee-ah) — the spiritual sorrow that shrinks from the demands of divine love. Aquinas identifies it as the vice directly opposed to charity itself , spiritual sloth, which "goes so far as to refuse the joy that comes from God" (CCC 2094). Aquinas calls acedia contrary to charity itself, because it is sorrow at the very good in which charity rejoices (Summa Theologiae II-II, q.35, a.3).

    The Difference Between Sacrifice and Self-Destruction · line 314

    A wife who stays with an abusive husband isn't practicing sacrificial love—she's enabling his sin and endangering herself and her children. Why? Because Sacrifice never means staying in harm's way. A wife whose husband puts her or her children in danger is not asked by God to stay and absorb it; the Church permits her to leave, and the marriage bond itself remains (CCC 1649; Code of Canon Law, can. 1153; see Chapter 4). Leaving is not a failure of sacrifice, because true sacrifice requires truth (acknowledging the abuse is real and wrong), justice (protecting herself and her children from harm), and mercy (seeking the abuser's true good, which may require separation to force him to face consequences and seek so that he faces the consequences and seeks help). A parent who gives money to an addicted child to buy drugs isn't sacrificing—they're participating in their child's destruction by ignoring , knowing it will buy drugs, is usually acting out of love and fear at once; but the gift feeds the child's destruction, because it ignores truth (the addiction), violating violates justice (enabling sin), and distorting distorts mercy (helping them continue in bondage). …
