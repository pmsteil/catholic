    Introduction

    Introduction assessment

    The Introduction has a clear evangelical purpose, a strong personal reason for asking “What is love?”, and an effective concern for children. Its central structural problem is that the proposed four-facet definition is repeatedly called the missing interpretive key that unlocks Scripture, salvation, grace, the sacraments, holiness, and the whole Christian life. That assigns a private synthesis work properly belonging to Christ, the apostolic deposit of faith, and the Church’s rule of faith. A second cluster confuses initial justification with lifelong sanctification and universal human dignity with supernatural adoptive filiation. A third cluster uses deterministic predictions and culture-war catalogues where precise propositions, moral objects, culpability distinctions, and paths of hope would make the Catholic case stronger. The remaining proposals address grammar, citation punctuation, capitalization, repetition, production markup, and overlong chapter summaries.

    INTRO-01 · Replace the fragmentary culture-war opening with a precise thesis

    Severity: Pastoral / clarity · Control: DC-25, DC-26 · Location: 3_introduction.md:4–6

    Issue. The second sentence is a fragment, and the opening places several disputed political labels in scare quotes before explaining the positive Catholic argument. “All of it boils down” also claims a single demonstrated cause for a wide range of personal, familial, ecclesial, and social evils.

    Why it matters. The Introduction can name contested questions directly without treating every person, movement, policy, and moral act as one undifferentiated phenomenon. The thesis should be strong but proportionate to what the book can establish.

    Source paragraphs with proposed inline redline:

    Our world feels like it's coming apart at the seams. Constant cries for justice, equity, inclusion, "transgender rights," and "reproductive rights." Public debate is filled with competing claims about justice, equity, inclusion, sexual identity and abortion, many of them framed in the language of rights and love. People are desperately searching for something, often calling it "love," but like the 1980 Johnny Lee song says, they're often "looking for love in all the wrong places." It's a deep ache in the heart of humanity, a longing for connection, meaning, purpose, and peace that seems increasingly elusive.

    I wrote this book because after walking with the Lord for over 37 years and deeply contemplating what "God is Love love" truly means, I've observed the seismic shifts in our culture and become absolutely convinced of one thing: the chaos we see—the lies, the partiality and cruelty, the indifference, the breakdown of social cohesion and the family—all of it boils down to has much to do with a failure to understand the most fundamentally human question that lies at the very heart of who we are as persons created in God's image:

    Editorial consequence. The opening remains urgent and culturally engaged while beginning with claims a bishop or skeptical reader can evaluate on their merits.

    INTRO-12 · Identify the definition as a subordinate synthesis, not the missing interpretive key

    Severity: Stop-ship · Control: DC-01, DC-19, DC-20 · Location: 3_introduction.md:90–92

    Issue. The definition is called “the interpretive key we’ve been missing,” and reading Scripture through it is said to make “everything” come into focus and resolve Christian interpretive division. This elevates the author’s synthesis to a quasi-magisterial rule of faith and implies that the Church lacked a key now supplied by the manuscript.

    Why it matters. Christ is the fullness and center of revelation. Scripture and Tradition form one deposit, authentically interpreted by the Magisterium. The four-facet definition can be defended as a useful pastoral synthesis only under those authorities.

    Source paragraphs with proposed inline redline:

    But to give them these tools, we first need to understand what authentic love actually is—not in vague, sentimental terms, but with precision and clarity. The definition of "God's Perfect Love" I'm about to share is the interpretive key we've been missing. When we read Scripture through this lens, everything suddenly comes into focus—what the Bible teaches, what Jesus did on the Cross, what the Church proclaims, what the saints lived. This definition shows how Jesus' invitation to love God and neighbor truly is the fulfillment of the law and the prophets, and it helps resolve much of the confusion that separates Christians in their interpretation of Scripture. The definition of “God’s Perfect Love” I am about to propose is a pastoral synthesis, not a new rule of faith or an independent interpretive authority. It must remain subordinate to Christ and to Sacred Scripture and Sacred Tradition as authentically interpreted by the Church. Used in that order, it can help readers see connections among what Scripture teaches, what Christ accomplished on the Cross, what the Church proclaims and what the saints lived. It may also help identify why Christian interpretations diverge without pretending to resolve every disagreement by itself.

    Controlling sources: CCC 65–67; Dei Verbum 7–10.

    Editorial consequence. The definition remains central to the book’s organization without competing with the Church’s received rule of faith.

    INTRO-13 · Relate love and salvation without identifying salvation with the framework

    Severity: Major · Control: DC-01, DC-06, DC-19, DC-26 · Location: 3_introduction.md:94

    Issue. “Modern Christianity has largely missed” is an unsupported ecumenical generalization. “We cannot understand salvation” overstates what follows from misunderstanding the word love, and “Salvation IS God’s perfect love” risks reducing the saving mystery to the book’s definition.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    Here's the crucial insight that modern Christianity has largely missed: If we don't understand what love truly is, we cannot understand what salvation truly is. The two are inseparable. When Jesus says "For God so loved the world that he gave his only Son, that whoever believes in him should not perish but have eternal life" (John 3:16), He's not just describing God's emotional disposition toward us—He's revealing the very nature of salvation itself. Salvation IS God's perfect love breaking into our broken world, transforming us from the inside out, liberating us from sin's slavery, and drawing us into eternal communion with Him. Here is a crucial connection: If we misunderstand love, we will also distort our understanding of salvation. When Jesus says, “For God so loved the world that he gave his only Son, that whoever believes in him should not perish but have eternal life” (John 3:16), He is not describing mere emotion; He reveals the Father’s saving love in action. By grace, God liberates us from sin, renews us inwardly and draws us into communion with Him through Christ.

    Editorial consequence. Love remains the motive and form of God’s saving action while salvation retains the full content of the Gospel.

    INTRO-14 · Distinguish initial justification from lifelong sanctification

    Severity: Stop-ship · Control: DC-06, DC-08, DC-09 · Location: 3_introduction.md:96

    Issue. The paragraph opposes “a moment” to “a journey,” as though Catholic teaching denied a real initial justification. It also uses the project’s banned commercial word and attributes the error broadly to “modern Christianity.”

    Why it matters. Catholic doctrine holds together initial justification—real forgiveness and interior renewal by grace, ordinarily in Baptism—and continuing growth in sanctification and charity. Lifelong transformation does not erase the initial gift.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    But what happens when we misunderstand love? We inevitably misunderstand salvation. And tragically, much of modern Christianity has drifted in this direction—treating salvation as a moment rather than a journey, a transaction rather than a relationship. When love is reduced to sentiment, salvation gets reduced too—stripped of its transformative power, its demands, and its glory. When love is reduced to sentiment, salvation can also be reduced. Catholic teaching holds together what must not be separated: initial justification is a real gift of forgiveness and interior renewal by grace, ordinarily given in Baptism, and the justified person is then called to lifelong growth in sanctification and charity. The journey does not erase the reality of its sacramental beginning.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1987–2029; CCC 1262–1274.

    Editorial consequence. The Introduction makes the distinctively Catholic transformation claim without creating a false choice between event and process.

    INTRO-15 · Preserve the gratuity of grace while naming the cost of discipleship

    Severity: Major · Control: DC-06, DC-08, DC-26 · Location: 3_introduction.md:98–100

    Issue. “A salvation that costs nothing” can suggest that salvation is purchased by human cost, while the surrounding polemic again generalizes from unspecified “incomplete formulas.” The intended point is valid: grace is unearned, yet the life grace creates includes self-denial, obedience, and transformation.

    Source paragraphs with proposed inline redline:

    This is not the salvation the Bible teaches. Scripture speaks of salvation as transformation, as dying to self and rising with Christ, as heeding St. Paul's command to "work out your own salvation with fear and trembling" (Philippians 2:12), as becoming "partakers of the divine nature" (2 Peter 1:4). Biblical salvation is nothing less than being caught up into God's own life of perfect love—learning to love as He loves, to give as He gives, to sacrifice as He sacrificed. It's comprehensive, demanding, glorious, and utterly transformative.

    How did we lose sight of this transformative vision? By separating what God has joined together. We'll address this more fully later in the book, but for now it's enough to say: when we reduce salvation to incomplete formulas, we create a salvation that costs nothing and changes nothing. We end up with a Christianity that can coexist quite comfortably with relativism—with selfishness, with injustice, with broken families, with lives unchanged by grace—because we've lost sight of what we're actually being saved for: to love as God loves. grace is freely given and can never be earned, yet the discipleship grace makes possible truly calls us to die to self and live in charity. When a formula obscures either God’s prior gift or our grace-enabled response, Christian profession can coexist with relativism, selfishness, injustice and lives resistant to transformation. We can lose sight of what we are being saved for: communion with God and a life of love in Him.

    Controlling source: CCC 1987–2029.

    Editorial consequence. The revision rejects both self-salvation and inert discipleship without making human sacrifice the price of grace.

    INTRO-16 · Remove the claim that the framework answers and unlocks everything

It's not just one theological concept among many—it's the key that unlocks everything else. When we understand what God's perfect love truly is, suddenly the entire Christian life comes into focus. We see that salvation is not escape from the world but transformation within it. We see that grace is not a legal fiction but real power to love authentically. We see that the sacraments are not empty rituals but encounters with divine love that change us. We see that holiness is not impossible perfection but growing capacity to participate in God's own life of self-giving love. Christians wrestle with great questions: What are salvation, sin and grace? Why did Christ institute the sacraments? What does holiness require? How do we resist evil and practice justice and mercy? The synthesis proposed in this book does not replace the Church’s answers or unlock every mystery, but it can help organize connections among them. Salvation includes deliverance from sin and real interior renewal; grace is God’s free gift that makes us participants in divine life; the sacraments are efficacious signs of grace instituted by Christ; and holiness is growth in charity and participation in God’s life.

    Source paragraphs with proposed inline redline:

    This is why this book matters. Not just for your own spiritual growth—though that's important—but for the children who are counting on us to get this right. The children in your family, your parish, your community and society. The children who will inherit the world we leave behind.
