## Chapter 2 · The Answer Is Love

### Chapter assessment

Chapter 2 performs an indispensable task well: it moves from the wound named in Chapter 1 to the book’s positive governing vision, gives the canonical definition its first sustained exposition, and introduces natural evil with unusual pastoral honesty. Its strongest passages are the opening Catechism quotation, the insistence that love must be concrete, and the closing promise of a Love strong enough to be trusted within suffering. The chapter’s central doctrinal repair is to distinguish four related realities that the present prose sometimes collapses: God is the objective supreme good; charity is the greatest theological virtue that unites us to God; natural human love is real but requires healing and supernatural elevation; and the author’s four-facet definition is a subordinate pastoral synthesis rather than the Church’s exhaustive answer to evil. The chapter also needs a clearer reason–revelation boundary, a guarded account of divine simplicity and sacrifice, a non-circular use of its five criteria, and safer language about redemptive suffering. These changes preserve the chapter’s thesis while locating it more securely in Christ, grace, the Trinity, and the whole Catholic faith.

**Why it matters.** These categories are useful if they are honestly identified as a pastoral diagnostic. They cannot, by themselves, prove that one proposed framework is the complete answer to evil.

**Controlling sources:** CCC 309–314; CCC 1849–1851; CCC 1886–1889.

### CH2-03 · Identify God—not an abstraction called love—as the supreme good

**Severity:** Stop-ship / doctrine and philosophical argument · **Control:** DC-01, DC-07, DC-19, DC-26 · **Location:** `chapter_02.md:45–55`

**Issue.** The text says every culture and every person gives the same answer—“Love”—to the question of the highest good, then says the Church calls “this” the *summum bonum*. Aquinas does not conclude in *Summa Theologiae* I–II, q.2 that love is the last created candidate standing; he argues that no created good constitutes final happiness. The objective supreme good and the final end of the person is God, consummated in the beatific vision. Charity is the greatest theological virtue because it unites us to God.

**Why it matters.** “God is love” does not permit the argument to reverse the terms and substitute an ambiguous human abstraction for God. The chapter’s thesis becomes stronger when the ultimate good, the virtue of charity, and ordinary experiences of love are related without being confused.

**Controlling sources:** CCC 27; CCC 1023–1029; CCC 1720–1722; CCC 1822–1829; *Summa Theologiae* I–II, q.2 and q.3, a.8.

**Source passage with proposed inline redline:**

### CH2-04 · Use privation theory without reducing every missing good immediately to love

**Severity:** Major / metaphysical precision · **Control:** DC-01, DC-05, DC-19, DC-26 · **Location:** `chapter_02.md:57`

**Issue.** Privation is the absence or corruption of a good that ought to exist in a subject. The due good may be life, health, sight, truth, justice, fidelity, order, or another perfection. It does not follow directly that the absent proximate good in every instance is “love,” even though rightly ordered love seeks the good and the loss of communion is a central consequence of sin.

**Why it matters.** The current syllogism appears to build the answer into its premise. A more exact account can preserve love’s integrating and restorative role without making privation theory say more than it does.

**Controlling sources:** CCC 385; CCC 309–314; St. Augustine, *Confessions* VII.12.18; St. Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* I, q.48, a.1.

**Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:**

~~And if evil is, at its core, the absence of a good that ought to be there, then the cure for evil is the restoration of the very good that is missing. Chapter 1 showed what is missing in every sphere of evil—personal, relational, social: each one is a communion broken. The absent good is love. This follows from the very logic of privation: where love is lacking, evil takes root; where love is given, evil is undone.~~ ++Evil is the privation or corruption of a good that ought to be present: truth in speech, justice in a relationship, health in a body, fidelity in a promise, or right order in a human act. Not every proximate privation can simply be renamed “an absence of love.” Yet love rightly understood wills the genuine good, and charity animates the Christian’s work of restoration. Personal sin, relational harm and social injustice all wound communion; God’s reconciling love in Christ heals that communion at its deepest source and sends us to seek the particular goods that have been denied.++

**Editorial consequence.** The Augustinian argument remains central without becoming circular.

**Why it matters.** The phrase “God is love” means more than a philosophical conclusion about benevolence. In its Christian fullness it names the one God whose eternal life is Father, Son and Holy Spirit.

**Controlling sources:** CCC 32–38; CCC 50; CCC 221; CCC 237; *Dei Verbum* 2–6.

**Source passages with proposed inline redline:**

### CH2-09 · Correct the four-Greek-words simplification and distinguish love from infused charity

**Severity:** Major / linguistic and theological sources · **Control:** DC-07, DC-09, DC-26, DC-28 · **Location:** `chapter_02.md:97–103`

**Issue.** Greek does not divide love neatly into exactly four non-overlapping words, and the familiar eros/philia/storge/agape scheme is not an exhaustive linguistic taxonomy. The paragraph also says “love gives form to all other virtues,” whereas Aquinas’s precise claim concerns the infused theological virtue of charity.

**Why it matters.** A popular teaching device can illustrate distinctions, but it should not become linguistic proof. The charity distinction is essential to the chapter’s natural/supernatural boundary.

**Controlling sources:** CCC 1822–1829; St. Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* I–II, q.26, a.4; II–II, q.23, a.8; DC-27.

**Source passage with proposed inline redline:**

`## Why Love ~~is~~ ++Is++ So Hard to Define`

~~Throughout history, philosophers and saints have offered profound insights—the Greek language's four distinct words for love—eros, philia, storge, and agape—each naming a different facet; Augustine's teaching that love is the ordering principle of all virtue; Aquinas's definition that "to love is to will the good of another" and that love gives "form" to all other virtues. Because love has many different facets—as even the Catechism acknowledges when it says "all the works of perfect Christian virtue spring from love" (CCC 25).~~ ++Philosophers, saints and theologians have illuminated different dimensions of love. St. Augustine explored how rightly ordered love shapes the moral life. **Editorial consequence.** The paragraph keeps its accessible intellectual history without depending on a popular linguistic myth or misattributing charity’s role to every kind of love.

### CH2-12 · Guard divine simplicity, the Trinity, and the economic meaning of sacrifice

**Severity:** Stop-ship / doctrine of God and Trinity · **Control:** DC-02, DC-03, DC-04, DC-27 · **Location:** `chapter_02.md:115`

**Issue.** “Truth is justice is mercy is self-giving love” gestures toward divine simplicity but can read as collapsing distinct concepts or equating every one with the book’s schema. “When that eternal self-gift enters a fallen world, it takes the form of sacrifice” treats sacrifice as if an eternal divine property changes form. The “Holy (wholly)” line is wordplay, not etymology or doctrine.

**Why it matters.** God is one simple, undivided divine essence in three distinct Persons. Eternal divine love involves no suffering, deprivation, cost, or death. Sacrifice in the strict sense belongs to the economy of salvation, where the Incarnate Son freely offers himself in a passible human nature.

**Controlling sources:** CCC 202; CCC 253–267; CCC 599–618; *Lateran IV*, *De fide catholica*; *Council of Florence*, *Cantate Domino*.

**Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:**

~~"Perfect" means these four facets exist as inseparable aspects of God's one nature. Our problem is dividing what God unites: truth without justice, justice without mercy, mercy without sacrifice, sacrifice without truth. In God's Perfect Love, they exist as one harmonious whole. They are "Holy" (wholly).~~ ++“Perfect” does not mean that four components are assembled inside God. **Why it matters.** Gift and covenant are central to the canonical definition. They need enough precision to survive later chapters on authority, marriage, Baptism, justice, and grace.

**Controlling sources:** CCC 1996–2001; CCC 2060–2063; CCC 2093; CCC 2196; CCC 2380–2386.
