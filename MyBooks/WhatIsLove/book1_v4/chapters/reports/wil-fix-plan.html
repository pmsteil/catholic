    Tier A — Doctrine and Accuracy (must fix)

    A1. Chapter 3 — No sacrifice inside the eternal Trinity

    Where: Chapter 3, "The Trinity: Love's Eternal Pattern," the paragraph beginning "This is why love is sacred."

    Now: "Love requires sacrifice (complete self-gift) because the Trinity is eternal self-giving. Love creates covenant (permanent relationship) because the Father, Son, and Spirit are eternally bound in perfect communion. Love binds together (truth, justice, mercy and sacrifice held as one) because the Father's justice, the Son's sacrifice, and the Spirit's mercy are not in tension — in the Trinity, distinct roles exist in eternal, undivided unity."

    Change to (draft): "Love requires sacrifice (complete self-gift) because the Trinity is eternal self-giving — and when that self-giving enters a fallen world, it takes the form of the Cross. Love creates covenant (permanent relationship) because God's fidelity to us flows from the eternal communion of Father, Son, and Spirit — a communion that cannot fail. Love binds together (truth, justice, mercy and sacrifice held as one) because these are not rival attributes inside God — they are one undivided love, which is why the justice, mercy, and sacrifice we meet in salvation history never work against each other."

    Why: There is no sacrifice within the eternal Trinity — sacrifice means cost, and God's inner life has none; the Son's sacrifice is Calvary, not an eternal role. Justice and mercy belong to all three Persons, not one each. Chapter 9 already grounds sacrifice correctly (Gethsemane, Calvary, the seven offerings); this brings Chapter 3 into line with the book's own best chapter. Doctrinal, not stylistic.

    A2. Chapter 3 — The mother participates in the Trinity's self-giving, not "the Father's self-gift to the Son"

    Where: Chapter 3, "God IS Love," first bolded practical example.

    Now: "She's participating in the Father's eternal self-gift to the Son."

    Change to (draft): "She's participating in the eternal self-giving of the Trinity."

    Why: The Father's generation of the Son is by nature, not an act of elective love a creature can join; what we participate in is the divine nature and its love (2 Pet 1:3–4, which this same section quotes). One-phrase fix, invisible to the reader.

    A3. Chapter 11 — Dignity is not beyond reason's reach; the Church says the opposite

    Where: Chapter 11, "The Shape of a Wise Decision," the paragraph on the image of God.

    Now: "This is the one truth we could never have reasoned our way to on our own; it had to be revealed, and it is the first thing lost whenever God is divorced from reality."

    Change to (draft): "This is a truth reason can glimpse — the Church teaches that human dignity 'is fully recognizable even by reason alone' (Dignitas Infinita, 1) — yet whole civilizations have managed not to see it, which is why God did not leave it to our seeing: He revealed it, and it is the first thing lost whenever God is divorced from reality."

    Why: As written, the sentence contradicts the Magisterium (Dignitas Infinita §1) and quietly contradicts Chapter 12's whole project of reasoning to the moral law. The both/and — reason recognizes the fact; revelation supplies its ground and its security — is the Church's actual position and harmonizes the two chapters.

    A4. Chapter 4 — Nazism was not an atheistic regime

    Where: Chapter 4, "Human-Authored Morality Requires Force."

    Now: "This is why atheistic regimes always end in mass murder—the inevitable result of grounding morality in human power rather than divine truth. Nazi fascism: 11 million dead. Soviet communism: 20 million. Chinese communism: 45 million."

    Change to (draft): "This is why the regimes that set out to ground morality in human power rather than divine truth drowned the last century in blood. Soviet communism: 20 million dead. Chinese communism: 45 million. And Nazi Germany — which dethroned the living God to enthrone race and state — 11 million more."

    Why: Nazism was anti-Christian and neo-pagan, but not atheist — and a hostile reader quotes that one label to discredit the whole argument. The revision keeps every ounce of the rhetorical force, removes the factual error, and drops the unguarded "always." This is the single most quotable vulnerability in the book.

    Change to (draft): "An ancient question stirs underneath this claim—it descends from the one Socrates pressed in Plato's dialogue Euthyphro, and in its Christian form it runs: is a thing good because God commands it, or does God command it because it is good?"

    Why: Euthyphro's actual question concerns piety and the gods' love; the command-formulation is the later monotheistic adaptation. "Descends from" is accurate; "asked it first" is not.

    A9. Chapter 3 — "Natural selection creates nothing" is a quotable biology error (revised per your note)

    Where: Chapter 3, "More Than Biology."

    Now: "But the evolutionary story begins in the middle. Natural selection creates nothing—it can only select among things that already exist, so it cannot explain why anything exists to be selected."

    Change to (draft): "But the evolutionary story begins in the middle. Before natural selection can favor anything, there must already be a universe rather than nothing; laws of physics steady enough to build stars and worlds; chemistry rich enough to build living cells; life that can reproduce and pass itself on; and creatures with something at stake. Selection cannot explain why any of that exists—it can only choose among gifts it was handed."

    Why: Standard evolutionary theory holds that mutation-plus-selection cumulatively generates novelty; the slogan will be scored as an error. The metaphysical point — existence itself is the unexplained gift — survives fully, and per your note the list of what must already exist is now explicit: universe, laws of physics, chemistry, life, creatures. The closing phrase hands off cleanly to the "free gift" paragraph that follows it in the chapter.

    A11. Chapter 1 — The Augustine "Confessions" quotation appears to be a paraphrase in quote marks

    Where: Chapter 1, "Where Sin Creates Evil."

    Now: "As St. Augustine recognized, "Evil is not a substance or a nature; it is rather a privation or absence of good. Evil has no positive existence of its own; it exists only as a corruption or distortion of good" (Confessions)."

    Change to (research done, text verified): "As St. Augustine recognized when he went searching for where evil comes from: "So long, therefore, as they are, they are good; therefore whatsoever is, is good. That evil, then, which I sought whence it was, is not any substance; for were it a substance, it should be good" (Confessions VII, 12, 18). Evil has no existence of its own—it is a privation, an absence and corruption of the good."

    Why: The current quotation is a modern summary that appears nowhere in the Confessions. The replacement is Augustine's authentic text, verified word-for-word against the Pilkington translation at New Advent, with the precise citation (Book VII, chapter 12, paragraph 18). The privation language the current version wanted is preserved — now as your own gloss after the real quote, where it belongs.

    A13. Chapters 2 and 3 — Aquinas demonstrated the restlessness; revelation names the rest (revised per your note)

    Where: Chapter 3, the summum bonum paragraph; Chapter 2 has the twin sentence.

    Your question: should one of the two near-identical paragraphs be removed or reworked so back-to-back chapters don't repeat themselves? Yes — and Chapter 2 is the one that keeps it. Chapter 2 is where the summum bonum is introduced and where the Aquinas claim is load-bearing (it sits beside Augustine's restless heart and CCC 27). Chapter 3's version then becomes a brief callback that keeps only what it adds — the means/end insight — and drops the duplicated Aquinas sentence entirely. This also quietly removes the "Philosophers from Plato to Aristotle" line, which absorbs item C3 below.

    Now (Ch. 2): "St. Thomas Aquinas demonstrated what reason discovers and revelation confirms: all human action aims at some good, but only one good can satisfy completely—union with God, who is Love itself."

    Change to (draft): "St. Thomas Aquinas demonstrated what reason discovers: all human action aims at some good, and no created good can satisfy completely—only union with God. And revelation confirms who that God is: Love itself."

    Now (Ch. 3, whole paragraph): "This is what the Church means when it teaches that God is the summum bonum—the highest good.     Change to (draft): "This is what the Church means when it teaches that God is the summum bonum—the highest good, the one Chapter 2 named: the only good that can satisfy the human heart completely. Every other good fails to fulfill us because every other good is a means to the end, which is love—none is the end in itself. When we participate in authentic love, we're not just doing something good—we're participating in the highest good Himself, touching the very source of all goodness and being."

    Why: One fix does three jobs: the reason/revelation split is restored in Chapter 2 ("who IS love" no longer sits inside the "demonstrated" clause), the back-to-back redundancy is gone, and the Plato-to-Aristotle wording problem disappears with the sentence that carried it.

    B3. Chapter 3 — Replace the "only way" sentence, don't garnish it (revised per your note)

    Where: Chapter 3, third observation, final sentences.

    Your doubt, answered honestly: you said you can imagine God being "nothing like us," with no personality, yet still creating us as we are — and that the "only way persons can have intrinsic value" claim may be indefensible and unnecessary. Two things are true at once here. First, the God you're imagining is actually personal in the Church's sense without your noticing: to freely choose to create — to know what you are making and to will that it exist — just is what "personal" means. Personal doesn't mean God has a personality like ours; it means He knows and He loves, which the Church does teach and the book cannot drop (we pray "Our Father," not "Dear Force"). But second, you're right about the sentence: "the only way persons can have intrinsic value..." is a contested philosophical claim, not a Catholic teaching, and it was the single most attacked sentence in the whole review. Cutting it concedes no doctrine — it trades a vulnerable argument for the solid one, and B6 now carries the real weight. So the revision below replaces it instead of qualifying it.

    Now: "An impersonal universe has no regard at all: matter and energy do not care, and abstract principles cannot cherish anyone. The only way persons can have intrinsic value is if the ultimate reality is itself personal — and if God is not just a Person but a communion of Persons — Father, Son, and Spirit, eternally giving and receiving love — then human dignity isn't an accident of evolution or a social agreement. It is a reflection of the deepest truth about reality."

    Change to (draft): "An impersonal universe has no regard at all: matter and energy do not care, and abstract principles cannot cherish anyone. But persons cannot come from a source that neither knows nor wills. A God who freely chose to create us—who knew what He was making and willed that we exist—is already personal in the only sense that matters: He knows, and He loves. And revelation will shortly tell us something more—that this God is not a solitary Person but a communion of Persons, eternally giving and receiving love—which means human dignity isn't an accident of evolution or a social agreement. It is a reflection of the deepest truth about reality."

    Why: Drops the contested "only way" claim, keeps the reason-alone method honest (the Trinity clause is now marked as revelation's forward glance), and grounds personhood where it is actually safe: in the act of creating itself.

    B6. Chapter 12 — Give it the treasure Chapter 3 doesn't have: God's love creates goodness

    Where: Chapter 12, in the space recovered by B5 (or within "Why Only God Can Ground Love").

    Add (draft): "And here St. Thomas Aquinas hands us one more treasure, in a single sentence: our love 'is not the cause of the goodness of things... whereas the love of God infuses and creates goodness' (Summa Theologica I, q.20, a.2). Read that again. Your love responds to goodness—you love your child because she is precious. God's love creates goodness—your child is precious because His love called her into being and holds her in being right now. Her dignity is truly her own, woven into what she is: rational, free, made for communion with Him. And it is truly a gift, because what she is, is His gift. This is why the Church can teach that every person's dignity 'is not conferred subsequently... it is prior to any recognition, and it cannot be lost' (Dignitas Infinita, 15). No one bestowed it on her—not her parents, not the state, not even her own achievements—and so no one can revoke it. The Love that grounds her worth is not anyone's opinion of her. It is the creative Love that is the reason there is a her at all."

    Why: This is the tradition's own answer to the hardest question anyone can ask about human dignity — and it is pastoral, not technical: a homily line, not a seminar. It resolves the review's number-one finding, differentiates Chapter 12 from Chapter 3, and it is verified word-for-word against New Advent's Summa text and the Vatican's Dignitas Infinita.
