    Phase 1–2 · The Skeptics' Rulings

    Rulings below are failures only. Where a first-run fix now answers an attack, it is not listed.

    Skeptic 1 — The non-theistic moral realist

    1.1 The epistemic-norms objection is still unanswered — and it is now the realist's best weapon. The book's case that obligation requires a person runs through Chapter 3's strongest-form answer ("mathematics describes, but morality commands... A command requires a voice") and Chapter 12's gift-requires-a-Giver movement. The realist's second exchange: norms bind without being uttered. "You ought to believe what your evidence supports" obligates every thinker, and no one posits an Epistemic Lawgiver. If some oughts bind without a voice, "a law requires a lawgiver" fails as a general principle, and the moral case must show why moral oughts differ. The text that would have to answer this — Chapter 12, the Atheist's Dilemma — answers the error theorist and the Kantian but not this. Ruling: UNANSWERED. The succinct answer is priced at D-1; it is two sentences and it closes the realist's last standing door.

    1.2 The impersonal analogies still testify for the other side — but the book now owns them. Gravity, mathematics, the piano, the fish in water: each illustrates normativity without a commander. The applied C12-1 explicitly converts them into setup ("Gravity binds bodies... neither ever asked our consent. But look at what this law holds out to us"). One instance escaped the conversion: Chapter 6, "Truth and Authority" — "it's like submitting to the 'authority' of gravity or mathematics: you're not being oppressed, you're aligning yourself with reality." That sentence, standing alone, is the realist's own picture of moral life. Ruling: DAMAGED (lightly). Cheapest repair: let Chapter 6's sentence echo C12-1's move in half a clause ("—though gravity, unlike love, never asked your consent"), or leave it and let Chapter 12 do the work. Recorded; author's call.

    Skeptic 2 — The evolutionary debunker

    2.1 The debunker's mirror survives one exchange, not two. Chapter 12's applied insertion ("the brain telling that story was built by the same blind process he says cannot be trusted about right and wrong") is the right move, but the debunker's reply is standard: the asymmetry is a finding, not a choice — reasoning was selected for truth-tracking (a predator mis-modeled is a predator met), moral emotion for cooperation, which needs no truth behind it. The text as it stands loses that exchange. Ruling: DAMAGED. The two-sentence closure is priced at D-2: the debunker's own norms of reasoning are oughts, not perceptions, so his asymmetry saws off the branch his argument sits on.

    Skeptic 5 — The rigorous Thomist (friendly fire)

    5.1 Chapter 10 reads the Son's economic obedience as the eternal structure of the Trinity. "Unity Requires Submission to the Authority of His Perfect Love":

    The Son does only what He sees the Father doing (John 5:19) and came "not to do my own will, but the will of him who sent me" (John 6:38); the Spirit speaks not on His own authority... The eternal communion of God is, in its very structure, self-gift ordered in love.

    John 6:38 is the Incarnate Son's human obedience (Constantinople III: two wills in Christ, one will in God). Presented as the inner structure of the eternal communion, the passage shadows eternal functional subordination — a live error a sharp seminarian will name. The paragraph's actual claim (the divine communion is self-gift, and the missions reveal it) is sound; it needs one clause marking that the obedience texts show in time what the Son is in eternity — the One who eternally receives Himself from the Father — not a submission of one divine will to another, since God has one will (the paragraph itself says "one will" three sentences earlier, which makes the repair natural). Ruling: DAMAGED; Tier A precision, surgical fix.

    5.2 "We physically consume Christ's Body" overshoots Trent. Chapter 10, the Body paragraph of the Eucharist section: "We physically consume Christ's Body and Blood—not spiritual metaphor but physical reality." The Church's words are truly, really, substantially — which the same chapter uses correctly forty lines earlier. "Physically" invites the Capharnaite reading Christ Himself corrected, and hands an interlocutor John 6:63 to quote back. Swap one word: "really consume... not spiritual metaphor but substantial reality." Ruling: DAMAGED; Tier A precision, one word.

    Question from your reviewer

    §5.3 — The 'only way' sentence is inside the C3-2 insertion you approved yesterday. May I treat it as open to the one-word softening ('the only way we can see how'), or is its full strength deliberate? Aquinas's caution that reason shows the Trinity's fittingness rather than proving it (ST I, q.32, a.1) is the only reason I ask.

    5.3 The approved Trinity insertion (C3-2) claims demonstration where Aquinas claims fittingness. Chapter 3: "The Trinity is not a puzzle bolted onto the Gospel; it is the only way 'God IS love' can be true from all eternity." The dilemma (eternal Beloved, or love God became) skips the classical third option: God eternally loving His own infinite goodness (ST I, q.20, a.1) — and Aquinas explicitly warns that reason cannot demonstrate the Trinity, only show its fittingness once revealed (ST I, q.32, a.1). Richard of St. Victor's argument — which this insertion is — lives there. The pastoral force survives the honest form entirely: either soften one word ("it is the only way we can see how 'God IS love' can be true from all eternity"), or add the clause that answers the third option ("a love that never leaves the self is self-regard, not gift"). This touches an approved insertion, so it is flagged as a refinement, not a reversal. Ruling: DAMAGED (friendly fire); Tier B.

    The two chosen skeptics

    6.1 The internal problem-of-evil philosopher — the remainder is quantity, and the book has chosen honesty over solution. The applied fixes closed his quotable targets (the "shakes his fist" restriction, the rebuilt natural-evil note). What remains is the distribution objection — one fewer childhood cancer would not have abolished freedom — which no book at this register can solve and Chapter 2's note honestly declines to. Ruling: DAMAGED by nature of the problem; Accepted, with its one-sentence answer recorded at D-4.

    6.2 The non-Christian classical theist still finds no warrant addressed to him. He grants a personal, transcendent, perfect God; Chapter 6's case for the Church argues from premises (apostolic authority, the New Testament record) he does not share, and the book nowhere argues the Resurrection as history — 1 Corinthians 15:14 is cited for its stakes, never for its evidence. For the book's stated audience this is the right scope. Ruling: UNANSWERED, Accepted-as-is; the zero-cost door is a Further Study pointer, priced at D-5.

    Phase 3 · The Accuracy Sweep

    3.1 Chapter 7 — the Aquinas citation under "integral acts" is wrong in range and term. "Aquinas also treats justice's integral acts — restitution, truth-telling, gratitude, honor, and reconciliation — as essential to what the virtue actually requires (ST II-II, qq.61–80)." Restitution is q.62; but truth-telling is q.109, gratitude qq.106–107, honor qq.102–103 — all outside qq.61–80 — and "integral parts" is Aquinas's name for something else (do good, avoid evil; q.79). These are the virtues annexed to justice (its potential parts). Fix: "...treats restitution, truth-telling, gratitude, and honor as belonging to justice's own household — the virtues annexed to it (ST II-II, qq.58–122)." Tier A: it is precisely the kind of citation a hostile reader checks.

    3.2 Chapter 11 — "Summa Theologica" appears once against the book's standard "Summa Theologiae." "As Aquinas explains in the Summa Theologica (II-II, q.26)" — the same chapter's Further Study uses Theologiae. One word. Tier A (consistency).

    3.3 Chapter 15 — "Psychologists call it post-abortion syndrome" is the sweep's most quotable hostage. The term is real and used by some clinicians and post-abortion ministries, but the professional bodies a hostile reviewer will cite (APA, ACOG) explicitly decline to recognize it as a diagnosis — so the sentence as written hands them the rebuttal. The paragraph's truth does not depend on the label. Fix, invisible to tone: "Some clinicians have named it post-abortion syndrome; the Church calls it what it is—a wound to the soul..." Tier A.

    3.4 Chapter 15 — "Nothing explains the soldier who falls on the grenade" contradicts its own row. The same cell has just said every apparent sacrifice "must be reduced to something else: self-fulfillment, reciprocal advantage, or the survival of one's genes" — and the debunker's literature does offer exactly such reductions for the grenade case (kin-instinct misfire among battle brothers). What the reductions cannot do is make the act admirable — obligatory to honor, the highest thing a man can do. Point the sentence there: "And no reduction can say why we are right to honor the soldier who falls on the grenade... as the highest thing a man can do rather than a misfire." Tier B.

    3.5 Chapter 9 — "the apostolic writer of Hebrews — writing while the Temple still stood" states a contested dating as fact. Pre-70 dating is well defended but not settled, and Hebrews argues from the tabernacle, not Herod's Temple. The argument loses nothing hedged: "writing, very likely, while the Temple still stood." Tier B.

    3.6 Chapter 13 — More's epitaph, pedant-proofed. "The King's good servant, but God's first": the earliest report (the Paris Newsletter) has "and God's first." The popular form is defensible; "and" is unassailable and reads just as well. Tier C, one word, author's call.

    3.7 Chapter 9 — "The first canonized saint of the New Covenant was a thief." Chapter 11 guards the same point correctly ("canonized by Christ Himself"); Chapter 9's version drops the guard, and Dismas has never been formally canonized. Echo Chapter 11's phrasing. Tier C.

    3.8 Chapter 4, footnote 2 — a refinement to an approved decision, offered once. The figures were author-decided and each is at or below the scholarly range. One packaging risk remains: "six million Jews and some five million other victims" is the arithmetic of the popularized "eleven million," and USHMM itself has publicly cautioned against that packaged figure (its origin is attributed to Wiesenthal, not to research). The armor-plated form names the categories, whose documented sum runs higher than five million (Soviet POWs ~3.3M; non-Jewish Polish civilians ~1.8M; Serbs, Roma, the disabled, and others beyond that). This is a footnote-only refinement of an approved fix — recorded here once; if declined, it joins the Accepted list and stays closed. Tier C.

    Phase 4 · The Whole-Book Re-Rule

    4.1 Chapter 9 — a forward reference wearing a backward word. The rich-young-man passage: "summed up, in the very words this book has already called the Shema's neighbor-love clause." The naming happens sixteen paragraphs later in the same chapter — and when it does, it is careful to say the Shema joined to love of neighbor (Leviticus 19:18 is not in the Shema itself), a care this shorthand loses. Fix both at once: "summed up in the words this chapter will shortly join to the Shema: 'You shall love your neighbor as yourself.'" Tier B.

    4.2 Chapter 17 repeats its own signature sentence nearly verbatim. "What IS the Gospel?": "Not faith plus love, as though love were a second requirement. Not faith and then love, as though love were the receipt." And "Living the Response": "not faith plus love, as though love were a second requirement, and not faith and then love, as though love were the receipt." The coinage is excellent once. Keep the first (where the argument is made), vary the second ("one living thing — as Chapter 9 showed and this chapter has argued from its first page") or let the second simply say "faith working through love — one living thing." Tier B.

    Phase 5 · The Strongest Objection, and the Verdict

    The strongest objection the book cannot yet answer, as the critic would write it:

    I grant you everything you think I must deny. Betrayal is really wrong; the child's dignity is really there; your four pillars name real, binding features of the moral landscape. I deny only your last step — that binding requires a Binder. You say a command requires a voice; but most of what binds us was never commanded. The norms of evidence obligate every mind and no one posits an Epistemic Lawgiver; you yourself submit to the "authority" of mathematics, your own analogy, which asked no one's consent and issued no decree. So the moral law may be what the logical law is: a standing fact about reality, not a speech act awaiting a speaker. You will answer that facts cannot oblige persons — but that is the very point in dispute, asserted where it needs to be argued. Both of us stop somewhere brute: you at a Person whose nature is the Good, I at the Good itself. Until you show me why your stopping point is better than mine — not merely warmer — you have described the moral life beautifully and grounded it no more firmly than I have.

    The book's materials for answering this exist (the guilt-and-forgiveness asymmetry; C12-1's gift-shape; the "Theism is simpler" paragraph) but are never assembled against this exact opponent. D-1 prices the assembly at two sentences.

    The honest verdict. Would a genuinely open-minded, intelligent skeptic who reads this book carefully be moved? Moved, yes; persuaded, no — and the book, to its credit, now says as much itself. After the applied run, the text claims a verdict rather than a theorem, a witness rather than a proof, reason-to-the-threshold with revelation supplying the name — and a skeptic reading carefully will notice that the concessions are made before he demands them, which is the single most disarming quality an apologetic can have. He will not leave a theist. He will leave knowing that the strongest form of his own position has been stated by his opponent, met at least one exchange deep, and answered with a vision whose internal coherence he cannot fault — only its premises. For a book whose actual audience is the catechist and the pew, that is not a limitation; it is the design working.
