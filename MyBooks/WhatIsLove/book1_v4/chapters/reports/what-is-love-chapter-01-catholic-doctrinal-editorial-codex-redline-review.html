    Chapter 1 · The Problem of Evil

    Chapter assessment

    Chapter 1 has a strong governing intuition: evil is parasitic upon the good, sin wounds communion, and neither personal conversion nor social repair can be detached from God. Its Augustinian privation account, the long quotation from CCC 309, and the insistence that the Church faces evil without flinching give the chapter a sound Catholic center. The largest doctrinal defect is the claim that freedom requires an equal capacity to choose evil. Catholic teaching says instead that the possibility of sin belongs to freedom that has not yet adhered definitively to God, while freedom becomes more perfect as it chooses the good. A second cluster reduces sin to taking a perceived good for oneself at another’s expense, which excludes interior sins, omissions, Godward sins, and sins against oneself. A third cluster weakens the argument through speculative motives, racialized single-cause analysis, deterministic family outcomes, and false binaries involving atheists, law, education, and science. The chapter can retain its urgency, pro-life conviction, Catholic anthropology, and call to conversion while becoming more doctrinally complete, evidentially disciplined, and pastorally credible.

    CH1-01 · Distinguish moral evil from physical evil at the chapter’s outset

    Severity: Major / scope · Control: DC-05, DC-19, DC-26 · Location: chapter_01.md:17–19

    Issue. “When love is absent” is too narrow to introduce the entire problem of evil. Catholic teaching distinguishes moral evil caused by the abuse of created freedom from physical evil within a creation still “in a state of journeying.” The chapter later defers natural evil to Chapter 2, but the reader needs that scope before the opening moral examples.

    Why it matters. The title and opening quotations promise the full Christian problem of evil. Naming the chapter’s principal focus prevents the reader from mistaking an account of moral evil for an exhaustive theodicy.

    Controlling sources: CCC 309–314, 324.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    ## Why we need to understand love

    Before we can understand love, we must confront what happens when love is absent. Before we can understand love, we must confront what evil does to communion. Moral evil arises from the abuse of creaturely freedom; physical or natural evil includes suffering, decay and death within a creation still journeying toward its perfection. This chapter begins with moral evil, while Chapter 2 returns explicitly to natural suffering.

    Editorial consequence. The chapter states honestly what it will and will not establish before presenting contested moral cases.

    CH1-02 · State the evil of abortion together with agency, culpability, mercy, and hope

    Severity: Major / pastoral · Control: DC-10, DC-25, DC-26 · Location: chapter_01.md:21

    Issue. The vignette asserts a woman’s motives and says “every” doctor, friend, and institution confirmed the decision. It names the cultural rationalization but does not name the unborn child, pressures that can diminish culpability, repentance, sacramental forgiveness, or healing.

    Why it matters. Catholic moral clarity does not require flattening personal responsibility. Evangelium Vitae itself holds together the grave wrong, external pressures, honest repentance, forgiveness, peace, and competent help.

    Controlling sources: Evangelium Vitae 58–59, 99; CCC 1735; CCC 2270–2275.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    A young woman aborts her third child. She's exhausted, already stretched thin between two kids and a job that barely covers rent. She loves her children—that's exactly why she doesn't want to bring another life into the chaos she already can't manage. She's not cruel. She genuinely believes this is the good choice. Every doctor, every friend, every institution around her has confirmed it. She has been told, from every direction, that this is healthcare. That this is responsible. That this is love. A young woman, exhausted and already stretched thin between two children and a job that barely covers rent, undergoes an abortion. She may believe she is protecting the children she already has, especially if doctors, friends, or institutions present abortion as health care, responsibility, and compassion. The act ends the life of her unborn child, but moral clarity must remain joined to mercy: fear, pressure, ignorance, and diminished freedom can affect personal culpability, and no one is beyond repentance, forgiveness, and healing in Christ.

    Editorial consequence. The passage becomes more Catholic, not less direct: it protects unborn life while refusing to abandon the woman to condemnation or despair.

    CH1-03 · Replace the counselor vignette’s sweeping professional accusation with a precise moral conflict

    Severity: Major / pastoral and empirical · Control: DC-24, DC-25, DC-26, DC-28 · Location: chapter_01.md:23

    Issue. The paragraph attributes one framework to “an entire generation of professionals,” assumes that secrecy always “dismantles” the parent-child bond, and omits the need to assess self-harm risk, family safety, the child’s embodied identity, and appropriate clinical care. The counselor’s sex is also irrelevant to the example.

    Why it matters. Catholic anthropology must be expressed with respect for every person’s dignity and with enough precision to protect a distressed child. Parent involvement is normally indispensable, but a responsible account must also recognize genuine safety concerns rather than making every case identical.

    Controlling sources: CCC 355–368; Dignitas Infinita 55–60; DC-25’s safety and competent-help requirements.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    A school counselor meets a thirteen-year-old girl who is miserable, isolated, and hinting at self-harm. The counselor has been trained that gender confusion is real, that affirmation saves lives, and that unsupportive parents are a danger—not a resource. He genuinely believes he is protecting this child. So he affirms the girl's new identity and keeps it from her parents. He is not malicious. He is following a framework that has convinced an entire generation of professionals that dismantling the parent-child bond is compassion. The child goes home with a secret her family isn't allowed to share. A school counselor meets a thirteen-year-old girl who is miserable, isolated, distressed about her sex, and hinting at self-harm. Believing immediate social affirmation to be protective, the counselor adopts a new name and identity for her at school and withholds this from her parents. Even when the intention is compassionate, secrecy can rupture family trust and foreclose careful assessment, appropriate parental involvement, and competent care. The child needs immediate protection from self-harm, respect for her inviolable dignity and truthful care for her as a unity of body and soul; unless disclosure would expose her to a credible danger, her parents should be treated as responsible partners rather than presumptive enemies.

    Editorial consequence. The example identifies the disputed act and the goods at stake without diagnosing a profession or sacrificing the child’s safety to a slogan.

    CH1-04 · Hold personal responsibility and structural injustice together without racialized single-cause analysis

    Severity: Major / social doctrine · Control: DC-15, DC-25, DC-26, DC-28 · Location: chapter_01.md:25

    Issue. The organizer vignette uses Black children as the vehicle for a disputed causal argument, implies that systemic racism and father absence are competing explanations, and attributes family patterns to public incentives without establishing causation. “Kids with no future” is also deterministic.

    Why it matters. The Catechism rejects the false choice between conversion of hearts and reform of unjust structures. A bishop-ready passage should not make a racial group carry a political diagnosis that the cited household statistics cannot prove.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1886–1889; CCC 1869; Gaudium et Spes 25–32.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    A community organizer has watched her neighborhood hollowed out for decades—unemployment, bad schools, kids with no future. She has seen real racism. She has buried real victims. When she points to systemic oppression as the cause, she isn't making excuses—she's fighting for people she loves within the framework she knows. But that framework never names fatherlessness. It never asks why 47 to 50 percent of Black children are growing up without a father in the home.<sup>1</sup> It never challenges the policies that made single motherhood financially rational and marriage economically punishing. She is fighting hard for her community—with a map that leaves out half the terrain. A community organizer has watched families in her neighborhood bear unemployment, unsafe streets, weak schools, racial injustice, and family breakdown. She is right to confront unjust structures, but any account remains incomplete if it ignores father absence, unstable relationships, personal choices, or public incentives that can unintentionally weaken family life. A truthful analysis holds personal responsibility and structural injustice together instead of forcing every cause into one political explanation.

    Editorial consequence. The paragraph now teaches Catholic social doctrine and avoids asking one statistic to establish motive, policy effect, and racial causation.

    CH1-05 · Compress the repeated culture-war catalogue into the chapter’s actual thesis

    Severity: Major / structure and rhetoric · Control: DC-25, DC-26, DC-29 · Location: chapter_01.md:27–33

    Issue. “These aren’t statistics” does not describe the preceding vignettes, and the next section immediately repeats all three examples in more combative language. The repetition adds heat but no new premise.

    Why it matters. The chapter’s strongest question concerns how sincere concern becomes morally misdirected. A concise transition lets the reader evaluate that proposition instead of asking the opening examples to function as partisan credentials.

    Source paragraphs with proposed inline redline:

    These aren't statistics—they're souls crying out in the darkness. Truth has become flimsy and negotiable and a matter of personal preference. These examples concern persons, not abstractions. They show how sincere concern can be misdirected when truth is treated as negotiable.

    The rampant evil in our world demands we ask: "What has gone so terribly wrong with humanity?" The moral evil in our world forces a deeper question: “What has gone wrong in the human heart and in the communities its choices shape?”

    ## The Crisis That Forces the Question

    The lies have become so extreme that people are waking up. Maybe that's you. Maybe you watched abortion get rebranded as "healthcare" while the body count climbed past a million a year.<sup>2</sup> Maybe you saw a school counselor secretly affirm your child's "gender identity" while your parental rights vanished. Maybe you watched fatherlessness destroy entire communities while leaders blamed the wrong cause. You looked at what we're being told and thought: something is deeply wrong here. Maybe you're searching for what's real and true. Perhaps one of these controversies woke you to a deeper problem: actions defended as compassion can end an unborn life, obscure the truth about the human body, rupture family trust, or ignore the interaction of personal choices and unjust structures. If so, you may be searching for a standard of good firmer than political loyalty, professional consensus, or personal preference.

    Editorial consequence. The opening becomes cumulative rather than repetitive, and the chapter arrives at its theological question sooner.

    CH1-06 · State the theological crisis without denying natural moral knowledge or caricaturing salvation

    Severity: Major / doctrine and argument · Control: DC-08, DC-09, DC-15, DC-19, DC-26 · Location: chapter_01.md:35–37

    Issue. The current text says that without explicitly involving God there is “no basis” for morality or dignity, even though the chapter will appeal to natural law knowable by reason. “Accept Jesus and go to heaven” caricatures Christian preaching, while “wreckage is immediate” overstates causation.

    Why it matters. Catholic teaching distinguishes humanity’s real natural knowledge from its ultimate ground in God. It also presents salvation as grace, conversion, sacramental life, discipleship, and charity—not as a slogan to be mocked.

    Controlling sources: CCC 27–49; CCC 1954–1960; CCC 1987–2029; CCC 1886–1889.

    Source paragraphs with proposed inline redline:

    The problem is we have disconnected society from God Himself. Without involving our Creator, we have no objective standard for truth, no basis for morality and no ground for human dignity. At the deepest level, the crisis is our attempt to order personal and social life without reference to God. Human reason can know real moral truths, including the natural law, but excluding the Creator obscures their ultimate ground and the full vocation of the human person.

    The crisis isn't just political or cultural—it's theological. We preach "accept Jesus and go to heaven" while the world burns. We emphasize individual salvation while families are self-destructing. When we abandon God's laws, the wreckage is immediate: death disguised as compassion, broken families, confused children, despair wearing the mask of freedom. "The wages of sin is death" (Romans 6:23). So true. The crisis is not only political or cultural—it is theological. Christian preaching is distorted when salvation is reduced to a private decision detached from conversion, discipleship, sacramental life, family, and neighbor. God’s law reveals the path of life; when persons and institutions reject the goods it protects, sin wounds both hearts and communities. “The wages of sin is death” (Romans 6:23).

    Editorial consequence. The passage makes the positive Catholic case without contradicting the natural-law section that follows.

    CH1-07 · Present the playground scene as an illustration of natural law, not proof of an unformed conscience

    Severity: Major / precision · Control: DC-10, DC-26 · Location: chapter_01.md:41–43

    Issue. “We all know better” and “not because someone taught her” treat a child’s reaction as proof that conscience is infallible and unformed by family or culture. Natural law is universal, but conscience must be educated and can err.

    Why it matters. A vivid scene can illustrate natural law without carrying more philosophical or developmental weight than it can bear.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1776–1802; CCC 1954–1960; Gaudium et Spes 16.

    Source paragraphs with proposed inline redline:

    Deep down, we all know better. Take a five-year-old to a playground. When one child pushes another off the swing, she doesn't pause to consider gender, race or political ideology. Her whole being erupts: "You're mean!" Even before children can explain moral theory, they can appeal to fairness. Take a five-year-old to a playground. When one child pushes another off the swing, another may erupt: “That’s mean!”

    She knows—not because someone taught her, but because it's written in her heart. This reflects the natural law written on every human heart (Romans 2:15). As Vatican II teaches, deep within our conscience we discover a law we have not laid upon ourselves but must obey (Gaudium et Spes 16). The child has learned words and expectations from others, so the scene is an illustration, not a proof. Yet the spontaneous appeal to a good that should govern both children reflects what Saint Paul calls the law written on the human heart (Romans 2:15). Vatican II teaches that deep within conscience we discover a law we did not place upon ourselves and are bound to obey; the same Church also teaches that conscience must be formed and can err (Gaudium et Spes 16; CCC 1783–1794).

    Editorial consequence. The example remains memorable while the doctrine of conscience becomes complete enough for pastoral use.

    CH1-08 · Reduce anthropological universals to what the evidence can establish

    Severity: Major / empirical reasoning · Control: DC-26, DC-28 · Location: chapter_01.md:45

    Issue. The paragraph says every society affirms the same list and the notes say the same prohibitions recur in “every studied culture.” The cited works do not justify that exact universal statement, and cultural convergence would corroborate rather than prove natural law.

    Why it matters. The theological argument does not need an empirical overclaim. It becomes stronger when cultural evidence is identified as limited corroboration rather than the foundation of doctrine.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    Across every culture, anthropologists find consistent moral prohibitions: killing the innocent, breaking trust, exploiting the vulnerable, stealing, violating family stability. Every society affirms honesty, loyalty, fairness, and keeping one's word.<sup>3</sup> These aren't "social constructs"—they reflect universal human nature made in God's image. As Saint Paul teaches, "what the law requires is written on their hearts" (Romans 2:15).<sup>4</sup> Human cultures differ greatly in moral language and practice, yet recurring judgments about unjust killing, trust, care, reciprocity, family, and property are consistent with shared moral capacities. Such convergence does not by itself prove natural law, and cultures often violate the goods they recognize; the Church’s claim rests more deeply on reason, revelation, and a common human nature made in God’s image.

    Editorial consequence. Two vulnerable footnotes and a repeated quotation of Romans 2:15 can be removed without weakening the doctrine.

    CH1-09 · Distinguish what reason infers from what revelation identifies in Christ

    Severity: Major / revelation · Control: DC-19, DC-20, DC-26 · Location: chapter_01.md:47

    Issue. The paragraph moves directly from creation, conscience, and covenant to the historical identity of Christ as though the entire conclusion arose from the same kind of evidence. Covenant and Christ are known through revelation, while reason can infer a divine source of order.

    Why it matters. The distinction prevents a circular apologetic and preserves Christ as the fullness of revelation rather than the final term of an unstated natural-law syllogism.

    Controlling sources: CCC 31–38; CCC 65–67; Dei Verbum 2–6.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    This confirms what the Church affirms: creation, conscience, and covenant all point to one moral Author.<sup>5</sup> In fact, there is one Author of all truth—the one who said, "I am the way, and the truth, and the life" (John 14:6). Creation and conscience point beyond themselves toward a divine source of moral order. Revelation goes further: the God who establishes covenant with his people has made himself definitively known in Jesus Christ, who said, “I am the way, and the truth, and the life” (John 14:6).

    Editorial consequence. Natural theology and revelation now cooperate without being collapsed.

    CH1-10 · Preserve ignorance, wounded freedom, and degrees of culpability in the account of sin

    Severity: Major / moral doctrine · Control: DC-10, DC-25, DC-26 · Location: chapter_01.md:49

    Issue. The present sentence defines sin as rebellion against what a person already knows is right. Some sins involve such knowing refusal, but ignorance, inadvertence, fear, habit, disordered attachment, and social or psychological factors can diminish responsibility.

    Why it matters. Objective evil and subjective culpability are distinct. Failing to say so can form consciences badly and contradict the Catechism.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1735; CCC 1790–1794; CCC 1854–1864.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    Yet here is the tragedy: We have deep intuitions about what's good and true, yet we still choose evil. This is the mystery of sin—not mere ignorance, but a heart that rebels against what it knows is right. Yet here is the tragedy: Conscience witnesses to the good, but ignorance, disordered desire, fear, habit, social pressure, and deliberate self-assertion can lead us away from it. Sin is not reducible to ignorance; at its gravest, it includes a knowing and freely chosen refusal of what God reveals to be good.

    Editorial consequence. The chapter can condemn moral evil without presuming full knowledge and consent in every person it describes.

    CH1-11 · Correct the personal, relational, and social taxonomy

    Severity: Major / moral and pastoral precision · Control: DC-10, DC-15, DC-23, DC-26 · Location: chapter_01.md:55–63

    Issue. “Anger” is not identical to the capital vice of wrath, “divorce” is not one undifferentiated moral act, and the conclusion changes a section about moral evil into a claim about every form of evil. The social category should also make clear that personal sins create and sustain structures.

    Why it matters. The three-sphere structure is useful if its examples do not erase legitimate anger, separation for safety, declarations of nullity, or the distinction between moral and physical evil.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1866, 1869; CCC 2382–2386; CCC 1886–1889.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    The Church recognizes that sin has both personal and social dimensions (CCC 1869, 1440). Our sinful choices create and sustain moral evil in three spheres:

    Personal Evil: Individual choices to disobey God—pride, lust, greed, envy, gluttony, anger wrath and sloth—corrupt our own souls and capacity for goodness.

    Relational Evil: The breakdown of covenant bonds—adultery, divorce, abandonment, betrayal, exploitation and other unjust choices that fracture family and friendship—destroys our families and friendships.

    Social Evil: Systemic injustices—slavery, trafficking, economic exploitation, political corruption, cultural lies that deny human dignity—corrupt our communities and nations and can in turn make virtuous action more difficult.

    Every form of evil assaults what we're made for—and sin is its root. Every form of moral evil assaults what we are made for; sin is the root of that moral disorder, while physical evil requires the wider account of creation and providence.

    Editorial consequence. The chapter retains its three-sphere framework without turning it into an exhaustive classification of evil.

    CH1-12 · Replace the single-cause answer to divine permission with the Church’s whole account

    Severity: Stop-ship / doctrine · Control: DC-05, DC-12, DC-19, DC-26 · Location: chapter_01.md:65–69

    Issue. “God permits evil because He will not create slaves” presents free will as the complete reason for every evil. It does not address physical evil, creation’s unfinished state, providence, or the Catechism’s warning that no quick answer suffices. “Freedom is the entire point” also displaces beatitude and communion with God as the human end.

    Why it matters. The chapter begins by quoting CCC 309’s whole-faith answer and then replaces it with a slogan. The revision should let created freedom explain part of moral evil without making it an exhaustive theodicy.

    Controlling sources: CCC 309–314, 324; CCC 1730–1738.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    Here you might rightly ask: "If God is all-good and all-powerful, why does He allow so much evil?" This is the oldest among the oldest questions in theology—and the Church does not flinch from it.

    God permits evil because He will not create slaves. The Catechism is direct: "God willed that man should be 'left in the hand of his own counsel,' so that he might of his own accord seek his Creator and freely attain his full and blessed perfection by cleaving to him" (CCC 1730, citing Sirach 15:14). Freedom is not a bug in God's design—it is the entire point. Love that is coerced is not love. Obedience extracted by force is not virtue. God wanted sons and daughters, not robots. Created freedom explains part of moral evil, but no single argument exhausts the mystery. God is never the cause of moral evil; he permits rational creatures to act freely and can bring good even from their sins. The Catechism also teaches that creation is still “in a state of journeying,” in which physical good exists alongside physical evil until creation reaches its perfection. Human freedom is ordered toward our full and blessed perfection in God: “God willed that man should be ‘left in the hand of his own counsel,’ so that he might of his own accord seek his Creator” (CCC 1730, citing Sirach 15:14). God desires a free response of love, not coerced behavior—but CCC 309 is right that only Christian faith as a whole answers the question of evil.

    Editorial consequence. The section now matches the Catechism quotation that opens the chapter instead of quietly contradicting it.

    CH1-13 · Correct the claim that freedom requires the power to choose evil

    Severity: Stop-ship / doctrine · Control: DC-06, DC-12, DC-26 · Location: chapter_01.md:71–73

    Issue. “There is no freedom to choose good without the freedom to choose evil” is false as a universal Catholic account. God, Christ, and the blessed are perfectly free without being able to sin. The ability to sin is an abuse possible to finite freedom not yet definitively united to its ultimate good, not a perfection constitutive of love. The account of heaven then implies that glory cannot be given until earned by a daily path.

    Why it matters. This is the chapter’s central doctrinal error and a likely episcopal objection. It affects freedom, grace, heaven, merit, and the logic of the whole book.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1731–1733; CCC 1996–2001; CCC 1023–1029.

    Source paragraphs with proposed inline redline:

    And here is the logic at the heart of freedom: There is no freedom to choose good without the freedom to choose evil. You cannot have one without the other. The moment God creates a being capable of genuine love, He creates a being capable of genuine rejection. To demand a world without evil is to demand a world without freedom—which is to demand a world without love. And here is the logic at the heart of created freedom: During our earthly pilgrimage, freedom that has not yet adhered definitively to God can choose between good and evil. But the ability to sin is not freedom’s perfection; it is its possible abuse. “The more one does what is good, the freer one becomes,” because true freedom reaches its perfection in God and in what is good and just (CCC 1731–1733). Genuine love requires a free response, but it does not require evil as an equal or everlasting option.

    And if you are already thinking of heaven—where the saints are perfectly free and sin no more—you have seen the point exactly. The freedom of the blessed is not freedom taken away but freedom fulfilled: a heart so filled with God that it no longer wants anything less. That fullness cannot be installed at the start, because it is the fruit of a love freely given and freely kept, day after day, until it is complete. The road of freedom must be walked for the arrival to be an arrival. Heaven makes the distinction clear: the saints are perfectly free and sin no more because, in the beatific vision, their freedom is fulfilled in the Good they see face to face. During this life we do not yet possess that vision; grace precedes, heals, and enables our free cooperation as God leads us toward glory. That glory is his gift, not a perfection we manufacture or earn by walking far enough.

    Editorial consequence. The book’s foundational freedom principle becomes Catholic rather than libertarian and remains fully compatible with real consent.

    CH1-14 · Replace the false atheist dilemma with an argument that addresses real alternatives

    Severity: Major / apologetic reasoning · Control: DC-22, DC-26 · Location: chapter_01.md:75

    Issue. The paragraph says an atheist must either borrow a moral order they deny or reduce morality to opinion. Non-theistic moral realism and other objective secular accounts are omitted, and the rhetoric confuses whether a person can know moral truth with what ultimately grounds that truth.

    Why it matters. A false dilemma invites an informed reader to dismiss the chapter before it reaches its stronger metaphysical claim. Catholic teaching does not deny that nonbelievers can know and practice genuine moral good.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    The very fact that you recognize evil bears witness to the existence of good. Evil has no substance of its own—it is the absence of good, the corruption of what should be. You cannot have darkness without light. You cannot call something evil unless you already know what good it is missing. The atheist who rages against evil borrows from a moral order he claims doesn't exist or grounds morality in personal opinion or some other "perceived good". The very fact that we recognize evil bears witness to a good that ought to be present. Evil has no substance of its own; it is the privation or corruption of a due good. People who reject belief in God can still recognize moral truth, do genuine good, and propose objective moral accounts. The Christian question is not whether they can be moral, but whether an impersonal universe can ultimately ground unconditional human dignity and obligations that bind every conscience. That argument must address those alternatives on their merits rather than reduce them to personal opinion.

    Editorial consequence. The apologetic claim becomes harder to refute and more charitable to the reader it hopes to persuade.

    CH1-15 · Credit grace and wounded nature instead of reducing the problem to unused tools

    Severity: Major / grace and pastoral theology · Control: DC-06, DC-10, DC-25 · Location: chapter_01.md:77

    Issue. “God gives us everything” is followed by “we refuse to use the tools,” treating the Christian life as a simple failure of technique. It neglects concupiscence, ignorance, fear, trauma, habit, unequal access to formation and sacraments, and grace’s initiative.

    Why it matters. Catholic teaching affirms real responsibility without imagining an unaided, uninjured will choosing among tools.

    Controlling sources: CCC 405; CCC 1735; CCC 1987–2029.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    And God has not left us helpless. He gives us everything we need to live rightly: conscience to know the good, grace to pursue it, the sacraments to heal us when we fail, the Church to guide us, Scripture to instruct us, a heart to "know" Him who is good, and the Saints to show us it can be done. The problem is not that God withheld the tools—the problem is that we refuse to use them. And God has not left us helpless. He sustains us, awakens conscience, gives grace, speaks through Scripture and Tradition, guides us through the Church, heals us in the sacraments, and gives us the saints as witnesses. Yet our nature is wounded, our knowledge is partial, our habits can bind us, and our circumstances can make the good difficult to see or choose.
