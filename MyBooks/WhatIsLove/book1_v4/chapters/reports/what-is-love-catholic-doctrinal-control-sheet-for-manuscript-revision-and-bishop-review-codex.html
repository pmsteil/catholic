Status of this document

This is a binding editorial control document for the manuscript, not an act of the Magisterium, a theological censure, a nihil obstat, or an imprimatur. It translates controlling Catholic teaching into revision rules. Where this sheet and an earlier project note conflict on doctrine, the official Catholic source governs; where competent ecclesiastical review corrects this sheet, that judgment governs. The author’s four-facet definition remains his proposed synthesis, subordinate to the deposit of faith and open to correction.

How to use this control sheet

  1. Attach one or more control codes—DC-01, DC-02, and so on—to every doctrinal or pastoral redline.
  2. Preserve the author’s voice and insight wherever the passage can be made accurate by a surgical change.
  3. Replace a passage only when clarification cannot cure its doctrinal, logical, factual, or pastoral defect.
  4. Do not “correct” a sentence merely for being devotional, analogical, emphatic, or long. Correct it when its literal sense misleads, its analogy erases a necessary distinction, its conclusion exceeds its evidence, or its repetition performs no new work.
  5. The book remains one volume. There is no numerical chapter target. Judge scale by robustness, progression, proportionality, and avoidable repetition.
  6. Use RSV for Scripture quotations. Verify wording, verse range, context, and permission status; never label an adaptation as a verbatim quotation.

Claim-status labels

Every load-bearing statement should be identifiable as one of the following:

Dogma / definitive teaching A truth taught as revealed or definitively held State in the Church’s sense; do not improvise contrary formulations.
Authoritative doctrine Binding Catholic teaching not necessarily defined as dogma Present confidently and accurately, with the relevant source.

I. Governing theological controls

DC-01 · The four-facet definition is a subordinate, non-exhaustive synthesis

Controlling formulation. The canonical sentence may be the book’s stable working definition, but it is the author’s fallible pastoral and theological synthesis. It is not a revealed formula, a new article of faith, the Church’s exhaustive definition of charity, the whole Gospel, the regula fidei, or a universal test by which every doctrine, virtue, sin, sacrament, person, or Christian community can be conclusively classified.

Required use. Say that the definition helps us see, offers a lens, draws together, proposes, illuminates, or traces. It may be strongly defended as consonant with Catholic truth while remaining subordinate to Scripture, Tradition, and the Magisterium.

Avoid. “This definition unlocks everything,” “the Gospel is the four facets,” “every virtue contains all four,” “anything missing one facet is not love at all,” or any argument that proves the framework only by categories the framework itself created.

Controlling sources. CCC 65–67; Dei Verbum 2, 7–10.

DC-02 · God is eternally Love; the Trinity is one God in three Persons

Controlling formulation. The Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are three really distinct, consubstantial divine Persons in the one indivisible divine nature. God has one divine intellect, one divine will, one power, and one inseparable divine operation. The Persons are distinguished by eternal relations of origin, not by three minds, three wills, three centers of being, roles, degrees, or competing authorities. God eternally is Love; neither creation nor the Incarnation makes God become loving or become Love.

Required use. Economic missions reveal the eternal Persons without dividing the divine being. Christ’s obedience in the economy of salvation must be attributed to the incarnate Son and expressed without implying that the Son is eternally lesser, coerced, or subordinate in divinity.

Avoid. Social analogies that become identity claims; three members of a divine family; the Father commanding a lesser Son; three separate acts later “bound” into unity; or language in which the Trinity needs creation in order to love.

Controlling source. CCC 232–267.

DC-03 · Eternal divine self-gift is not strict sacrifice, cost, pain, or loss

Controlling formulation. “Self-gift” may be used analogically for the eternal communion of the divine Persons if the prose safeguards divine fullness, simplicity, immutability, and blessedness. Sacrifice in the strict sense entails an offering in the created order and reaches its perfect saving expression in the incarnate Son’s human self-offering. The divine nature does not suffer, lose, pay a cost, die, or become diminished. The one divine Person of the Son truly suffers and dies in his assumed human nature.

Required use. Distinguish (a) eternal Trinitarian communion, (b) creation as God’s free gift, and (c) the historical sacrifice of Christ. Say “the Son suffered in the flesh,” “Christ freely offered himself,” or “the divine Person suffered according to his humanity.”

Avoid. “Sacrifice is the eternal inner life of God” without qualification; cost or pain inside the divine essence; the Father suffering the Passion in the same manner as the incarnate Son; or the Cross changing God from wrathful to loving.

Controlling sources. CCC 456–483; CCC 595–630.

DC-04 · Creation, Incarnation, and redemption are free gifts, not divine necessities

Controlling formulation. God freely creates from no need, deficiency, compulsion, loneliness, or requirement to complete divine love. In the Incarnation, the eternal Son assumes a complete human nature without ceasing to be God and without change or confusion in either nature. At Christ’s death, his human soul is separated from his body while the divine Person remains united to both; his divinity does not die.

Required use. Say that God creates and saves from superabundant goodness and love. When describing Calvary, preserve the one divine Person, two natures, Christ’s free human self-offering, and the gratuitous character of forgiveness, adoption, and glory.

Avoid. God “becoming love”; God needing creation to have another to love; divinity turning into humanity; the divine nature dying; salvation as something human beings are strictly owed.

Controlling sources. CCC 293–301, 317; CCC 456–483; CCC 624–630.

DC-05 · The Paschal mystery admits complementary Catholic images

Controlling formulation. Christ’s Passion is one saving mystery that Scripture and Tradition describe through sacrifice, covenant, satisfaction, obedience, ransom, victory, liberation, reconciliation, filial adoption, healing, and participation. These images illuminate one another. No single metaphor should be made exhaustive, and no true Catholic image should be rejected by a false binary.

Required use. Present Christ as freely offering himself in love, bearing and overcoming sin, satisfying for us, reconciling us to the Father, conquering death and the devil, healing fallen humanity, and opening communion in the Spirit.

Avoid. A merely commercial payment, a punitive transfer that divides Father and Son, “not a tribunal but a Father,” or an account in which God must be persuaded to become merciful.

Controlling source. CCC 595–623.

DC-06 · Grace always precedes, enables, accompanies, and perfects our response

Controlling formulation. God takes the first initiative. Sanctifying or habitual grace is the stable, created gift by which we participate in divine life; actual graces are divine helps for conversion and action; the Holy Spirit is the uncreated divine Person given to us. These must not be collapsed into one thing. Human cooperation is real, free, and itself enabled by prevenient grace.

Required use. Say that grace awakens, opens, heals, enables, accompanies, and brings the response to completion. Our “yes” is genuinely ours without becoming an independent contribution that puts God in our debt.

Avoid. Grace waiting passively until an unaided human first move; “we simply stop blocking grace” if the sentence does not say grace enables that opening; grace as a substance mechanically poured; or the Holy Spirit reduced to a created energy.

Controlling sources. CCC 1987–2029; CCC 1996–2005.

DC-07 · Natural love is real; infused charity is supernatural participation

Controlling formulation. Fallen human beings can perform genuinely good acts, possess acquired virtues, show natural affection, and love imperfectly. Supernatural charity is the infused theological virtue by which we love God above all things for his own sake and our neighbor as ourselves for love of God. Charity heals, elevates, orders, and gives supernatural form to the virtues; it does not render every natural good unreal.

Required use. Distinguish “real but imperfect natural love” from “infused charity” and from the perfection of charity. A missing facet may identify a defect, disorder, incompleteness, or counterfeit claim without proving that no love whatsoever is present.

Avoid. “Incomplete love is not love”; “an atheist cannot truly love”; “every virtue contains all four facets by definition”; or making visible cost the universal measure of every loving act, including contemplation, rest, reception, and duties to self.

Controlling source. CCC 1812–1829.

DC-08 · Initial justification and growth in holiness must be distinguished

Controlling formulation. Justification includes forgiveness of sins and interior renewal. Initial justification is a real transition by grace, ordinarily sacramentally effected in Baptism; the justified then grow in sanctification and charity throughout life. The lifelong process does not erase the reality of the initial gift.

Required use. Distinguish initial justification, ongoing sanctification, restoration after grave sin, and final perseverance. Merit is possible only because God first adopts and empowers us; the merits of the Christian are gifts of grace, not autonomous earnings.

Avoid. “Justification is not a single moment” as an unqualified denial of initial justification; justification as mere moral improvement; or works that independently purchase salvation.

Controlling sources. CCC 1987–2029; CCC 1262–1274.

DC-09 · Faith and charity are distinct but ordered to living unity

Controlling formulation. Faith is a supernatural theological virtue and genuine assent to revealed truth under grace. Faith without charity is dead or unformed, but it remains faith unless faith itself has been lost. Charity gives faith its living form. Saving faith is not reducible to intellectual opinion, feeling, obedience, or a list of works.

Required use. Use “faith working through love,” “living or formed faith,” and “dead or unformed faith” accurately. Preserve the organic unity of grace, faith, hope, charity, obedience, and sacramental life without treating the terms as synonyms.

Avoid. “Dead faith is no faith,” “faith is obedience,” or claims that any moral failure proves the total absence of faith.

Controlling sources. CCC 1812–1829; Pope Benedict XVI, General Audience, November 19, 2008.

II. Moral and pastoral controls

DC-10 · Sin is broader than one framework; mortal sin requires all three conditions

Controlling formulation. Sin is an offense against reason, truth, and right conscience; it fails in genuine love for God and neighbor because of a perverse attachment to goods. The four facets may diagnose aspects of sin, but they are not the Church’s exhaustive definition. Mortal sin requires together grave matter, full knowledge, and deliberate consent. Venial sin wounds charity without destroying it.

Required use. Distinguish objective gravity from subjective culpability. State the three conditions together whenever classifying mortal sin. Acknowledge ignorance, fear, habit, duress, psychological factors, and diminished consent where relevant without denying the objective moral truth.

Avoid. Calling every sin mortal; defining every sin as seeking one’s good at another’s expense; saying lying is literally the root of every sin; or treating a framework-cell failure as a complete moral analysis.

Controlling sources. CCC 1849–1851; CCC 1854–1864.

DC-11 · Temptation without consent is not sin; avoid feeding scrupulosity

Controlling formulation. A spontaneous thought, attraction, feeling, memory, bodily response, or temptation is not itself a sin without sufficient advertence and consent. Culpability concerns the free act of the person. Pastoral examples must distinguish temptation, entertained desire, chosen act, habit, and grave culpability.

Required use. Describe rejected temptation as resistance, not as a venial sin. When forming consciences, give clear thresholds and direct readers with scrupulosity toward a regular confessor rather than multiplying private rules.

Avoid. “An immediately rejected impure thought is venial sin,” moral blame for involuntary experience, or diagnostic language likely to make a reader confess mere temptation.

Controlling source. CCC 1854–1864.

DC-12 · Freedom is perfected in the good, not defined by access to evil

Controlling formulation. Human freedom is the God-given power, rooted in reason and will, to act or not act and to take responsibility. Freedom reaches its perfection when directed toward God and the good. The ability to sin is a defect or abuse of freedom, not a perfection necessary for love. Grace does not destroy freedom; it heals and elevates it.

Required use. Preserve real human consent and the possibility of refusal while crediting grace with the initiative. A gift can be freely received, but do not claim that love logically requires an everlasting possibility of evil or that the saints in glory are less free.

Avoid. Freedom as mere indifference between good and evil; evil as necessary so love can exist; or unaided autonomous choice as the cause of salvation.

Controlling source. CCC 1730–1738.

DC-13 · Justice and mercy are harmonious perfections, not rivals

Controlling formulation. Justice renders what is due; mercy responds to misery and gives beyond strict due. In God, justice and mercy are never competing parts. Mercy fulfills and surpasses justice without making truth, responsibility, restitution, protection, or proportionate consequences unreal.

Required use. Distinguish forgiveness, reconciliation, trust, restitution, and public accountability. A Christian can forgive while prudently maintaining boundaries; reconciliation ordinarily requires truth and a restored relationship; trust may need to be rebuilt. Legitimate punishment can serve redress, protection, deterrence, and correction and must respect human dignity.

Avoid. “Mercy is owed,” “mercy cancels justice,” “forgiveness requires immediate restored trust,” “punishment is only medicinal,” or rhetoric that pressures an abuse victim to resume unsafe contact.

Controlling sources. CCC 1897–1904; CCC 2258–2267.

DC-14 · Authority is real, participatory, morally bounded, and capable of binding

Controlling formulation. Legitimate authority participates in God’s governance, serves the common good, and binds conscience within its competence when it acts according to the moral order. Its authority does not become real only after voluntary submission. No human authority may command what is contrary to God’s law, violate human dignity, or exceed its just competence.

Required use. Distinguish the source, subject, competence, and limits of authority. Voluntary obedience can perfect the disciple’s response, but it does not create the authority being obeyed.

Avoid. “Authority is active only when I submit,” absolute obedience to any human officeholder, or treating all resistance as rebellion against God.

Controlling sources. CCC 1897–1904; Pope Leo XIV, address to the Roman Rota course, November 21, 2025.

DC-15 · Hearts and structures both require conversion

Controlling formulation. Sin is personal, but sinful acts form, sustain, and are encouraged by unjust social structures; reform therefore requires conversion of persons and just transformation of institutions. Structural analysis never eliminates personal responsibility, and personal conversion never excuses institutional injustice.

Required use. Name precise acts, policies, incentives, and consequences. Distinguish the moral object from party labels, slogans, populations, or speculative motives.

Avoid. “All evil comes only from hearts, never structures,” or the reverse claim that structures alone explain personal wrongdoing.

Controlling source. CCC 1886–1889.

III. Sacramental and ecclesial controls

DC-16 · Covenant begins in God’s grace and is not always negotiated consent

Controlling formulation. Biblical covenant is established by God’s gracious initiative and calls for a real human response. Covenant is not adequately defined as a transaction negotiated by equal parties. Sacramental covenant can validly include those unable to make an explicit adult decision, above all infants in Baptism.

Required use. Distinguish establishment of the covenant, personal reception, sacramental fruit, fidelity, violation, and restoration. Mortal sin destroys charity and the state of grace but does not erase the indelible baptismal character or make Baptism cease to have occurred.

Avoid. “No covenant exists without conscious reciprocal consent,” rebaptism after grave sin, or language implying that human promise creates sacramental grace.

Controlling sources. CCC 1246–1255; CCC 1262–1274.

DC-17 · The Eucharist is Christ’s real sacramental presence and the one sacrifice made present

Controlling formulation. In the Eucharist, by transubstantiation, the whole substance of bread and wine becomes the whole substance of Christ’s Body and Blood while the appearances remain. God does not “become bread.” The Eucharist is a true sacramental sign, not a mere symbol; the one sacrifice of Calvary is sacramentally re-presented, not repeated and not accessed by literal time travel. Christ is present whole and entire under each species and in each part after division; this does not make the Host an infinitely divisible physical body.

Required use. Call the Eucharist the source and summit of the Christian life. Distinguish baptismal incorporation into Christ from Eucharistic communion. For worthy reception, use the Church’s criteria: baptism and ecclesial communion, proper disposition, Eucharistic fast, and sacramental confession before Communion when conscious of grave sin.

Avoid. “God becomes bread,” “only a symbol,” “not a symbol at all,” a new sacrifice at every Mass, literal time travel, “state of perfect love” as the reception test, or Baptism for a person already validly baptized.

Controlling sources. Compendium of the CCC 271–294; CCC 1382–1401; Lumen Gentium 11.

DC-18 · Penance includes the acts of the penitent and sacramental absolution

Controlling formulation. Contrition, confession, and satisfaction are acts of the penitent; the priest’s sacramental absolution is essential to the sacrament and effects reconciliation by Christ’s power. Perfect contrition reconciles with God before sacramental confession when it includes the firm resolution to confess as soon as possible.

Required use. Distinguish contrition from emotion, confession from therapy, satisfaction from earning forgiveness, and sacramental absolution from merely hearing reassuring words.

Avoid. A “four-facet” list presented as the Church’s sacramental form; satisfaction as payment that purchases absolution; or perfect contrition with no intention to confess.

Controlling source. CCC 1450–1460.

DC-19 · Christ is the fullness of revelation; the Gospel exceeds the framework

Controlling formulation. Jesus Christ, the incarnate Son, is the Father’s definitive Word and the fullness of revelation. The Gospel proclaims his person, Kingdom, teaching, death, Resurrection, lordship, saving grace, call to conversion and faith, sacramental incorporation, gift of the Spirit, and hope of glory. The four-facet framework may illuminate this mystery but may not replace or exhaust the kerygma.

Required use. Keep Christ, not the private definition, as the interpretive center. Present the Church as the sacrament and seed or beginning of the Kingdom, inseparably related to it but not simply identical to the Kingdom in its eschatological fullness.

Avoid. “The four facets are the Gospel,” “the definition is the missing key the Church lacked,” or reducing salvation to moral execution of a schema.

Controlling sources. CCC 65–67; Lumen Gentium 5.

DC-20 · Scripture, Tradition, and Magisterium serve one deposit of faith

Controlling formulation. Sacred Scripture and Sacred Tradition form one sacred deposit entrusted to the Church. The Magisterium authentically interprets the Word of God and is its servant, not its master or replacement. Scripture is inspired by the Holy Spirit through true human authors; the inspiration of Scripture must not be transferred to this manuscript or its author.

Required use. Attribute doctrines and quotations accurately. Distinguish the biblical text, an ecclesial interpretation, a saint’s theological opinion, and the author’s synthesis. Read each passage according to literary form, context, the unity of Scripture, living Tradition, and the analogy of faith.

Avoid. “The Holy Spirit is the author of this book,” “God dictated this framework,” isolated proof texts, or claims that the Magisterium produces new revelation.

Controlling source. Dei Verbum 7–12.

DC-21 · Personal illumination is not public revelation or manuscript inspiration

Controlling formulation. God’s grace and the Holy Spirit can illuminate, guide, prompt, and assist a person. Personal experiences and private revelations, even when authentic, do not belong to the deposit of faith, do not improve or complete Christ’s definitive revelation, and require ecclesial discernment. Ordinary learning through Scripture, prayer, teachers, and providence should normally be described as coming to understand rather than receiving a new revelation.

Required use. “I came to understand,” “the Lord used this passage to awaken me,” “I believe grace guided my study,” “I submit this synthesis to the judgment of the Church.”

Avoid. “God revealed this definition to me,” “the Holy Spirit authored the true parts,” or a disclaimer that denies revelation while surrounding rhetoric continues to imply it.

Controlling sources. CCC 65–67; Dei Verbum 2, 11.

DC-22 · Catholic fullness must be stated with Catholic ecumenical and interreligious precision

Controlling formulation. The fullness of the means of salvation subsists in the Catholic Church. Those baptized outside her visible structure can be joined to Christ and possess real elements of sanctification and truth; responsibility for Christian division is not assigned indiscriminately to people born into separated communities. The Church rejects nothing true and holy in other religions while proclaiming Christ as the fullness of salvation. Jews as a people are not collectively guilty for Christ’s Passion.

Required use. Make the positive Catholic claim without denying the real Christian faith, gifts, martyrdom, sanctification, or good faith of separated brethren. Distinguish objective ecclesial deficiency from individual culpability.

Avoid. “Only Catholics can truly love,” “all non-Catholic worship is false,” collective blame, contemptuous labels, or treating respectful engagement as compromise.

Controlling sources. Lumen Gentium 14–16; Unitatis Redintegratio 3–4; Nostra Aetate 2, 4.

IV. Vocation, anthropology, and vulnerable readers

DC-23 · Marriage has inseparable unitive and procreative goods; family life is not reducible to fertility

Controlling formulation. Marriage is ordered to the good of the spouses and the procreation and education of children. A valid marriage is a family and remains a true marriage when children are not conceived. Infertile spouses can be profoundly fruitful. Consecrated virginity and celibacy for the Kingdom are genuine, fruitful vocations and eschatological signs, not failures of communion or sacrifice.

Required use. Defend indissolubility while distinguishing a valid bond, separation for safety or grave cause, civil divorce tolerated for necessary legal protection, and a declaration of nullity. Distinguish objective wrong, coercion, abandonment, and personal culpability.

Avoid. “Marriage without children is barren love,” contraception described as literally sacrificing an existing child, divorce applied as one undifferentiated moral category, or single/celibate persons portrayed as incomplete.

Controlling sources. CCC 1643–1654; CCC 2382–2386.

DC-24 · The human person is a body–soul unity with inviolable dignity

Controlling formulation. The human person is created in the image of God as a unity of spiritual soul and material body. Intellect, memory, imagination, emotion, and will are powers or operations of the person, not a third substance called “mind” alongside body and soul. Male and female sexual difference is a created good; every person retains inviolable dignity and must be treated with truth, compassion, and respect.

Required use. Distinguish sex, bodily development, subjective experience, psychological distress, moral acts, and pastoral care. State Catholic anthropology without mocking, diagnosing from a distance, or reducing a person to a struggle.

Avoid. A body–mind–soul three-substance anthropology; stereotypes presented as nature; any language that implies dignity is lost through sin, disability, infertility, sexuality, gender distress, or family history.

Controlling sources. CCC 355–368; Dignitas Infinita 1, 55–60.

DC-25 · Pastoral exhortation must preserve agency, hope, safety, and access to competent help

Controlling formulation. Moral truth and mercy belong together. Pastoral prose should name wrong clearly while recognizing wounds, coercion, fear, ignorance, unequal responsibility, social pressure, trauma, and the possibility of repentance, sacramental restoration, clinical care, protection, and growth in holiness. Spiritual and professional help are not rivals.

Required use. Give a concrete path forward. When writing about abortion, abuse, divorce, sexuality, addiction, mental illness, trauma, or children, separate the act from the person; avoid diagnosing motives; acknowledge varied culpability; protect victims; and identify competent pastoral, medical, psychological, or legal help when appropriate.

Avoid. Deterministic child profiles; inherited shame; “therapy cannot heal”; claims that one wound guarantees later evil; framing abuse endurance as sacrifice; or language that closes the door to mercy.

Controlling sources. Evangelium Vitae 99; CCC 1854–1864.

V. Evidence, reasoning, and editorial controls

DC-26 · Universal claims require universal warrant

Controlling formulation. Words such as “always,” “every,” “only,” “nothing,” “no one,” “must,” “guarantees,” “proves,” and “cannot” are appropriate only when the doctrine, logic, or evidence supports that scope. A vivid example can illustrate a principle but cannot by itself prove a universal causal claim.

Required use. Name the level of certainty. Prefer “can,” “often,” “tends to,” “may,” “in this respect,” or “under these conditions” when the evidence is limited. Distinguish theological judgment, philosophical inference, empirical correlation, personal experience, and rhetoric.

Avoid. “Atheism’s strongest argument always,” “every social evil has this one cause,” “this outcome is guaranteed,” or a five-part test whose conclusion simply restates its premises.

DC-27 · Analogy, etymology, and wordplay may illustrate but not prove doctrine

Controlling formulation. Analogies disclose likeness within greater unlikeness; etymologies explain word history, not necessarily current meaning or theological reality. Puns and visual wordplay can aid memory but cannot bear a doctrinal conclusion.

Required use. Mark analogies as analogies. Verify Greek, Hebrew, Latin, and English claims in authoritative lexicons and primary texts. Let doctrine rest on revelation, sound philosophy, and authoritative teaching rather than a resemblance between words.

Avoid. holy derived from whole as proof; missa said to mean literally “sent”; ekklesia etymology used to prove a complete ecclesiology; alicui said to contain sacrifice; or human family structure treated as the Trinity’s literal internal anatomy.

DC-28 · Source, quotation, translation, and permission claims must be exact

Controlling formulation. Every direct quotation must match the named edition or document, preserve material context, carry a precise locator, and have its permission or fair-use status recorded. A shortened, combined, modernized, or adapted passage is a paraphrase or adaptation and must be labeled as such. RSV is the manuscript’s required Scripture translation.

Required use. Maintain a source ledger with edition, publisher or official URL, paragraph/page/article, exact or adapted status, permission status, and every manuscript location. Verify saint stories, martyr counts, papal language, council texts, canon history, science, medicine, psychology, sociology, and statistics against suitable primary or expert sources.

Avoid. “Used with Permission” without documentation; a quotation silently assembled from several verses; memory-based CCC numbers; or a secondary quotation treated as a primary source.

DC-29 · Repetition is judged by function, not word count

Controlling formulation. A chapter is robust when every major movement is needed for its unique task. Repetition is warranted when it deepens doctrine, develops a new implication, answers a new objection, applies the truth to a materially different situation, forms a deliberate motif, or creates a necessary pastoral cadence. Repetition is unnecessary when it merely restates a conclusion the reader already possesses.

Required use. Give each doctrine, image, Scripture passage, and extended example a principal home. Elsewhere, use a concise callback unless the later context performs distinct work. Preserve depth and one-volume integrity.

Avoid. Compression for its own sake, numerical chapter targets, four full re-expositions where one anchor treatment and three callbacks would do, or repeated emotional climaxes that compete with the Gospel chapter.

DC-30 · Bishop-readiness gate

The manuscript should not be represented as final for episcopal review until:

  1. every doctrinal stop-ship issue has an approved redline tied to this control sheet;
  2. the entire four-facet framework is consistently labeled as the author’s subordinate synthesis;
  3. Trinity, Incarnation, grace, justification, faith, Baptism, Eucharist, Penance, Church, revelation, marriage, anthropology, and moral culpability use stable Catholic distinctions;
  4. high-risk pastoral chapters preserve truth, hope, agency, victim safety, and paths of healing;
  5. every quotation, citation, empirical claim, etymology, and permission statement has been verified and logged;
  6. robustness and repetition have been reviewed across the one-volume arc;
  7. a qualified dogmatic/sacramental theologian and a qualified moral/pastoral theologian have reviewed their domains;
  8. the exact submission PDF and EPUB pass visual, metadata, citation, link, and permissions preflight; and
  9. the diocesan office’s preferred submission and censor-review process under canons 827–830 has been confirmed.
Editorial decision rule

If preserving a memorable sentence would force the reader to hold a materially false doctrinal picture, accuracy wins. If Catholic accuracy can be secured by a small qualifier, distinction, or attribution, preserve the sentence’s voice and force.
