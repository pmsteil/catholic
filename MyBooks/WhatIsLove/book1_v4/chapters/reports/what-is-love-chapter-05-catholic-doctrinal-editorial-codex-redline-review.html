    Chapter 5 · Binding: What Love Unites

    Chapter assessment

    Chapter 5 has an excellent received Catholic anchor in CCC 1827: infused charity is the form of the virtues and binds their Christian practice into harmony. Its central image can carry the book. The difficulty is that the chapter repeatedly turns genuine truth, justice, mercy, sacrifice, faith, hope and acquired virtue into their contrary whenever charity is absent. A real virtue remains a genuine natural perfection; false, partial or disordered claims masquerading as virtue can become cruelty, vengeance, enabling or self-harm. The chapter also presents the author’s four facets as Aquinas’s hidden “interior anatomy,” although bonum is not exhausted by truth, justice and mercy and alicui does not mean cost or sacrifice. The John 8 reading treats manipulated accusation as true Mosaic justice, the atonement is reduced to debt-payment language, and the Eucharistic section blurs Christ’s Eucharistic Body, his Mystical Body and the distinct roles of Baptism and Holy Communion. These redlines preserve “binding” while locating it precisely in infused charity, Christ, the sacraments and the whole Catholic account of virtue.

    CH5-02 · Present the Paschal mystery through the full Catholic synthesis

    Severity: Stop-ship / atonement · Control: DC-05, DC-13, DC-19 · Location: chapter_05.md:17

    Issue. “Our sin creates a debt only God can pay” and “the debt paid in full” reduce Calvary to a financial transaction. Catholic doctrine includes free loving obedience, sacrifice, expiation, satisfaction, merit, ransom, reconciliation and victory; justice and mercy are harmonious divine perfections rather than sequential parts of a payment.

    Controlling sources: CCC 599–618; CCC 654; Salvifici Doloris 14–18.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    Consider the Lord's Passion. In a single act, every element of the teaching and mission of Jesus comes together. Christ offers truth (our sin creates a debt only God can pay), justice (the debt paid in full), mercy (forgiveness freely offered), and sacrifice (the gift of Himself, holding nothing back) himself in free and loving obedience to the Father for us: revealing the truth of sin and divine love, fulfilling righteousness, expiating and making satisfaction for sin, meriting grace, reconciling humanity and conquering death. None cancels the others. All serve the others. That is what binding looks like—and Chapter 9 will dwell there fully, at the foot of the Cross.

    Editorial consequence. Calvary remains the governing image without narrowing Catholic soteriology.

    CH5-03 · Distinguish genuine virtues from counterfeits and partial claims

    Severity: Stop-ship / virtue and divine perfections · Control: DC-07, DC-13, DC-26 · Location: chapter_05.md:19–27

    Issue. True truth, justice and mercy do not become cruelty, vengeance or weakness. Cruel speech can invoke truth; revenge can masquerade as justice; permissiveness can be mislabeled mercy. Acquired human virtues are real, though grace heals and elevates them and charity gives the Christian virtues their supernatural form.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1803–1811; CCC 1822–1829; CCC 1990–2001.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    Truth without mercy becomes the Pharisees demanding the adulteress be stoned. Mercy without truth becomes the parent who pays for their child's addiction rather than insisting on treatment. Justice without mercy becomes endless cycles of retaliation. Mercy without justice becomes the abuser forgiven while victims suffer. A claim to truth severed from charity can be wielded cruelly. Permissiveness can masquerade as mercy when a parent finances addiction without prudent limits or help. Revenge can masquerade as justice, while premature reconciliation can be called mercy even as victims remain unsafe. The defect lies in the counterfeit or disordered exercise, not in genuine truth, justice or mercy.

    These are fragmented virtues. Isolated from each other, truth becomes cruelty, justice becomes vengeance, and mercy becomes weakness. Even sacrifice, when isolated, can become a weapon of manipulation or a path to self-destruction. These are counterfeits or partial claims, not virtues changing their nature. Genuine virtues remain ordered to real goods, though human judgment and action can be incomplete, and sacrifice severed from prudence and justice can become manipulation or self-harm. When our understanding of God's love is incomplete or lopsided, we inevitably can distort how we practice virtue. An overly justice-oriented view of God's love makes us judgmental and harsh. An overly mercy-oriented view makes us enablers of sin. A punitive caricature of justice can make us harsh; a permissive caricature of mercy can enable sin. Both are wrong because neither reflects the full picture of God's love.

    This is why our definition of perfect love includes the word "binds"—love holds truth, justice, mercy and sacrifice together in life-giving communion. Without authentic love infused charity as their supernatural form, these virtues fragment and become distorted lack their full Christian orientation to God, although genuine acquired virtue remains a natural good. With authentic love charity, they work together as one harmonious whole. Holy, you could say.

    Editorial consequence. “Binding” is preserved without teaching that divine or human virtues become vices.

    CH5-04 · Make the relativism section a warning, not an inevitable political sequence

    Severity: Major / authority, history and scope · Control: DC-14, DC-15, DC-22, DC-26 · Location: chapter_05.md:29–43

    Issue. The section says relativism leaves no principled barrier to tyranny, treats Judges’ lack of a king as lack of a binding authority, and says a dictatorship is “already at the door.” Relativism can erode shared rational standards, but democratic institutions, rights and natural-law reasoning require a more careful account. Judges 21:25 belongs to Israel’s covenant history and cannot simply stand for the later Church hierarchy.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1897–1917; Evangelium Vitae 20; Dignitatis Humanae 2–8.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    When a society no longer agrees that truth, justice, mercy and sacrifice are objectively real—when each is reduced to "my truth," "my idea of fairness," "my feelings," "my preferences"—the threads have nothing to hold them together shared moral reasoning becomes harder. Substitutes rush in to fill the vacuum: sentiment, majority opinion, utility, personal authenticity. Each feels like a standard, until two of them collide. Then there is no arbiter left except power power can displace reasoned appeal to truth and the common good.

    He did not call relativism a dictatorship because relativists are tyrants by temperament. He called it that because relativism has no principled stopping point against tyranny can deprive freedom of an objective moral limit and leave preference vulnerable to domination. Scripture diagnosed the same disease a related covenantal disorder long before Benedict gave it a modern name. The book of Judges closes with the refrain: "In those days there was no king in Israel; every man did what was right in his own eyes" (Judges 21:25)—a refrain sounded four times as the structural indictment of a people without a binding authority to tell them what God's love actually required a refrain that belongs to Israel’s own narrative of covenant infidelity and political disorder, not a direct proof of the Catholic hierarchy. Why Christ established an authoritative Church belongs to Chapter 6. Here it is enough to name the pattern: when no authority binds a people to God's love, every person becomes his own authority, and the dictatorship of relativism is already at the door truth is reduced to preference, law and freedom can be detached from the objective good of the person.

    Editorial consequence. Benedict’s warning remains while the historical and ecclesiological inference becomes proportionate.

    CH5-05 · Remove deterministic claims about children from fractured homes

    Severity: Stop-ship / pastoral safety and evidence · Control: DC-24, DC-25, DC-26 · Location: chapter_05.md:45–63

    Issue. One high-conflict scenario is generalized to fractured homes, and the text says fragmentation deforms children, teaches manipulation and transactional love, and is carried into every future relationship. Children respond differently; separation can create safety; trauma effects are risks, not destinies.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1649; CCC 2382–2386; Amoris Laetitia 241–246.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    Consider the child in a fractured home a child caught in severe parental conflict:

    Two houses. Two sets of rules. Two warring factions. The child isn't just hurt—they're torn. And here is the most tragic consequence: fragmentation doesn't just wound a child, it deforms their capacity for authentic love a grave risk: sustained conflict and triangulation can wound trust and distort a child’s developing expectations of love.

    Watch what happens. The child learns to play one parent against the other Some children learn to play one parent against the other; others withdraw, become anxious or try to keep peace. In a unified healthy family, both parents present a unified front cooperate in the child’s good—truth, justice, and mercy work together because the parents themselves are bound in love. When that binding breaks, the child discovers that factions can be manipulated.

    They learn that love is transactional—conditional on obedience, revocable at will. They learn that loyalty itself is a weapon... They carry these discoveries into every future relationship A child may begin to fear that love is conditional, authority unsafe or commitment unreliable. These are wounds and learned defenses, not the child’s fault and not a fixed future. Stable care, grace and competent help can foster healing.

    Editorial consequence. The vignette remains urgent without stigmatizing children or separated families.

    CH5-06 · Label the four-facet reading of Aquinas as the author’s analogy

    Severity: Stop-ship / Aquinas and private synthesis · Control: DC-01, DC-07, DC-27, DC-28 · Location: chapter_05.md:65–75

    Issue. The text says the four facets are already inside amor est velle alicui bonum, calls them its “interior anatomy,” restricts bonum to truth, justice and mercy, and makes alicui mean costly sacrifice. Aquinas’s bonum is the good in its full scope; alicui means “to another.” Cost is neither semantic content nor a necessary proof of every act of love.

    Controlling sources: Summa Theologiae I–II, q.26, a.4; II–II, q.23, a.1 and a.8; CCC 1822–1829.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    The classical definition of love is St. Thomas Aquinas's: amor est velle alicui bonum—"to love is to will the good of another". It is a definition the whole Christian tradition has held for nearly eight hundred years a classic Thomistic formulation, and it is exactly right. But it is dense. Unpack it, and the four threads of this book are already inside it. The book’s four facets can be placed alongside it as an authorial meditation, not extracted from its grammar.

    Aquinas's formula has two halves. "The good"—bonum—names what love wills. "For another"—alicui—names that it is willed for them, not for me. Aquinas did not spell out what follows—but pull those two halves apart in the light of this book's definition, and the four pillars appear inside them: Aquinas does not identify bonum or alicui with the following fourfold scheme. The analogy proposed here is mine:

    The good willed is truth, justice, and mercy. The good willed must be real and therefore known truthfully; justice and mercy often specify what willing it requires. These three do not exhaust every human good. And the willing-it-for-you, at cost to me, is can require sacrifice. Wanting your good in the abstract is not yet love; wanting it enough to pay for it is—sacrifice is what proves the for you is real and not disguised self-interest, and a love that costs nothing has not yet shown that it is for the other at all love must become effective when action is due, but not every act of love entails visible cost, and cost alone does not authenticate love.

    This is why the four threads are co-constitutive rather than ranked... they are, I am convinced, its interior anatomy. The four facets are therefore a pastoral synthesis for examining love’s practice, not Aquinas’s hidden anatomy or an exhaustive definition proved by his Latin.

    Calvary is Aquinas's definition at full volume. The good willed: our salvation—the truth about our condition spoken without flinching, the justice of God satisfied, mercy poured out without limit. Willed for us: "for us men and for our salvation"—at the cost of everything accomplished through Christ’s loving obedience, sacrifice, expiation, satisfaction, merit, reconciliation and victory. Truth, justice, mercy and sacrifice, bound in one act.

    Editorial consequence. Aquinas supports the chapter without being made the source of a private taxonomy.

    CH5-07 · Do not call a living person’s damnation or merely restorative punishment “technically accurate”

    Severity: Stop-ship / judgment and justice · Control: DC-10, DC-13, DC-25 · Location: chapter_05.md:77–81

    Issue. “You’re going to hell” is not technically accurate about any living person’s final destiny. The paragraph also treats justice without love as cold retribution and implies consequences are just only when restorative. Catholic punishment includes redress of disorder, protection, deterrence and medicinal aims.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1021–1037; CCC 1861; CCC 2266; CCC 2477–2478.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    Truth without love becomes harsh and condemning—"You're a sinner and you're going to hell"—technically accurate, perhaps “Your act is gravely sinful, therefore I know you are going to hell”—not a technically accurate judgment of a living person’s final destiny, but destroying rather than healing. Yet when love binds truth, it becomes medicine: "You're caught in sin, and it's destroying you. Let me help you find freedom." Justice without love becomes cold retribution—"You broke the law, so you must be punished"—with no path to restoration. But love transforms justice into something restorative: "There are consequences, but they're meant to heal you, not just punish you." is falsely reduced to vengeance. True justice can require proportionate punishment to redress disorder, protect others, deter wrongdoing and, when possible, foster reform. Charity does not transform false justice into true justice; it animates the just act and orders the Christian toward the offender’s ultimate good. Mercy without love becomes enabling weakness—"I'll forgive you no matter what you do" premature reconciliation without repentance or safety—allowing evil to continue and victims to suffer. Yet love ensures mercy serves transformation: "I forgive you, but you must stop. I'll help you change, but I won't enable your sin." Forgiveness, reconciliation, restored trust and resumed contact remain distinct.

    Editorial consequence. The application warns of hell and upholds consequences without presuming damnation or erasing retributive justice.

    CH5-08 · Re-read John 8 as exposure of a manipulated accusation, not justice interrupted by mercy

    Severity: Stop-ship / Scripture and justice · Control: DC-13, DC-19, DC-20, DC-28 · Location: chapter_05.md:83–99

    Issue. The account says Mosaic law clearly required this woman’s execution, that Jesus upheld that demand, and that stoning would satisfy the law. Deuteronomy required both parties in adultery cases, witnesses and lawful procedure; John explicitly says the accusers were testing Jesus. Their selective, procedurally corrupt trap is not true justice.

    Controlling sources: John 8:1–11; Leviticus 20:10; Deuteronomy 17:6–7; 19:15–21; 22:22–24.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    The scribes and Pharisees drag a woman before Jesus in the Temple courts. She has been caught in adultery—the Law of Moses is clear, and they invoke it: she must be stoned they selectively invoke Moses while producing neither the man nor a complete lawful case. They have engineered a trap with no good exit. If Jesus upholds the Law, He is the harsh judge... The trap works precisely because it forces a choice between fragments: truth or mercy, justice or compassion. John tells us they act “to test him.” The trap falsely presents manipulated accusation as law and mercy as lawlessness.

    He does not deny her sin—He acknowledges the truth of it. He does not undermine the Law—He upholds its authority while exposing the accusers' own guilt he exposes the accusers and refuses to ratify their corrupt use of it ("Let him who is without sin among you be the first to throw a stone at her"). He does not excuse her—He demands real transformation ("go, and do not sin again"). And He does not abandon her.

    Truth without mercy sends the stones flying—the Law is satisfied, but nothing is healed... Justice without compassion means execution—the accusers are vindicated... compassion without justice lets the Law collapse—future victims lose their protection. The stones would not have represented truth or satisfied justice in the case they staged. Mere indulgence would also fail the woman’s good. Christ neither condemns her through their manipulation nor calls adultery good; he dismisses the accusers and commands conversion.

    Justice is upheld through compassion... Sacrifice is made through the willingness to bear the cost of standing with her... she is restored. Justice and mercy meet without validating an unlawful prosecution. The text does not say she is sacramentally reconciled or that Jesus incurred a defined social cost; it shows the Judge refusing condemnation and calling her from sin.

    Editorial consequence. The scene becomes a genuine integration of truth and mercy rather than a misreading of Mosaic justice.

    CH5-09 · Preserve acquired virtue and distinguish unformed faith from no faith

    Severity: Stop-ship / virtue, faith and hope · Control: DC-07, DC-09, DC-19 · Location: chapter_05.md:101–111

    Issue. The chair analogy calls virtues without charity “raw moral material,” and the examples say faith becomes mere assent, hope selfish calculation, prudence cunning and justice retribution. Acquired virtue is genuine; infused charity orders virtue to the supernatural end. Unformed faith is real though not living or salvific, and hope is a distinct theological virtue.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1803–1813; CCC 1817–1829; Council of Trent, Decree on Justification, chapter 7.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    Without charity as their form, the virtues remain raw moral material—potentially good, but not yet ordered toward their true purpose acquired virtues can remain genuine natural perfections, but they are not thereby given their supernatural form or ordered meritoriously to friendship with God. CCC 1827 tells us that charity "articulates and orders them among themselves," binding them into a coherent whole that both finds has God as its source and its goal.

    This is why Paul insists that without charity, "I am nothing" and "I gain nothing." You can have brilliant faith—but without charity, it's mere intellectual assent. You can have burning hope—but without charity, it's self-interested calculation. You can exercise careful prudence—but without charity, it's cunning manipulation. You can practice perfect justice—but without charity, it's cold retribution. You can display heroic courage—but without charity, it's recklessness or pride. the gift of faith without its living form in charity; that faith is real but “dead” and does not unite the sinner to God salvifically. Christian hope is a distinct infused virtue, not calculation. Prudence, justice and fortitude can be genuine acquired virtues; charity elevates and animates their Christian practice rather than turning their absence automatically into cunning, vengeance or pride.

    Editorial consequence. The chapter teaches Aquinas and the Catechism’s actual form-of-virtues doctrine.

    CH5-10 · Remove anti-Pharisaic legalism and state Catholic faith-and-works doctrine positively

    Severity: Stop-ship / Judaism, ecumenism and justification · Control: DC-08, DC-09, DC-22 · Location: chapter_05.md:111–115

    Issue. “The Pharisees stood before God as slaves to the Law” is a sweeping anti-Jewish characterization. The next paragraph makes “faith alone” a trap and says works cannot save, without distinguishing initial justification, living faith, grace-enabled merit and Catholic–Protestant terminology.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1987–2029; Nostra Aetate 4; Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification 15–18, 37–39.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    This is why mere rule-following fails. The Pharisees stood before God as slaves to the Law rather than sons responding to love. Without charity to bind the virtues together, even perfect external compliance becomes dead legalism. Jesus condemns hypocrisy and external observance severed from justice, mercy and faithfulness; this must not become a judgment on Jews or the Torah as such. External compliance cannot substitute for conversion and charity.

    This is why our works—if separated from God's love grace and turned inward toward ourselves—cannot save us cause initial justification or earn salvation apart from Christ. Even "faith alone" becomes a trap when it's reduced to "how do I get to heaven?" Catholic teaching does not oppose faith to grace-enabled obedience: we are justified gratuitously by grace through faith, and living faith works through charity. Terminological disputes over “faith alone” should be handled ecumenically rather than as a slogan. Without the sacrificial aspect of love charity, faith becomes merely transactional is unformed and does not bring salvific union.

    Editorial consequence. The passage becomes Catholic, non-polemical and free of a harmful Jewish caricature.

    CH5-11 · Keep the four dimensions subordinate to the received taxonomy of virtues

    Severity: Stop-ship / private synthesis and divine simplicity · Control: DC-01, DC-02, DC-07, DC-13 · Location: chapter_05.md:115–117

    Issue. The text says every virtue is an aspect of charity and expresses some combination of the four facets. Prudence, justice, fortitude and temperance have their own formal objects; faith and hope are distinct theological virtues. Divine simplicity does not prove that every created virtue decomposes into the author’s scheme.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    When we speak of truth, justice, mercy and sacrifice as the essential facets of love this book’s fourfold pastoral synthesis, we are not replacing the other virtues. The theological virtues, the cardinal virtues, the human virtues—all remain a cohesive whole. After all, God is "simple" (not made of parts) and "one" and so is His love distinct yet ordered within the Christian moral life. Divine simplicity means God is not composed of parts; it does not prove a four-part taxonomy of created virtue (CCC 202, 253).

    Rather, truth, justice, mercy and sacrifice are the human words we use to explain the dimensions through which all virtues express themselves four lenses the author uses to examine how charity coordinates Christian action. Prudence for example operates through truth and justice discerns the true good and right means; fortitude expresses itself through sacrifice gives firmness in difficulty and may sustain costly self-gift; patience is mercy extended through sacrifice endures present evil without surrendering the good; humility is truth about ourselves before God. Every virtue is an aspect of charity, expressing itself through some combination of these four dimensions. None is reducible to the four facets, and charity remains a distinct infused virtue that gives the others their supernatural form.

    Editorial consequence. The framework helps organize the book without replacing Catholic virtue theory.

    CH5-12 · Distinguish Christ’s Eucharistic Body, Mystical Body and ecclesial authority

    Severity: Stop-ship / Eucharist and ecclesiology · Control: DC-14, DC-17, DC-19, DC-20 · Location: chapter_05.md:119–123

    Issue. Authority is said to “culminate in the Pope,” and the One Bread and One Body are called “one reality.” The Pope has supreme, full, immediate and universal authority, but bishops truly share in sacred authority. Christ’s sacramental Body and Blood are substantially present in the Eucharist; the Church is his Mystical Body. They are inseparably related, not simply identical.

    Controlling sources: CCC 771–810; CCC 880–896; CCC 1322–1419; Lumen Gentium 18–27.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    This binding of these four dimensions of love is exactly why the Church's sacred authority—culminating in the Pope entrusted to the apostles and their successors, the bishops in communion with the successor of Peter—is so profound and essential: Christ Himself instituted this hierarchy so that we might know His love definitively receive and faithfully confess the apostolic faith, and through that shared truth, unite as one Body of Christ in the one bread and the one baptism. As St. Paul reveals: "Because there is one bread, we who are many are one body" (1 Corinthians 10:17). The One Bread and the One Body are not two separate realities—they are one. The Eucharistic Body of Christ and the Church as his Mystical Body must be distinguished without separation: by receiving Christ’s true Body and Blood, the baptized are united more closely to him and to one another in the Church.

    This binding power of love doesn't just integrate virtues within us—it binds our very souls to one another. When we live truth, justice, and mercy sacrificially, we become more deeply connected to every other soul in the Body of Christ builds ecclesial communion in those joined to Christ by Baptism, faith, the sacraments and charity and of course to Christ Himself.

    Editorial consequence. The paragraph preserves Eucharistic ecclesiology without confusing bodies or reducing authority to one office.

    CH5-13 · Replace speculative “soul-to-soul” mechanics with sacramental communion and social nature

    Severity: Major / anthropology and sacramental causality · Control: DC-07, DC-17, DC-24, DC-26 · Location: chapter_05.md:125

    Issue. The text says souls are dependent on connection to one another, that every loving act strengthens a metaphysical bond between two souls and the whole Body, and contrasts this with evolutionary “herding.” CCC 1878–1885 teaches social nature, not a literal soul-to-soul mechanism. Membership in Christ’s Body comes through Baptism and is deepened by Eucharistic communion and charity.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    This isn't evolutionary herding for protection, nor merely merely a biological account of social behavior, nor only spiritual fellowship or shared beliefs—it is participation in Christ's own life through the sacraments, making us genuinely one Body as Jesus desired for us in John 6:56 and John 17:20-23. This is the reality of our human nature—our souls are actually dependent on being connected to one another for our flourishing (CCC 1878-1885). Just as the Trinity exists in eternal communion, we were created for soul-to-soul communion. When you practice authentic love toward your neighbor, you're strengthening the bonds that connect your soul to theirs, to the whole Body, and to Christ the Head. Human beings are social by nature and flourish through communion, but CCC 1878–1885 does not teach a literal bond passed from soul to soul. Baptism incorporates us into Christ; the Eucharist renews and deepens that incorporation; charity builds communion in his Mystical Body. Natural acts of love serve genuine human unity, while grace gives supernatural union with Christ.

    Editorial consequence. The communion language remains real without speculative metaphysics.

    CH5-14 · Do not promise that forgiveness produces a felt release or restored relationship

    Severity: Stop-ship / pastoral safety · Control: DC-13, DC-25, DC-26 · Location: chapter_05.md:127

    Issue. “You feel the weight lift,” “the barrier dissolves,” and “your soul is reconnecting” make psychological relief and relational restoration automatic effects of forgiveness. A victim may forgive by renouncing vengeance and willing the offender’s good while trauma symptoms, boundaries and broken trust remain.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    Think about what happens when you truly forgive someone. You feel the weight lift. The barrier dissolves. That's not just psychological—it's spiritual. Your soul is reconnecting to theirs through the love that flows from Christ. When you speak truth in love, you feel the connection deepen. That's your soul binding to theirs through shared reality. You renounce vengeance and entrust judgment to God, even if no emotional relief follows. Forgiveness can open a path toward peace, but reconciliation requires the offender’s repentance and the repair of justice; restored trust and contact require prudence and safety. In Christ, forgiveness is a spiritual act of charity, not a guarantee of a felt bond with the offender.

    Editorial consequence. The passage becomes safe for trauma survivors and doctrinally precise about forgiveness and reconciliation.

    CH5-15 · Distinguish mortal rupture from venial wounds and ordinary relational harm

    Severity: Stop-ship / sin and communion · Control: DC-10, DC-17, DC-25 · Location: chapter_05.md:129–131

    Issue. Every lie, injustice, merciless act or refusal of sacrifice is said to sever communion and cut the soul off from the Body.
