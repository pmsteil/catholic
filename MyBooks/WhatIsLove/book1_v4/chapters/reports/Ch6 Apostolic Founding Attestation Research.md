# Attestation Strength — Ch. 6 Apostolic-Founding List (lines 220–229)

Research compiled 2026-08-20 for the Ch. 6 review (undercurrent item 6). No prior research on this specific question was found in the trajectory archive — the one relevant file ("Review Chapter 6 Flow.md") only discusses *where the list should sit in the chapter*, not the strength of the historical claims themselves. This document fills that gap.

Four tiers, ranked by evidentiary strength.

## Tier 1 — Scriptural, direct (strongest)

Attested explicitly in Acts or the apostle's own epistles. No serious scholarly dispute.

- **James & the Twelve → Jerusalem (c. AD 33).** Acts 2 (Pentecost).
- **Peter, Paul & Barnabas → Antioch (c. AD 40).** Acts 11:19-26.
- **Barnabas → Cyprus (c. AD 45).** Acts 13:4; Acts 15:39 (his return there with Mark).
- **Paul → Philippi, Thessalonica, Corinth, Ephesus, Crete.** Documented both in Acts and in Paul's own letters to these churches (Philippians; 1-2 Thessalonians; 1-2 Corinthians; Ephesians; Titus for Crete) — the strongest possible evidence, since Paul is writing directly to the communities he founded.

## Tier 2 — Sub-apostolic, very early patristic witness (strong, non-scriptural)

Not in the New Testament itself, but attested within decades by writers who had direct or near-direct contact with the events.

- **Peter & Paul → Rome.** 1 Clement (c. AD 96, written from Rome itself) references their martyrdom there; Ignatius of Antioch (c. AD 107) writes to the Roman church with evident deference; Irenaeus (c. AD 180) explicitly calls Rome "founded and organized" by Peter and Paul. The specific arrival date "c. AD 42" for Peter is a later traditional dating (from Eusebius's chronicle), not itself scriptural — the presence and martyrdom of both apostles at Rome is well-attested; the precise year is traditional.
- **John → Smyrna, handed to Polycarp.** Irenaeus (c. AD 180) — who as a young man personally knew Polycarp — states directly that Polycarp was "appointed bishop of the Church in Smyrna by the apostles" and had been instructed by John himself. One of the best-attested successions on the list.

## Tier 3 — Moderate: early but non-scriptural tradition, partially corroborated

Earliest witnesses are 2nd–4th century, resting on apocryphal or patristic tradition rather than Scripture or 1st-century documents. Consistent within their own tradition, but later and thinner than Tier 1–2.

- **Thomas → India (c. AD 52).** Earliest written source is the "Doctrine of the Apostles" (probably 2nd century) and the *Acts of Thomas* (apocryphal, before c. AD 220). Origen (184–253) and Eusebius (writing 315–340) attest Thomas evangelized "the Parthians," a claim linked to, but not identical with, the India tradition. Partial external corroboration exists: coins bearing the name of King Gondophares, a king named in the *Acts of Thomas*, were discovered from 1834 onward, lending some historical plausibility to the setting — though scholars still dispute several particulars (e.g., which southern Indian king corresponds to "Mazdai" in the text).
- **Mark → Alexandria (c. AD 42).** The earliest clear witness is Eusebius's *Ecclesiastical History* (composed c. AD 313–325, drawing on earlier now-lost sources) — no surviving 1st- or 2nd-century document directly attests it. The Catholic Encyclopedia itself notes that everything known about earliest Alexandria comes "chiefly through" Eusebius. The tradition is old, unanimous within the Alexandrian/Coptic church, and unchallenged internally, but considerably later-attested than Rome or Antioch.

## Tier 4 — Weak: later legend, actively disputed by scholars

- **Andrew → Byzantium (c. AD 38).** One Catholic commentator directly states this claim "is not supported by documented history and is generally considered a medieval legend" — the church at Constantinople "originated out of the 4th century Eastern Roman Empire," with no 1st- or 2nd-century source connecting it to Andrew. This is the single weakest claim on the list.
- **Bartholomew & Jude (Thaddeus) → Armenia (c. AD 45).** Tied to the Abgar legend (the story of King Abgar of Edessa's conversion), which mainstream historians — Bart Ehrman, Han Drijvers, Walter Bauer among them — treat largely as fiction. No 1st- or 2nd-century document supports the specific apostolic-founding narrative; the Armenian Church's own tradition dates the claim considerably later, and it remains actively disputed rather than merely "later-attested."

## Recommendation

The current Ch. 6 text presents all ten entries with identical certainty. Given the book's own commitment to truth over rhetorical convenience, recommend one of:

1. **Keep the full list but add a single qualifying sentence** before or after it — something like: "Some of these founding dates rest on strong first-century documentation; others rest on ancient and consistent, if later-attested, Church tradition" — without re-litigating each one in the text itself (this document backs it up if ever challenged).
2. **Trim the list to Tier 1 and 2 only** (eight of the ten — everything except Andrew/Byzantium and Bartholomew & Jude/Armenia), which would make the paragraph's claim ("every one of these churches...") airtight without needing any hedge language at all.
3. **Leave the list as-is** and treat this document as background research on file, not a required edit — your call, since the passage is making a cumulative apologetic point, not resting its whole weight on any single entry.
