    Row 1 — Major: virtues do not become vices

    > Genuine truth, justice, and mercy do not become cruelty, vengeance, or enabling. Counterfeits and partial claims masquerading as virtues can do so. Preserve the reality of acquired natural virtue and the supernatural informing role of charity.

    Verdict: UPHOLD. Lands on line 23, with line 21 setting it up.

    Line 23: Isolated from each other, truth becomes cruelty, justice becomes vengeance, and mercy becomes weakness.

    Genuine justice never is vengeance. What the sentence describes is a counterfeit wearing the virtue's name, and conceding the name to the counterfeit is what makes the claim wrong. Real truth stays true however badly it is spoken; real justice stays just.

    The chapter already knows this, twice, and contradicts itself. Line 79 says the harsh truth is "technically accurate, perhaps" — granting that the truth remains true and only the manner turns cruel. And the whole section at 101–107 states the correct doctrine: charity is the form of the virtues, and without it they "remain raw moral material—potentially good, but not yet ordered toward their true purpose." Raw material that is potentially good is exactly not a vice. Line 107 is right; line 23 is the same thought overstated.

    The Thomistic precision is available and cheap: without charity the acquired virtues are real but imperfect — virtue secundum quid, not simpliciter (ST II-II q.23 a.7). Detached from its form a virtue becomes deficient; it does not transform into its opposite. The vice takes the name; the virtue does not become the vice.

    This is the same shape as the Chapter 2 privation overreach in entry 37 — the argument is sound, the universal formulation reaches past it.

    Row 2 — Major: the Aquinas reading

    > Aquinas's account of bonum is not exhausted by truth + justice + mercy, and alicui does not semantically mean sacrifice/cost. Label this as the author's analogy rather than Thomas's hidden anatomy.

    Verdict: UPHOLD on alicui. DOWNGRADE on bonum.

    The citation at line 67 is correct — amor est velle alicui bonum, ST I-II q.26 a.4. Checked.

    The bonum half is already labelled, twice. Line 69: "pull those two halves apart in the light of this book's definition." Line 73: "they are, I am convinced, its interior anatomy." Both mark it as your reading rather than Thomas's text. Codex asked for labelling that is already there. Downgrade.

    The alicui half is a real overreach. Line 71: "And the willing-it-for-you, at cost to me, is sacrifice." And line 73: "sacrifice specifies the for another." Alicui is the dative — "to someone," "for someone." It carries no notion of cost whatsoever. The cost is a true thing about willing another's good; it is not a thing the Latin says. Reading it out of the word is the one place the passage claims Thomas's authority for something Thomas's words do not contain.

    The insight survives intact without the claim: willing a real good for someone who is not me will cost me — that is an argument, and a good one, and it does not need the Latin to have said it.

    Downstream echo, to flag not sweep. chapter_09.md:43 repeats this reading — "Recall Aquinas's definition from Chapter 5… sacrifice is what makes it for another — willed at cost to me." If line 71 changes, that sentence inherits the same issue. One line, later, deliberately — not now.

The Cross is total consecration and atonement and reparation and priestly mediation and substitution and liberation and communion."* That is the row's whole request, answered, and Chapter 5 line 17 already defers to it by name.

    Row 4 — Scripture: the woman caught in adultery

    > John 8 does not show true Mosaic justice demanding the woman's execution until mercy interrupts. The missing man, witness/procedure requirements, trap, and Christ's call from sin matter. Manipulation of law is not "truth without mercy."

    Verdict: UPHOLD — and correcting it makes the chapter's argument stronger, not weaker.

    Lands on line 87 and line 95.

    Line 87: "She has been caught in adultery—the Law of Moses is clear, and they invoke it: she must be stoned." Line 95: "Justice without compassion means execution."

    The Law was not being followed, and two details give it away:

    The man is missing. Leviticus 20:10, RSV: "If a man commits adultery with the wife of his neighbor, both the adulterer and the adulteress shall be put to death." Both. She was taken "in the very act," so the man was identifiable. He is not there. Before Jesus says a word, the proceeding is already lawless.

    And Jesus invokes the Law's own procedure, not an exception to it. Deuteronomy 17:7, RSV: "The hand of the witnesses shall be first against him to put him to death." The witnesses throw first. That is the rule. So "Let him who is without sin among you be the first to throw a stone at her" is not mercy overruling law — it is Jesus applying the statute to witnesses who cannot satisfy it. He upholds the Law by enforcing it against the men holding the stones.

    So line 95's "Justice without compassion means execution" concedes that stoning her would have been justice. It would have been a lynching with a proof-text.

    This is Row 1 again, in narrative form. The Pharisees were not offering truth and justice that needed tempering; they were offering counterfeits wearing those names, which is precisely what Row 1 says the chapter should be distinguishing. The trap at line 87 — "it forces a choice between fragments" — is real, but the trap is a false dilemma rather than a genuine collision of two goods, and saying so costs the chapter nothing and gains it the sharpest illustration in the book of what a counterfeit virtue looks like.

    Line 91 is already half-right: "He upholds its authority while exposing the accusers' own guilt." The fix extends what that sentence already does.

    Row 5 — Judgment

    > "You are going to hell" is never technically accurate as a judgment about a living person's final destiny. Warn of grave danger without presuming damnation.

    Verdict: UPHOLD. Clean, small, and the only appearance of the phrase in the manuscript.

    Lands on line 79. Truth without love becomes harsh and condemning—"You're a sinner and you're going to hell"—technically accurate, perhaps.

    The Church canonizes saints and has never declared any individual damned. No living person's final destiny is known to anyone, so "you're going to hell" is not accurate, technically or otherwise — it is a claim no one is in a position to make.

    ⚠ This fix has to be made carefully, because that clause is doing a second job. "Technically accurate, perhaps" is the phrase that concedes the truth stays true — which is the chapter's own best evidence against Row 1's overreach. Replace the example, not the logic. The sentence needs a statement that genuinely is accurate and genuinely is cruel without mercy — something like naming the gravity of what someone is doing, rather than pronouncing where they will end up. Delete the concession and Row 1 loses its internal witness.

    Row 6 — Sacraments

    > Distinguish Christ's glorified Body, Eucharistic Body, and Mystical Body; Baptismal incorporation and Eucharistic communion; venial wounds and mortal loss of charity; forgiveness, reconciliation, trust, and safety.

    Verdict: UPHOLD on two specific lines. DOWNGRADE the rest of the list.

    Line 121 — "The One Bread and the One Body are not two separate realities—they are one." This flattens a distinction the tradition keeps: the Eucharistic Body is Christ's own body; the Mystical Body is the Church. They are joined as tightly as two things can be — the Eucharist makes the Church — but they are not identical, and Pius XII wrote Mystici Corporis partly to stop the members being collapsed into the Head. Paul's "because there is one bread, we who are many are one body" is a claim about what the bread does, which is stronger and more interesting than an identity claim.

    Line 129 — *"This is why sin is so devastating—it severs the bonds of communion… When you lie, you create distance."* Not all sin severs. CCC 1855, verbatim: mortal sin "destroys charity in the heart of man"; venial sin "allows charity to subsist, even though it offends and wounds it." Most lies are venial. And note the distinction is made of this chapter's own metaphor — wounding a bond versus cutting it — so stating it correctly costs a clause and gains precision the section is already reaching for. The word "venial" appears only in chapter_14.md; this is its natural second home.

    The rest of the row is more than this chapter needs. The glorified Body, baptismal incorporation versus Eucharistic communion, and the forgiveness/reconciliation/trust/safety distinctions belong to a sacraments chapter. Line 121 already defers the unity to Chapter 10 by name. Downgrade.

    Row 7 — Pastoral

    > Divorce does not deterministically deform every future relationship. Justified separation exists, and catechesis is not a substitute for trauma-informed clinical help.

    Verdict: UPHOLD. This is the one I would fix first, for the same reason the safety row went first in Chapter 4.

    Lands on lines 57, 61 and 63.

    • 57: fragmentation doesn't just wound a child, it deforms their capacity for authentic love.
    • 61: They carry these discoveries into every future relationship.
    • 63: The wound creates harm. The fragmentation that follows corrupts.

    Stated as mechanism, without exception. And the reader in front of this page is very often a child of divorce, or a parent who divorced.

    It also now collides with what went into Chapter 4 this afternoon. Chapter 4 line 177 tells a woman in a dangerous marriage that she may leave, that leaving is not a sin against her vows, and that getting her children to safety honours the covenant rather than breaking it. Chapter 5 then tells her, without qualification, that the fragmentation deforms her child's capacity for love and that they will carry it into every future relationship. She has been given permission in one chapter and the bill for it in the next. Chapter 5 never mentions that some separations are necessary and right.

    The chapter does answer itself — ninety lines later. Lines 151–153 are good and should stay: *"The wounds remain real. The patterns run deep. But they are not permanent."* But the strong claim is unqualified where it lands, and a reader who is winded at line 61 may not still be reading at 151.

    On the clinical point. Lines 149–153 make the catechist a physician of the soul, and I would keep that — it is the chapter's warmest passage and it is aimed at your actual readers. But formation and therapy are not substitutes for one another, and one clause saying so protects both the claim and the reader who needs more than a catechist can give.
