# Option 1: Classical form, Premise 1 narrowed to duties

## Opponent

There are two voices, and they argue against each other as well as against the book.

**Voice A: a non-naturalist secular moral realist** (the "robust realism" or "godless normative realism" school: Enoch, Wielenberg, Shafer-Landau, Parfit in his later work). This voice says moral facts are objective, necessary and brute. They are not made of matter, they need no author, and they bind every rational agent the way logical truths hold in every possible world. This is the strongest opponent for Option 1 because the option was built to answer it. Narrowing Premise 1 to duties assumes the realist will concede "no God, no one with authority." The realist concedes that happily and then denies that duties need *anyone* with authority. The whole debate is fought on that ground.

**Voice B: a moral error theorist** (Mackie's queerness argument, as developed by Joyce, plus evolutionary debunking in the line of Street). Voice B is the realist's natural enemy and an awkward ally of Patrick's. It agrees that a categorical, inescapable "ought" would need something extraordinary behind it. Mackie himself granted, in his later book on theism, that objective prescriptive values would lend some support to theism. But Voice B denies Premise 2: nothing really binds. It is brought in because Option 1's Premise 2 ("every person is bound") must survive someone who denies it outright, not only someone who grants it.

## The argument as tested

**As submitted:**
1. If there is no God, there is no one with the authority to bind every person to love.
2. Every person is bound to love: to truth, justice, mercy and sacrifice toward others.
3. Therefore God exists.

**Best-defended form after repair:**
1. Every person is bound to love. We must never betray the truth, deny another what is his due, or harm the innocent, and we must give of ourselves when another's real good requires it. Some of these demands hold even at the cost of our lives.
2. No human being, and not even the whole human race acting together, can release anyone from these demands.
3. A demand that binds every person and that no human can release is owed to Someone above every human being. A duty is something *owed*, and what is owed is owed to someone. Here the argument turns on the difference between guilt and error.
4. If there is no God, there is no such Someone.
5. Therefore God exists: the One who loved us first, whose claim on us is love.

Three changes were needed. The hidden premise (3) is now stated and argued. "Authority to bind" (which sounds like a commander) became "Someone to whom it is owed" (which sounds like a person in communion). And Premise 2 now rests on the clearest, least contested duties, with the facets serving as illustration.

## Debate highlights

**1. The validity gap. (Voice A, opening)**
*Objection:* "As written, the argument is invalid. Premise 1 says no God means no one with authority to bind. Premise 2 says everyone is bound. The conclusion needs a third premise: if everyone is bound, then someone binds them. I deny that premise. I grant your Premise 1 completely, since no human person has that authority. Duties need no binder. Narrowing Premise 1 hasn't removed the load. It has moved the load to a premise you didn't write down."
*Best reply:* Grant it, and write the premise down. The binding the book means is answerability, and answerability is always answerability *to* someone (line 105: "an ought is a claim laid on a person").
*Held?* **Failed as submitted. Held with repair.** This is the central finding. The narrowed Premise 1 is now almost uncontested, which is what Patrick wanted. But the argument's real weight sits in the unstated premise, and that premise is Option 2's Premise 2. Option 1 does not avoid the dispute. It hides it.

**2. Brute duties, like mathematics. (Voice A)**
*Objection:* "'Gratuitous cruelty is wrong' is as necessary as '7 + 5 = 12', and no one authored arithmetic. Your own chapter says so at line 77: mathematics binds quantities without asking our consent. Necessary truths need no lawgiver."
*Best reply:* The two kinds of law bind in different ways. Arithmetic binds *quantities*. It has never asked anything of anyone. A duty *addresses a person*, and breaking it leaves you guilty, not merely mistaken (line 107). Getting a sum wrong makes you wrong. Betraying a friend makes you wrong *toward someone*. Guilt has the form of answerability, and answerability has a "to whom."
*Counter:* "Guilt is a feeling. Its fitting object is the victim. You've shown duties are owed to persons, not that they're owed to God."
*Held?* **Held with repair, partly.** The address/guilt point is real and the book should make it. But it pushes the debate to the next question (exchange 3), and line 77's mathematics analogy works against this option. A careless reader will hear "laws bind without consent and without persons," which is the realist's point.

**3. Authority is shared out among us. (Voice A, the second-personal / contractualist move associated with Darwall and Scanlon)**
*Objection:* "Premise 1 is false after all. Every person has the standing to make demands on every other person, and to hold them accountable. That shared standing is all the 'authority' morality needs. Duties are owed to the neighbor, and the neighbor is enough."
*Best reply, in three parts:*
(a) *Where does the standing come from?* It is either one more brute moral fact, which takes us back to exchange 2, or a presupposition built into the practice of making claims. The chapter already answers the second version at line 103: a necessity of our practice is not yet a truth about the world.
(b) *Unwaivability.* If the duty were owed only to the neighbor, the neighbor could release me from it. He cannot, at least not for the gravest duties. A people cannot vote away the wrong of enslaving a minority, and the whole human race could not do it together. Some claims also bind with no one present to make them: the newborn who cannot yet demand anything, the dead whose wishes I promised to keep, the wrong done in secret that no one will ever discover. If no one on earth can cancel a claim, no one on earth laid it.
(c) *The book's own ground.* The standing each person has is exactly the dignity *Dignitas Infinita* 15 calls prior to any recognition, the dignity the chapter grounds in the Creator's love (line 99; ST I q.20 a.2). The second-personalist is right that the neighbor has a claim on me. He has not explained why that claim cannot be bought, sold or voted away.
*Held?* **Held with repair.** This exchange forced the wording change from "authority to bind" to "Someone to whom it is owed, and whom no human can overrule." Unwaivability turned out to be the option's strongest support. The chapter does not yet use it.

**4. A Euthyphro dilemma about authority. (Voice A)**
*Objection:* "Why must anyone obey God? If you answer 'because He commands it,' you are going in a circle. If you answer 'because He is good,' then goodness is doing the binding, God's authority adds nothing, and we are back to a brute moral fact. Your Premise 1 rests either on voluntarism (the view that duties exist because God wills them) or on nothing."
*Best reply:* The Church is not voluntarist. Chapter 3 already holds that goodness is neither above God nor invented by Him. It is His own nature. The natural law is our share in His eternal wisdom (ST I-II q.91 a.2), and its first principle, that good is to be done and pursued, is self-evident to reason (q.94 a.2). It is not an extra decree added on top. So the binding does come from the Good, as the realist says. The difference is that this Good is a Person. That is the only place where "what binds" and "Someone to whom it is owed" are the same thing, because in God goodness, wisdom and personhood are not three parts but one (simplicity).
*Counter:* "So your 'authority' language was voluntarist after all."
*Held?* **Held with repair.** Phrases like "authority to bind" and "laws require lawgivers" (lines 23, 133, 137), set beside the gravity analogy, sound like a commander-God whose will makes the rules. That invites exactly this attack. Reframe as owing and being owed, not commanding and obeying.

**5. "Your own scholastics disagree." (Voice A, citing the Catholic tradition against itself)**
*Objection:* "A late-medieval scholastic line, often traced to Gregory of Rimini, held that sinning against right reason would still be wrong even if, impossibly, God did not exist. Gabriel Vázquez, SJ, held that natural law binds by its own rationality, not by command. Modern Catholic natural-law theorists (Grisez, Finnis) say the basic goods and first practical principles are self-evident without reference to God. Your Premise 1 is not even the Catholic consensus."
*Best reply:* Francisco Suárez, SJ, answered this in his own day (*De legibus* II, ch. 6; verify the chapter). The goodness or badness of an act is intrinsic to it, but *law in the strict sense*, with obligation, needs the will of a superior. That is why the natural law is truly a *law* and not just good advice. Aquinas defines law as reason's ordering for the common good, given by the one who has care of the community and made known (ST I-II q.90 a.4). And the Catechism holds both sides: sin is an offense against reason and right conscience (CCC 1849) *and* an offense against God (CCC 1850). Knowing the law without God is possible (line 119). The law existing without God is a different claim.
*Held?* **Held with repair, and this is the most important caution for the book.** The tradition is divided about whether the *thin* sense of wrongness ("this act is bad") would survive without God. It is united that law, obligation and guilt as offense against Someone require God. The argument must claim only the thick sense. The book should never suggest the Church teaches that without God nothing would be bad. Line 41 ("Without God, morality becomes subjective") and line 133 ("they collapse into nothing more than human opinion") both overreach on this point.

**6. Nothing binds at all. (Voice B, the error theorist)**
*Objection:* "Voice A has just admitted that inescapable authority is the strange part. I agree with your Premise 1: such authority would be queer, the kind of thing only a god could supply. So I deny Premise 2. Moral thought presupposes a categorical 'must' that the world does not contain. Evolution explains why we feel it."
*Best reply:* (a) The Moorean answer: I am more sure that torturing a child for fun is wrong *for everyone, whatever they want* than I am of any premise in the queerness argument. A clever argument against something more certain than its own premises refutes itself. (b) The "companions in guilt" answer, already at lines 43 and 105: the error theorist asks us to believe him because the evidence *requires* it, a categorical "ought" of reasoning. If such oughts are too queer to exist, his case asks nothing of us. If they exist, queerness is no objection.
*Counter:* "Epistemic 'oughts' are only hypothetical: *if* you want truth, follow the evidence. And my Moorean certainty is just what evolution would install."
*Reply:* If debunking by evolution cancels conscience, it cancels the reasoning faculty that runs the debunking (line 43). And "if you want truth" does not capture what we mean when we call someone who believes against the evidence *irrational*. We mean he has failed at something every mind owes.
*Held?* **Held.** Premise 2 survives the error theorist at full strength. But line 103's claim that the denial "refutes itself in the speaking" is too quick. A careful error theorist denies *moral* oughts, not every ought, so the self-refutation has to be argued through the parity with reasoning, not asserted.

**7. Debunking turned on the realist. (Patrick's side going on offense, Voice A defending)**
*Patrick's side:* The error theorist's debunking hits the godless realist harder than it hits the theist. If moral facts are causally inert and brute, why would minds shaped by survival track them? The theist has an answer: the same Love that grounds the law wrote it on the heart (Romans 2:14-15; CCC 1954-1956).
*Voice A's reply:* The "third-factor" answer associated with Enoch. Survival and flourishing are themselves good, so selection pressures pointed us roughly toward the good.
*Patrick's side:* That works for caring for kin and returning favors. It works worst exactly where the book's love is most demanding: mercy to enemies, giving yourself for a stranger, telling the truth when a lie would pay. Those are the claims the book puts at the center (Matthew 5:44; the Good Samaritan, Luke 10:29-37), and they are what selection would have filtered *out*.
*Held?* **Held.** This is a real gain the chapter does not yet make. Line 43 uses debunking only against materialism. It works against the godless realist too.

**8. Love cannot be commanded. (Voice A, as an internal critique of the book)**
*Objection:* "Chapter 4 says a gift that is forced is no gift, and line 77 says this law 'is not imposed the way gravity is.' Option 1 says every person is *bound* to love by an authority. Which is it? Love done under orders is not love."
*Best reply:* The tradition holds both. Charity falls under a precept (ST II-II q.44 a.1). Benedict XVI gave the reason in a single line: love can be "commanded" because it has first been given (*Deus Caritas Est* 14; verify through the quote cache). He loved us first (1 John 4:19). The New Law is a law of love, not fear (CCC 1972). The claim on us is the claim of a Lover who gave first, not a ruler who imposes. Freedom is not cancelled. A claim is something you can refuse, and only a free creature can be bound by one.
*Held?* **Held with repair.** The option has to show grace first inside the argument itself, by naming the Lawgiver as the One who loved first. Otherwise the syllogism contradicts Chapters 4 and 12's own "law as gift" passage.

**9. Bound by Someone they have never heard of? (Voice A)**
*Objection:* "If every person is bound by God's claim, then the honest atheist and the person who has never heard the Gospel are bound by an authority they do not know. Either they are guilty for not knowing, which contradicts your CCC 847, or God's claim does nothing and the duties stand on their own."
*Best reply:* The natural law is made known through nature itself: God puts it in the mind (ST I-II q.90 a.4 ad 1; Romans 2:14-15). One can be bound by a law without knowing the name of the One who wrote it, which is exactly line 95's "light without yet knowing its name." How guilty someone is depends on knowledge and consent (CCC 1857-1860). Ignorance that is no one's fault reduces or removes guilt, and grace can be at work in a person who does not yet know the Father's name (CCC 847; LG 16). Knowing and grounding are different (line 119): God's claim is the ground, not a fact everyone has to be told before they are bound.
*Held?* **Held.** This exchange fits the book's rules well. The option treats unbelievers as bound and known by God, not as guilty.

**10. Are mercy and sacrifice really duties for everyone? (Voice A)**
*Objection:* "Even many realists hold that forgiveness and self-sacrifice go beyond duty: admirable, not required. Your own Church separates what is commanded from the counsels, which are invitations. Premise 2 claims too much."
*Best reply:* Narrow the premise to its hardest core. Some acts must never be done, by anyone, in any circumstance (*Veritatis Splendor* 52). In those cases the duty of sacrifice is the duty to suffer rather than do evil, which is the witness of the martyrs (VS 90-94). And the duty to give of oneself when another's real need requires it binds everyone (Luke 10:29-37). Forgiveness is commanded for Christians (Matthew 6:14-15; CCC 2843). The argument does not need the opponent to grant it, though. One duty that binds every person is enough for the premise.
*Held?* **Held with repair.** As submitted, Premise 2 is hostage to its most debatable clause. Use the facets as illustration and rest the premise on the duties no serious realist rejects.

**11. Stopping points on equal terms. (Voice A, closing)**
*Objection:* "In the end, your God's goodness is as brute as my moral facts. You stop where I stop, and you add a very large extra entity. By parsimony, and by any honest weighing of probabilities, my view is the simpler one."
*Best reply:* God is not one more brute item on the list. He is the One whose essence is to exist (ST I q.3 a.4). One stopping point explains being, mind, persons, our knowledge of the moral law (exchange 7), why the law cannot be waived (exchange 3), why breaking it is guilt toward Someone (exchange 2), and why it calls us to communion. The realist's facts explain only what is right and wrong, and they leave open why they apply to us, how we know them, and to whom we answer. That is line 107's honest comparison of stopping points.
*Held?* **Held only as a comparison.** This is the second central finding. At the decisive point, Option 1's deductive form becomes an inference to the best explanation. Against a well-armed realist, the syllogism alone does not force the conclusion. It wins only when paired with line 107's comparison. A rational realist can remain unpersuaded, which is consistent with CCC 35: reason's witness, not a replacement for faith.

## Faults found (ranked by severity)

1. **Hidden premise. Critical; fixable.** The argument is invalid without "if every person is bound, someone binds (or is owed)." That premise is exactly what the strongest opponent denies. Narrowing Premise 1 makes Premise 1 nearly uncontested but moves the whole burden into a premise that is never stated. Fix: state it and argue it through guilt as answerability, unwaivability, and address. At that point Option 1 contains Option 2.
2. **Thin and thick "bound." Severe; fixable.** Premise 2 is most obviously true in the thin sense ("this is wrong"). Premise 1 and the hidden premise are true only in the thick sense ("I am answerable to Someone"). If the two senses blur, the argument equivocates. Fix: Premise 2 must claim, and show, the thick sense (unwaivable, answerable, guilt rather than error).
3. **Voluntarist overtones. Severe; fixable.** "Authority to bind," "laws require lawgivers" and the gravity analogy (lines 23, 133, 137) suggest a commander-God whose will makes the rules. That invites a Euthyphro dilemma about authority, and it sits badly with Chapter 3 and with ST I-II q.91 a.2 / q.94 a.2. Fix: "One to whom love is owed, who loved us first."
4. **Overreach against the tradition. Moderate to severe; fixable.** Lines 41 and 133 ("morality becomes subjective," "collapse into nothing more than human opinion") claim more than the Catholic tradition holds (Gregory of Rimini's line, Vázquez, new natural law). Claim only that law, obligation and guilt as offense against Someone need God (Suárez; CCC 1849-1850).
5. **Premise 2 hostage to mercy and sacrifice. Moderate; fixable.** If these are read as universal positive duties, opponents (and the precept/counsel distinction) push back. Rest the premise on exceptionless negative duties and "suffer rather than do evil" (VS 52, 90-94).
6. **Grace first not visible. Moderate; fixable.** A bare "every person is bound" reads as law before gift and seems to contradict Chapter 4 and line 77. Name the Lawgiver as the One who loved first (1 John 4:19; DCE 14).
7. **The mathematics analogy cuts the wrong way. Moderate; fixable.** Line 77 says mathematics binds without consent, which is the realist's best point. Keep the contrast it goes on to draw (this law addresses persons) and drop or sharpen the parallel.
8. **The deductive form cannot win alone. Structural; only partly fixable.** At the bottom, the dispute is which stopping point explains more. Option 1 needs line 107's comparison to finish the job. Fix: present it as one strand of a converging case (CCC 31), not as a proof that stands by itself.
9. **"The denial refutes itself" is too quick. Minor; fixable.** Line 103 should argue the parity with reasoning (as lines 43 and 105 do), not assert self-refutation.
10. **"Toward others" leaves out duties to God and to oneself. Minor; fixable.** Either drop "toward others" or note that love's demands also run toward God and oneself.

## New insights

- **Unwaivability is the strongest evidence for a personal ground, and the book doesn't use it.** No victim, nation or human race can release anyone from the gravest duties. A claim no one on earth can cancel was not laid by anyone on earth. This answers the most sophisticated secular view (that obligation is shared among persons) better than anything now in the chapter.
- **Guilt as answerability.** Line 107 names "guilty rather than merely mistaken" but doesn't unpack it. Guilt has a "to whom" built into it. That is the bridge from "bound" to "Someone."
- **Debunking hits the godless realist too, at love's most demanding points.** Mercy to enemies and self-gift for strangers are what selection would filter out. A realist who relies on "evolution tracked the good" has his worst case where the book's love is most itself.
- **Owed, not imposed.** Recasting the Lawgiver as the One to whom love is owed, who gave it first, fits the book's covenant and communion themes better than the language of legislation. It also defuses the Euthyphro dilemma about authority.
- **An honest map of the Catholic tradition.** The Church's thinkers have argued over whether "bad" would survive without God. They agree that *law* and *sin as offense against God* would not (CCC 1849-1850). Saying so makes the book harder to refute, not easier.
- **A claim can be refused; a force cannot.** Being bound to love is evidence of freedom, not a threat to it. That ties Option 1 to Chapter 4.

## Lines worth keeping

- "Arithmetic binds numbers. It has never once asked anything of you."
- "Guilt is not the feeling of getting a sum wrong. It is the knowledge that you have wronged someone."
- "Your victim cannot release you. Your nation cannot vote it away. The whole human race together could not repeal it. A claim no one on earth can cancel was not laid by anyone on earth."
- "The One who commands love is the One who gave it first."
- "You can be bound by a law without knowing the name of the One who wrote it."
- "Every account stops somewhere. The question is whether it stops at a fact that explains nothing else, or at a Person who explains everything else."

## Risks to the book's rules

- **Overclaiming (CCC 35).** The classical syllogism *looks* like a proof. Present it as reason's witness and pair it with line 107's honest comparison. "Therefore God exists" should be framed as what the argument points to, not a checkmate.
- **Doctrinal drift toward voluntarism.** "Laws require lawgivers" and "authority to bind" can read as duties existing *because* God wills them. That contradicts Chapter 3's Euthyphro answer and Thomistic natural law. Keep Chapter 3's "His own nature" language in view.
- **Misstating the tradition.** Saying "without God nothing would be wrong" goes beyond what Catholic thinkers agree on. Claim law and guilt, not bare badness.
- **Divine simplicity.** If Premise 2 lists sacrifice as a demand of the law and the law is God's nature, avoid saying God's essence is sacrifice. Keep the Trinitarian clarification: total self-gift, no loss.
- **Culpability.** "Every person is bound" must not slide into "every unbeliever is guilty." Keep CCC 847 / LG 16 and CCC 1857-1860 nearby (exchange 9).
- **Protestant authorities.** This argument's modern popular form is strongly tied to Protestant apologists. Don't cite them, and don't echo their exact syllogism (the current lines 33-35 are that formula nearly word for word). Ground it in Catholic sources instead: Aquinas (ST I-II q.90-94), Suárez, *Veritatis Splendor*, *Deus Caritas Est*, CCC 1849-1850, 1954-1960.
- **Grotius.** The opponent's best historical citation ("even if God did not exist") is often credited to Grotius, a Protestant. If the book addresses the objection, attribute it to the earlier Catholic scholastic line, or to no one. Never cite Grotius as an authority.
- **Vocabulary.** Watch for "pillars," "the four facets," "transaction" and "performed" in any new prose.
- **Verification.** Run DCE 14, VS 52 and 90-94, ST I-II q.90 a.4 ad 1, Suárez *De legibus* II.6, and CCC 1849-1850 / 1857-1860 / 2843 through the quote cache before anything is quoted.

## Verdict

| Criterion | Score |
|---|---|
| Soundness | 5/10 as submitted (invalid without the hidden premise); 7/10 as repaired |
| Robustness against the best opponent | 4/10 against the godless realist as submitted; 6/10 repaired; 8/10 against the error theorist |
| Rhetorical power | 7/10 (familiar, crisp; unwaivability lines raise it) |
| Fit with the book | 6/10 as submitted (legislative tone, grace-first absent); 8/10 repaired |

**Bottom line.** Narrowing Premise 1 to duties does what Patrick wanted for that premise. "Without God, no one has authority over every person" is something even a secular realist will grant. But the burden doesn't disappear. It moves into an unstated step, "if everyone is bound, someone binds," and that step is exactly what the strongest opponent denies. Left unstated, the argument is invalid. Stated, it becomes Option 2's claim that obligation is something laid on a person by a person, and it has to be argued. It can be argued well: through guilt as answerability, through duties no human can release, and through the debunking problem that hits the godless realist hardest where love is most demanding. Even then, at the bottom it is a comparison of stopping points (line 107), not a deduction that forces the conclusion. Option 1 is worth keeping only in repaired form, with the hidden premise written out, "authority" recast as "the One to whom love is owed, who loved us first," and the whole presented as one strand of a converging case. By itself it is the weakest of the four shapes against a professional opponent, because its classic form hides where the real fight is.

## Suggested wording

1. Every person is bound to love: never to betray the truth, deny another his due, or harm the innocent; and to give himself when another's good requires it, some things even at the cost of his life.
2. No one can release us from this: not our victim, not our nation, not the whole human race together.
3. What binds every person, and what no human can cancel, is owed to Someone above every human being. That is why breaking it leaves us guilty, not merely mistaken.
4. If there is no God, there is no such Someone.
5. Therefore God exists: the One who loved us first, and whose claim on us is love.

*Placement:* this replaces the classical three-line syllogism at lines 31-35, with the Premise 1 and 2 subsections retitled to match. It should be followed directly by the honest comparison of stopping points now at line 107, moved up so the reader sees at once that this is a comparison and not a checkmate.
