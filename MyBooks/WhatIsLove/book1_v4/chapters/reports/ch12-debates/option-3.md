# Option 3: The Argument from Communion (mock debate)

## Opponent

**Two voices, taking turns.**

1. **A Buddhist teacher** trained in the Theravada texts and familiar with Madhyamaka. She holds three things. Craving (*taṇhā*), and above all the craving to go on existing as a self, is the root of suffering. There is no enduring self that could be "loved wholly and forever." The cure for the ache is to end the craving, not to find something that fills it.
2. **A philosophical naturalist** who works on evolutionary psychology and philosophy of mind. Her fallback position is the absurdist one: the gap between what we long for and what the world gives is simply the human condition.

**Why these two.** Option 3 rests on a longing, so the strongest opponents are the ones who can explain that longing without God. The Buddhist accepts the premise that no finite thing satisfies us (premise 3), then turns it against the argument. On her reading the restlessness is the disease, not a signpost. The naturalist attacks premise 4 (longings correspond to real goods) at its root. Natural selection explains why hunger is paired with food, and that same mechanism predicts an open-ended desire with no matching object. Together they cover both ways the argument can fail. One says the longing is a disorder. The other says it is a by-product of evolution.

---

## The argument as tested

This is the final, best-defended form after the repairs made during the debate. Changes from the brief's version are marked [R].

1. **We are made for communion.** Beneath every lesser want, we long to love and to be loved, wholly and without end. The evidence is experience: loneliness, grief, and love's refusal to accept the death of the beloved. [R: P1 now rests on experience. CCC 27 is cited only after the conclusion.]
2. **Only a person can love us back.** A law, a force or an idea can bind us or move us. It cannot know us, and it cannot receive or return a gift of self. (The chapter already says this at line 119.)
3. **No finite person can love us wholly and forever.** No creature knows us all the way down, so none can receive the whole of us. None can hold us past death. Even the marriage vow is for "all the days of my life." [R: "no human" became "no finite person". "Wholly" now means *knows and receives the whole of us, without limit*, not "gives everything it has", which a spouse truly does.]
4. **The deepest longings built into our nature are not the kind of thing that has no object.** Every other natural hunger has its bread. The will reaches for good as such, which is why every finite good shows up to us as finite (ST I-II q.2 a.8, read as an account of the will, not as a premise). [R: P4 is no longer stated as a universal law of correspondence. It is now a weighted clue inside an explanatory comparison.]
5. **So the best explanation is a Person without limit, who knows us wholly and can hold us forever, and who loved us first** (1 John 4:19). Revelation gives that Person His name: God is Love (1 John 4:8). [R: framed as a best explanation under CCC 35, not as a proof. Grace comes first: God starts the love and does not merely return ours.]
6. **Patrick's insight, repaired.** The most perfect love is one given whole and received whole in turn. No one else can receive the whole of us, because no one else knows the whole of us; not even we do. The Author can. When we give ourselves to Him, we are given back to ourselves whole (GS 24; Mt 16:25). [R: "who could *return* our love" became "who could *receive* it whole, having first given His".]

## Debate highlights

### Exchange 1: Counterexamples to premise 4
**Objection (naturalist).** People long to fly, to be eighteen again, to be adored by a stranger they have never met. Those longings have no object. "Longings correspond to real goods" is false as stated.
**Reply.** The argument concerns *natural* desires: universal, present before any culture shapes them, and not made up out of fantasy. Hunger, thirst, the desire to know, and the desire for companionship are natural. The wish to fly is a picture we form, not a need we have. Aquinas reasons from natural desire in exactly this sense (ST I q.12 a.1; I q.75 a.6).
**Did it hold?** **Held with repair.** P4 must say "natural" and define it. The repair opens the next exchange, though, because the naturalist now asks what makes a desire natural and why natural ones should come paired with an object.

### Exchange 2: Selection explains the pairing, and the explanation does not reach this case (the hardest blow)
**Objection (naturalist).** Hunger matches food because animals that hungered for things that do not exist died out. Selection explains the pairing only where the object exists and affects survival. A longing for infinite, everlasting love could not have been selected *for* its object. It is what you would expect as a by-product of the open-ended dissatisfaction that keeps a primate striving, the hedonic treadmill. Your examples of hunger and food are explained by a mechanism that predicts the opposite in your key case. The teleological version ("a natural desire cannot be in vain") assumes that nature is ordered by a mind, which is the conclusion you are trying to reach.
**Reply.** First, a parity argument from line 43. The desire to know also outruns survival: pure mathematics, the age of galaxies. The naturalist still trusts it. Second, a shift in what P4 claims. Aquinas's point in ST I-II q.2 a.8 is not "I feel a longing, therefore its object exists." It is structural: the will's object is good *as such*, so every finite good registers as *not enough*. Treadmill dissatisfaction is a craving for *more of the same*. What the lonely or grieving person aches for is a *someone*: to be known and kept. The question then becomes which account explains a longing with *that* content.
**Counter.** The naturalist trusts reason where its predictions are checked. The longing for infinite love makes no checkable prediction.
**Did it hold?** **Failed as a deductive premise. Held with repair as a clue in a best-explanation argument.** No reply forces the naturalist to grant P4 as a law. P4 can survive only as one weight in a comparison of explanations, the way line 107 compares stopping points.

### Exchange 3: Citing God to prove God
**Objection (naturalist, who has read Catholic theology).** Your premise 1 is credited to CCC 27, which states that man is "created by God and for God". ST I-II q.2 a.8 and CCC 1718 are about *where* happiness lies, *given* God. All three assume the conclusion. A second point: if a natural desire *must* be fulfilled, then God owes you heaven, and your own Church denies that (Pius XII, *Humani Generis* 26).
**Reply.** Both points are conceded. The Church's texts name what reason finds; they are not premises for someone outside the faith. They should follow the conclusion ("the Church has always said this"). The gratuity point is serious. The argument may conclude only that the Good *is real*, not that our nature is owed Him or can reach Him on its own. Beatitude is the "entirely free gift of God" (CCC 1722).
**Did it hold?** **Held with repair.** Move the citations after the conclusion. State the conclusion as "the Good exists", not "we are owed it". This exchange also supplied a useful clarification: hunger shows that food exists, not that every hungry person eats. The argument leaves room for freedom.

### Exchange 4: The longing is the disease (Buddhist, on premise 1)
**Objection (Buddhist).** You have described craving perfectly and called it a signpost. A desire to be held wholly and forever is craving for existence in its purest form. Your premise 3 is our First Noble Truth, and we agree on the diagnosis. The cure is to let the craving go, not to posit an infinite object for it.
**Reply.** The Church also respects this diagnosis: Buddhism recognizes the "radical insufficiency" of this changing world (*Nostra Aetate* 2). Catholic teaching likewise separates disordered craving (concupiscence, CCC 2515) from the natural desire for the good, and it calls for detachment from creatures. But the Buddhist path keeps one desire: the wholesome aspiration to be free. The Pali texts themselves speak of abandoning desire *by means of* desire (SN 51.15), and of nirvana as the highest happiness (Dhammapada 204). So even the Buddhist does not end all longing. She keeps the longing for the Unconditioned.
**Counter.** That aspiration is a raft. It falls away when the shore is reached, and the Unconditioned is not a someone.
**Did it hold?** **Held in part.** The exchange narrows the dispute to a real fork. Both sides agree that the conditioned cannot satisfy. They disagree on whether the Unconditioned can love. This is the honest place to stand, and it is not polemical.

### Exchange 5: Wanting to be loved back is attachment (Buddhist, on Patrick's insight)
**Objection (Buddhist).** Patrick says the most perfect love is "given back whole in turn." That is love with strings attached. *Mettā* and compassion flow out and ask nothing. Your own Gospel says to love enemies who will never love you back.
**Reply.** Love *gives* without conditions, but its *fulfilment* is communion. Aquinas calls charity a friendship with God, and friendship requires love on both sides (ST II-II q.23 a.1). Wanting the beloved to love you back is not grasping. It is wanting the beloved's own good, because to love is that person's deepest good. A love that never hoped for return would not want the beloved fully alive. The highest form is the Trinity: the Father gives Himself wholly, and the Son gives Himself wholly back, with total self-gift and no loss. The order matters, though. With us, God gives first (1 John 4:19). He does not "return" our love; our love is the answer to His.
**Did it hold?** **Held with repair.** The insight survives, and it is the best thing in the option, but it needs wording that puts grace first.

### Exchange 6: No one is there to be loved forever (Buddhist, on no-self)
**Objection (Buddhist).** Premise 1 needs a lasting self, and there isn't one. "You" are a changing stream of form, feeling, perception, mental formations and consciousness. The "whole you" you want received is a construct.
**Reply.** Augustine agrees that we are a mystery to ourselves (*mihi quaestio factus sum*, Conf. IV.4; X.33). That is exactly why only the Author can receive the whole of us. Every vow and covenant (Ch. 4) also assumes someone who persists to keep it.
**Counter.** Persons are real only conventionally, and a conventional truth is enough for vows.
**Did it hold?** **Failed against this opponent on her own ground.** The argument assumes that persons are real. Settling that question takes a separate metaphysical case, which the book makes elsewhere (Ch. 3 on dignity; *Dignitas Infinita* 15). For readers who grant that persons are real, which is nearly all of Patrick's readers, it holds.

### Exchange 7: A good marriage does return love wholly (naturalist, on premise 3)
**Objection (naturalist).** Your Church calls marriage a *total* gift of self, and your Chapter 10 praises it. Happily married people report deep fulfilment. Either premise 3 is false or your theology of marriage is. The "infinite" longing is a theologian's inflation.
**Reply.** A spouse can truly give *everything they have*. What no spouse can do is know and receive *everything we are*, or hold us past death. The Church's own marriage vow is for "all the days of my life," and the bond ends at death (Rom 7:2; Mt 22:30). The evidence that love wants more than that is grief. Augustine's account of the friend he lost (Conf. IV) shows a man who loved a mortal as though he would never die. The Catholic philosopher Gabriel Marcel held (paraphrased) that to love someone is somehow to refuse their death. We do not grieve because love failed. We grieve because love was meant to last.
**Did it hold?** **Held with repair.** P3 must say "no *finite person*" (which also covers angels and any other created minds) and spell out "wholly". It must also honour human love as a real communion and an image of God's love, never treat it as a failure.

### Exchange 8: Projection (naturalist)
**Objection (naturalist).** Attachment research explains the longing. The infant needs a caregiver who seems all-powerful, and the adult projects that need onto the sky. This is wish-fulfilment.
**Reply.** Where a belief comes from does not settle whether it is true, and the parity argument of line 43 applies again. The God this longing meets also judges, commands the Cross and asks for sacrifice (Ch. 9), which is not what a wish would invent. The longing also persists in adults who had good parents, and they still grieve.
**Did it hold?** **Held weakly.** The reply neutralizes the objection but gains no ground.

### Exchange 9: The argument makes hiddenness worse (naturalist)
**Objection (naturalist, using the divine hiddenness argument).** The more you insist that a perfect Lover made us *for communion*, the worse His silence looks. A perfectly loving person would make Himself available to every sincere seeker, yet many seek and hear nothing.
**Reply.** Love woos; it does not overpower. A gift forced is no gift (Ch. 4). God "never ceases to draw" (CCC 27); the Church does not say He compels. Communion can be real before it is recognized: line 95 speaks of following a light without yet knowing its name, and CCC 847 of grace at work in those who do not know Him. St. Teresa of Calcutta lived for decades in felt darkness while in deep union. Feeling God's absence is not the same as His being absent.
**Did it hold?** **Held with repair, at a cost.** The chapter must name this objection if it adopts Option 3. Otherwise the argument reads as naive. The reply must not suggest that every non-believer is resisting God (CCC 847).

### Exchange 10: The absurdist fallback (naturalist)
**Objection (naturalist).** Grant everything. The longing could still simply be tragic. We are the creature whose hunger outruns its world. Honesty means living with that, not inventing bread.
**Reply.** Apply line 107: every account stops somewhere. The absurdist stops at a nature that gave one animal, alone, a hunger the world has no food for, and calls the deepest thing in us a mistake. The absurdist also goes on living as though love keeps its promises: keeping vows, grieving, refusing to betray. The communion strand does not stand alone. The same love that *longs* also *binds* (the obligation strand). The hunger and the law point to one Person, and that convergence is what the chapter's title says: the Lawgiver *is* Love.
**Did it hold?** **Held as a best explanation inside a converging case. It does not hold alone.**

## Faults found (ranked by severity)

1. **Premise 4 as a universal correspondence law (critical).** Natural selection explains the hunger-and-food pairing and predicts no such pairing for an infinite object. The teleological version assumes a designer. *Fixable only by changing its role:* turn it into a weighted clue in an explanatory comparison and ground it in Aquinas's account of the will's object (good as such), not in the hunger analogy alone.
2. **Circular citations (serious, easy fix).** P1 is credited to CCC 27, and ST I-II q.2 a.8 and CCC 1718 support the conclusion. All three assume God. Move them after the conclusion as the Church naming what reason found. Note also that CCC 27 does not say "love and be loved wholly". CCC 1604 (love as the "fundamental and innate vocation" of every human being) and GS 19 (dignity rooted in the call to communion with God) fit P1 better.
3. **Logical gaps (serious, fixable).** "Corresponds to a real good" does not yet mean "the object exists". "No human" leaves room for other finite persons. "The One who is Love" goes beyond what reason reaches, which is an unlimited personal Good. Fixes: write "no finite person"; conclude "a Person without limit"; then give the name from Revelation (1 John 4:8).
4. **"Wholly" collides with the theology of marriage (serious, fixable).** Spouses do give themselves totally (Ch. 10). Split "wholly" into *knows the whole of us / can receive the whole of us / forever*.
5. **Gratuity of grace (doctrinally serious, fixable).** If natural longing *guarantees* fulfilment, beatitude becomes owed (*Humani Generis* 26). Conclude only that the Good is real. Fulfilment is free gift (CCC 1722) and depends on our free yes.
6. **Patrick's insight puts grace second (moderate, fixable).** "Who could *return* our love" makes God the responder. Reword so that God gives first and receives our gift whole.
7. **Exposure to the hiddenness objection (moderate, fixable).** Option 3 invites this objection more than Options 1 and 2 do. It needs one paragraph that names it (see Exchange 9).
8. **Assumes that persons are real (moderate, not fixable inside this argument).** Against a convinced Buddhist the argument does not reach the fork without a separate case. It is mitigated by Ch. 3 and by the shared-diagnosis approach (*Nostra Aetate* 2).
9. **Projection (minor).** Neutralized, not answered.
10. **The hunger-and-food analogy is widely associated with a Protestant apologist (minor, fixable).** Root it in Augustine (Conf. I.1) and Aquinas, cite Kreeft (Catholic) if a modern source is wanted, and keep the wording Patrick's own.
11. **Existing line 111 overclaims (minor).** It moves from the body's reactions to "evidence that we were *designed* for love". If Option 3 goes in, line 111 should be absorbed into it and toned down to "a clue".

## New insights

- **The title needs both strands.** "The Lawgiver Is Love" makes two claims. The obligation strand shows a *Lawgiver*; the communion strand shows that the Lawgiver *is Love*. Option 3 is the missing second half, not a replacement for the moral argument.
- **The real lack is in *receiving*, not just giving.** No human can receive the whole of us, because no one knows the whole of us; we are a question even to ourselves (Augustine). Only the Author knows the work, so only He can receive the gift whole and give us back to ourselves (GS 24; Mt 16:25). This sharpens Patrick's insight from "return" to "receive and give back whole".
- **Love itself refuses death.** The evidence for P1 and P3 is not only desire but the shape of love: grief, Augustine's Book IV, Marcel, and the Church's own vow, "all the days of my life." This evidence comes from inside love, which is the book's own ground.
- **Wanting to be loved back is not selfish.** It is wanting the beloved's good, because to love is the beloved's highest good (ST II-II q.23 a.1). This answers both the Buddhist and the "pure, disinterested love" objection.
- **Hunger shows that food exists, not that everyone eats.** The argument leaves freedom intact (Ch. 4). The Good is real, and receiving Him is our yes.
- **The chapter already holds this strand in pieces.** Line 95 (the pull toward the Good, which is really the argument of ST I-II q.2 a.8), line 111 (the ache), line 119 ("can never love anyone back; communion happens only between persons"), line 179 (hunger and thirst), and line 191 (CCC 1718). Option 3 gathers what is already there; little new text is needed.
- **A respectful way to address the Buddhist named at line 121.** Agree on the diagnosis (*Nostra Aetate* 2), then name the fork: can the Unconditioned love?

- "No one can receive all of you, because no one knows all of you — not even you. The One who made you does."
- "Give yourself to anyone else, and they receive a part of you. Give yourself to the One who made you, and you are given back whole."
- "We don't grieve because love failed. We grieve because love was meant to last."
- "Even the marriage vow is for 'all the days of my life.' Love wants more days than that."
- "Every hunger built into us has its bread. It would be strange if the deepest had none."
- "He doesn't return our love. He starts it. Ours is the answer."

## Risks to the book's rules

- **Overclaiming.** The brief's form ends "So there is a Person", which reads as a proof. It must be framed as a best explanation that predisposes the reader to faith (CCC 35) and sits within a converging case (CCC 31).
- **Protestant authority.** This argument is best known in a Protestant apologist's version. Do not name him, echo his wording, or rely on his formulation. Source it to Augustine, Aquinas, CCC 1718, Benedict XVI (*Spe Salvi* 11–12, on not knowing exactly what we long for), and Kreeft.
- **Gratuity of grace.** "Natural desire cannot be in vain", pressed too hard, makes heaven owed (*Humani Generis* 26; CCC 1722).
- **Grace first.** God loves first (1 John 4:19). He never merely "returns" our love.
- **Polemic.** Describe Buddhism accurately and with respect (*Nostra Aetate* 2). Do not caricature it as a "religion of craving".
- **Marriage.** P3 must not belittle human love (Ch. 10). Human love is real communion and an icon, not a failure.
- **Culpability.** The answer to hiddenness must not imply that every unbeliever resists God (CCC 847; LG 16).
- **Simplicity and sacrifice.** When invoking the Trinity as love "given and returned whole", speak of total self-gift without loss, not of sacrifice in the divine essence. The Persons are not parts.

## Verdict

| Criterion | Score |
|---|---|
| Soundness | **6/10** as repaired (about 3/10 as the brief states it: P4 as a universal law and the circular citations sink the deductive form) |
| Robustness against the best opponent | **5/10** (survives the naturalist only as a best-explanation clue; does not reach the Buddhist without a separate case that persons are real) |
| Rhetorical power | **9/10** (the strongest of the four for an ordinary reader: everyone has been lonely and everyone has grieved) |
| Fit with the book | **9/10** (it is the definition's last words, "life-giving communion", made into an argument, and it completes the title) |

**Bottom line.** As a standalone replacement for Premise 1, Option 3 does not work. Premise 4 cannot bear deductive weight against a competent naturalist, and the Catholic sources it leans on assume what it sets out to prove. The repaired argument is a different and better thing: a best-explanation strand built from love's own evidence (grief, the refusal of death, and the longing to be known and received whole), set beside the obligation strand. Together the two do what neither does alone, which is to show that the Lawgiver *is Love*. Patrick's insight is the jewel of the option, once it is reworded to put grace first and sharpened from "returned" to "received whole". It should go in, as the second half of a converging case (Option 4), with the CCC 27 and 1718 citations moved after the conclusion and one honest paragraph on God's hiddenness.

## Suggested wording

1. We are made for communion. Beneath every want, we ache to love and be loved, wholly and forever.
2. Only a person can love us back. A law can bind us but cannot know us.
3. No finite person can: no one knows us all the way down, and even the marriage vow is for "all the days of my life."
4. Yet love refuses that limit. We grieve because love was meant to last.
5. Every hunger built into our nature has its bread. It would be strange if the deepest had none.
6. The best explanation: a Person who knows us wholly, loves us forever, and loved us first (1 John 4:19). Give yourself to Him, and you are given back whole.

**Placement:** directly after line 107 ("every account of morality stops somewhere"), as the communion strand that completes the obligation strand. It should absorb line 111's "ache" paragraph and be followed by CCC 27 and CCC 1718 as the Church naming what reason has found.
