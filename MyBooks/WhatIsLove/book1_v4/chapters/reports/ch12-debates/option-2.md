# Option 2: The Argument from Obligation (debate report)

## Opponent

**Two voices working as a team. Neither is a straw man.**

- **The Secular Realist (R).** R is a robust non-naturalist moral realist of the "godless normative realism" school (Erik Wielenberg; also David Enoch, Derek Parfit and Russ Shafer-Landau). For R, moral facts are objective, necessary and brute: they are just there, and nothing grounds them. R adds the strongest secular account of obligation in print, the "second-personal" view (Stephen Darwall's school). On that view an obligation really is a demand that persons can address to one another. But the person who makes the demand is the one I would wrong, my neighbor, and the authority behind it is the moral community of free and equal persons. No God is needed.
- **The Debunker (D).** D is an evolutionary debunker. D uses the "Darwinian dilemma" (Sharon Street's school) and the evolutionary case for moral error theory (Richard Joyce's school), with cognitive science of religion added: we are built to over-detect agents, and that bias explains the feeling that "Someone" is making a claim on us.

**Why they are the strongest pair against this option.** R grants premises 1 and 3 and puts all of the pressure on premise 2 and the step to premise 4. That is exactly where Option 2 carries its load. R's second-personal view is especially dangerous because it **agrees with the letter of premise 2** ("an obligation is a claim one person lays on another") and then fills the "person" slot with the neighbor. D attacks premises 1 and 3 and the reliability of the felt "claim." Between the two of them, every premise is under fire. As it turns out, D can also be turned against R, which is the most useful thing the debate found.

---

## The argument as tested (final, best-defended form)

1. **Love binds us.** We really owe one another the truth, owe each person his due, owe our children our very selves, and owe mercy, at least an end to revenge, to anyone who sincerely asks for it. Breaking these leaves us *guilty*, not merely *mistaken*. *(Repaired: mercy and self-gift are given in the minimal forms every culture recognizes. See Exchange 9.)*
2. **Whatever we owe, we owe to someone.** An obligation has a shape that only persons can fill. It can be *wronged*, it gives someone standing to *hold us to account*, and it can be *forgiven*. No rock, rule or bare fact can do any of these. *(Repaired: argued from these three marks, not stipulated as a definition. See Exchange 1.)*
3. **No human being can release us from love's claim.** No society can, and neither can I. The neighbor's claim is real, but it is not his to cancel: he cannot sign away his own dignity (*Dignitas Infinita* 15), and some duties bind where no human claimant can press them. *(Repaired: "no one invented them" became "no one can release us." See Exchanges 3 and 8.)*
4. **So the best explanation is that love's claim reaches us from a Person beyond every human being**, Someone to whom we owe everything because from Him we have received everything. *(Repaired: abductive, not deductive. The argument shows a personal source of claim above every human claim. That He is one and that He is Love are handed on to the integration section, Ch. 3, and the communion strand. See Exchanges 2, 7 and 10.)*

## Debate highlights

### Exchange 1: "Premise 2 is a stipulation."
**R:** "An obligation is a claim one person lays on another" is a definition chosen to win. Moral facts are neither rocks, nor rules, nor equations. They are a fourth kind of thing: normative facts. The fact that lying would hurt her *counts against* lying, full stop. Your list is a false menu.
**Reply:** We are not stipulating. We are reading the grammar of obligation, which shows three marks. You can *wrong* someone, which is more than making an error. Someone gains *standing to blame* you. And the wrong can be *forgiven*. A fact that merely "counts in favor" yields the "should" of advice ("you should take the faster road"). It does not yield the "owe" of duty. The chapter already relies on this difference: breaking the law leaves us "guilty rather than merely mistaken" (line 107). A Catholic philosopher, G. E. M. Anscombe, argued that the special binding sense of the moral "ought" belongs to a "law conception of ethics" and makes no sense without a lawgiver.
**Did it hold?** **Held with repair.** As worded, premise 2 asserts what it needs to prove. It has to be *argued* from the three marks. "Rocks, rules and equations" also misses R's actual candidate, so it should become "no rock, rule or bare fact." One honest caution: the Catholic tradition itself has debated this point. The Spanish scholastics argued over whether natural law obliges by rational nature alone (Gabriel Vázquez) or needs God's command to be law in the full sense (Francisco Suárez). Premise 2 is a reasoned position, not a self-evident definition.

### Exchange 2: The second-personal move (the most serious attack)
**R:** I grant premise 2 completely. Obligations are claims persons lay on persons. But the person who lays the claim is the one I would wrong: my neighbor. When I lie to her, *she* is wronged, *she* may blame me, *she* may forgive me. That is the whole structure, and it needs a community of persons, not a God. Your premise 4 does not follow from 2 and 3.
**Reply (first attempt):** But who gives your neighbor the authority to lay that claim?
**R:** Her dignity does. It is a brute fact about persons, and your own chapter says dignity is "prior to any recognition."
**Did it hold?** **Failed as stated.** This is the decisive fault in Option 2. The neighbor fits the "person" slot in premise 2 perfectly well. Moving from "a person lays the claim" to "a Person above every human being lays it" needs a hidden premise, and the syllogism leaves it out. The repair comes in Exchange 3.

### Exchange 3: Claims the holder cannot cancel
**Reply (escalated):** Test whether the claim is simply hers. A claim that belongs to its holder can be waived by its holder, the way I can forgive a debt you owe me. Some claims cannot be waived, though. She cannot validly consent to be enslaved, or to be tortured for someone's amusement, and the Church teaches that her dignity "cannot be lost" (*Dignitas Infinita* 15). A claim its holder cannot cancel is not merely hers. Some duties also bind where no human claimant can press them: a promise to the dying, the child not yet born, the patient who will never wake, the wrong done in secret against oneself. In each case love still binds, and no human voice is there to make the claim.
**R:** Inalienability is just part of the brute fact of dignity. Duties to the dead are a puzzle for everyone, and realists say we owe them to the person as he was in life.
**Did it hold?** **Held with repair.** This breaks the pure "the neighbor is the source" view. R has to retreat to "dignity is brute," and the debate becomes a contest between stopping points, which is line 107's ground. The cost is that Option 2 stops being a deduction and becomes an inference to the best explanation. That change is the right one.

### Exchange 4: "The felt claim is an adaptation."
**D:** Your premise 1 is a strong feeling, and the "Someone" behind it is a projection. Your ancestors who felt the tribe's eyes on them left more descendants. Our agency-detection is set to hair-trigger, so we hear a speaker behind every "ought," much as we hear a stalker in rustling grass. The feeling would exist whether or not anyone stands behind it, so it is no evidence.
**Reply:** The chapter has already shown the mirror (line 43). The same blind process built the reasoning D trusts, and "follow the evidence" is itself an "ought." If "built to get along" cancels conscience, it cancels thinking too. The argument also does not rest on a *feeling of presence*. It rests on the logic of being wronged and being forgiven (Exchange 1).
**D:** Epistemic norms are only instrumental: "if you want truth, weigh evidence." Truth-tracking was selected for because true beliefs help survival. Moral beliefs only had to be *useful*, not true.
**Reply:** Then D's own conclusion, "you ought to give up moral belief," shrinks to "if you happen to want accuracy...". Meanwhile D still blames, still protests, and still says "that's not fair" (line 103).
**Did it hold?** **Held with repair, partially.** Against a global debunker it holds. Against an instrumentalist about epistemic norms it ends in a stalemate, and honesty requires saying so. The firm repair is this: the chapter must not let "you feel the pull, so you hear God's voice" (line 135) do any argumentative work. Keep it as devotion *after* the argument.

### Exchange 5: Turning the Debunker against the Realist (the strongest new move)
**Patrick's side:** D's best weapon actually hits R hardest. If moral facts are brute and causally inert (they cause nothing), and our conscience came from a blind process aimed only at survival, then the two meeting would be pure luck. Nothing would explain why a survival-tuned conscience tracks facts that never touched it. Street's dilemma was aimed at realists like R for exactly this reason. Theism has an answer the godless realist lacks: the same Wisdom that is the source of the law also made the mind that reads it. Natural law is the rational creature's *share* in the eternal law (*ST* I-II q.91 a.2), and line 77's "mathematics our minds are uncannily fitted to read" hints at this.
**R:** I have a "third-factor" reply. Survival and the real good overlap. Pain really is bad, so evolution teaching us to avoid pain also teaches us something true.
**Reply:** That works for welfare goods. It fails exactly where love's claims are hardest and most distinctive: truth told at great cost, mercy to an enemy, self-gift for people who are not our kin, protection of the unborn and the dying who can never repay us. Evolution pulls *against* these, and these are the ones the conscience of the saints tracks most surely.
**Did it hold?** **Held.** This is the best move the debate produced, and the chapter does not yet make it.

### Exchange 6: Euthyphro aimed at the Lawgiver, and "Why obey God?"
**R:** Option 2 brings back the divine command theory that your Ch. 3 rejected. Does God lay the claim because the act is good? Then the good does the work and the Person adds nothing. Or is the act good because God lays the claim? Then it is arbitrary. Also, what obliges you to obey God's claim? It can't be another claim of God's, because that would be bootstrapping.
**Reply:** In God, the claim and the good are not two things (divine simplicity, *ST* I q.3). Law, for St. Thomas, is a work of *reason*, not bare will (*ST* I-II q.90 a.1), and the eternal law is God's own wisdom ordering all things (*ST* I-II q.93). As for "why obey?": not because He is strong, but because He *gave*. We owe God what is due to Him in justice and gratitude, and in St. Thomas religion is part of justice (*ST* II-II q.81). The first claim God lays on us is not an order barked at us. It is the gift of our very being, and a creature who has received everything owes everything (Ch. 4: covenant and gift). Grace comes first (1 John 4:19). Pope Benedict XVI made the same point in *Deus Caritas Est*: love can be commanded because it has first been given (paraphrased; verify the paragraph, which is likely §14, against vatican.va before use).
**R:** Gratitude is itself an obligation, so you are relying on a norm that comes before God's claim.
**Reply:** Gratitude is the clearest case of a person-to-person claim there is. Where the Giver is the source of everything, the gift *is* the claim.
**Did it hold?** **Held with repair.** Unless the chapter says outright that God's claim is His wisdom and love, not raw will, Option 2 contradicts Ch. 3. With that sentence added, this becomes one of the strongest "from love" points in the debate.

### Exchange 7: "Your stopping point is as brute as mine."
**R:** You stop at "God's nature just is good." I stop at "these moral facts just are." At least my stopping point posits no infinite mind.
**Reply:** Theism stops at one necessary Being whose existence and goodness are not two (*ST* I q.3 a.4), not at a scattered set of brute principles. That single stopping point also explains things R's cannot: why there are persons to be bound at all, why conscience tracks the law (Exchange 5), why the law's whole content is personal (line 77), and why the facets of love never truly contradict (line 125).
**R:** God is a far larger posit, divine simplicity is disputed, and Ch. 1's problem of evil counts against your hypothesis.
**Did it hold?** **Held, but not decisively.** The comparison is a weighing of explanations, and line 107 is honest about that. Lines 81 ("God must exist"), 133 ("Take God away, and they collapse") and 131 ("far more faith") overclaim against it.

### Exchange 8: The Kantian rival, "We are the lawgivers."
**R (borrowing Kant):** Premise 3 says no human *invented* the obligations. Agreed: reason *legislates* them. Every rational being gives the moral law to himself. That makes it universal and uninvented, and it needs no God.
**Reply:** Someone who binds himself can always untie the knot. Anscombe judged the idea of legislating for oneself incoherent for this reason. The Church gives the true account of what Kant saw: reason does carry its own law within it, but as a share in God's wisdom. St. John Paul II called this "participated theonomy" (*Veritatis Splendor* 41).
**Did it hold?** **Held with repair.** Premise 3 should be about **release**, not **invention**. The test of authority is who can dispense from the law. No human, no society, and not even I can release me from the duty not to torture a child.

### Exchange 9: "Mercy and self-gift are not duties."
**R:** I accept truth-telling and justice as duties. Forgiveness, though, is a gift, and an obligatory forgiveness is no forgiveness. Self-sacrifice goes beyond duty: your own firefighter (line 67) is a *hero* precisely because he did more than duty asked. Your Ch. 4 says a gift forced is no gift.
**Reply:** Every culture recognizes the minimal forms. Parents owe their children their very selves at real cost. One owes the sincerely sorry an end to endless revenge. Heroism is the fullness of a self-gift that everyone owes in some measure. Christ's command to love even enemies (Matt 5:44) belongs to the Gospel's fuller law, and it cannot be a premise for an unbeliever.
**Did it hold?** **Held with repair.** Premise 1 must state the minimal universal forms, or R simply drops mercy and sacrifice and the argument loses its "from love" character.

### Exchange 10: "Why *one* Person above every human?"
**R:** Even if a non-human claimant is needed, why one? Why not many gods, or the idealized community of all rational beings?
**Reply:** Love's claims never truly contradict one another (line 125; Ch. 5). Human claims constantly collide and need something above them to settle them, and otherwise "nothing arbitrates except force" (line 47). The inalienable core is identical in every human being, which points to one source rather than a committee.
**R:** That coherence could itself be a brute feature of the moral facts.
**Did it hold?** **Held with repair.** The obligation strand earns a *personal source of claim above every human claim*. That this source is one, simple and Love itself comes from Ch. 3, the integration section, and the communion strand. Premise 4 as written ("a Person above every human being") claims too much for this step alone.

### Exchange 11: The forgiveness test (the closing exchange)
**Patrick's side:** Only the one you have wronged can forgive you. So consider the guilt no human being can lift: the sin nobody saw, the wrong done to someone now dead, the conscience that keeps accusing *after* the victim has said "I forgive you." The Church names what is going on. Sin is first of all an offense against God and a rupture of communion with Him (CCC 1440, 1850; cf. Ps 51:4), and that is why the Sacrament of Reconciliation exists. St. John Henry Newman built his whole argument from conscience on this point: the shame and responsibility of conscience have the shape of answering to a Person. He called conscience the "aboriginal Vicar of Christ" (*Letter to the Duke of Norfolk* §5). The Council says the same: conscience is the "most secret core and sanctuary," where a person is alone with God (*Gaudium et Spes* 16).
**R:** Lingering guilt is psychology, or the self is the one wronged. **D:** Guilt feelings are adaptive.
**Reply:** Granted, this is not proof. But a wrong with no one wronged is exactly what R cannot make sense of, and it is exactly what the hypothesis of a personal Lawgiver predicts.
**Did it hold?** **Held as evidence, not proof.** This is the most distinctively "from love" datum in Option 2, because it runs through the facet of mercy.

## Faults found (ranked by severity)

1. **Critical: premise 2 does not lead to premise 4 (the neighbor as claimant).** The second-personal realist accepts premise 2 and names the neighbor as the claimant, so the syllogism is invalid without a hidden premise. **Partly fixable:** add the cannot-release and no-human-claimant data (Exchange 3) and recast the conclusion as inference to the best explanation. Option 2 cannot be made a valid deduction from premises an atheist realist must accept.
2. **Major: premise 2 is asserted as a definition.** That begs the question against R, and Catholic scholastics themselves disputed it (Vázquez and Suárez). **Fixable:** argue it from the three marks of being wronged, standing to blame, and being forgiven.
3. **Major: tension with Ch. 3's Euthyphro answer.** "A claim laid by a person" reads as divine will, which brings back the divine command theory Ch. 3 rejected. **Fixable:** one sentence saying God's claim is His wisdom and love, law as a work of reason (*ST* I-II q.90 a.1; q.93), grounded in gift.
4. **Major: premise 4 overreaches to "one Person above every human being."** Oneness and supremacy are not established by obligation alone. **Fixable:** give a modest conclusion and hand off to the integration section, Ch. 3 and the communion strand.
5. **Moderate: premise 1 lists forgiveness and self-gift as universal obligations.** The book's own firefighter example (line 67) frames sacrifice as going beyond duty. **Fixable:** state the minimal universal forms.
6. **Moderate: the chapter equivocates on "bind" and "law."** Line 77 says gravity "binds bodies" and mathematics "binds quantities," while line 105 says no equation has ever laid a claim on anyone. Lines 23, 133 and 137 ("a law as real and universal as gravity... laws require lawgivers") invite the reply that descriptive laws need no lawgiver. **Fixable:** keep "bind" and "law" for what obliges persons, and say that gravity only *describes* what bodies do.
7. **Moderate: the felt claim as evidence.** Agency-detection explains the felt "voice," so line 135 cannot bear argumentative weight. **Fixable:** frame it as devotion after the argument.
8. **Moderate: premise 3 is common ground with R and leaves Kantian self-legislation open.** **Fixable:** shift from "no one invented" to "no one, not even I, can release."
9. **Minor to moderate: line 43's companion-in-guilt reply only reaches a stalemate** against a debunker who treats epistemic norms as instrumental. **Partly fixable:** pair it with Exchange 5, which turns debunking against the godless realist.
10. **Minor: the "God is a bigger posit" rejoinder.** Answered by explanatory scope and simplicity. It holds but is not decisive. The chapter should keep line 107's honesty and drop the contrary overclaims.

## New insights the book does not yet state

1. **Use the debunker against the brute realist.** If moral facts are inert and conscience was tuned only for survival, their meeting is a lucky accident. If the same Love wrote both the law and the heart that reads it, the match is no accident (*ST* I-II q.91 a.2). Love's hardest claims, such as mercy to enemies and self-gift for those who cannot repay, are where evolution pulls against them and conscience still tracks them.
2. **Forgiveness is the decisive datum.** Only the wronged can forgive. Guilt with no human victim, or guilt that outlives human forgiveness, points to the One against whom all sin is first committed (CCC 1440, 1850; Ps 51:4). This makes the moral argument specifically an argument *from love*, through the facet of mercy, rather than a generic moral argument.
3. **Test authority by release, not by origin.** A claim its own holder cannot waive is not merely his. Inalienable dignity (*Dignitas Infinita* 15) is the bridge from the neighbor's claim to the Lawgiver's.
4. **Obligation grounded in gift.** God's claim on us is the claim of the Giver of our being, which answers "Why obey God?" without voluntarism and keeps grace first. A law of love can be commanded only by someone who has loved first (cf. *Deus Caritas Est*; 1 John 4:19). This joins the obligation strand to the communion strand.
5. **A sharper version of the "atheist's dilemma" (line 103), following Anscombe.** One can keep moral obligation in its full sense of guilt, blame and forgiveness and look for the Lawgiver. Or one can drop it and keep only "flourishing and defect" (the neo-Aristotelian route of Philippa Foot's school), and then a liar is *defective*, like a wolf that will not hunt, but never *guilty*. That fork is cleaner and fairer than the current option (2), which is labeled "incoherent."
6. **Answer autonomy with "participated theonomy" (*Veritatis Splendor* 41).** The chapter never meets the Kantian rival. The Church's account keeps everything true in it: reason really does carry its law within.
7. **Be honest about the Catholic tradition's own debate.** Natural law is knowable by reason alone (CCC 1954 to 1960), and the goodness or evil of acts lies in their object (*Veritatis Splendor* 79 to 80). So a realist who says "lying is wrong because of what lying is" is right as far as he goes. The dispute is about the *ultimate* ground, not the immediate one. Saying so wins the realist's trust.

## Lines worth keeping

- "A rule can tell you what to do. Only a person can be wronged when you don't."
- "Only the one you have wronged can forgive you. So who forgives the sin no one else saw?"
- "Your neighbor's claim on you is real. But he cannot sign away his own dignity, so it was never only his."
- "The man who binds himself can always untie the knot."
- "Gravity describes what bodies do. Love tells you what you owe, and to whom."
- "If moral facts are just there and conscience is just evolved, it is a lucky accident they ever met. If the same Love wrote the law and the heart that reads it, it is no accident at all."
- "God's first claim on you was not an order. It was your life."

## Risks to the book's rules

- **Overclaiming (CCC 35).** Premise 4's "So there is a Person" reads as a proof. Lines 81 ("God must exist"), 131 ("far more faith"), 133 ("Take God away, and they collapse") and 109 cut against line 107's honest weighing of stopping points. The Church calls these "converging and convincing arguments" (CCC 31). Keep that register.
- **Doctrinal slide into voluntarism.** Without the eternal-law sentence, "a claim laid by a person" contradicts Ch. 3's answer to Euthyphro (goodness is God's nature, not His fiat).
- **Making unbelievers culpable.** An argument from guilt can sound as though every atheist is suppressing his conscience. Keep CCC 847 and *Lumen Gentium* 16 in view. The atheist realist who honors obligation is reading the law written on his heart (line 119), not defying it.
- **Protestant authorities.** The best-known modern forms of the argument from obligation come from Protestant writers (C. S. Lewis's opening of *Mere Christianity*, Robert Adams's divine command theory, C. Stephen Evans, John Hare). The temptation to borrow their phrasing or name them is real. Use Catholic sources instead: St. Thomas, St. John Henry Newman, G. E. M. Anscombe, St. John Paul II (*Veritatis Splendor*), Benedict XVI (*Deus Caritas Est*), *Gaudium et Spes* 16, *Dignitas Infinita*.
- **Banned vocabulary.** The "why obey God" answer invites "debt-payment" or "transaction" language for gratitude. Say "gift," "due in justice," "covenant."
- **Simplicity and sacrifice.** Don't call God's claim on us His "sacrifice." Self-gift is said of us, and within the Trinity it is total self-gift with no loss.
- **Quotation verification.** The phrases from *Veritatis Splendor* 41, *Gaudium et Spes* 16, *Deus Caritas Est* (paragraph to confirm) and Newman's *Letter to the Duke of Norfolk* §5 must go through the quote cache and vatican.va before entering the text.

## Verdict

| Criterion | Score | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Soundness | **5/10** as written; **7/10** repaired | Invalid without a hidden premise (the neighbor as claimant). Premise 2 is contested. The repaired abductive form is respectable. |
| Robustness against the best opponent | **5/10** | The second-personal realist sinks the deductive form. The repaired form survives only as a weighing of stopping points, though Exchanges 3, 5 and 11 give it real teeth. |
| Rhetorical power | **8/10** | "Only a person can be wronged" and the forgiveness test land hard, in plain words. |
| Fit with the book | **8/10** | Fits the chapter title and line 105, and runs through mercy and gift. It loses points because it conflicts with Ch. 3 unless the eternal-law sentence is added. |

**Bottom line.** As a free-standing syllogism, Option 2 fails at its hinge. A secular realist can accept that obligations are claims persons lay on persons and name the neighbor as the claimant, and nothing in the four premises forces the step to a Person above every human being. Premise 2, stated as a definition, also asserts the conclusion it needs to prove. The option still holds the most distinctively *loving* material in the whole moral argument: being wronged and being forgiven, claims their holders cannot cancel, a claim grounded in gift, and the debunker turned against the godless realist. Recast it as an inference to the best explanation, test authority by who can *release* us, give mercy and self-gift in their minimal universal forms, and tie God's claim to His wisdom and love rather than bare will. In that form it is strong. It belongs as the obligation strand of the converging case (Option 4), not as the chapter's lead argument on its own.

## Suggested wording (120 words)

1. Love binds us. We really owe one another the truth, owe each person his due, owe our children ourselves, and owe mercy to anyone who sincerely asks it. Break these, and we are guilty, not merely mistaken.
2. Whatever we owe, we owe to someone. Only a person can be wronged, call us to account, or forgive. No rock, rule or bare fact can do any of these.
3. Our neighbor's claim is real, but not his to cancel: he cannot sign away his own dignity. No one, no society, not even I, can release me from love's law.
4. So love's claim reaches us from beyond every human being: from Someone to whom we owe everything, because from Him we have received everything.

**Placement:** This would replace the "One last skeptic remains" paragraph (line 105), as the obligation strand placed before line 107. Line 107's honest comparison of stopping points would follow directly, and the integration section and communion strand would carry the argument from "a personal Lawgiver" to "the One who is Love."
