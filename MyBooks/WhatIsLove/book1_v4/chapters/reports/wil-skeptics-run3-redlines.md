### Edit 10 · The Trinity dilemma, held as revelation's answer (finding 11)

Now:

> And see what this means: a God who is love *before anything exists* must have a Beloved before anything exists. Either love is eternal in God—and God is eternally a communion of Persons—or love began only when creation finally gave Him something to love, and "God is love" would name something He became rather than who He is.

Proposed:

> And see what this means: if God is love *before anything exists*, then love was being given and received before anything existed. Whom did the Father love before there was a world? Reason can only stand before that question; revelation answers it: from all eternity, God is a communion of Persons, eternally giving and receiving the one love that God is. Were it otherwise, love would have begun only when creation finally gave Him something to love, and "God is love" would name something He became rather than who He is.

The Church herself teaches that the Trinity cannot be demonstrated by reason alone — and the surrounding callout already has the right register ("the Trinity, just ahead, shows how it can be true"). This keeps the full force of the insight while letting revelation, not a syllogism, carry it. The next sentence ("The Trinity is not a puzzle bolted onto the Gospel…") stands unchanged.

Now: the section **"Sacrificing FOR the Person, Not FOR Benefits"** repeats Chapter 4's covenant test at full length — the same bolded couplet, the same "Profound Implication," the same *Gaudium et Spes* 24 quotation, the same roll of examples, the same contract/covenant close.

Proposed (full replacement of the section, keeping its callout):

> ## Sacrificing FOR the Person, Not FOR Benefits
>
> Chapter 4 gave covenant love its test: do we love WHO the person is, or WHAT they give us? Sacrifice is where that test is graded.
>
> A husband sacrifices for his wife—working long hours, giving up hobbies, putting her needs first. The test comes when the benefits disappear: when Alzheimer's takes her recognition, when illness ends his ability to provide, when age diminishes beauty or strength, when depression or disability replaces what each used to give. The same test runs in both directions, husband to wife and wife to husband.
>
> **If the sacrifice was FOR the benefits, it stops when the benefits cease.**
> **If the sacrifice was FOR the person, it endures because their dignity endures.**
>
> This is covenant, not contract—"I give because you are," not "I give because you give"—and it is how God loves: He needs nothing from us, and loves us for who we are, persons He "willed for themselves" (Vatican II, *Gaudium et Spes* 24). This is also why covenant love is ordered toward children but never contingent on them: children are the fruit of authentic love, not the reason for it. And it is why sacrifice does not diminish when the beloved can no longer reciprocate—the elderly parent, the disabled child, the spouse ravaged by disease. **Their worth doesn't depend on their utility. They are persons, made in God's image, worthy of sacrificial love simply because they exist.**
