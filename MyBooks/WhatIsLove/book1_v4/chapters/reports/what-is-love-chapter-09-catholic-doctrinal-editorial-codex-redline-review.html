    Chapter 9 · Sacrifice: Love's Eternal Self-Gift

    Chapter assessment

    Chapter 9 has a compelling pastoral center: Christ freely offers Himself in love; Christian discipleship has a bodily and costly form; grace-enabled self-gift can be joined to Christ’s one sacrifice; mercy, justice and truth must govern what is offered; self-destruction and coerced suffering are not Christian sacrifice. The chapter also gives the Eucharist its proper centrality and repeatedly denies that our works add to Calvary.

    The governing language nevertheless makes “cost” the test by which every love becomes real and places “sacrifice” inside God’s eternal life without distinguishing eternal Trinitarian self-gift from the incarnate Son’s historical offering in His humanity. Dense debt, price, punishment and payment language narrows the Paschal mystery. The open-hand account assigns the first operative movement to human deeds instead of prevenient grace; faith, obedience and charity are repeatedly treated as identical; dead faith is called no faith; ordered self-love, hope for one’s own salvation, contemplation and rest disappear. Several Mass explanations, sacramental-consent claims, Old Testament classifications, historical attributions, family applications and promised outcomes also need correction. The following redlines keep the chapter one robust, substantial unit while removing repetition that currently magnifies the errors.

    CH9-01 · Distinguish eternal Trinitarian self-gift from sacrifice in the strict sense

    Severity: Stop-ship / Trinity and Christology · Control: DC-02, DC-03, DC-04 · Location: chapter_09.md:1

    Issue. The title can imply that cost, suffering, privation or death belongs eternally to the divine nature. The Son’s sacrifice is of infinite value because the divine Person freely offers Himself in His assumed humanity; God’s eternal communion is perfect self-gift without loss.

    Controlling sources: CCC 202, 232–267, 456–483, 606–618.

    Source heading with proposed inline redline:

    Sacrifice: Love's Eternal Self-Gift Christ’s Perfect Self-Gift

    Editorial consequence. The chapter can still relate Calvary to Trinitarian love without locating strict sacrifice inside the impassible divine nature.

    CH9-02 · Do not make visible cost the universal condition that turns every love into love

    Severity: Stop-ship / definition of love · Control: DC-01, DC-07, DC-26 · Location: chapter_09.md:39–45

    Issue. Love normally bears cost when fidelity requires it, but loving contemplation, receiving another’s gift, prudent rest, gratitude and ordered self-care are not unreal merely because no present deprivation is visible. Sacrifice manifests and perfects love in the face of cost; it does not create every act of love from an otherwise loveless opinion.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1766, 1809, 1822–1829, 2099–2100, 2288.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    Sacrifice is where love stops being theory and becomes reality proves its fidelity when the beloved’s true good carries a cost. This is where the rubber meets the road, where faith becomes works of love, where words become flesh.

    Truth, justice, and mercy specify the good; sacrifice is what makes it for another — willed at cost to me, rather than merely wished for from a safe distance. Without sacrifice, the for another never lands. You have an opinion about someone's good; you do not yet have love is the freely accepted cost of willing that good when fidelity requires it. Love also receives, contemplates, rests and rightly cares for the self God created; but it does not abandon the good merely because the cost becomes real.

    Love is sacrificial . Not in ideas. Not in theory. Not in good intentions. In action. In cost. In putting another's good before our own when the true good requires costly self-gift. It must become action and cannot remain a good intention that refuses every real demand.

    Editorial consequence. The central thesis remains demanding without defining all love by felt deprivation.

    CH9-03 · Correct the identification of sloth, lukewarmness and refusal to sacrifice

    Severity: Major / moral theology · Control: DC-10, DC-20, DC-28 · Location: chapter_09.md:47–50

    Issue. Acedia is sorrow or aversion toward the divine good and the demands of charity; it is not simply failure to pay a cost. Lukewarmness is related but not a synonym coined by Christ for “the antithesis of sacrifice.” Failure to rise early to pray or missing a game for Mass also needs circumstances and the Sunday obligation stated accurately.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1866, 2094, 2180–2183, 2733; Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II, q.35.

    Source callout with proposed inline redline:

    The antithesis of sacrifice is sloth — what Christ Himself named lukewarmness. Sloth can make the soul shrink from sacrificial love.

    Sacrifice is the costly self-gift that makes love real; sloth is the refusal to pay. Not the inability, but the refusal. The Christian who loves God “in his heart” but will not rise early to pray, will not give to the poor, will not tell an inconvenient truth, will not miss a game for Mass — this is not love acedia is sorrow or aversion toward the divine good and the demands of charity. It can appear as a culpable refusal to pray, serve, speak truth or fulfill the Sunday obligation, though inability and legitimate impediments must be distinguished from refusal. Revelation warns against lukewarmness, and Aquinas identifies acedia as a vice opposed to the joy of charity.

    Editorial consequence. The warning retains its force while using the Church’s categories rather than treating three related ideas as synonyms.

    CH9-04 · Preserve parental duties without making career sacrifice or divorce outcomes categorical

    Severity: Major / family and pastoral care · Control: DC-23, DC-25, DC-26 · Location: chapter_09.md:55–63

    Issue. Parents owe children presence and care, but prudence may require demanding employment, and children do not all experience the same schedule in the same way. Separation can be legitimate, and civil divorce can be tolerated for legal rights or children’s care. “They are made whole” promises a spiritual and psychological result from sacrifice itself.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1646–1651, 2201–2231, 2383.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    Children know instinctively what adults in our moment have been taught to forget: they were supposed to be worth it. They know a parent is supposed to sacrifice career for bedtime, income for presence, personal dreams order career, income, rest and personal hopes prudently around the serious duty of raising a child who matters. Families in poverty, illness, military service, caregiving or other grave circumstances may bear necessary absences that are not refusals of love.

    When a parent walks away from the covenant — not because of abuse or grave betrayal, but because the covenant has become inconvenient apart from legitimate separation, protection from abuse, grave betrayal or other serious circumstances — the child learns may be wounded by the apparent lesson that love lasts only until the cost gets too high.

    The parent who sacrifices for her is not diminished by it — they are made whole by it rightly ordered self-gift can deepen virtue and communion, even when its earthly fruit is hidden or incomplete.

    Editorial consequence. The passage honors fidelity without judging families from appearances or guaranteeing outcomes.

    CH9-05 · Qualify formation by sacrifice and correct the incurvatus in se attribution

    Severity: Major / sanctification and history · Control: DC-06, DC-08, DC-28 · Location: chapter_09.md:67

    Issue. No isolated sacrifice automatically conforms a soul to Christ; acts formed by grace and charity dispose and shape us, while imprudent or sinful “sacrifices” can deform. The fixed Latin formula homo incurvatus in se is commonly associated with Luther’s development of an Augustinian theme, not a verified quotation from Augustine.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    Each act of sacrifice conforms the soul more closely to Christ's. Each grace-enabled, rightly ordered act of self-gift can conform the soul more closely to Christ. ... Over time we do not just do good things; by grace and repeated virtuous action we become good persons, conformed to Him. ... It frees us from the prison of the self — what St. Augustine called the soul curved in on itself (homo incurvatus in se) an inward curvature of sin developed in the Augustinian tradition and later expressed as incurvatus in se — and makes us more fully alive in love.

    Editorial consequence. Sanctification remains participatory rather than automatic, and a vulnerable historical attribution is removed.

    CH9-06 · Present the sevenfold Old Testament scheme as a selected typology, not the sacrificial system’s own taxonomy

    Severity: Major / Scripture and typology · Control: DC-20, DC-27, DC-28 · Location: chapter_09.md:71–77

    Issue. The Day of Atonement includes several rites and offerings, and the scapegoat is a rite involving a live goat, not a separate sacrifice parallel to burnt, purification, reparation and well-being offerings. The selected seven can be a fruitful Christian meditation, but they are not a standard exhaustive biblical classification.

    Controlling sources: Leviticus 1–7, 16; Hebrews 8–10; CCC 128–130.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    Here I want to touch on seven sacrifices God established at the heart of Old Covenant worship — each a real cost paid by the worshiper seven offerings or rites selected from Old Covenant worship. They are not seven mutually exclusive biblical categories: the Day of Atonement contains several rites, including the live scapegoat, and offerings served more than one purpose. Read together as a Christian typological meditation, they reveal dimensions of consecration, atonement, liberation and communion — each finding its complete fulfillment in Christ. Take them in together — not as seven separate things, but as seven facets of the one act we will encounter at Calvary.

    Editorial consequence. The rich typology survives without being presented as the Levitical system’s formal structure.

    CH9-07 · Do not generalize hand-laying and confession to every Israelite sacrifice

    Severity: Major / biblical accuracy and Jewish continuity · Control: DC-20, DC-22, DC-28 · Location: chapter_09.md:75

    Issue. Hand-laying occurred with several offerings, but confession over the animal is explicit in particular rites, especially the scapegoat, not every lamb. Romans 11:29 concerns Israel’s election and gifts; it should not be made proof for each cultic assertion. The New Covenant fulfills and surpasses the Old without suggesting that every Israelite already possessed the same sacramental access in an undisclosed form.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    None of what follows means the Old Covenant sacrifices were empty rehearsals, and none of it diminishes the faith of the Israelite who laid his hand on the head of the lamb and confessed his sin faithful Israelites who offered the rites God commanded; particular offerings involved hand-laying, and the Day of Atonement explicitly included confession over the scapegoat. They truly worshiped the living God within the covenant then given. What the Cross gives us is not contemptuous replacement but fulfillment and perfection — the same God, the same covenant love the one faithful God bringing His promises to their divinely intended fullness in the New Covenant.

    Editorial consequence. Continuity is affirmed without homogenizing distinct rites or covenants.

    CH9-08 · Broaden the guilt-offering account beyond commercial payment

    Severity: Stop-ship / atonement · Control: DC-05, DC-13 · Location: chapter_09.md:83

    Issue. Christ truly makes satisfaction and reparation, but the asham did not always include the same restitution scenario, and “Christ paid an infinite debt in full” becomes the chapter’s controlling explanation. Catholic doctrine integrates loving obedience, representation, sacrifice, expiation, satisfaction, reconciliation and victory.

    Controlling sources: Leviticus 5:14–6:7; Isaiah 53; CCC 599–618.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    The asham required not only an animal but full restitution to the wronged party plus an additional fifth sometimes accompanied restitution plus an additional fifth, especially when holy things or a neighbor had been defrauded, because sin can steal and damage what justice requires us to repair. Christ pays the infinite debt sin incurred to God Himself ... something real was owed, and Christ paid it in full with infinite measure to spare is the definitive offering for sin: in free and loving obedience He represents us, bears our sins, makes superabundant satisfaction and reconciles us to the Father. Payment is one biblical image within that larger mystery, not a commercial mechanism that exhausts it.

    Editorial consequence. Satisfaction remains fully Catholic without making the Paschal mystery a single transaction.

    CH9-09 · Label detailed scapegoat, tamid, grain and red-heifer correspondences as typological readings

    Severity: Major / exegesis · Control: DC-20, DC-27 · Location: chapter_09.md:87–93

    Issue. The New Testament explicitly identifies Christ with several sacrificial images, but it does not say He was crucified outside the gate “precisely” to fulfill the scapegoat, nor does it explicitly assign each later correspondence. These are legitimate traditional or theological typologies when labeled as such.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    Christ was crucified “outside the gate” of Jerusalem precisely to fulfill this and Hebrews itself connects His suffering there with sanctifying the people by His blood; Christian readers may also see here an echo of the scapegoat.

    Beyond these seven, the whole Levitical world echoes Him. The daily perpetual offering prefigures ... The grain offering anticipates ... The ashes of the red heifer prefigure can be read typologically in Him. The daily offering may call to mind the Church’s continual Eucharistic worship; the grain offering can call to mind the Bread of Life; and Hebrews itself compares the red-heifer rite with Christ’s purifying blood. These correspondences illuminate the unity of Scripture; they should not all be presented as explicit predictions with only one possible referent.

    Editorial consequence. The biblical meditation becomes candid about the difference between inspired assertion and Christian typological application.

    CH9-10 · Remove uncertain dating and claims to know God’s exact pedagogical timetable

    Severity: Major / history and divine-intention claims · Control: DC-20, DC-26, DC-28 · Location: chapter_09.md:97–99

    Issue. Hebrews’ date is disputed, so “very likely while the Temple still stood” should not bear interpretive weight. “God spent fifteen centuries precisely so” claims an exact duration and exclusive divine purpose that the text does not reveal.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    The apostolic writer of Hebrews — writing, very likely, while the Temple still stood — inspired author of Hebrews tells us what was happening in that moment ...

    God spent fifteen centuries forming His people through the sacrificial system precisely so that formed Israel through the covenants and sacrificial worship, which gave God’s people a divinely prepared language by which, when His Son hung upon the tree, His people would the Church could have eyes to see many dimensions of what was happening.

    Editorial consequence. The argument no longer depends on a disputed date or unprovable specification of providence.

    CH9-11 · Do not say every Old Covenant sacrifice asked worshipers to make partial payment

    Severity: Stop-ship / biblical theology · Control: DC-05, DC-20 · Location: chapter_09.md:103–111

    Issue. Israel’s offerings included praise, thanksgiving, communion, consecration and atonement; they were not all partial human payments for an unpayable fracture. The later allocation of rites into failures of truth, justice and an “absence of mercy” is the author’s devotional matrix, not the inspired system’s own explanation.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    Each one took a real fracture in the bond between God and man ... and asked the worshiper to pay, in part, for what could not be paid in full served a divinely appointed purpose within Israel’s worship—consecration, atonement, thanksgiving, reparation or communion—and together they prepared for Christ’s definitive offering.

    Each Old Covenant sacrifice exposed one facet of love’s broken bond Without claiming that Scripture itself assigns the rites to this book’s four-part definition, we may use the framework devotionally to notice several dimensions ... The Passover and Peace Offering answered the absence of mercy display liberation and restored communion, gifts of divine mercy.

    Seven selected rites. Three facets of the one separation. And every one of them required cost ... because the bond is not restored by sentiment, only by something paid. Many dimensions of covenant worship, all fulfilled in the one Paschal mystery. Their costly character matters, but God’s covenantal gift cannot be reduced to what a worshiper paid.

    Editorial consequence. Israel’s worship is allowed to speak in its own categories before the manuscript offers its secondary synthesis.

    CH9-12 · Preserve divine impassibility when saying human freedom wounds God

    Severity: Stop-ship / doctrine of God · Control: DC-02, DC-03, DC-10 · Location: chapter_09.md:117

    Issue. Sin truly offends God and Christ truly suffered in His humanity, but the divine nature cannot be injured or diminished. Sin can also arise through weakness and disordered passion, not only a fully lucid free choice of destruction.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1849–1860, 470, 612.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    In moment after moment, we freely choose things less than God ... in real destruction causing real damage to ourselves, our families, our societies. The same freedom that lets us love Him is the freedom by which we wound Him offend Him and by which creatures made Christ suffer in His humanity; God’s divine nature is not injured or diminished. Sin is not a misunderstanding or a weakness; it is merely misunderstanding or weakness, though ignorance, fear, habit and weakness can diminish culpability. Sin remains real evil introduced through human acts.

    Editorial consequence. The prose can remain personal and grave without attributing passibility to the divine essence or flattening culpability.

    CH9-13 · Correct the claims about sacramental effects and teleios

    Severity: Major / sacraments and exegesis · Control: DC-08, DC-16, DC-27 · Location: chapter_09.md:119, 149

    Issue. Baptism does more than clothe us externally: it forgives sin, gives new birth, sanctifying grace and incorporation into Christ. Penance forgives post-baptismal sins and reconciles. Greek teleios can signify complete, mature or perfect by context; “not flawless, but whole” falsely excludes moral perfection in charity from Matthew 5:48.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1213, 1262–1274, 1422, 2013.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    In Baptism, His Cross washes us clean and clothes us in His own righteousness gives new birth, sanctifying grace, adoption and incorporation into Christ; in Confession, His mercy reaches into the cracks our love keeps opening and binds them with His blood forgives sins committed after Baptism and reconciles us with God and the Church.

    The Greek behind both is teleios: not flawless, but whole, undivided, holding nothing back a word that can mean complete, mature or perfect according to context; here Christ calls disciples toward the perfection of charity patterned on the Father.

    Editorial consequence. Sacramental renewal and Christ’s command are stated in received Catholic terms rather than a lexical slogan.

    CH9-14 · Distinguish mortal sin, judgment and the full Catholic doctrine of atonement

    Severity: Stop-ship / sin, hell and atonement · Control: DC-05, DC-10, DC-13 · Location: chapter_09.md:121

    Issue. Not every sin merits eternal separation; mortal sin requires grave matter, full knowledge and deliberate consent. Hell is definitive self-exclusion, not a punishment God indiscriminately assigns. Mercy does not flow only after a debt has been “completely paid”; the Father lovingly initiates redemption and the Son freely offers Himself.

    Controlling sources: CCC 393, 599–618, 1033–1037, 1854–1864.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    To sin against our Creator is a severe injustice, and God's wrath and eternal separation are completely justified and completely deserved. We are not unfortunate; we are guilty grave sin freely chosen with full knowledge and deliberate consent makes us guilty and, unless repented of, excludes us from communion with God. Lesser sins wound charity without destroying it, and God predestines no one to hell. Christ pays in full what we could never pay ... on the Cross, mercy flows through a justice that has been completely paid freely offers the loving obedience, sacrifice and superabundant satisfaction we could not supply, reconciling us to the Father and conquering sin and death. The Father’s mercy initiates the saving mission; justice and mercy are never opponents within God.

    Editorial consequence. The chapter no longer implies universal mortal guilt or a Father who becomes merciful only after payment.

    CH9-15 · Remove the false opposition between tribunal and Father

    Severity: Stop-ship / atonement and judgment · Control: DC-05, DC-13 · Location: chapter_09.md:125–127

    Issue. Scripture and Tradition use filial, judicial, cultic, covenantal, victorious and medicinal images together. The Father who adopts also judges. Mercy is offered before merit but must be freely received through conversion; desire detached from repentance is not enough.

    Controlling sources: CCC 545, 678–679, 982, 1038–1041, 1427–1433.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    Because that mercy is freely given, what waits on the other side of the Cross is not a tribunal but a Father the just Judge who is also the Father calling sinners into adopted life; communion is offered before we have done a single thing to deserve it.

    God's love is so powerful that literally anyone who desires mercy can be healed. No sin exceeds God’s mercy; every living sinner may repent and receive forgiveness, though mercy does not abolish the need for conversion or freedom’s possibility of final refusal.

    Editorial consequence. Divine fatherhood, judgment, universal invitation and human response are all retained.

    CH9-16 · Do not say Christ’s divinity itself was poured out or treated as part of the cost

    Severity: Stop-ship / Christology · Control: DC-03, DC-04 · Location: chapter_09.md:129–131

    Issue. “Body, Blood, Soul and Divinity” is a Eucharistic formula for the whole Christ substantially present; it should not imply that the divine nature was depleted, killed or paid as sacrificial matter. Christ offers Himself through His human nature, and the divine Person gives the act infinite worth.

    Controlling sources: CCC 464–483, 606–618, 1374.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    It is the whole self of God incarnate poured out: body, blood, soul, and divinity. Only one Person could pay it the whole human life of the incarnate Son freely offered unto death—His Body and Blood given, His human soul sorrowful unto death—by the divine Person who remains fully God. His divinity is neither depleted nor destroyed; it gives His human offering infinite worth. As man, He suffers and represents us from within our condition; as God, He alone has the standing and the freedom to pay its full price the one acting is the eternal Son whose self-offering is superabundantly sufficient.

    Editorial consequence. The chapter preserves Chalcedonian precision while still proclaiming the staggering identity of the Crucified.

    CH9-17 · Do not say divine attributes are poured out and paid for

    Severity: Stop-ship / divine simplicity and atonement · Control: DC-02, DC-05 · Location: chapter_09.md:131–133

    Issue. Truth, justice and mercy are not components that become sacrificial currency. “Perfect mercy satisfies the justice we deserve” and attributes “poured out and paid for” obscure that Christ satisfies for sin by loving obedience and self-offering.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    His infinite mercy satisfies the justice we deserve, so that we can enter into the truth of His communion. In mercy, Christ freely makes satisfaction for sin and fulfills justice through loving obedience, reconciling us to the Father and opening communion.

    Christ is the one act in which the perfect truth, justice, and mercy that God is are poured out and paid for God’s truth, justice and mercy are perfectly revealed through the incarnate Son’s free human self-offering, all at once.

    Editorial consequence. The four facets interpret Calvary without becoming metaphysical parts or objects of payment.

    CH9-18 · Say the one sacrifice is made present, not made present “again”

    Severity: Major / Eucharistic sacrifice · Control: DC-17 · Location: chapter_09.md:135

    Issue. The paragraph correctly denies repetition but then says the sacrifice is “made present again, in every place.” The Mass sacramentally re-presents the numerically one sacrifice; “again” can suggest a series of new presencings or sacrifices.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1362–1372; Council of Trent, Session XXII, chapters 1–2.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    At the Last Supper, Christ instituted the Eucharistic sacrifice of the New Covenant ... so that the one sacrifice of Calvary would be made present again sacramentally made present, in every place and age, until He comes. For two thousand years the Church has done exactly that—not adding to Christ’s sacrifice, not repeating it, but offering sacramentally the one sacrifice He entrusted to her.

    Editorial consequence. The paragraph teaches re-presentation without verbal ambiguity about repetition.

    CH9-19 · Make prevenient grace the cause of the hand’s opening

    Severity: Stop-ship / grace and semi-Pelagian risk · Control: DC-06, DC-08 · Location: chapter_09.md:147–159, 181

    Issue. The image repeatedly says a person “just stops refusing,” and then says the hand opens by our acts. Catholic doctrine teaches that God’s prevenient grace awakens and enables the first movement of conversion and every meritorious act; cooperation is real but never an unaided first step.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1989–2002, 2006–2011; Council of Trent, Decree on Justification, chapters 5–8.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    The open hand does not earn the gift. It just stops refusing it is itself awakened and enabled by grace to stop resisting and freely receive.

    The only question is whether our hand is open or closed. Even the desire to open is already God’s merciful initiative at work within us.

    And here is the part that matters: the hand opens by giving because grace moves us to believe, hope, repent and act in love. Our truthful, just, merciful and sacrificial acts are real cooperation with grace; they do not pry open a hand before grace can enter.

    Every act of love we manage is His love borrowed for a moment, set down in a hand that finally consented to be open. The clenching was ours. The gift is His a genuinely human act enabled and elevated by His prior grace. Resistance is ours; both the gift and the power to cooperate are first His.

    Editorial consequence. The metaphor now teaches Catholic synergism rather than an unaided human initiative.

    CH9-20 · Distinguish faith from obedience while preserving their living relation

    Severity: Stop-ship / theological virtues · Control: DC-09 · Location: chapter_09.md:161–173

    Issue. Faith and obedience are inseparable in mature discipleship but are not “one thing.” Faith is the supernatural virtue by which we assent to God and what He reveals; obedience is a moral response. A disobedient act does not prove that faith as such is absent.

    Controlling sources: CCC 142–165, 1814–1816.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    This is also why putting our faith in Jesus and obeying Jesus are not two different things — they are one thing seen from two sides distinct but ordered realities: genuine faith calls for obedience, and obedience expresses faith in action. To trust someone normally disposes us to do what they say; failure to obey can reveal weak or dead faith, but the terms are not synonyms.

    Obedience is simply the relational word for what trust does when it has hands and feet. It is faith working through love, in the body one embodied fruit of living faith working through charity.

    Editorial consequence. The organic unity remains without collapsing theological and moral virtues.

    CH9-21 · Do not say dead faith is no faith at all

    Severity: Stop-ship / justification and faith · Control: DC-09 · Location: chapter_09.md:175–179

    Issue. James calls faith without works dead; Catholic theology calls it unformed faith. It remains faith, though it does not fully unite the believer to Christ or save without charity. The text’s “no faith at all” contradicts the Catechism and then makes “faith working through love” a denial that faith and love are distinct.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1814–1816; James 2:14–26; Benedict XVI, General Audience, November 19, 2008.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    A faith that produces no truth, no justice, and no mercy in the world is not weak faith or shallow faith — it is no faith at all, because there is nothing for it to be in dead or unformed faith: it remains assent, but without charity it does not fully unite the believer to Christ and cannot save.

    Not faith plus love. Not faith and then love. Faith working through love — one organic reality. Faith and charity remain distinct gifts, but saving or formed faith acts through charity as one living Christian life.

    Faith without love is nothing spiritually barren; even extraordinary faith avails nothing for salvation without charity.

    Editorial consequence. James and Paul are synthesized in the Church’s own categories rather than by denying unformed faith.

    CH9-22 · Restore ordered self-love and Christian hope for one’s own salvation

    Severity: Stop-ship / charity and hope · Control: DC-07, DC-23, DC-25 · Location: chapter_09.md:177, 263–267

    Issue. Love is not “never oriented inward.” Christ commands love of neighbor as oneself; one owes care to one’s body, soul, vocation and salvation. Hope for one’s own eternal life is a theological virtue, not self-centered Christianity. Salvation bears fruit in mission without ceasing to be personally received.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1817–1821, 1825, 2288; Matthew 22:39.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    Love is never oriented inward. Love is always oriented outward not self-enclosure. Ordered self-love receives one’s own life as God’s gift and seeks one’s true good in Him; charity then loves the neighbor as oneself. A faith that never serves the neighbor is dead, but contemplation, repentance, rest and hope for one’s own salvation are not selfish merely because their immediate object includes oneself.

    Christ’s sacrifice was not just for “my personal salvation.” This is a far cry from ... mainly about how “I” get to Heaven. Christian hope rightly desires Heaven for oneself and others; that personal hope is purified, not abolished, by charity and mission.

    Editorial consequence. The chapter can oppose selfish individualism without condemning a theological virtue or a duty of charity toward oneself.

    Issue. “Our sacrifice deepening our faith” and a self-reinforcing spiral can suggest acquired theological virtues. Faith, hope and charity are infused by God; our grace-enabled acts exercise and deepen them, while God remains the first cause.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    There is no either/or. There is only His grace becoming our love through our sacrifice, and our sacrifice deepening our faith, and that faith opening us further to His grace. Faith leads us to love. Love grows us in faith. Round and round it goes ... the spiral of life itself God’s grace awakening faith, hope and charity, and our free cooperation exercising those infused gifts in obedience and self-gift.     Ite, missa est — “Go forth; the Mass is ended.” The very word missa itself means sent. rendered in the Roman Missal by dismissal formulas such as “Go forth, the Mass is ended.” The word missa is associated with this dismissal, and the rite itself sends the faithful on mission.

    Issue. All legitimate authority comes from God and is morally bounded, but non-Christians can exercise real natural virtue and just authority under natural law and actual grace. “Tyranny, every time” is a universal historical assertion the chapter cannot establish.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1897–1904, 1954–1960.

    Issue. The Mass makes the one sacrifice sacramentally present; it is not literal time travel, and the faithful do not leave chronological time. Revelation portrays the risen Lamb bearing the marks of sacrifice and interceding; it does not mean Christ is continuously being sacrificed in heaven.

It's like a curtain is pulled back, and we are suddenly standing at the foot of the Cross uniting the Church sacramentally to the once-for-all offering and its enduring saving power.

    The one sacrifice offered on Calvary is perpetually present before the Father ... what Christ is eternally doing. We step out of ordinary time and into sacred time, where past, present, and future converge complete and unrepeatable; the risen Christ eternally intercedes, and the liturgy sacramentally makes His Paschal mystery present here and now.     Severity: Major / Roman rite · Control: DC-17, DC-28 · Location: chapter_09.md:233–237

    Issue. We do not place “our sin” on the altar as an offering; we offer ourselves, repentance, work, suffering, prayer and praise united to Christ. The water prayer asks that we share in Christ’s divinity because He shared our humanity; the drop does not simply “represent the humanity of Christ,” nor are our offerings mingled with His Blood by that action.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1368, 2100; General Instruction of the Roman Missal 75; Roman Missal, preparation of the chalice.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    We place our own sacrifices—our sin repentance, struggles, sufferings, mourning, daily acts of love—on the altar with Christ's. Like the drop of water the Priest adds to the chalice of wine to represent the humanity of Christ, our small, imperfect human offerings are mingled with His infinite and perfect blood spiritually within the Church’s offering, asking that they be united to Christ’s one sacrifice. The water-and-wine prayer asks that we come to share in Christ’s divinity, who humbled Himself to share in our humanity; it should not be made a direct diagram of our offerings entering His Blood.

    Editorial consequence. The application now follows the rite’s actual words and Eucharistic doctrine.
