# Argument Lab, Round 5: Secular Neo-Aristotelian Naturalism / Virtue Ethics vs. v5

## 1. School and voice

This is the school of Philippa Foot, Rosalind Hursthouse, Michael Thompson, John McDowell and Martha Nussbaum. Goodness is measured against the form of life of one's kind. Justice is bipolar: a wrong is done *to* someone, and she has the complaint. In Round 4 this school reported "As written: NONE" with seven Minor points. This round checks whether v5 took those repairs (R4.1–R4.9), and whether v5's new wording broke anything.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

*(The Round 1–4 answers stand. Briefly:)*
- **Q1. Justice.** Each is given what is due to her, and the wrong is done to her (Thompson; NE V). **Holds.** It borrows only the *equality* of all parties.
- **Q2. A hard case.** Do not take a ventilator from a stable disabled patient to give it to a younger one. That is doing harm, not failing to help (Foot). **Coherent.**
- **Q3. Equal dignity.** Membership in the rational kind, plus the shape of justice. **Holds** for equality and for "not for others." **Breaks**, by the Church's lights, on chosen death (Foot, 1977).
- **Q4. Ultimate good.** *Eudaimonia*: a complete life of virtuous activity among friends, bounded by death. **Coherent.** It is the strongest secular reason to deny that W3's longing belongs to human nature.

### 3b. Exchanges

**E1. A4's "not for the many" undoes A6's honest label.**
*Opponent:* A6 is the most honest paragraph in the case. It says "fewer hold the line when the numbers grow vast," that the Church "holds the line at every number," and that "those are this book's claims … not shared ones." W1(2) says the same: "Many let the line bend when the numbers grow vast." Yet v5's A4, which offers shared truths that "most people find … they cannot give up," now says every innocent is never "to be deliberately killed for others' sake, not for the many and not for the few." That is the every-number claim, placed among the shared premises. The God row does the same thing in a softer form: it says the rule "that no total may be weighed against one person" is one "the best rivals share." So the premises contradict each other on the very point the lab spent Rounds 3 and 4 getting honest.
*Defense:* Conceded as a textual fault. No step needs the phrase. W1(2) is written for both readers ("a reader who holds the line only at five … a reader who holds it at every number"). The God row's every-number claim is already limited to "those who hold it with the Church." So the fix is to delete the phrase, not to change the argument.
*Opponent's rejoinder:* For this school the damage is small. Foot and Anscombe hold the line far. Hursthouse and Nussbaum allow tragic dilemmas, but they say that even then a wrong is done and a remainder is owed. That is nearer the Church's "a wrong done to *her*" than the totals view is. Most of us would grant A4 as written. The fault is the contradiction, not the datum.
*Result:* **Held with repair (Minor for this school).** This is the most important point in this report. For a school that denies the datum outright, it weighs more (R5.1, R5.2).

**E2. "A life that its owner asks to end."**
*Opponent:* A4's new parenthetical calls the person the *owner* of her life. Two rows later, the flourishing row charges my school with treating a life "as though a person owned his life rather than being the one who lives it (CCC 2280)." The book cannot say "its owner" in its own premises and then use that same word against Foot.
*Defense:* Conceded. It is a slip, and the flourishing row already has the right words: "the one who lives it."
*Opponent's second point:* Is the flourishing row fair to Foot? Her ground was not ownership. It was that a right can be waived by the one who holds it, together with the claim that charity serves the good of the one whose life it is.
*Defense:* A right to life that its holder can waive is title over that life, so "as though a person owned his life" is a fair diagnosis. It is marked as the book's reading ("as though"), not put in Foot's mouth.
*Result:* **Held with repair (Minor)** (R5.1). The row stands.

**E3. "Holds her life for her": the trust image turned inside out.**
*Opponent:* In Round 4 your answer to the paradox of deontology rested on the agent being a *steward*. He was given a life to keep, with no title to destroy it. v5 keeps that ("held in trust from the One who alone is Lord of life"). Then it adds that God "holds her life for her, never against her." That makes God the trustee and her the beneficiary. In the law of trusts, a beneficiary who is of age and alone entitled can end the trust and call for what is held for her. So the new phrase gives my school the waiver the book denies: if God holds her life *for her*, she may ask for it. "Never against her" also reads as if God never judges.
*Defense:* Conceded. The Church's image is the reverse (CCC 2280): she is the steward, and God remains Master. The thought v5 wanted ("He never spends her") is already said in GS 24 and SCG III.112. The fix keeps the warmth and restores the structure: her life was given to her *keeping*, not to her *disposal*.
*Result:* **Held with repair (Minor)**, but it matters. As written, the image argues for the claim it is meant to deny (R5.3).

**E4. "He who gives life, and can give it back, may call it home; we, who can do neither, may not take it."**
*Opponent:* This grounds the creature's absolute in an incapacity, not in what the act is. Imagine a surgeon who could kill the innocent patient and restore her an hour later. Does your line now permit him? And "may call it home" because He can give life and restore it sounds like power, which the brief forbids as a ground.
*Defense:* No to both. The line says "neither": no creature gives being, so no creature has title, and restoring a life one has taken grants no title after the fact. The surgeon who kills and restores has still violated her. Power is not the ground either. The same sentence rests on One "who willed her for her own sake," and the case rests His authority on His being the Good (W1 closing; Chapter 3). The line also answers what this school pressed in Round 4 (ST II-II q.64 a.6 ad 1). Only the Giver of life may take it back, and the Giver spends no one. It does this without raising Abraham.
*Result:* **Held.**

**E5. "A man may lay down his life, but he may not hand it over to be destroyed."**
*Opponent:* My school has the right tools here: what is intended versus what is foreseen and accepted. The book's verbs do not draw that line. St. Maximilian Kolbe asked to take a condemned man's place, and in plain English he handed his life over to be destroyed. Scripture says Christ gave Himself up. The line the Church draws is in what the will chooses. One may give one's life and bear the death that others inflict or that danger brings. One may not choose one's own death, and no one may kill a person at his request.
*Defense:* Conceded. The sentence sits inside A6's "this book's claims," so it misleads no one about what is shared. But as written it is false of a canonized martyr. The repair uses this school's own distinction, which the book already holds ("Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent").
*Result:* **Held with repair (Minor)** (R5.4). The censor raises the same point (N1).

**E6. The paradox of deontology, re-run on v5's new sentence.**
*Opponent:* "What protects her is not a weight that enough lives could outweigh, but a right over her life that no number of others has." I pressed this in Round 4: a ground in worth gives you a goal to promote, not a constraint on the agent.
*Defense:* That sentence is the answer, stated more sharply than in v4. It does not deny that she is worth a great deal. It says the protection is a *title* the others lack, not a *weight* they could outweigh. A title is relative to the agent by its nature. "Who willed her for her own sake" says why the title is withheld from them. Worth alone gives a goal; lack of title alone gives a rule with no reason behind it. Together they give a constraint with a reason.
*Opponent's rejoinder:* And my school reaches the same shape and stops at "what justice is," or at inviolable standing. v5 now credits both of those answers.
*Result:* **Held.** This is parity on "for others," with a reason the rival takes as given.

**E7. "A person it never gives away."**
*Opponent:* You now grant, rightly, that the lion lives by the slaying of other animals. But for Aquinas the child's death is also willed: as the evil of penalty, by willing justice (ST I q.19 a.9; q.49 a.2). And what is the difference between letting the parasite take her and giving her to it?
*Defense:* There is a difference in what the order is *for*. The fawn's loss is part of what the lion's life is ordered through. The child's loss is not part of what anything is ordered to: rational creatures are governed for their own sake, and other things are governed for them (SCG III.112). Mortality as a penalty belongs to the human condition. It is not a sentence on this child, and it does not give her to a lower creature. v5 also does not hide that the SCG point makes the child heavier, not lighter ("that weight is on the other side of the scale").
*Result:* **Held.** One optional improvement: put the source on the new claim, "(SCG III.112)," as the censor also suggests.

**E8. W2: "what does not follow from form is that its goods fit."**
*Opponent:* The unity of the virtues is Aristotle's own thesis. Practical wisdom is one, and the virtues fit together because they perfect one form of life. Our "one nature" answer explains the fit.
*Defense:* Then the question moves back one step: is that one nature's harmony an accident or a wisdom? W2 already asks that question and says "it weighs, it does not settle."
*Result:* **Held at parity.**

**E9. Smaller points.**
(a) The W1 frame names "the realist, the contractualist and the Buddhist" as the near rivals who hold that guilt is owed to her. Thompson's bipolar normativity is the paradigm statement of that view. "Among them" leaves room for it, so this is optional (R5.5).
(b) "Few say why the line is not even hers to waive (Kant, who did …)." This is accurate. Foot did not say why, because she denied it.
(c) "(Not every *ought* wrongs someone when it is broken, A7; these two rest on the wrongs that do.)" This is good. It matches Thompson's distinction between monadic and bipolar norms.
(d) "Being human explains why the newborn belongs" credits this school's answer to Q3 exactly.
*Result:* (a) **Optional.** (b)–(d) **Held.**

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**Against v5 as written:**

**As written: NONE.**

Every Round 4 point this school raised is fixed. The ground the book adds over this school, a life held in trust from One who willed her for her own sake, holds against the paradox of deontology (E6) and against Aquinas's own exception (E4), both at parity or better. What v5's new wording broke is wording: two phrases in A4, one image in W1(2), one verb pair in A6, and one phrase in the God row. Each is a one-clause fix. In each case the text around it already says the right thing: A6's label, the flourishing row's "the one who lives it," GS 24 and SCG III.112, and "side effects may be weighed." None breaks a step.

**Minor (wording and precision):**
1. **A4 "not for the many" and the God row's "which the best rivals share"** state the every-number line as shared, against A6 and W1(2) (E1). This is a regression introduced in v5 and the most important Minor point. Schools that deny the datum may fairly rate it higher.
2. **A4 "its owner"** contradicts CCC 2280 and the flourishing row (E2).
3. **W1(2) "holds her life for her, never against her"** makes God the trustee, which hands this school the beneficiary's waiver (E3).
4. **A6 "hand it over to be destroyed"** is false of Kolbe. The line is in what the will chooses (E5).
5. *(Optional)* Name the Aristotelian among the rivals who hold that guilt is owed to her (E9a). Cite SCG III.112 on "a person it never gives away" (E7).

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**
- Whether a nature's standards need a source, or whether asking for one wants a standpoint outside the life of reason (McDowell).
- Whether necessary truths, the core of the moral law among them, need a ground in a Mind.
- Whether the reach of minds to what holds for any mind needs explaining beyond evolution (W2), and whether the fit of the goods is explained by one nature or by one Source (E8).
- The tragic view of the dead (a wrong fixed forever that no one receives) against "she is still someone" (W1(1)).
- Whether the shape of justice that protects her is simply "what justice is" or inviolable standing, or is explained by a title no number of others has, from One who wills each for her own sake (E6).
- Whether a life that is no longer a good to the one who lives it may be ended at his request: Foot against CCC 2280. This remains the one real moral disagreement between this school and the Church.
- Whether W3's longing belongs to human nature or is a Christian shaping of the wish for more.
- The theist's heavier load on the lion and the parasite, against the naturalist's weaker hold on the tortured child (E7).

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**R5.1 A4 (E1, E2).**
- Replace "never, if innocent, to be deliberately killed for others' sake, not for the many and not for the few, and not lost when life grows hard" with:
> never, if innocent, to be deliberately killed for others' sake, and not lost when life grows hard
- Replace "(What the Church holds beyond this, about a life that its owner asks to end, A6 names as this book's claim.)" with:
> (Whether that line holds however vast the numbers, and what the Church holds about a life that the one who lives it asks to end, A6 names as this book's claims.)

**R5.2 C, the God row (E1).** Replace "a ground for the rule that no total may be weighed against one person, which the best rivals share and take as given;" with:
> a ground for the rule that one person may not be spent for others, which the best rivals share, as far as each holds it, and take as given;

**R5.3 W1(2), the trust (E3).** Replace "who willed her for her own sake and holds her life for her, never against her." with:
> who willed her for her own sake and gave her life to her keeping, not to her disposal.

*(This fits the censor's N4. If the censor's "remains its sovereign Master" is added after "Lord of life," keep "to her keeping, not to her disposal" as the closing clause.)*

**R5.4 A6, the consent line (E5).** Replace "a man may lay down his life, but he may not hand it over to be destroyed, and no one may destroy it so." with:
> a man may give his life for another and bear the death that comes, but he may not choose his own death, and no one may kill him at his asking.

**R5.5 (Optional) W1 frame (E9a).** "the realist, the contractualist and the Buddhist among them" → "the realist, the contractualist, the Aristotelian and the Buddhist among them". Evil, *Second*: "A person it never gives away." → "A person it never gives away (*Summa contra Gentiles* III.112)."

## 7. New insights and lines worth keeping

**New insights**
- **Title, not weight.** v5's sentence "not a weight that enough lives could outweigh, but a right over her life that no number of others has" is the cleanest answer the book has given to the paradox of deontology. Keep it in any condensation.
- **Keep the steward on the human side of the trust.** If God is made the trustee, the waiver argument comes straight back: a beneficiary may call in her trust. "Given to her keeping, not to her disposal" keeps the image working for the book (E3).
- **When a repair adds words to A, test them against A6's label.** This round's A4 fault came from wording added around another school's fix. A phrase can put a contested claim back among the shared premises just as a clause can (E1).
- **Double effect draws the martyr's line better than verbs of giving do.** The line falls between accepting a death one does not choose and choosing one's death. That separates Kolbe and the firefighter from euthanasia and the lethal experiment, and it uses the book's own "side effects may be weighed" (E5).
- **The tragic-remainder virtue ethicists are allies on "a wrong done to her."** Hursthouse and Nussbaum say that even when the line bends, a wrong is done and a remainder is owed. That puts them nearer the Church than the totals view is, and the book could say so when it credits the rivals (E1).

**Lines worth keeping**
- "What protects her is not a weight that enough lives could outweigh, but a right over her life that no number of others has."
- "Given to her keeping, not to her disposal." *(proposed)*
- "He who gives life, and can give it back, may call it home; we, who can do neither, may not take it."
- "A person it never gives away. The parasite takes from the child; God does not give her to it."
- "The risen Christ kept His wounds: what a finite love lived through is not erased but kept, and healed."
- "Not a second injury beside hers, but His love refused in her."
