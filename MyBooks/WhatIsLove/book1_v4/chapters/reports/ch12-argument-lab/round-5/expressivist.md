# Round 5: Humean Sentimentalism / Quasi-Realist Expressivism vs. v5

## 1. School and voice

The voices are David Hume (*Treatise* Book 3; *Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals*, EPM; *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion*; the essays "Of Suicide" and "Of Passive Obedience"), Adam Smith (*Theory of Moral Sentiments*, TMS), Simon Blackburn, Allan Gibbard, David Wiggins and John McDowell. On this view, moral judgment voices refined attitudes and plans, and the quasi-realist then earns the realist's words at first order. This round checks v5's handling of my Round 4 Serious objection and its seven Minor repairs. It then reads v5's new wording for anything that misstates this school.

## 2. The four standing challenges (short)

**1. Justice.** For Hume, justice is conventions approved through sympathy with public utility. For Smith, it is fitting resentment of injury to the individual. *Coherent. It breaks at the edge, as Hume admits:* the weak are owed gentleness but not justice (EPM §3 pt 1). v5's C row states this exactly.

**2. A hard case.** The secret promise to a dying friend (A2). Hume's theory of promising misses the wrong. The modern school finds it at first order. *Holds in result. The founding theory breaks on the reason, and the modern school repairs it by commitment.*

**3. Equal dignity.** A stance of equal regard, held as independent of our attitudes. *Coherent; it declines to ground.* It is at parity on proof.

**4. The ultimate good.** Plural happiness, and a review of one's life that one can approve. *Coherent, and smaller.* Hume's calm death shows the longing in W3 can be borne. It does not show the longing is answered.

### Exchange 1. "Of Suicide" against the new placement: does W1(2) still hold?

*Expressivist:* The placement is now honest. But A6 says the chosen-death claim is "argued in W1(2)," so test the argument. Hume has two arguments, not one. The first is the reductio: if disposing of life belongs to God alone, then saving a life trespasses too. The second is the argument from providence: everything happens by general laws God set up. If I turn a few ounces of blood from their course, I disturb providence no more than when I turn a river. If I fall on my sword, I receive my death from the Deity as much as if a lion had killed me.

*Patrick's side:* The first argument runs together caring for what was entrusted and disposing of it. W1(2) now says this directly: the trust is "held for her, never against her," and "He who gives life, and can give it back, may call it home; we, who can do neither, may not take it." The second argument proves too much. If acting within the general laws never encroaches on providence, then murder does not encroach either. Hume therefore has to show that murder is wrong on other grounds: it harms others and society. That moves the whole question to one point: whether a person's life is his own to end. W1(2) gives a reason why it is not, and it scopes that reason: "as the Church holds," "on this account."

*Expressivist:* Yes. Hume does argue that suicide is wrong only if it breaks a duty to God, to neighbor or to self, and the providence argument is there only to clear the first of these. So the disagreement is first-order: whose life it is. Your account gives a reason. Mine denies the premise the reason needs. Neither side compels the other, and v5 now says so.

*Verdict: HELD at parity.* This is the honest standoff I logged in Round 4, and v5 now states it as a standoff. The care/disposal distinction and the providence point belong in the notes to W1(2).

### Exchange 2. The God row's new first clause: "the rule that no total may be weighed against one person, which the best rivals share and take as given"

*Expressivist:* v5 split the old clause in two. The second half is right: "for those who hold it with the Church, why that line has no exceptions and is not even her own to cross." The first half says the best rivals "share and take as given" the rule that "no total may be weighed against one person." Read literally, "no total" means every number. That is the claim A6 calls "this book's claim ... not a shared one," and that A6 and W1(2) both say many let bend "when the numbers grow vast." It is also false of my founder in two ways. Hume did not share the rule: he called "let justice be done though the heavens fall" false, because it sacrifices the end to the means ("Of Passive Obedience"), and he held that the rules of justice are suspended in extreme necessity (EPM §3 pt 1). Nor did he take the rule as given: he explained justice, and its limits, by public utility.

*Patrick's side:* The intended meaning is narrower. Persons are not the kind of thing that totals are weighed against: the five, the riot, the prisoner. The every-number version is the second clause. But "no total" says more than that, and "the best rivals" says more than some of them hold. Kant, the contractualist and the inviolability realists do share the narrower rule and take it as a stopping point. Hume and the rule consequentialist do not. The wording should say "persons may not be spent for totals" and "many of the best rivals."

*Expressivist:* Then I have no complaint. It is the same kind of fault as Round 4, but much smaller. Nothing is put into a shared premise, the next clause scopes the absolute correctly, and the conclusion does not depend on it.

*Verdict: HELD WITH REPAIR (Minor).* One clause has to be reworded so the row stays consistent with A6. Wording is in §6, R1.

### Exchange 3. "Wrong of necessity, not up to anyone": can Hume say it?

*Expressivist:* *Treatise* 3.1.1 denies that morality can be demonstrated. Moral distinctions rest on a constitution of human nature that is uniform, not necessary.

*Patrick's side:* The clause defines its own term: "of necessity, not up to anyone." Hume says that although the rules of justice are artificial, they are not arbitrary (*Treatise* 3.2.1). The moral sentiments are natural, and no one's choice or vote can change them. Blackburn's rigidified attitudes give the stronger reading at first order: cruelty would be wrong even if we approved of it. The book's own claim that the wrong is necessary in the full sense rests on "goodness is His nature" and on W2, where it is argued, not assumed.

*Expressivist:* Granted. As glossed, my school agrees, and the color heirs can say it without strain. A reader who takes "of necessity" in the logician's sense is corrected by the next four words.

*Verdict: HOLDS.* The gloss does the work.

### Exchange 4. The three roads and the C row's last line now agree

*Expressivist:* In Round 4, the C row ended on remorse and road 1 was the road of remorse, so readers would put this school on road 1. Now road 1 "denies" A3. Road 2 belongs to "philosophers without God, who hold that the dead can be wronged." The C row ends: "at the grave, on this account, no one is left to give it." That is road 2's cost, stated in road 2's terms: "what can never be repaid, no one ever releases." The placement is right.

*Patrick's side:* And the weighting sentence now reads "what he owes the one he wronged" instead of "the guilt he actually knows." That moves the weight from the feeling to the debt, which is where your school puts it too.

*Expressivist:* I preferred the old wording for its warmth. The new wording is fairer to the debunker and costs this school nothing.

*Verdict: HOLDS.*

### Exchange 5. C, Feeling, refined: one comma splice left from my own wording

### Exchange 6. W3's new science clause, and Adam Smith's account of the longing

*Expressivist:* v5 adds: "those paths explain a longing for a protector more easily than a longing to be known wholly and told that it is good that we exist." Smith is the counterexample. He traces from sympathy the wish not only to be loved but to be lovely, and the wish to be seen truly. When every human judge misjudges us, he explains, we appeal to the all-seeing Judge. Your own C row quotes that passage. That is a natural history of the longing to be known wholly, not of a wish for a protector.

*Patrick's side:* Smith explains the wish to be judged truly and to be approved as worthy. That comes close to W3's longing but is not the same: W3's word is not "you are worthy" but "it is good that you exist," said before any worth is earned. Still, the comparative claim "more easily" is stronger than the text needs. The sentence before it already has the honest form, which matches the sentence about speaking to the dead in W1(1): "That tells us how we long, not whether there is Someone to long for." If the comparative stays, it should give Smith his due.

*Expressivist:* Keep the honest sentence. Either drop "more easily" or credit Smith and keep the narrower point you just made. On that narrower point, worthiness against existence, I grant there is a real difference.

*Verdict: HELD WITH REPAIR (Minor).* The fairness rule asks for this: a genealogy the book uses for its own point has to be stated at the rival's best. Wording is in §6, R3.

### Exchange 7. The conclusion: "wronged," and Hume's weak creatures

*Expressivist:* Round 4's step ran from "it was wrong" to "no less than yours," and Hume's middle position (wrong because inhumane, but no rights of justice) slipped between the two. v5 asks whether "she was wronged," and sets "any argument that says she was not" beside it. Hume's middle position is one of those arguments. So the reader now has to weigh my founder, under his own terms. Then I press the next step: "If she was wronged, her worth never followed her strength or her mind. Then it follows what she is." It might follow something else: someone's love for her, or her future.

*Patrick's side:* The scene rules out love. She is the newborn "whom instinct passed by." The future belongs with "her mind," because what she is not yet able to do is "know herself." What is left is what she is. "And she is that as fully as you are" is the step to equality, and it is now explicit. This is an appeal, not a proof, and it is addressed only to the one who gives up A4.

*Expressivist:* As an appeal it is fair, and it is second-personal in the way A2 is. A future-of-value theorist could still push, but he does not belong to this school, and the appeal does not claim to compel him.

*Verdict: HOLDS.* v5's answer is better than my question form.

### Exchange 8. The evil section concedes Philo's own conclusion

*Expressivist:* v5 adds: "A world with no one behind it would put pain where survival puts it, which is where we find it." That is Philo's verdict in *Dialogues* XI. Of the four hypotheses about the first causes, the one that fits the mixed phenomena best is a source with neither goodness nor malice. The book concedes it.

*Patrick's side:* It does, and it answers on the right ground. The indifferent source is answered "only by the whole case": the debt that outlives every creditor on earth, worth that does not follow ability, and the longing. The book does not infer goodness from the pattern of pain, which is what Philo forbids. It infers from the moral data and weighs the pain against that.

*Expressivist:* That is the honest structure. Philo's quarrel was with Cleanthes, who argued from the world's order to its Author's goodness. v5 does not make that argument.

*Verdict: HOLDS.* This is an insight: the concession could credit Philo in a note (§7).

### Exchange 9 (our side presses). Smith's own answer to "why justice has that shape"

*Patrick's side:* W1(2) gives two answers rivals offer for why justice will not spend one person for many: "simply what justice is" and "the standing of one who may not be violated." Your school has a third. Smith holds that our concern for the injured individual does not come from our concern for society. We demand that a wrong be punished out of regard for the one wronged (TMS II.ii.3, verify). That is the best sentimentalist account of why the wrong is done to *her*, and it is anti-aggregative at its root.

*Expressivist:* Yes, and it is a stopping point as well: fitting sympathy with her resentment. When the case is the newborn, who can feel no resentment, it reaches her only through an impartial spectator who resents on her behalf.

*Patrick's side:* Then giving Smith his place in the list costs the book nothing. His answer also shows where the book's question bites: the spectator has to stand in for her.

*Verdict: An insight, not a defect.* There is an optional clause in §6, R4.

### Exchange 10. The A heading's new debunking test

*Expressivist:* "Or shows that what produced the belief had nothing to do with its truth." At first order, the quasi-realist reads this as meeting the test. We condemn cruelty because we respond to what cruelty does, which is what makes it wrong. "W2 and the conclusion take up the claim" is accurate: the conclusion now adds "or that you cannot know it."

*Verdict: HOLDS.*

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**As written: NONE.**

No Fatal or Serious objection stands against v5 as written. v5 fixed the Round 4 Serious objection on the placement of the chosen-death teaching in all three places. It also says in the A heading, more plainly than I asked, that many readers deny that teaching. Hume's "Of Suicide" now meets W1(2) where the book claims to argue it, and there it is an honest standoff. Of the new wording, only one clause (Minor 1) comes near the Round 4 fault. It is far smaller: nothing sits in a shared premise, the next clause scopes the absolute correctly, and no conclusion depends on it.

**Minor (wording and scope; none changes a conclusion)**
1. **God row:** "the rule that no total may be weighed against one person, which the best rivals share and take as given." Hume neither shares this nor takes it as given. Read literally, it also conflicts with A6's "Fewer hold the line when the numbers grow vast ... not a shared one" (Exchange 2).
2. **C, Feeling, refined:** the comma splice "it holds, no further why is needed" (my own Round 4 wording; Exchange 5).
3. **W3 ¶1:** "those paths explain a longing for a protector more easily than ..." overstates the case against Smith (Exchange 6).

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- **Whose life is it?** "Of Suicide" against life held in trust. Care and disposal answer Hume's reductio. The providence argument reduces to the same first-order question. v5 now scopes it as the Church's claim.
- **Is there a further "why"?** The quasi-realist's first-order answer against the book's demand for a ground. The C row now states both sides.
- **The debt to the dead.** Road 2 (this school's road: owed forever, never received) against road 3, weighed "as much as the reader finds the third road truer than the second."
- **The coherent dissenter** (the knave, Singer). Neither side's argument compels him.
- **Certainty.** Philo's proportioning against CCC 31 and Newman's convergence.
- **Necessary being** (*Dialogues* IX) against "the One whose very being is to be." Each side has a first principle.
- **The indifferent source** (*Dialogues* XI). The book concedes it fits the pattern of pain and answers it by the whole case.
- **The longing.** Hume's serene death against W3. A longing that can be borne is not thereby answered.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**R1 (Minor, God row). Replace** "a ground for the rule that no total may be weighed against one person, which the best rivals share and take as given; and, for those who hold it with the Church, why that line has no exceptions and is not even her own to cross;" **with:**
> a ground for the rule that a person may not be spent for others' totals, which many of the best rivals share and take as given; and, for those who hold it with the Church, why that line holds at every number and is not even her own to cross;

**R2 (Minor, C, Feeling, refined). Replace** "and once we have said what cruelty does to the child, it holds, no further why is needed." **with:**
> and it holds that once we have said what cruelty does to the child, no further why is needed.

**R3 (Minor, W3 ¶1). Replace** "and those paths explain a longing for a protector more easily than a longing to be known wholly and told that it is good that we exist." **with:**
> Adam Smith traced how, when every human judge misjudges us, we appeal to the all-seeing Judge; that explains a longing to be judged truly. It does not as easily explain a longing to be told, before we have earned anything, that it is good that we exist.

(Or simply delete the clause. The sentence before it already makes the honest point.)

**R4 (optional, W1(2)). Replace** "some answer that this is simply what justice is; others, that a person has the standing of one who may not be violated." **with:**
> some answer that this is simply what justice is; others, that a person has the standing of one who may not be violated; and Adam Smith, that we resent a wrong for the sake of the one it was done to, not for the sake of the whole.

**Optional (A5), carried from Round 4:** after "never by itself tells us which to choose," insert "Hume made this point famous, and on it the Church agrees with him."

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **Hume has two arguments in "Of Suicide," and both reduce to one question.** The reductio (saving life would trespass too) runs together care and disposal. The providence argument (diverting blood is no more an encroachment than diverting a river) proves too much: it would clear murder of encroaching on providence as well, so Hume has to rest everything on whether the life is the bearer's own. The trust account answers exactly that question, and it is scoped as the Church's claim. A note on W1(2) could say this in two sentences, with ST II-II q.64 a.5 and Locke, *Second Treatise* §6.
- **Hume rejected "let justice be done though the heavens fall"** ("Of Passive Obedience"). That makes him the clearest witness that the every-number line is the book's claim and not a shared one. It is also why the God row has to say "many of the best rivals."
- **v5's concession to Philo is the strongest thing in the evil section.** The indifferent source fits the pattern of pain best, which is Philo's own verdict in *Dialogues* XI. The book does not argue from the world's order to its Author's goodness, which is the argument Philo defeated. It argues from guilt, worth and longing, and weighs the pain against them. A note could credit Philo for the concession.
- **Smith is the sentimentalist the book should quote most.** His all-seeing Judge (TMS III.2.33) is already in the C row. His regard for the injured individual over the total (TMS II.ii.3) is this school's best answer to A6's shape. His "to be loved and to be lovely" (TMS III.2.1) is the nearest secular account of W3's longing. Each time, what is left is the gap the book names: worthiness is not existence, and the spectator stands in for her.
- **"Wronged," not "wrong."** The change in the conclusion is small and decisive. It makes the appeal second-personal (A2), and it gives Hume's middle position (wrong because inhumane, but not a wrong done to her) a fair place as "an argument that says she was not."

**Lines worth keeping**
- "A man may lay down his life, but he may not hand it over to be destroyed." (v5)
- "He who gives life, and can give it back, may call it home; we, who can do neither, may not take it." (v5)
- "Not a second injury beside hers, but His love refused in her." (v5)
- "That she was wronged when she was left there, weak and not yet able to know herself, or any argument that says she was not." (v5)
- "Remorse can be lived through; only forgiveness can heal the bond." (v5)
- "Worthiness is not existence: a judge can tell us we are worthy; only love tells us it is good that we exist."
