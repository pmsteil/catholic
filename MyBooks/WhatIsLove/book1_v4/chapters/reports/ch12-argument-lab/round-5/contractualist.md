# Round 5: Contractualism and the Second-Personal Standpoint vs. v5

## 1. School and voice

The voice is a composite of T. M. Scanlon (a wrong is what could not be justified to the one it is done to), Stephen Darwall (obligation is the standing of free and equal persons to make claims on one another), John Rawls, and R. Jay Wallace (guilt and blame as the attitudes by which we hold one another to account), with H. L. A. Hart's will theory of rights and Locke's "workmanship" argument, read as Darwall reads it, as the sharpest tools against a God-grounded claim. All positions are paraphrased; there are no direct quotations.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

1. **Justice.** What anyone is owed follows from principles that no one affected could reasonably reject. *Holds. It presupposes the equal standing it then applies.*
2. **A hard case.** The TRC amnesty (*AZAPO*, 1996; verify): a state may waive punishment, but it cannot forgive what was done to Biko. *Holds, and it supports A3. It has no account of mercy as a gift that heals.*
3. **Equal dignity.** Being born of human parents is reason to count her among those we answer to, through a trustee. *The scope holds. The ground is a reason that "simply holds."*
4. **Ultimate good.** Mutual recognition in a social union of social unions. *Coherent, and it ends at death.*

**Exchange 1. Does moving the doctrine under A6 leave A4 shared?**
*Contractualist:* In Round 4 my complaint was that A4 put a conclusion my school's founders deny (the 1997 Philosophers' Brief, which Rawls and Scanlon signed; verify) into a premise offered as common ground. v5 limits A4 to "never, if innocent, to be deliberately killed for others' sake, not for the many and not for the few." I grant all of that; it is the individualist restriction in plain words. A6 now says openly: "Fewer still think that a man's own request or consent could never make his death right ... Those are this book's claims, argued in W1(2), not shared ones." The A heading names "many who hold all the rest." That is honest.
*Defense:* And the distinction A6 adds, "a man may lay down his life, but he may not hand it over to be destroyed," shows why the firefighter is not a counterexample to the Church's line.
*Contractualist:* Granted as a distinction. Whether it settles the case of the dying man who asks is the euthanasia dispute itself, and the text now calls it that.
*Verdict: Held.* My Round 2 and Round 4 fault is closed.

**Exchange 2. New wording that broke something: "a life that its owner asks to end."**
*Contractualist:* The new A4 parenthesis reads: "What the Church holds beyond this, about a life that its *owner* asks to end, A6 names as this book's claim." Your own flourishing row condemns exactly this word: rivals allow a life to be ended "as though a person owned his life rather than being the one who lives it (CCC 2280)." W1(2) rests the whole absolute on the trust that a life is not owned. Hart's will theory needs only this one premise: if she is the owner, she may waive, and a principle that let a third party override an owner's considered will about her own property is one she could reasonably reject. In one parenthesis the book grants my premise, and in its own voice.
*Defense:* It is a slip. The book holds CCC 2280 throughout ("stewards, not owners"), and A4's parenthesis only points to A6; no step in the argument rests on the word. But a careful opponent will quote it, and a shared premise ought not to concede in passing what W1(2) spends a paragraph denying. Change one word: "the one who lives it."
*Contractualist:* With that change I withdraw it.
*Verdict: Held with repair (Minor).* This is a one-word fix, but it should be made first, because it is the one place where v5 contradicts itself on the very point my school presses.

**Exchange 3. "A right over her life that no number of others has": the landlord comes back.**
*Contractualist:* R3 put this in: God "holds her life for her, never against her." Two sentences later v5 adds: "what protects her is not a weight that enough lives could outweigh, but a right over her life that no number of others has." Grammatically, *what protects her is a right over her life*. Whose right? Not hers, because the sentence before says it is "not hers to dispose of." Not ours. So it is God's right, and her protection becomes a third party's title. That is Locke's workmanship again, and Darwall's charge that the victim is the second creditor in her own case.
*Defense:* The intended sense is the reverse. What protects her is that *no one else has* any right over her life, however many they are; that is what "that no number of others has" was meant to say. And the trust is "for her, never against her," so God's claim and hers are one claim. The sentence should say what it means, without making a right over her life the thing that protects her.
*Contractualist:* Put that way it is my own thought: no one has the standing to spend her. The trust then explains the one thing my school cannot, why she cannot waive it, and it does so without a title that competes with hers.
*Verdict: Held with repair (Minor).* Wording in section 6.

**Exchange 4. "He who gives life, and can give it back, may call it home": is authority now power?**
*Contractualist:* The new line grounds God's standing to end a life in His ability to give it and to give it back. Ability is not authority. A being able to restore what it destroys still needs standing to destroy it. If the line means "He can, so He may," that is voluntarism by another road.
*Defense:* It does not mean "He can, so He may." It means two things. First, He is the giver, and He holds the life *for her* (R3), so calling her home is not done against her. Second, because He can give it back, calling her home takes nothing from her that is not returned, which is exactly what we cannot do. Our "may not" rests on our inability to do either. The authority itself rests where the brief and W1 closing put it: "The law binds because He is good," not because He is strong. And the Judge "lets Himself be asked" (Genesis 18:25). That is second-personal: Abraham holds Him to His own justice.
*Contractualist:* Then what remains is the old standoff: whether a standing that is not equal to ours can still be second-personal. Genesis 18 and Job are your evidence that it can.
*Verdict: Held, at parity.* The line is strong because it explains our "may not" by a lack we really have, not by a title we lack.

**Exchange 5. "Before all else an offense against Him" and "only because we act against the good He wills for us."**
*Contractualist:* v5 now puts two sentences side by side. CCC 1440: the wrong is "before all else" an offense against God. SCG III.122: He is offended only because we act against the good He wills for us. If God's offense is derived from the wrong to her, how is it "before all else"?
*Defense:* They speak of two orders, and the frame states both. In the order of what makes the deed wrong, she comes first: "We see it first in what it does to the child"; "The wrong is wrong because of what it does to her, and so it offends Him." In the order of being, His love is first: her preciousness is His gift, so the wrong done to her is "His love refused in her." It is one wrong seen at its root. "Before all else" names the root, not a second creditor.
*Contractualist:* The phrase "not a second injury beside hers" closes the door I pushed at in Round 4. I have nothing left here.
*Verdict: Held.* No repair is needed.

**Exchange 6. The God row: "which the best rivals share and take as given."**
*Contractualist:* This is my own Round 4 wording, and it is a little loose. My school does not take the rule against weighing a total against one person as given. It *derives* the rule from the individualist restriction: a principle must be justifiable to each person, taken one at a time. What we take as given is the ground, the worth of standing with each person in mutual recognition, as v5's own contractualism row now says correctly. As written, the God row says less than the contractualism row it summarizes.
*Defense:* Granted. The God row claims to supply the ground, so it should say that the rivals rest the rule on something they take as given.
*Verdict: Held with repair (Minor, optional).*

**Exchange 7. The conclusion: "Then it follows what she is." A false choice?**
*Contractualist:* The hillside test runs: her worth did not follow her strength or her mind, "Then it follows what she is." There is a third option: her *relation* to us. Scanlon's ground for counting the newborn is that she is born of human parents.
*Defense:* Being born of human parents is a fact about what she is, her kind and her origin, and not anyone's choice to relate to her. The hillside infant was related to no one who would keep her; A4 makes worth "prior to anyone's recognition." The contractualism row already says your school "cannot explain" why that origin puts her inside the circle. So the step holds, and your ground falls inside "what she is."
*Contractualist:* Granted. One wording point: "Then it follows what she is" can be misread as "then it follows logically what she is." Say "her worth."
*Verdict: Held. Minor wording point.*

**Exchange 8. A quick check of the rest of the new wording, as it touches this school.**
- *A6:* "many ... would let ... a volunteer give his life to an experiment." True of many. As I noted in Round 4, my school mostly would not, because those pressed to consent could reasonably reject such a principle. "Many" does not claim us. *Held.*
- *W1(2):* "few say why the line is not even hers to waive." Fair. My school says why in some cases (lethal experiments), through the reasonable rejection of others, but not in the case of the dying adult. *Held.*
- *Evil, para. 2:* "Among animals that order does give one creature's loss to another's life ... A person it never gives away," with SCG III.112. This is the individualist restriction applied to Providence, and it separates the book cleanly from the Stoic row's Providence that "spends the part" for the whole. On an ex ante reading it still passes my test. Whether the good "offered" is kept remains the promise I cannot inspect. *Held, at parity.*

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**As written: NONE.**

No Fatal or Serious objection from this school stands against v5 as written. Every Round 4 repair was taken. The two faults that v5's new wording introduced are slips of a word or a clause, and the text itself already supplies the answer to each (CCC 2280 in the flourishing row; "for her, never against her" in W1(2)). Neither one changes a premise or a conclusion.

**Minor (wording; none changes a conclusion):**
1. **A4, "a life that its owner asks to end."** This contradicts CCC 2280, the flourishing row and W1(2), and it grants the will theory its one premise in the book's own voice (Exchange 2). It is the most important of the four to fix.
2. **W1(2), "but a right over her life that no number of others has."** As written, the thing that protects her is a right over her life, which can only be God's, and that is the title reading R3 closed (Exchange 3).
3. **God row, "which the best rivals share and take as given."** For this school, the rule is derived and the ground is given (Exchange 6). Optional.
4. **Conclusion, "Then it follows what she is."** This can be misread as a logical "it follows." Say "her worth" (Exchange 7). Optional.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects; v5 names nearly all of them):**
- The dead are owed forever as they were and never answer (road two), or each is still someone who can forgive (road three). The text weighs this "as much as the reader finds the third road truer."
- Whether "each must be answered one by one" needs a ground beyond the worth of mutual recognition (W1(2), C).
- Whether truths about reasons need a home (W2; the realist standoff).
- How her pardon and Christ's healing of the guilty meet at the end. The Church does not spell this out, and the text says so.
- Whether the competent, suffering adult may ask for death. This is the euthanasia dispute, now labeled as this book's claim, argued and not assumed (A6, W1(2)).
- Whether the good "offered" to each sufferer is kept. That is an ex ante promise this school cannot inspect.
- Whether a Creator's standing, which is not equal to ours, can still be second-personal ("may call it home"; Genesis 18:25; Job).
- Whether a final self-exclusion respects persons or exceeds all proportion; CCC 1735 and 1859–1860 narrow this.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**R1. A4** (one word). Replace "about a life that its owner asks to end," with:
> about a life that the one who lives it asks to end,

**R2. W1(2)** (one clause). Replace "what protects her is not a weight that enough lives could outweigh, but a right over her life that no number of others has." with:
> what protects her is not a weight that enough lives could outweigh; it is that no number of others has any right over her life.

**R3. C, A God who is Love** (optional). Replace "which the best rivals share and take as given;" with:
> which the best rivals share and rest on something they take as given;

**R4. Conclusion** (optional). Replace "Then it follows what she is, and she is that as fully as you are." with:
> Then her worth follows what she is, and she is that as fully as you are.

## 7. New insights for the book

- **The protection is that no one else holds a right; the trust explains why she cannot waive it.** These are two separate jobs. A contractualist will gladly say the first: no one has the standing to spend her. Only the trust, held *for her*, explains the second. Keeping them separate in the text lets the rival agree with the first half and see clearly what he lacks for the second.
- **"May call it home" grounds our "may not" in a real lack.** We cannot give life or give it back. The line explains the asymmetry between God and us without saying "He owns it" and without saying "He can, so He may." It is the best non-possessive statement of the Lord of life this lab has produced.
- **"Owner" is the word to watch.** Each time the book speaks of a life someone "owns," it hands the will theory its premise. CCC 2280's "stewards, not owners" should govern every sentence on this topic, the labels included.
- **The individualist restriction now runs from A4 to the evil section.** "Not for the many and not for the few" (A4), "no total answers her" (W1(2)), "a person it never gives away" (evil). This is the place where the book and this school stand together most fully, and the place where the book then asks the one question this school cannot answer: why she is inside the circle at all.

**Lines worth keeping:**
- From v5: "Not a second injury beside hers, for nothing harms God, but His love refused in her."
- From v5: "Who willed her for her own sake and holds her life for her, never against her."
- From v5: "He who gives life, and can give it back, may call it home; we, who can do neither, may not take it."
- From v5: "A person it never gives away."
- From v5: "It asks what could be justified to *her*, not whether anyone chooses to speak for her."
- New: "No one else holds a right over her life; and she holds it in trust, not as an owner."
