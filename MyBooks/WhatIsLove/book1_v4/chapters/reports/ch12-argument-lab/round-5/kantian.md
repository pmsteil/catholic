# Round 5 — Kantian Constructivism vs. v5

## 1. School and voice

The opponent speaks for Kant (*Groundwork*, the second and third *Critiques*, *Metaphysics of Morals*, *Religion*, the Collins lectures) and his heirs Korsgaard, O'Neill, Hill and Wood. His core claims: the moral law is the law of a free will as such; humanity is an end in itself, with dignity and not price; God is a postulate of practical reason, never the ground of obligation. In Round 4 he logged "As written: NONE" against v4 and offered six Minor repairs. This round he checks those repairs, then tests v5's new wording where it touches Kant: the scoped teaching on chosen death, SCG III.122, "He who gives life ... may call it home," and the credit to Kant in W1(2).

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

1. **Justice.** One innate right, freedom from another's forcing choice, so far as it fits everyone's freedom under universal law (*Doctrine of Right* 6:237). Duties of virtue are added to it. *Holds and borrows nothing.*
2. **Hard case.** The murderer. Kant requires execution (6:333) and allows clemency only for crimes against the sovereign (6:337). *Coherent, but it is justice with mercy torn away.* Kantians who reject the death penalty bring mercy back in as respect for the convict.
3. **Equal dignity.** Humanity, as capable of morality, has worth above all price (4:434–435), and the vicious keep it (6:463). *It holds for the enemy. For the newborn it holds only if "humanity" is read as a rational nature present where no reasoning happens.*
4. **Final end.** The highest good, virtue crowned with proportionate happiness. *Kant borrows openly by postulating God and immortality. His secular heirs drop the borrowing and lose the dead.*

All six were taken, at least in substance. Nothing in v5 now misstates Kant. What is left is wording.

### Exchange 1: "The very shape" of W1(1)

- *Kantian:* My rule says the sovereign may not remit punishment for a crime one subject commits against another. Your God remits the eternal punishment of the murderer who repents. So your structure is not "the very shape" of mine. It is mine applied to a mixed case, in which every crime is also against the Sovereign.
- *Patrick's side:* That is exactly the shape. The Church's own division fits it. What absolution remits is the eternal punishment, which answers to the turning away from God. The temporal punishment remains, and so does the duty to repair what one did to her (CCC 1459, 1472–1473). And God "does not take her place in giving the pardon that is hers to give." What is against Him, He forgives wholly. What is owed to her, He does not remit in her name.
- *Kantian:* Granted, and that is a better fit than I expected. But the row says "the very shape" and leaves the reader to rebuild this. One clause would do it.
- **Verdict: Held. Minor (§6, repair 1).**

### Exchange 2: W1(2)'s credit to Kant aims its criticism at the wrong place

- *Kantian:* W1(2) says that few explain why the line "is not even hers to waive," and credits me: "Kant, who did, grounds it in a reason that must stretch to reach the newborn." But only someone who can consent can waive anything, and that means a rational adult. There my ground does not stretch at all. My first worked example is exactly this case (*Groundwork* 4:421–422, 4:429; *Doctrine of Virtue* 6:422–423). The newborn cannot waive anything. So in this sentence the "stretch" objection lands on the one place where my ground is strongest.
- *Patrick's side:* The sentence means Kant's ground for the line as a whole. That one ground protects the newborn and forbids the adult to waive, and it stretches at the newborn. But the objection is fair as to how the sentence reads. There is also an honest gap at the place where waiver is at issue, and it is a better one to name. A duty owed to oneself seems to be one the self could release, and Kant needs two selves in one person to block that: the rational self binds the embodied self (*Doctrine of Virtue* 6:417–418). He also left cases of self-sacrifice open, such as Curtius, and the man bitten by a rabid dog (6:423–424). Some of his heirs let the line bend: Thomas Hill defended a modified Kantian view that allows some self-regarding suicide. W1(2)'s trust ground answers the paradox without two selves. She cannot release what is held for her by Another, and it is held "for her, never against her."
- *Kantian:* I accept the paradox as the real place where we part. My answer, the rational self, is a stopping point, as the book's trust is to me. That is parity. Still, the parenthesis should say where the stretch lies.
- **Verdict: Held (parity). Minor wording (§6, repair 2).**

### Exchange 3: "He who gives life, and can give it back, may call it home"

- *Kantian:* In Round 4 the Abraham problem was settled at parity: a ground of the form *not yours, because it is His* leaves the Holder free. v5 now states that freedom in so many words: He "may call it home." Read against Aquinas, who says one who kills at God's command does not murder (I-II q.94 a.5 ad 2), it invites the voice in Genesis 22. I would answer the voice as I always have: I am certain I must not kill my son, and I am not certain that this voice is God.
- *Patrick's side:* "Call it home" is death given by the Giver, who does not lose her (CCC 998). It is not a licence handed to any creature. The text cites CCC 2258, which says that no one can, under any circumstance, claim for himself the right to destroy an innocent human being directly. The next sentence also binds every creature: "we, who can do neither, may not take it." Your answer to the voice is also the Church's in practice: no command can make an intrinsically evil act good (VS 80).
- *Kantian:* Then the parity holds as it did in Round 4. The new line does not worsen it. If the chapter version wants to shut the door in the text itself, it can add half a clause.
- **Verdict: Held (parity). Optional clause (§6, repair 4).**

### Exchange 4: The God row's list of what rivals leave out

- *Kantian:* The God row lists, among "what the best rivals leave out or must take as given," the reason why the line "is not even her own to cross," scoped "for those who hold it with the Church." I hold that line without the Church, and I argue for it. I do not merely take it as given.
- *Patrick's side:* You argue for it *from* the dignity of rational nature, and that dignity you take as given. So "or must take as given" is true of you. W1(2) also credits you by name.
- *Kantian:* Fair. A reader of the row alone may still think that no rival gives any reason. That is a Minor point, and the row is no longer false.
- **Verdict: Held. Minor, optional (§6, repair 3).**

### Exchange 5: The chosen-death teaching moved under A6

- *Kantian:* I check this as an ally. A6 now says that "fewer still" hold that consent can never make a man's death right, and that "a man may lay down his life, but he may not hand it over to be destroyed." That is close to my own distinction. A man may risk his life in duty, but he may not dispose of himself as a mere means (6:422–423), not even of a part of himself for gain (6:423). It is honestly marked as the book's claim, "not shared." Nothing here misstates Kant, who is one of the "fewer still." Crediting him there is optional.
- **Verdict: Held.**

### Exchange 6: SCG III.122 and impassibility

- *Kantian:* In Round 4, "what it does … to Him" invited the charges of impassibility and pseudo-service. v5 says that "nothing harms God" and calls the offense "His love refused in her." It also cites Aquinas: God is offended only because we act against the good He wills for us.
- *Patrick's side:* That makes morality no service done to God and no favor curried with Him. What He asks is our good. It also fits CCC 1440 ("before all else") as the depth of the same act, not as a second victim.
- *Kantian:* That answers my charge in *Religion* 6:167ff in full. I still deny that ethics contains duties *to* God (6:443–444), and that stays a standoff. The SCG wording is unverified (see §7).
- **Verdict: Held.**

### Exchange 7: Smaller checks on v5's new wording

- **W1 frame, "not every *ought* wrongs someone when it is broken (A7)."** Kant's duties to oneself complicate this, since a lie wrongs the liar's own humanity (6:429). But W1(2) treats self-destruction as a wrong done to her and an offense to Him, and the trust is held for her, so the frame stays consistent. *Held.*
- **W1(2), "our love answers to a worth already there; God's love gives it, by giving her being."** Kant can accept the first half: worth belongs to the nature, and is not conferred by any will that recognizes it. That is the anti-voluntarism Kant demands. The second half is the creation standoff. *Held (parity).*
- **W1(2), GS 24, the only creature willed for itself.** Kant agrees from his own side: man as a moral being is the final end of creation (*Critique of Judgment* §84). *Held, with an ally.*
- **W2, "what does not follow from form is that its goods fit … Christians hold the rest on God's promise."** This is Kant's own antinomy of practical reason. That virtue and happiness fit is exactly what he says requires the postulate of God (CPrR 5:110–114, 5:124–125). *Held, with an ally.*
- **Evil, the parasite and the lion: "a person it never gives away."** This is a double-effect distinction, applied to God as one who wills an order. My school accepts the parallel distinction between intending and foreseeing for human agents. Kant's 1791 essay rejects every theodicy of reason, and v5 offers "a setting, not an explanation" (CCC 309). *Held.*
- **Conclusion, "she is that as fully as you are."** The step to equal worth is now explicit. Kant agrees at the conclusion, and the ground stays a standoff. *Held.*

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**As written: NONE.**

No Fatal or Serious objection from this school stands against v5 as written. v5 took all six of the Kantian's Round 4 repairs in substance, several of them better than proposed. Its new wording breaks nothing for this school:
- the chosen-death teaching is honestly marked as the book's own claim, and Kant is one of the "fewer still";
- SCG III.122 and "His love refused in her" answer the pseudo-service and impassibility charge;
- "may call it home" leaves the Abraham problem at the same parity as Round 4;
- nothing in v5 now misstates Kant.

**Minor (wording only; the arguments stand without these changes):**
1. **Kant row, "has the very shape of W1(1)."** The row should say in one clause how the shape holds: what is against Him He forgives wholly, and the pardon that is hers He does not give in her place (Exchange 1).
2. **W1(2), the parenthesis crediting Kant.** "Must stretch to reach the newborn" is aimed at the question of waiver. Only a rational adult can waive, and there Kant's ground is strongest. The stretch belongs to the newborn's protection, not to waiver. The honest parting point on waiver is the paradox of a duty to oneself (6:417–418) (Exchange 2).
3. **God row (optional).** A half-clause would show that Kant also grounds "not even her own to cross," so that a reader of the row alone does not think no rival gives a reason (Exchange 4).

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**
- **Postulate vs clue.** Kant holds the moral Ruler as rational faith; the book counts it as a clue in a converging case. v5 now states this in the Kant row.
- **Self-standing dignity vs dignity given with being.** Kant grounds dignity in an end in itself; the book grounds it in image, call and life held in trust. Both are stopping points.
- **Duties to oneself.** Kant blocks self-release with the rational self binding the embodied self (6:417–418); the book blocks it with a trust held for her by Another.
- **Abraham and the divine prerogative.** Kant answers the voice from his certainty that he must not kill; the Church answers from CCC 2258 and VS 80. Both refuse in practice. Only the book's ground leaves the Giver free to "call it home," and it cannot be a creature's licence.
- **Duties to God.** Kant puts them outside ethics (6:443–444); the book holds that every wrong is before all else an offense against Him.
- **Form vs end; where necessity lives; certainty.** These are unchanged from Round 4.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**1. C, Kant row: replace** "And his own rule for clemency, that a ruler may remit punishment only for crimes against himself and never for what one subject did to another, has the very shape of W1(1)." **with:**
> And his own rule for clemency, that a ruler may remit punishment only for crimes against himself and never for what one subject did to another (*Doctrine of Right* 6:337), has the shape of W1(1): what is against Him, God forgives wholly; the pardon that is hers, He does not give in her place.

**2. W1(2): replace** "(Kant, who did, grounds it in a reason that must stretch to reach the newborn)" **with:**
> (Kant, who did, grounds it in the humanity in her own person, a ground strongest where she can reason and one that must stretch to reach the newborn; and he must split her into two selves, one binding the other, so that she cannot release herself)

*(Shorter alternative, if the chapter version needs brevity:* "(Kant did, grounding it in her rational nature, which must stretch to reach the newborn who has no say)".*)*

**3. God row (optional): replace** "and, for those who hold it with the Church, why that line has no exceptions and is not even her own to cross;" **with:**
> and, for those who hold it with the Church, why that line has no exceptions and is not even her own to cross, which Kant alone among the rivals also grounds, in a reason that must stretch (W1(2));

**4. W1(2) (optional, to close Abraham inside the text): replace** "we, who can do neither, may not take it." **with:**
> we, who can do neither, may not take it, and no voice that claims His name can make it ours to take (CCC 2258).

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **The Church's two punishments fit Kant's rule of clemency exactly.** Absolution remits the eternal punishment, which answers to the turning away from God. The temporal punishment and the duty to repair what was done to her remain (CCC 1459, 1472–1473). In Kant's terms, the Sovereign remits what was against Himself and does not remit, in her name, what was done to her. This is the clause the Kant row needs (repair 1). *Verify CCC 1472–1473 before use.*
- **Kant's paradox of duties to oneself is the "not even hers to waive" problem.** Kant says that if the one who binds and the one who is bound were the same, one could always release oneself; he answers with two selves in one person (*Doctrine of Virtue* 6:417–418, paraphrase; verify). The book's trust, held "for her, never against her," answers the same paradox without dividing her. This is the exact point where W1(2) does better than Kant, and it is stronger than the newborn point in that place.
- **Kant is an ally on GS 24 and on W2's "fit."** He held that man as a moral being is the final end of creation (*Critique of Judgment* §84; paraphrase, verify). He also held that the fit of virtue and happiness, which form alone cannot secure, requires the postulate of God (CPrR 5:110–114, 5:124–125). The book's "Christians hold the rest on God's promise" is Kant's own moral argument, held as faith in a Person rather than as a postulate.
- **Kant's conscience as a court before Another.** Kant held that conscience must be thought of as a judgment before a person other than oneself, an ideal judge who knows the heart, and that this leads one to think of God, though only as an idea (*Doctrine of Virtue* 6:438–440; paraphrase, verify). This is a second Kantian witness for W1 beside *Religion* 6:98–99, and it may serve the chapter better, because it concerns guilt directly.
- **Kant's denial that God authored the moral law** (Collins 27:283; *Metaphysics of Morals* 6:227: God can be thought of as lawgiver, not as author of the law) is the Kant row's sentence, and it is accurate. It is also the book's Euthyphro answer from the other side.
- **SCG III.122 is still unverified.** The line "we do not offend God except by doing something contrary to our own good" circulates widely in Catholic sources, but this session found no primary-text confirmation of the chapter number. A vatican.va page earlier thought to quote it (Pius XII, 1949) does not contain the phrase. Check it against a Latin critical edition (Leonine) or the Pegis/Bourke translation before it enters the book.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "Not a second injury beside hers, but His love refused in her." (v5)
- "The wrong is wrong because of what it does to her, and so it offends Him who loves her." (v5)
- "What is against Him, He forgives wholly; the pardon that is hers, He does not give in her place."
- "She cannot release what is held for her."
- "He who gives life, and can give it back, may call it home; we, who can do neither, may not take it." (v5)

## 8. Score

**9.5/10 as written (9.5 with the Minor repairs, which are polish).** v5 took every Kantian repair from Round 4, several of them better than proposed. Its new wording breaks nothing for this school. What remains between Kant and the book are honest standoffs (postulate vs clue, self-standing vs given dignity, two selves vs a trust), each stated fairly in the text.
