# Argument Lab, Round 5: Buddhist opponent vs v5

## 1. School and voice

The same composite voice as Rounds 1–4: the Theravada Pali Canon and its commentaries, and Indian Madhyamaka (Nagarjuna, Candrakirti, Santideva), with the Pure Land (Shinran) brought in where it bears on the text. ## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges (short)

1. **Justice.** No one is dearer to anyone than himself, and each holds himself just as dear, so harm no one (SN 3.8; Dhp 129–130); the wronged one must be asked for pardon (Vinaya, Cullavagga I). **Holds**; nothing borrowed.
2. **Dilemma** (a dying man asks a monk to help him die). No: the Vinaya expels a monk who praises death to the sick or helps one to die (Parajika 3); the will to end a life comes with aversion; what is owed is care (Mahavagga 8.26). **Holds**, without borrowing (Exchange 6).
3. **Equal dignity.** Every being suffers and holds its life dear; the mind is luminous by nature (AN 1.51); Mahayana adds buddha-nature. **Holds for equal compassion; bends for equal dignity of persons**, since the highest good is open by capacity (AN 8.29).
4. **Ultimate good.** Nirvana, or emptiness realized, in which wisdom and compassion cannot be separated. **Holds; coherent.** Love is not the ground of things.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1. "They can receive what the living dedicate to them."**
*Buddhist:* Thank you for naming rebirth. But the sentence says more than our texts do. The Buddha told the brahmin Janussoni that a gift offered for a dead relative reaches him only if he has been born among the departed spirits; one born in hell, as an animal, as a human or as a god lives on the food of that realm, and the gift does not reach him, though the giver is not without reward (AN 10.177, checked on accesstoinsight). The Tirokudda verses (Khp 7) are about those departed spirits too. "The dead ... can receive" makes it general.
*Patrick's side:* Granted; it was the Buddhist's own wording from Round 4, and the correction costs nothing. It even helps the contrast: on that account, whether the dead can receive anything from us depends on where her deeds took her. The Catholic says she lives to God, and the living can reach her in love wherever she is (*Spe Salvi* 48).
*Verdict:* **Held with repair (Minor).** (Repair W1(1).)

**Exchange 2. "Will not know what was done."**
*Buddhist:* Said twice (W1(1) and the Dhamma row), flatly. On the night of his awakening the Buddha recalled his past lives in detail (MN 4, MN 36), the Jatakas are built on that recollection, and the texts allow it to disciples of deep concentration. So the one born again *can*, in principle, come to know.
*Patrick's side:* Granted on the wording: "as a rule." The point survives. Ordinary beings do not recall; and the one who does recalls a stream, "there I was so named," not a self who was wronged, by his own account. The Round 4 standoff over whether persons are real at the ground of things is untouched.
*Verdict:* **Held with repair (Minor).** (Repairs W1(1) and C-Dhamma.)

**Exchange 3. "His path ends the debt by the doer's own purification, not by her pardon."**
*Buddhist:* The same sentence that names the Thai funeral rite then says our path does not run through her pardon. But the rite *is* a request for her pardon: the mourners pour water over the dead person's hand and ask her to forgive any wrong done (checked against an ethnographic description of the Thai rite). The Thai phrase asks that the deed be let lapse between the two, so that no enmity follows them into lives to come. And the Vinaya sends Sudhamma to Citta for pardon, as your own Dhamma row now says. So the sentence contradicts the half before it, and contradicts the row.
*Patrick's side:* There are two debts in your teaching, and the sentence ran them together. The debt to her, the broken bond and the enmity, you answer by asking her pardon, living or dead; the text grants that and should keep it. The deed's own power over the doer is ended only by his purification or by its ripening: Angulimala was freed without one word of pardon from those he killed (MN 86), and Ajatasattu's confession was accepted yet he still bore the fruit of his deed (DN 2). Say exactly that. And notice where it lands: the Church too says absolution heals the bond with God but "does not remedy all the disorders sin has caused" (CCC 1459). Both traditions say a pardon heals the bond and does not undo everything. We part over whether *she* can still give hers.
*Verdict:* **Held with repair (Minor), and a gain.** The parting is now drawn more exactly. (Repair W1(1).)

**Exchange 4. Two third roads, one clue.**
*Buddhist:* The clue "weighs as much as the reader finds the third road truer than the second." But v5 now calls rebirth "a form of the third road." A reader who finds rebirth truer than the second road has met the clue's condition and has not moved toward God.
*Patrick's side:* The sentence just before the clue answers it: guilt asks for what it could have only if she is still someone, *herself*. Rebirth, on its own account, does not keep her as herself. The third road the clue weighs is the one on which she is kept.
*Buddhist rejoinder:* Then say so in the clue, or the careful reader will count my road as yours.
*Patrick's side:* Fair. Four words.
*Verdict:* **Held with repair (Minor).** The argument was sound; the wording let it slip. (Repair W1(1) clue.)

**Exchange 5. The new parenthesis: "a world in which pain does not follow justice, as ours does not."**
*Buddhist:* I asked for this honesty in Round 4, and v5 gives it: if fit points to a Mind, the lack of fit weighs against one. Good. But "as ours does not" is said flatly, inside a row that grants karma only conditionally. I hold that across lives it does follow, so far as the Buddha would allow (SN 36.21). You have denied karma by assertion in the middle of describing it.
*Patrick's side:* The row means what can be seen. The evil section says pain in nature falls where survival would put it, not where justice would; that is what one life lets us see, and karma's fit is across lives no one sees. Add that, and the row is fair to both: if karma is as the Buddha taught, its fit asks for a mind; if pain falls as we see it fall, that weighs against a just Mind, and the book weighs it.
*Buddhist (Madhyamaka) rejoinder:* And the fit, where it holds, arises from conditions and is empty; it asks for no coordinator.
*Patrick's side:* That is the Round 4 standoff over whether order asks for a ground. Parity.
*Verdict:* **Held at parity, with a wording repair (Minor).** (Repair C-Dhamma.)

**Exchange 6. Chosen death: the Buddhist holds the line too, and has his own reason.**
*Buddhist:* v5 moved the Church's teaching on chosen death under A6 as "this book's claims," and the God row claims to explain, "for those who hold it with the Church," why the line has no exceptions and is not even hers to cross. We hold the first half without the Church. The precept against killing knows no numbers. A monk who praises death to the sick, or helps one to die, is expelled (Parajika 3). Our reason is not taken as given: the mind that wills a death always comes with aversion, so no number of lives saved makes that will wholesome (the Abhidhamma commentaries; Keown's reading). So the line is neither "left out" nor "taken as given" by us.
*Patrick's side:* Granted, and the book does not say otherwise: A6 says "many," not all, would let a dying man ask for death, and the Buddhist is not among them. He is an ally here. But look at what his reason explains. It says why *I* must not will her death: the will to kill harms the one who wills it. It does not say why her death would be a wrong done to *her*, which is A6's own question of every account. And the Pali texts do not hold the second half, "not even her own to cross," as the Church does: the Buddha declared the monk Channa blameless after he took his own life in great pain (SN 35.87; similarly Vakkali, SN 22.87).
*Buddhist rejoinder:* The commentaries read Channa as reaching awakening at the moment of death, which is why he was blameless; it is no licence for anyone else. And our reason protects her as surely as yours.
*Patrick's side:* It protects her as surely; it does not say she was wronged. That is the A6 parting, met honestly, and the God row's claim is limited to "those who hold it with the Church."
*Verdict:* **Held at parity.** No defect; v5's wording ("many," "for those who hold it with the Church") already leaves room for the Buddhist. Optional insight below.

**Exchange 7. The Pure Land: "a buddha who was once bound as we are."**
*Buddhist (Pure Land voice):* In the Larger Sutra, Amida was once the monk Dharmakara who made his vows, so the row's line fits the story. But Shinran sings that Amida reached buddhahood in the infinitely remote past and took the form of Sakyamuni out of compassion for foolish beings (*Jodo Wasan* 88, checked in translation), and he reads Amida as the form that formless suchness takes in order to reach us. On that reading the compassion that saves us was never bound. And then is it true that for us "love is not the ground of things"?
*Patrick's side:* Two answers. First, the row should say "in the sutra's telling," since Shinran reads the story more deeply; that is a fair correction. Second, the row's last line still holds on Shinran's reading: suchness taking form as compassion saves beings already there; it gave no one being, and it does not know her before she was. "Ground of things" in the God row means the source of beings, and there even Shinran does not place Amida, since beings arise from ignorance and deeds without beginning. This is the nearest point between the two traditions in the whole case, as it was in Round 4.
*Buddhist rejoinder:* Then your God row's "love is the last word" is not a contrast with us: for Shinran, Amida's compassion is the last word too.
*Patrick's side:* Then let the row say what is ours alone: love is the first word as well as the last. That is the true contrast with every Buddhist school.
*Verdict:* **Held with repair (Minor; the second part optional).** (Repairs C-Dhamma and C-God.)

**Exchange 8. The lion and the fawn: "A person it never gives away."**
*Buddhist:* v5's new answer on natural evil says the order God wills gives one animal's loss to another's life, but never a person. For us the fawn is a being who holds its life dear, who may once have been your mother (Sn 1.8, paraphrased; the teaching that every being has been one's mother is in SN 15.14–19). Your line between persons and beasts is where your God's order spends someone.
*Patrick's side:* The book puts this on the heavy side of the scale, says so in plain words, concedes that the Buddhist bears it more easily, and says the fawn's hope is thinner than the child's. It does not pretend. But your account has a price here too: on it the fawn is born a fawn by the fruit of its own past deeds. We do not say the fawn deserved the fire.
*Buddhist rejoinder:* Nor do we say it was given to the lion by anyone.
*Verdict:* **Held at parity.** No repair. Each account is honest about its cost for the animal.

**Exchange 9. The weighing itself (checked again).**
*Buddhist:* With every concession v5 makes, does "best explanation" still beat us?
*Patrick's side:* It names the points it beats you on, and they are unchanged: a guilt that asks to be forgiven by *her, herself*, after she is gone; a worth that is a call to this one, not a nature held whole by all; love as the first word, the ground of things, and not only the seed and fruit of a path; and that it is good that she exists, not only that her birth is a chance. It claims a judgment, not compulsion.
*Verdict:* **Held at parity.** The deepest standoff, honestly drawn.

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**As written: NONE.**

No Fatal or Serious objection stands against v5 as written. Every Round 4 repair was taken, and the new wording does not misstate this school on anything the case rests on. I tested each new sentence that touches Buddhism (rebirth on the third road, "this hope promises it," the fit parenthesis, the moved teaching on chosen death, the lion) and found only small faults of fact or wording. The strongest candidate, the chosen-death claim in the God row (Exchange 6), is not a defect: v5 limits it to "those who hold it with the Church," and A6 says "many," not all.

**Minor**
1. **W1(1):** "the dead ... can receive what the living dedicate to them" is too general; the texts say only those born among the departed spirits receive it (AN 10.177; Khp 7) (Exchange 1).
2. **W1(1) and C-Dhamma:** "will not know what was done" should read "will not, as a rule, know"; recollection of past lives is taught (MN 4, MN 36) (Exchange 2).
3. **W1(1):** "his path ends the debt by the doer's own purification, not by her pardon" runs the debt to her (answered by asking pardon, as the funeral rite in the same sentence shows) together with the deed's power over the doer (ended by purification or ripening; MN 86, DN 2) (Exchange 3).
4. **W1(1) clue:** "the third road" should name the road on which she is kept as herself, since rebirth is now called "a form of the third road" (Exchange 4).
5. **C-Dhamma:** "as ours does not" denies karma by assertion; add "so far as one life lets us see" (Exchange 5).
6. **C-Dhamma:** "a buddha who was once bound as we are" fits the Larger Sutra's story but not Shinran's reading of Amida as awakened from the remote past (*Jodo Wasan* 88); add "in the sutra's telling" (Exchange 7).
7. **C-God (optional):** "love is the last word" does not separate the Catholic from Shinran; "the first word and the last" does (Exchange 7).
8. **C-Dhamma (optional):** cite Vinaya, Cullavagga I, for Sudhamma and Citta.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Whether persons are real at the ground of things: rebirth keeps a stream; guilt asks for her, herself (Exchanges 2, 4).
- Whether a pardon from the dead victim herself is still possible, given that both traditions agree a pardon heals the bond and does not undo everything (Exchange 3; CCC 1459; MN 86).
- Whether the fit of hearts and fortunes asks for a mind, or arises from conditions and is empty; and, on the other horn, whether a world in which pain does not follow justice weighs against a just Mind (Exchange 5).
- Whether the line against killing is grounded in the killer's mind or in a wrong done to her (Exchange 6).
- Whether compassion that takes form to save beings already there (Shinran) is the same as love that gives beings their being (Exchange 7). The nearest point between the two traditions.
- Whether the animal's loss is given by an order God wills or by its own past deeds (Exchange 8).
- Whether it is good that we exist, or only that a human birth is a precious chance to be free of birth. The root of every other parting.

## 7. New insights; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **Both traditions say a pardon heals the bond and does not undo everything.** The Thai mourners ask the dead to let the deed lapse between them; yet Angulimala was freed without his victims' pardon (MN 86), and Ajatasattu's accepted confession did not spare him the fruit (DN 2). The Church says the same of absolution (CCC 1459). The parting is narrower and truer than "pardon versus purification": it is whether *she herself* can still give hers.
- **On chosen death the Buddhist is an ally, with a different reason.** The precept against killing knows no numbers, and the Vinaya expels the monk who praises death to the sick (Parajika 3). His reason lies in the killer's mind; ours lies in a trust held for her. The book could credit him by name under A6, as it now credits him under A2.
- **Shinran is the nearest point.** For him the compassion that saves was never bound and takes form to reach us. What still separates us is the first word: it saves beings already there; it did not give them being.
- **Merit reaches only the departed spirits (AN 10.177); the Church's prayer reaches the dead wherever they are (Spe Salvi 48).** A quiet contrast the book can use if it wishes.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "A pardon heals the bond; it does not undo the deed. We part over whether she can still give hers."
- "His reason protects her as surely as ours; it does not say she was wronged."
- "Love is the first word and the last."
- "Rebirth keeps a stream; guilt asks for her, herself."

**Checked this round:** AN 10.177 (gifts reach only the departed spirits; the giver is not without reward), accesstoinsight; the Thai water-pouring funeral rite as asking the dead person's forgiveness, USC folklore archive; Shinran, *Jodo Wasan* 88 (Amida awakened in the remote past, appearing as Sakyamuni), in translation. **To verify before use:** MN 4, MN 36 (recollection of past lives); MN 86; DN 2 and its commentary on Ajatasattu; SN 35.87 (Channa), SN 22.87 (Vakkali); SN 15.14–19; Vinaya Parajika 3 and Cullavagga I; the Abhidhamma teaching that killing always arises with aversion (and Keown's use of it).
