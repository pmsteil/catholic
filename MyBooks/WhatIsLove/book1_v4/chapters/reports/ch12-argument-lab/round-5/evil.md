Version under test: v5 (EVOLUTION.md, lines 194–235). Read first: BRIEF.md (all additions through Round 5), round-4/evil.md (this school's Round 4 report), round-4/synthesis.md. ## 1. School and voice

The staunch "a good God would never allow this" atheist: Mackie (the logical problem, and his own 1955 question: why not free creatures who always choose the good?), Rowe (the fawn, the policy question, friendly atheism), Draper (indifference), Schellenberg (non-resistant non-belief), Maitzen (theodicy against ordinary morality), Law (the evil god), Adams and Talbott (hell as a horror never defeated), and Ivan Karamazov. In Round 5 he checks v5's repairs, then turns v5's new sentences against each other and against Thomism.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

1. **Justice.** Brute badness of agony (Rowe), invention (Mackie), ordinary intuition (Maitzen). *Holds as a position; Rowe's moral force borrows A1.*
2. **Hard case.** The newborn in pain that cannot be relieved: many in the school would end her life. *Coherent; it rests on a premise the book denies (A4, A6).*
3. **Equal dignity.** Invented, brute, or interests that come in degrees. *Breaks, or stops at a brute fact. The argument from evil weighs most on our premise (A4).*
4. **Flourishing.** Love, knowledge and relief of suffering, inside mortality. *Coherent and bounded. On his view history's victims are never answered, and v5 rightly declines to count that as evidence.*

### Exchange 1. Mackie and Adams: the good of giving love back, and the unequal road

*Attack:* You now say God can give the highest good as pure gift, and does, to the baptized child who dies. He also willed creatures who give their love back in a life where it could be refused. Then there are two roads, and God chooses who walks which. The infant reaches beatitude with no risk. The adult reaches it at the risk of losing it forever. If the good of giving love back is a good *for her*, it is no good to the one who is finally lost. For him the road ended in the worst thing your faith knows. A God who has a riskless road, and uses it for some, has put others on a road with infinite stakes.

*Reply:* (a) v5 claims only that the probation is a good, and that its worth against this world's cost is "part of the weighing, not settled by it." So the unequal road is a weight inside a weighing v5 already owns. It is not a contradiction. (b) The good is real for each person who walks it. The one who gives love back gives something the infant, though blessed, did not give. That is the dignity of a cause (CCC 306), and it is offered to every person. (c) The stakes are not handed to a toddler. v5 holds no one to account beyond what he knew, or could and should have known, and freely chose, and it says fear and wounds can lessen responsibility or remove it (CCC 1735, 1857, 1859–1860). God predestines no one to the refusal (CCC 1037).

*Rejoinder (Adams):* A finite, damaged creature still carries infinite stakes.

*Final reply:* That is the hell standoff, and v5 puts it on the scale in so many words.

**Held at parity.** The repair did what it was meant to do. The question of unequal stakes is an honest standoff (Section 5).

### Exchange 2. The infanticide reductio against the infant clause

*Attack:* If the baptized child who dies before she can choose goes straight to the highest good, risk-free, then whoever kills baptized infants secures their salvation. Your infant clause makes early death the best lot.

*Reply:* v5's new wording answers this itself. The infant who dies is blessed, but she does not receive the further good v5 now names: love given back of her own accord. So early death is not the best lot. It is a different gift, and it lacks something. And even if it were the best lot, A6 forbids the act: one may never do evil so that good may come of it (CCC 1789). A4 adds that the innocent may never be deliberately killed for others' sake. That holds a fortiori when the killing is claimed to be for the victim's own sake against her life.

*Rejoinder:* None worth pressing.

**Held.** The R1 wording ("That is a good, not a necessity") closes a reductio that a bare "pure gift" sentence would have left open.

### Exchange 3. Mackie 1955: free creatures who never in fact sin, and Mary

*Attack:* My original argument was never "why not begin with heaven?" It was this: if freedom is compatible with always choosing the good, an omnipotent God could have made free creatures who always choose it. Your own faith supplies the case. Mary "remained free of every personal sin her whole life long" (CCC 493). She lived in this life, where love could be refused, and freely never refused it. So the good v5 names, love given back in a life where it could be refused, requires that refusal be *possible*, not that any refusal *happen*. v5 says "Free will answers much of the evil we do to one another." On your own Thomism it does not. God can move a will infallibly without forcing it (ST I-II q.10 a.4; q.112 a.3), and He did so for Mary. Free will tells me whose act the sin is. It does not tell me why God did not do for Adam what He did for Mary.

*Reply:* (a) Granted on the shape of the question. Free will answers *whose* the evil is (CCC 311), and that answer is real and it is needed. Why God did not prevent the first sin is a further question. (b) The Church asks exactly that question and answers it. CCC 412: "why did God not prevent the first man from sinning?" Leo the Great answers that Christ's grace gave us blessings better than those the devil's envy had taken away. Aquinas answers that God permits evil to draw forth some greater good, where sin increased, grace abounded all the more, and the Exsultet's "happy fault." That is CCC 324's principle (a good from that very evil) applied to the first sin, and v5 already carries CCC 324. (c) Catholics may also give Plantinga's answer, which Round 1 accepted. On the Molinist view, which the Church permits, it may not have been within God's power to make a world of free creatures who all freely never sin, though it was within His power for one. Either way the logical problem is answered: there is no contradiction.

*Rejoinder (Mackie):* "A greater good from the Fall" is a good bought with every horror since.

*Final reply:* It is not bought. CCC 312 says the evil never becomes good, and v5 keeps *why He permits* apart from *what He brings*. Whether the good is worth the cost is the evidential question, and v5 weighs it there.

**Held with repair (Minor).** As written, "Free will answers much of the evil we do" is not false. "Much of" is modest, and CCC 324 is present. But v5 now answers Mackie's weaker form ("heaven first") and leaves his famous form unstated. One clause would place CCC 412 where it belongs (R1). The Molinist/Thomist split is not a defect: both give a coherent answer.

### Exchange 4. Rowe and the Thomist: "his own act, which God in no way causes"

*Attack:* v5's second reason says: "God is at work in every cause (CCC 308), but the evil a free creature does is his own act, which God in no way causes." Read the grammar. "Which" points to "his own act." Your own Aquinas says God *is* the cause of the act of sin, though not of its defect (ST I-II q.79 a.2). Then the sentence contradicts both Aquinas and the clause before it. Either God causes the act, and you owe me the distinction between causing an act and causing its evil, or He does not, and "at work in every cause" is false.

*Reply:* Granted as wording. The intended claim is CCC 311's: God is in no way the cause of *moral evil*, that is, of the defect. He is the first cause of the act's being, and the defect is the creature's alone (ST I-II q.79 a.2). That distinction is the Church's, and it is coherent: the evil is a lack, and a lack needs no cause of its being (Chapter 1; v5's first reason). The sentence should say that the *evil* in the act is the creature's own, not that the act is.

*Rejoinder (Mackie):* The distinction is a dodge.

*Final reply:* It is the same distinction v5's first reason already defends, that evil has nothing of its own. If evil is a privation, the distinction is exact.

**Held with repair (Minor).** One relative clause (R2). The doctrinal censor may flag this as well.

### Exchange 5. Draper: "A person it never gives away" beside "He wills some things to be naturally corrupted"

*Attack:* v5 says that in willing the order God "wills some things to be naturally corrupted." Then: "A person it never gives away. The parasite takes from the child; God does not give her to it." But the order does give the child's body to corruption. Children die of parasites, and the order in which they die is willed. And the parasite's good is in fact made out of her loss, exactly as the wolf's is made out of the fawn's. "Never gives away" is either false as a description, or it is a claim about intention that no evidence could reach.

*Reply:* It is a claim about ends, and it is the precise Thomist one. Among animals the order *ordains* one creature to another's life: the fawn is, in the order, for the wolf (the lion text). No person is ordained so. The child is not, in God's order, *for* the parasite. Her body is subject to corruption in a world of real causes, but she herself is governed for her own sake (SCG III.112, which v5 cites three sentences earlier). So "never gives away" does not deny that the parasite takes. The very next sentence says it does. It denies that her loss is what she is for. That is not beyond all evidence. It is the claim that the case as a whole (W1(2), GS 24) supports, and that the hope of her resurrection fills out.

*Rejoinder (Draper):* Then the difference between the fawn and the child is in what comes after, and that is "what He brings," which you promised to keep apart from "why He permits."

*Final reply:* No. The difference is in what each is *for*, which belongs to *why He permits*: the order is willed with persons in it as ends, not as fodder. What comes after shows that God keeps faith with that end. Whether this is enough is Draper's datum, and v5 puts the parasite on the scale in so many words.

**Held at parity.** The wording is right, and SCG III.112 is close by. The parasite remains Draper's best datum and an owned standoff.

### Exchange 6. Ivan, Law and Maitzen: "may call it home" against "does not give her to it"

*Attack:* W1(2) now says: "He who gives life, and can give it back, may call it home." The evil section says: "The parasite takes from the child; God does not give her to it." When the parasite kills her, which is it? If God called her home, He used the parasite as His instrument and gave her to it. If He did not, your Lord of life stood by at her death. And Maitzen: if every death is God calling her home, and He holds her life "for her, never against her," then the doctor who saves her resists a call made for her good. That is my paralysis again.

*Reply:* (a) The two sentences name two different directions. God may take her *to Himself*. He never gives her *to what took her*. Her death is, in His providence, her going home, not the parasite's meal. That is the same distinction as Exchange 5, read from the other side. (b) On Maitzen: v5 has already closed this. We are not Lord of life, and we do not know the hour. "The hands that pull a child from the fire are part of what He made real, so His permission never excuses ours (CCC 2269)." If the doctor saves her, that too is the order God wills, with real causes in it. The trust is "held for her, never against her." That is why we must guard her life, not why we may let it go.

*Rejoinder (Ivan):* I do not want her called home at two.

*Final reply:* Neither does the book. That is the weight, and Ivan's refusal is love's protest, not a counter-argument.

**Held.** No inconsistency. An optional line for the book (Section 7): "He may take her to Himself; He never gives her to what took her."

### Exchange 7. Adams and Talbott: hell, "taught, not shown," and grace that wins without force

*Attack:* v5 now says God "does not force His love on anyone able to refuse it," and that the finality is taught, not shown. Honest. But on your own Thomism God can move a will infallibly and freely, as He moves the elect. Not forcing is not the same as not winning. Why does He win some and not others?

*Reply:* v5 makes no claim to answer that, and it should not. The Church teaches that God predestines no one to hell (CCC 1037) and desires all to be saved (1 Timothy 2:4), and she has never settled the Thomist–Molinist dispute about how grace and freedom meet. v5 says the finality "is the Church's teaching, not something this case shows, and so hell stays on this side of the scale." That is exactly the right scope.

**Held.** Talbott's question is an honest standoff, stated against the Church's teaching, not against v5's reasoning.

### Exchange 8. Draper on the repaired comparison

*Attack:* You now name the indifferent world as the rival that bears evil most easily, and you add the converse to the Dhamma row. So what keeps that rival from winning?

*Reply:* The first reason says it: the indifferent source is "answered only by the whole case" (the debt that outlives every creditor, worth that does not follow ability, the longing). The section is now consistent. Each rival's advantage on evil is counted, and each rival's cost elsewhere is counted.

**Held.** This was Draper's misstatement by omission. It is fixed, and the comparison is now fair to him.

### Exchange 9. Schellenberg on hiddenness at full size

*Attack:* "For many it does not come in this life at all. Yet ... it can be made before the name is known." A choice made without knowing whom you choose is not the conscious relationship a loving parent gives.

*Reply:* Granted, and v5 says so ("not a full answer to why Love would choose this way"). What v5 now claims is narrower and true: the lifelong non-believer is not shut out (LG 16; GS 22), and God's way through persons has its ground (CCC 306). "A child is loved long before he can say who loves him" is offered as a picture of the claim, not as proof.

**Held.** The cost is stated at full size, and the answer is clearer for it. Schellenberg's standoff is unchanged.

### Exchange 10. Rowe's friendly atheism: the conclusion restored

*Attack:* Do "certainty is not out of reach" and "an honest reader may weigh it differently" still pull against each other?

*Reply:* Newman's clause joins them: certainty "from many signs converging in one mind, not from any one of them compelling every mind." CCC 847 keeps the dissenter honest.

**Held.** I accept the certainty claim as the Church's claim, which I deny. That is a standoff.

### Exchange 11. The Stoic row beside Aquinas's lion

*Attack (Draper, for consistency):* v5 criticizes the Stoic Providence that "cares for the whole and spends the part for it." Three sections later it says that Thomist providence gives one animal's loss to another's life for the order of the whole. A reader sets the two side by side and sees the same move.

*Reply:* The Stoic charge concerns persons. The person is the part the Stoics spend, and the row's next clause ("does not keep the dead") says so. v5's lion sentence is scoped to animals, and "A person it never gives away" is the contrast.

**Held.** At most, an optional two-word scope would make the contrast explicit (R3).

## 7. New insights and lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **The repaired "heaven first" pays twice.** It answers Mackie at parity, and it also closes the infanticide reductio that "pure gift to the infant" would otherwise invite. The infant who dies is blessed, but she has not given love back, so early death is not the best lot (Exchange 2).
- **Free will answers *whose* the evil is, not *why it was permitted*.** The Church keeps these apart too. CCC 311 answers the first, and CCC 412 and 324 answer the second. Mary shows why the second question cannot be skipped (Exchange 3).
- **"Taken to Himself" and "given to what took her" are opposite directions.** That is how the Lord of life of W1(2) and the parasite sentence of the evil section fit together (Exchange 6).
- **Ends, not outcomes, separate the fawn from the child.** In the order, the fawn is for the wolf, and the child is for no one but herself and God. That belongs to *why He permits*, not only to *what He brings* (Exchange 5).

**Lines worth keeping**
- "That is a good, not a necessity." (v5)
- "A person it never gives away. The parasite takes from the child; God does not give her to it." (v5)
- "He did not keep her from what was done to her; that weight is on the other side of the scale, and this book weighs it there." (v5)
- "He who gives life, and can give it back, may call it home; we, who can do neither, may not take it." (v5)
- "He may take her to Himself; He never gives her to what took her." (new, optional)
- "The hands that pull a child from the fire are part of what He made real, so His permission never excuses ours." (v5)
- "A yes said to everything is said to no one." (v5)
