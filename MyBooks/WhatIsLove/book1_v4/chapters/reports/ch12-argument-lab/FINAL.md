# The Moral Argument — Final (Argument Lab, 2026-10-19)

*Tested over seven rounds against eleven non-theist schools (realist, debunker, Aristotelian, Kantian, contractualist, utilitarian, expressivist, Nietzschean, Buddhist, Confucian, and the problem-of-evil atheist) and a doctrinal censor. Final round: 11 of 11 "As written: NONE"; censor No Errors. Full history in EVOLUTION.md. Citations marked in the round reports as "verify" must be checked before this enters the chapter.*

**Form:** a converging case (CCC 31). Things we take to be true (A), witnesses that point beyond them (W), and an honest comparison of where each account stops (C). Reason's witness: the Church teaches that arguments of this kind can bring a person to certainty (CCC 31, 36), and it can predispose one to faith (CCC 35), but it does not replace faith.

**A. What we take to be true.** These are offered as truths, not as feelings people happen to share. What still seems true to us after honest reflection, including honest reflection on how we came to believe it, we have reason to trust until something more certain shows it false, or shows that what produced the belief had nothing to do with its truth. (W2 and the conclusion take up the claim that evolution has shown this.) None is beyond denial: Mackie denies that any of them is objectively true; Nietzsche denies A1 through A4, the second half of A6, and that A7 binds everyone; Singer denies parts of A2, A4, A6 and A7; and many who hold all the rest deny the Church's teaching that the line against killing the innocent holds at every number and against a man's own request or consent, which A6 names as this book's claims. This is not a vote. The comparison (C) shows what each denial costs; the reader can ask whether he can pay it.
- **A1. Some things are really wrong, for everyone.** Torturing a child for fun, betraying a friend for gain, enslaving the weak. Not merely disliked: wrong even if every culture approved.
- **A2. Doing them leaves us guilty: not merely mistaken, and not merely flawed.** A wrong sum needs correcting; a flawed character needs reforming; a wrong done to a person needs forgiving. Making the world worse and wronging someone are not the same. If I promise my dying friend to keep a secret she trusted to me, and after her death tell it for a laugh to a stranger in a far city who never knew her and will never repeat it, nothing in anyone's experience is worse, and still she was wronged. Had the same stranger found it in a letter blown into the street, her wish that it stay hidden would be just as frustrated, and no one would have wronged her. Guilt faces a person: I did this to *her*. Shame, left to itself, says I am less; guilt says I wronged her; only guilt asks her pardon. Traditions differ on what heals guilt, but almost none treats it as a mere mistake.
- **A3. No one on earth can make the gravest wrongs right.** A friend can release a promise as a gift. But no one could consent his own enslavement, or a child's torture, into being right, beforehand or after. The one we wronged can forgive us; that heals the bond, but it does not make the wrong not wrong. No one else on earth can give that pardon in her place: not her friends, not a nation by vote, not the whole human race. And when she is gone, the wrong does not go with her: we still owe her something we can no longer fully pay her.
- **A4. Every human being has the same dignity, whatever he can or cannot do.** The newborn, the dying, the severely disabled, the enemy: equal, prior to anyone's recognition, never to be used as a mere means, not for the many and not for the few; never, if innocent, to be deliberately killed for others' sake, nor "for his own good" without his asking; and not lost when life grows hard, nor even by the worst of crimes (*Dignitas Infinita* 1, 15; CCC 2261, 2267). (The Church teaches that those lines hold however vast the numbers, and that a man's own asking can never make it right to kill him: CCC 2277; *Evangelium Vitae* 65–66. A6 names both as this book's claims, not shared ones.) Equal worth is not equal duty: we owe more to our own parents and children than to strangers, but no one is worth less. Nor is it equal holiness: the saints are higher than I am in how well they love, never in what they are worth. Our instincts favor the healthy, the beautiful and our own; where instinct fails, for the infant no one is drawn to hold, we learn whether what we meant was worth or only attraction. Reason can see this (DI 1), yet history shows how easily it is lost (CCC 37–38): the ancient world that taught that slaves are men also left weak and deformed infants to die, and Christians too were slow to see what slavery denied.
- **A5. What we are does not by itself settle what we ought to do.** We are capable of truth and lies, justice and injustice, mercy and cruelty, giving and grasping, and we carry good desires and disordered ones. Listing what we can do, or what we want, never by itself tells us which to choose. On reflection we find certain goods good and to be pursued, and find that good is to be done and evil avoided. Aquinas, who called this the first light of practical reason, names them: life; the union of man and woman and the raising of children; knowing the truth, the truth about God included; and living in society with others (ST I-II q.94 a.2). Whether that means we are *for* something, and whether anyone made us for it, is what W must show.
- **A6. Real good for another always asks something of the giver** (time, attention, comfort, sometimes far more). That is how reality works for creatures like us. And some things must never be done: not to save ourselves, and not to buy a better outcome for many. A surgeon may not kill one healthy patient to give his organs to five, even if no one would ever know; a city may not secretly hang an innocent man to calm a riot; no prisoner may be experimented on to death, though many would be saved. Almost everyone sees these at once. Fewer hold the line when the numbers grow vast, and many who would never hang the innocent man would bomb a city to end a war. Fewer still hold that a man's own request or consent could never make it right to kill him: if he may lay down his life for others, as a firefighter does (John 15:13), why may he not ask for death? The Church holds the line at every number (*Veritatis Splendor* 52, 80; CCC 2314), and even against a man's own request or consent (CCC 2277, 2280–2282, 2295; *Evangelium Vitae* 65–66; *Samaritanus Bonus* III): a man may lay down his life for another, or for the truth, and accept the death that comes, even at the hands of those who wrongly kill him: so Christ, who laid down His life of His own accord (John 10:18; CCC 609), and so St. Maximilian Kolbe, who offered himself in a condemned man's place; but a man may not aim at his own death, as an end or as a means, by his own hand or another's, and his asking never makes it right for anyone to kill him. Those are this book's claims, argued in W1(2), not shared ones: many who hold the rest of A would let a dying man ask for death, or a volunteer give his life to an experiment. Yet most who would cross the line for a city will not cross it for five. The best accounts that count only totals can say why it is good that we hold the line firmly, and why a rule against crossing it does more good than a rule that allows it; they cannot say why crossing it would be a wrong done to *him*, the one spent, and not only the breaking of a useful rule. Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent. The absolute can cost lives, even many. Without it, no innocent is safe from being spent. One may never do evil so that good may come of it (CCC 1789).
- **A7. We are bound to the truth.** We may not believe against the evidence because a falsehood would comfort us, even if no one would ever know; and we may not stop looking because we fear what we will find. "Follow the evidence" is not advice for those who happen to want truth. It is something every reasoning mind ought to do (CCC 2104). It binds believer and unbeliever alike.

**W. Witnesses**
- **W1. Wronging points to Someone.** The Catholic philosopher Elizabeth Anscombe saw that our words "obligation," "duty" and "morally wrong" came to us from faith in a divine law, and she asked whether they keep their sense without it. She did not answer that question with God; she proposed setting the words aside. The best accounts without God keep them, and keep a great deal: justice is owed to another, a wrong must be set right, and no vote can make cruelty good. On one point every account that keeps these words agrees: the wrong is wrong of necessity, not up to anyone. We see it first in what it does to the child, and those who have never heard of a revealed law can see it (Romans 2:14–15): it is not wrong merely because it is forbidden. Not even God makes cruelty right; He cannot, because He is the Good (Chapter 3). And the Church adds that, because His love made her precious, every such wrong is also, and before all else, an offense against Him (CCC 1440, 1850): not a second injury beside hers, for nothing harms God (Job 35:6–8), but His love refused in her, and so a turning away from Him. He is offended by us, St. Thomas says, only because we act against our own good, the good He wills for us (*Summa contra Gentiles* III.122). So the fact that no one can release us does not decide between the accounts. Nor does the fact that guilt is owed. An order of cause and fruit can say the deed's fruit will come, and a necessary truth can say the deed was wrong; neither is anyone the guilt could be owed to. But the nearest rivals, the realist, the contractualist, the Aristotelian and the Buddhist among them, answer that guilt is owed to the one we wronged, and this book agrees: it is owed to her, truly and in her own right, and not to God alone (A2). We agree she is owed. We part at the grave, and over what gives her the claim at all. Two witnesses take up those two questions. (Not every *ought* wrongs someone when it is broken, A7; these two rest on the wrongs that do.)
  (1) *The debt still owed.* A wrong done to a person needs forgiving, and the pardon that heals the bond between us is hers to give. When she has died, no one on earth can give it in her place, and still we owe her (A3). There are three ways to take that. Some deny it: the debt died with her, and what is left is remorse, a wound that will never be healed by the one it was done to. Or the debt outlives her, owed to her as she was, and the living repay what they can: they keep her memory, honor her wishes, care for those she loved. That is the road of many philosophers without God, who hold that the dead can be wronged as they were and that the wrong stays fixed forever; and it is the road of Xunzi, who taught that the cultivated man keeps the rites for the dead as the human way of completing a bond, without holding that the dead hear. Confucius himself sacrificed to them as though they were present, and would say no more. Or she is still someone, and the debt can one day be received by her. The second road is noble, and the Church walks much of it. But on that road, what the living pay, she is not there to take up or answer; what can never be repaid, no one ever releases; and many of the dead have no one left to remember them by name. The ancient critic Mozi said that to deny the spirits and still study the rites is like learning the ceremonies for guests when there are no guests. Guilt does not only mourn her. It asks to be forgiven by her, and only someone can forgive. Science can show why minds like ours would speak to their dead whether or not anyone hears, so the speaking alone settles nothing; it cannot show that no one does. Most who have kept the ancestral rites, in China and among many peoples, walk a form of the third road too: the named dead come to the offering, and are told, thanked and asked. But on that road the dead are kept by the living; each is fed as long as her own line remembers her, and those whom no one remembers become the wandering dead, whom a whole people feeds without a name. What keeps her there is the memory of her kin, and memory fails. The Buddhist walks another form of the third road without a Keeper: the dead are born again, those born among the departed spirits can receive what the living dedicate to them, and in some lands the living ask the dead person's pardon at the funeral. But on his own account the one born again is neither the same as she was nor another, will not, as a rule, know what was done, and is not, at the ground of things, a self at all; and on his path what finally frees the doer is his own purification, whether or not she ever pardons him.
  The Catholic answer begins with God. Every wrong against a person is also, and before all else, an offense against the One whose love made her precious (CCC 1440, 1850), and only God forgives sins (CCC 1441). He does so through His Church, ordinarily in the Sacrament of Reconciliation (CCC 1442, 1484), and His forgiveness is whole. Yet it does not cancel what we owe her: absolution "does not remedy all the disorders sin has caused" (CCC 1459), so we must still repair what we can, and God does not take her place in giving the pardon that is hers to give. Nor has she ceased to be. The soul does not perish at death (CCC 366); she lives to God (Luke 20:38), who forgets no one (Isaiah 49:15); and on the last day He will raise her body too (CCC 998). Death does not cut the bond: the living can still reach the dead in love, with prayer and even with a request for pardon, and it is never in vain (*Spe Salvi* 48; CCC 958). And no one dies with no one left to speak for her: at every Mass the Church prays for the dead, including, in one of her prayers, those whose faith God alone has known. He did not keep her from what was done to her; that weight is on the other side of the scale, and this book weighs it there. What He keeps is her claim, and her, so that what was done to her is not the last word over her. How the last reconciliation between a victim and the one who wronged her is completed, the Church does not spell out. She says that grace "does not make wrong into right," and that the wrongdoer does not sit at the eternal banquet beside his victim as though nothing had happened (*Spe Salvi* 44); and Benedict XVI, taking up the view of some recent theologians, proposes that the fire which both burns and saves is Christ Himself: before His gaze "all falsehood melts away," and His touch heals whoever is open to Him, the guilty too (*Spe Salvi* 47). Reason cannot prove that hope. It can see that guilt asks for what it could have only if she is still someone, herself, and that this hope promises it: to be forgiven by her. Like the longing in W3, this is a clue, and it weighs as much as the reader finds the third road, on which she is kept as herself, truer than the second to what he owes the one he wronged. A truth can be true; only a person can forgive.
  (2) *A worth no deed confers and no order wants.* The victim's claim is truly her own. She did not make it, cannot sign it away (A3), and it belongs as fully to the newborn who has done nothing to earn it as to the man who has done much. Being human explains why the newborn belongs. The best accounts without God also explain much of why she may not be killed for others: the wrong would be done to *her*; each person must be answered one by one, and no total answers her; the five who need her organs have no claim on her life; and doing harm is not the same as failing to help, which is Philippa Foot's own answer to the magistrate and the mob. Asked why justice has that shape, some answer that this is simply what justice is; others, that a person has the standing of one who may not be violated. Many let the line bend when the numbers grow vast (A6). What they say is true as far as it goes, and each is a stopping point; and few say why the line is not even hers to waive (Kant did, grounding it in the humanity in her own person, which must stretch to reach the newborn, and, so that she cannot release herself, regarding her in two ways, her free reason binding her as a natural being). That she can suffer explains why harming her matters; it does not explain this either, for others can always suffer more. An order of deeds and their fruits returns to each what each has done; it was never meant to say why anyone matters, and the Buddhist, who grounds regard in suffering, does not ask it to. A law or an order gives a nature to every member of a kind; it does not want any one of them. If her worth lies in what she is, then it is a gift exactly as far as her being is a gift, and here the moral question and the question of creation meet. Our love answers to a worth already there; God's love gives it, by giving her being (ST I q.20 a.2). Her worth rests on what she is, a person made in God's image, and on the call He gives with her being, to communion with Him, which the Church calls the root reason for human dignity (GS 19; CCC 1700). It is not added later, and this dignity, the dignity of what she is, cannot be lost, not even by her own refusal, though the dignity of a good life can be (DI 7, 15, 22). The human person is the only creature on earth God willed for itself (GS 24): not for the species, not for the total, not for us (so too Aquinas, *Summa contra Gentiles* III.112). So her life is not ours to spend, not even for many, and, as the Church holds, not hers to dispose of, even when it has grown hard: it is held in trust from the One who alone is Lord of life and remains its sovereign Master (CCC 2258, 2280), who willed her for her own sake, gave her life to her keeping and not to her disposal, and never spends her as a mere means against her own good (GS 24). She may refuse treatment that is burdensome or out of proportion to what it can do, and be given relief from pain even at the risk of shortening her days; but the ordinary care owed to the sick may not be withdrawn, and food and water are part of it for as long as they nourish her without causing harm or intolerable suffering (CCC 2278–2279; *Samaritanus Bonus* V.3); what the trust forbids is that her death be the thing aimed at. That is why, on this account, the line has no exceptions for any of us: what protects her is not a weight that enough lives could outweigh; it is that no number of others has any right to take her life. No one may take what is not his to take, even to keep others from taking more. He who gives life, and can give it back, may call it home; we, who can do neither, may not take it. That right is never ours, and no one, whatever voice he says he heard in His name, may claim it for himself (CCC 2258). A reader who holds the line only at five still faces the question why justice has that shape; a reader who holds it at every number has here a reason for it. All share one origin and one calling (GS 29); an equal call, which does not wait on what anyone can do, explains an equal worth, and measured against one end that is God Himself, the genius and the newborn are for the same thing. Not every child is wanted by those who made her; every child was wanted by the One who did, before anyone saw her (Jeremiah 1:5).
  This Lawgiver is not a commander who picks rules. A will that invents values can command, but it cannot bind. The wrong is wrong because of what it does to her, and so it offends Him who loves her. That there is a *her* at all, made for an end, is His gift; and what we are, as He made us, is good, our desires included. Their disorder, their pull against reason and against the good of others, is not His work but the wound of sin: the loss of a harmony that was His gift (CCC 405; ST I q.95 a.1). He did not choose whether cruelty is evil, for goodness is His nature. He chose to make creatures who can be wronged, established what they are for in the very natures He created, and defined what love must answer to, because He is Himself the Good He asks of us. Our share in that law is reason's own light, not a foreign rule (*Veritatis Splendor* 41). The law binds because He is good; the offense against Him can be forgiven because He is Someone; and the one we wronged is kept, because He forgets no one. And the Judge lets Himself be asked (Genesis 18:25).
- **W2. The law and the mind that reads it point to one Source.** Begin with truth (A7). Evolution explains well why our senses and reasoning mostly get the world right: a creature that misreads cliffs and lions does not last. (The Church has no quarrel with that, so long as it is not taken to explain the mind itself: the spiritual soul is not produced by matter but created by God for each one of us, CCC 366; the argument here does not assume it.) It does not explain why we *ought* to believe what is true when a falsehood would serve us better; whoever denies that anything binds must say that no one ought to care whether a belief is true, including his own. Then ask about our nature. Both the Church and her best rivals agree that what is good for a creature depends on what it is, and that much of the natural law is fitted to our kind. So the question is not whether our nature could have been otherwise, but whether it is an accident or a wisdom. Darwin imagined rational creatures raised like hive bees who would think it a sacred duty to kill their brothers; if our nature is only an accident, nothing in it had to aim at the good at all. Yet the core of the law is not fitted to our kind only: gratuitous cruelty, betrayal, treating a person as a thing would be wrong for any creature that can reason and suffer. If some of the law holds for any rational mind whatever, there are eternal truths about what is good and what we owe, and minds can know them. The Church places such truths in God's own wisdom, where every possible nature is first known (ST I q.15; q.16 a.7; I-II q.91 a.1–2; *Veritatis Splendor* 43). The best rivals give two answers. One says the order is a fact that needs no mind, and that evolution and the truth meet without luck: the features that make a deed wrong (pain, trust, betrayal) are the very ones survival taught us to notice, and the same reasoning that serves us everywhere does the rest. The other says mind and law belong to one nature, so that a rational animal simply reads its own form. Both are right as far as they go. What neither explains is the reach. Survival can teach us that pain is bad for us; it does not explain how we can see that cruelty would be wrong for any mind in any world. The debunker answers that survival would give us the sense of seeing it, true or not. But survival no more selected for seeing what holds in every possible world when we read a proof, and he does not distrust the proof. The same question hangs over both, and one Source of law and mind answers both. This is a question of fit, and the rivals contest it; it weighs, it does not settle. Our conscience fails where self-interest pulls (the slave, the stranger, the infant who cannot repay), which is what the Church expects, because disordered self-love is the root of sin (ST I-II q.77 a.4; CCC 1850, 405). Yet conscience also corrects itself, against interest and toward those who can never repay, and the engine of that correction is reason holding itself to consistency with goods it already sees: the law of truth again. And this law holds together: its demands can be very hard (Chapter 11), but one can always refuse to do evil (*Veritatis Splendor* 52, 102); that much follows from the form of its prohibitions. What does not follow from form is that its goods fit: truth needs mercy to be told well, justice needs mercy to stay just, and mercy needs truth to be mercy (Chapters 5–9). Christians hold the rest on God's promise (1 Corinthians 10:13). The law of truth belongs to that unity too: if truth and life have one Source (John 14:6), the will to truth cannot finally lead away from life. Unity without reduction: a law and a mind fitted to each other, with truth binding both, are best explained by one Source of both. (This speaks most strongly to those who say conscience is only what survival built, and so binds no one; to those who say moral facts simply exist, it puts the question of reach. Those who say our moral words voice refined feeling need no meeting of law and mind; for them the question is the one in C.) W1 and W3 show why that Source is Someone and not something, and not less than personal: knowing and loving are in Him more truly than in us.
- **W3. Communion is a clue that points to One who is Love.** Only a person can love us back; a law, a force, an order or an idea cannot. We long to be known and loved wholly, and no one on earth can do it, because no one knows the whole of us, not even ourselves; even if we are made of our relationships, the longing remains, to be received whole, across every role and every year, by someone who will not die. Science can trace the paths this longing travels in us, as it traces the paths of sight: attachment, the wish not to die, a mind that pictures being seen. That tells us how we long, not whether there is Someone to long for, and the longing alone settles nothing. Adam Smith observed that when the world misjudges us and the judge within us is shaken, we appeal to the all-seeing Judge of the world (*Theory of Moral Sentiments* III.2.33); a longing to be judged truly could be explained that way. Smith's account does not as easily explain a longing to be told, before we have earned anything, that it is good that we exist. Part of that longing grasps, and both the Buddhist and the Catholic say grasping love must die; the Buddhist keeps a wholesome desire for the path until it reaches its end, and the Catholic adds that desire itself, eros too, is not to be killed but healed (*Deus Caritas Est* 5). We part over what is left. The Buddhist says what is left is boundless kindness, compassion, joy in another's good, and an even mind that holds every being alike, without clinging. The Catholic says all of that is real, and that beneath it is love's first word to another, in Josef Pieper's phrase: "it is good that you exist." Love does not say that for a while only. Some wise friends of nature answer that a human life must end to be human, and that to want more is to want to stop being human; but the Christian hope is not more of the same years. It is that the one we love will go on being herself, body and all, made whole (CCC 1025, 999). The risen Christ kept His wounds (John 20:27): what a finite love lived through is not erased but kept, and healed. A longing does not prove its answer, but it shows what answer would fit: a Person who said that word to each of us first, who knows us wholly, and who loved us first (1 John 4:19). That knowing is promised in full only at the end, when we shall understand fully, even as we have been fully understood (1 Corinthians 13:12); for now it is begun. If the highest good knows and loves no one, then love, the best thing in us, is answered in the end by no one. That is not evidence, for we may not believe a thing because it comforts (A7); but it is where that account stops.
  This witness raises the hardest question: if such a Person loves us, why do many honest seekers not find Him? Some of the most disciplined seekers in history, the Buddhist contemplatives, looked inward for a lifetime and report a peace beyond the self rather than a Person; and the Neo-Confucians, who for centuries sat in stillness and searched things and their own hearts, found a principle rather than a Person. The Church rejects nothing that is true and holy in their way, even as she proclaims Christ, in whom all may find the fullness of religious life (*Nostra Aetate* 2). Her own mystics speak of a darkness on the way to God too, though theirs is the night of faith before Someone. Each tradition can read the other's summit as a stage on its own road; neither reading is evidence, and this book does not count it as such. The Church does not presume that such seekers are hiding from Him (CCC 847), though she knows that any of us can (CCC 29). She teaches that God can be known with certainty by the light of reason (CCC 36), yet that knowing Him by reason alone is hard for us as we are (CCC 37). He makes Himself known through the world He made and the conscience He gave (CCC 32–33), fully in Christ (*Dei Verbum* 4), and, for most of us first, through persons: a mother's love before a creed, a neighbor's mercy before a name (Matthew 25:37–40). He has entrusted us with making Him known to one another (CCC 848), as He gives His creatures the dignity of being causes for one another in everything else (CCC 306). And He does not withhold what is needed for salvation from anyone who, through no fault of his own, has not yet come to an explicit knowledge of Him and, by grace, tries to live a good life (*Lumen Gentium* 16). She hopes that such a person is already following a light without yet knowing its name, and that when he turns from a wrong out of love for the good, he is already turning toward the One who forgives. A child is loved long before he can say who loves him. That is a real answer to why many do not find Him, though not a full answer to why Love would choose this way, and it has a real cost: for many honest seekers the conscious knowing comes late, and for many it does not come in this life at all. Yet this life is where the choice is made (CCC 1021), and it can be made before the name is known: the Spirit, in a manner known only to God, offers every person the possibility of sharing in Christ's Paschal Mystery (GS 22), and God can lead him, in ways known to Himself, to the faith without which it is impossible to please Him (CCC 848). For the infant no one holds, there is no mother's love to carry His love to her; yet she is held by Him, and if she dies unbaptized, the Church, which holds that God is not bound by His sacraments, entrusts her to His mercy and hopes that there is a way of salvation for her (CCC 1257, 1261). The Church does not hide that cost; she calls it a summons to mission. Revelation gives the Lover's name: God is Love (1 John 4:8).

**C. An honest comparison.** Every account stops somewhere, God's included. Each rival below *accommodates* much of A, often in its own words; the question is what each *explains* and what it must give up. Each is stated as its best defenders state it. No rival's origins are used against it that we would not let it use against ours.
- **Nothing really binds** (error theory, often joined to an evolutionary account of conscience and of religion) is consistent and borrows nothing. With one cause it explains why we feel bound, guilty and watched. Its price: torturing a child for fun is not really wrong (A1); the newborn has no worth except what we are built and taught to give her (A4); and even "follow the evidence" becomes advice for those who happen to want truth (A7), so it cannot say why anyone ought to care whether its own case is true.
- **Feeling, refined** (Hume, Adam Smith, and today's quasi-realists: moral words voice our attitudes, made informed, consistent and sympathetic) can say nearly everything in A in its own voice, even "her worth does not depend on us" and "my guilt is owed to her." Where it parts from us is in *why* that is true. On its account, in the end, "worth" is the name our refined love gives to what it answers to; and it holds that once we have said what cruelty does to the child, no further why is needed. Some of its heirs say worth and feeling arise together, like color and sight; then worth is real, but it is worth to a human heart, and when refined hearts part, it can say which one sees truly only from within the heart we share. Two refined hearts can still part over whether the newborn who will never think is worth as much as you, and then equal worth wins only by being ours. Its founders were honest about the cost. Hume granted that creatures too weak to make us feel their resentment would be owed gentleness under the laws of humanity, but not justice, which for him meant the rights of those who can hold us to account. He told the "sensible knave" who cheats when it pays that he is a dupe, trading peace of mind for gain; but he admitted that if the knave's heart does not rebel, no answer will satisfy him. No argument compels such a man, ours included; the difference is what he is missing. On Hume's account he has lost a feeling and the peace it brings, and if he never misses it, he will never know what he lost; on ours he is turning from a good that is there whether he knows it or not, and from Someone. Smith pictured conscience as an impartial judge above everyone we know, and wrote that when that judge within is shaken, we appeal to a higher tribunal, the all-seeing Judge of the world (*Theory of Moral Sentiments* III.2.33). On the account that keeps only feeling, both judges are no one. Remorse can be lived through; only forgiveness can heal the bond, and at the grave, on this account, no one is left to give it.
- **Reason's own law** (Kant) explains universality, and its "never merely as a means" stands with A4 and A6. Kant held that no one, not even God, is the author of the moral law. If reason made it, reason could unmake it. If it is the very form of a free will, as Kant's best readers say, it still holds for every possible reasoner before any exists: no reasoner set it, and each finds himself under it. Then the question of W2 returns: where does a law for every possible mind hold, and how did minds that live by it arise in a world of nature? Kant answered that how freedom is possible we cannot understand. Dignity grounded in the capacity to reason must stretch to reach the newborn and the dying. And reason can condemn us but cannot forgive us. Kant himself held that a people cannot be its own lawgiver in matters of the heart, which no human court can see, so that an ethical community can be thought of only as a people under a moral Ruler who knows the heart (*Religion* 6:98–99). He did not make that Ruler the author of the law, and neither does this book (Chapter 3). But where guilt and goodness are inward, the philosopher of autonomy found that he had to think of Someone, though he held that thought as rational faith, not as evidence; this book counts it as a clue. And his own rule for clemency, that a ruler may remit punishment only for crimes against himself and never for what one subject did to another, has the shape of half of W1(1): no ruler can give, in the victim's place, the pardon that is hers. God does what Kant's ruler may not, forgiving the sin whole (CCC 1441), yet not as impunity: the one forgiven must still repair what he can and make satisfaction for his sins (CCC 1459), and God does not take her place.
- **What we owe each other** (contractualism: a wrong is what could not be justified to the one it is done to) explains why a wrong is done to someone, why we answer to the one we wronged, and why no total can justify spending one person for others, since each must be answered one by one. It reaches the abandoned child as surely as the wanted one, for it asks what could be justified to *her*, not whether anyone chooses to speak for her. But it stops at two things. Why being born one of us puts her among those to whom we must answer, it cannot explain. Why answering to each one is what right and wrong are, it explains by the worth of standing with each person in mutual recognition, and that worth it takes as given. Its best defenders hold these as truths that simply hold, and there it meets the realist's question (W2). And when the one we wronged is gone, it can say she is still owed, but not that she can ever forgive.
- **Well-being, counted impartially** (utilitarianism) counts every pain equally, refuses to let the stranger count for less, and is right that consequences matter; the Church weighs them too (CCC 1754). Its best defenders can say why we should hold firm rules against killing the innocent (rule consequentialism), and even live by them day to day as if they had no exceptions (Hare's two levels). But it reduces wronging to worsening (A2: the betrayed secret, and the letter blown into the street that frustrates her wish just as much and wrongs no one); it makes every person, in principle, someone who may be deliberately killed for better totals (A4, A6); and some of its masters have allowed that its truth might be better kept from the public (Sidgwick; de Lazari-Radek and Singer defend that secrecy still), so that the many would live by what their teachers think false (A7). Sidgwick himself gave up his fellowship rather than profess what he could not believe; his life kept A7 better than his theory could.
- **The flourishing of our kind** (secular Aristotelian) sees the most: what we need, why virtue is good for us, why justice is owed to another and a wrong must be repaid, why the weak still belong, and why one may not be killed for others. Its modern heirs have much to say about forgiveness between persons. But it must say whether our nature, and the good it carries, is an accident or an order it cannot explain; its best defenders answer that the question asks for a place to stand outside the life of reason, and there is none: for a rational animal, seeing what is good is part of being what it is. That is a stopping point. When the one we wronged is gone, it has no one left to receive what we owe. And some of its best defenders allow that a person's life may stop being a good to him and be ended, as though, the Church would say, a person owned his life rather than holding it in trust (CCC 2280; this book's claim, A6).
- **Necessary truths that belong to no one** (godless realism) stand with us against relativism and matter alone. Its best defenders gather those truths under a few deep principles, and say the truths need nothing behind them: their necessity lives in the natures of pain, persons and promises themselves, or, as Parfit held, needs no dwelling at all, as the truths of logic need none. Every account has a first principle, ours included. Where we part is where persons are: the debt to the dead, which on this account can never be answered (W1); why worth follows kind and not ability, which it must take as given (W1); and whether the reach of conscience to truths that hold for every mind can be explained without a Mind (W2). It says the law holds of itself. We say it holds first in a Mind, the same Mind that made the natures it is about and the minds that read it.
- **An impersonal sacred order** (Heaven's principle as the Neo-Confucians, above all Zhu Xi, understood it; the Stoic Logos) is among the strongest rivals a book about love can face. It explains a great deal (A1, A3, A5, A6, W2). It holds that every person, however clouded in body or mind, carries the whole of Heaven's principle, as the one moon shines whole in ten thousand rivers, so that worth follows nature, not capacity (A4). Its sages say the heart of Heaven and Earth is to give life. But it gives life to each thing and intends none: it keeps each kind true to itself and lets each one go. It can say that each one holds the whole nature, not that *this* one is wanted for her own sake; when one river is drained, the moon has lost nothing. Heaven and Earth give life to things; God wanted *you*. An order cannot be wronged or forgive (A2), and it cannot know or love anyone (W3). Its most systematic teacher, Zhu Xi, said that principle has no intention, and that Heaven both has a mind and has none; Confucius, in danger, in grief, and when no one understood him, spoke of Heaven as the one who gave him his virtue and would not let the culture he carried perish, who bereaved him when his best student died, and who alone knew him (*Analects* 7.23, 9.5, 11.9, 14.35). Minds under threat reach for a knowing Heaven, ours as well; this shows where the account strains for its own teachers, not that it fails. The modern Confucian Mou Zongsan would refuse the word "impersonal": for him the ground is a living moral heart, the same in all of us, that is itself the infinite. Then the law is mine at my deepest, and others love me back; but at the ground of things there is no one besides me who knows me as myself, and no one at all who can release what I did to another or keep the one I lost. Such an order is free of the problem of evil only if it intends no one in particular. Zhu Xi's answer, that principle is wholly good and the unevenness lies in a material force it does not steer, is close to the Church's own (CCC 310); but a principle that does not steer can promise the seeker his own completion, not that the one he lost will be kept. The more such an order cares, the more it faces our question, as the historian Sima Qian did when he set the good men who starved beside the robber who lived long and asked whether the Way of Heaven is just. (Xunzi's Heaven, the bare constant course of nature, is free of that question because it cares for no one; but for him the moral Way was made by the sages, and he belongs nearer the flourishing of our kind. The Stoic Logos sits nearer a Person: it was prayed to as Providence, and the Stoics had to answer for evil; but their Providence cares for the whole and spends the person, as a part, for it, and does not keep the dead for good.)
- **The Buddha's Dhamma** (karma, and beyond it the unconditioned; for the Madhyamaka, emptiness) is the most searching account of the moral life without a Creator. It explains why intention matters most, why real good costs (A6), and why we must follow the truth (A7). It holds with A2 that guilt is owed to the one we wronged: the Buddha sent a monk who had insulted a layman to ask his pardon (Vinaya, Cullavagga I). And its ground for compassion is among the most beautiful anyone has offered: as a mother would guard her only child with her life, so one should hold every being in a boundless heart (Sn 1.8). It also bears the weight of suffering more easily than we do, because it never said that being born is good. Birth itself, the Buddha taught, is suffering, as is all that is conditioned, and a human birth is prized as rare because it is the chance to be free of birth; craving keeps the round turning; and it can end. Karma explains some of how suffering falls, though the Buddha would not say that all of it comes from past deeds (SN 36.21). No one judges, the Buddhist says; the fruit simply ripens. But so far as bodies and fortunes follow the moral quality of each heart, across lives, the world has justice built into it, and justice built into a world looks like the work of a mind (by the same measure, a world in which pain does not follow justice weighs against a just Mind; so far as one life lets us see, ours is such a world, and this book weighs that in the section on evil below). When the one we wronged is gone, the Buddhist says she is born again; but the one born again will not, as a rule, know what was done, and on his own account is neither the same as she was nor another (W1). What remains is a ripening, and a ripening is owed to no one and cannot forgive. No one, the Buddha taught, can purify another (Dhammapada 165); the Catholic says that One can, and does (1 John 1:7). Its highest good is an insight each must reach, which some will never reach in this life (AN 8.29); where Buddhists look for higher help, as in the Pure Land, it comes from a buddha who, in the sutra's telling, was once bound as we are (the Council itself speaks of Buddhists who seek the highest light "by their own efforts or through higher help," *Nostra Aetate* 2). The Catholic's highest good is also a seeing, but a seeing given, which God can give to the infant and to the one who will never think (CCC 1250, 1261). Some of its teachers say every being has the nature of a buddha, so that worth follows nature, not capacity; but a nature held whole by all, like the moon in ten thousand rivers, is not a call to this one. And where its buddhas know and love, they love beings already there; they gave no one being. For the Buddhist, compassion is the seed and the fruit of the path; for the Catholic, love is also the root of the world. Here the two part honestly: both say grasping must die, and they part over what is left, and over whether it is good that we exist at all.
- **Power and rank** (Nietzsche) is the most honest rival, and it explains a great deal: why guilt grew so large, how moral feeling has changed across history, and why equal worth spread where Christianity spread (the Church agrees that equal worth was slow to be seen and spread with the Gospel, CCC 37–38). It stays consistent by giving up A1 through A4, and the second half of A6, openly, and asks the hardest question of all: why should truth matter more than life? Nietzsche kept his own honesty, which he called the virtue his kind could not get away from, but he denied that it binds every mind. So the case puts a choice. Either truthfulness binds every reasoning mind, and something binds beyond power (A7); or it binds only those who hold it, and then his unmasking can turn our own honesty against us, as he meant it to, but it cannot show that anyone ought to keep that honesty, his own included. He hoped that truthfulness would at last turn on Christianity itself; whether honest inquiry ends there or at the Lawgiver is the question of the whole case, which this dilemma does not settle. His own account gives the will to truth the same Christian ancestry he gives equal worth, so neither ancestry settles anything. To his hardest question this book answers that truth and life have one Source, so that honesty may cost a life but can never finally be the enemy of life; on his account it might be, which is why he said we need art to bear the truth.
- **A God who is Love** is also a stopping point, but not, the Church holds, a brute one: He is the One whose very being is to be (Exodus 3:14; ST I q.3 a.4), who needs nothing outside Himself to explain Him. The realist says the same of his necessary truths, and the difference is not fewer first principles but one source for three things he must take separately: the law, the beings it is about, and the minds that read it. Against the flourishing view and the sacred order, which also stop at one, it is the one that knows and loves us, and is Someone besides ourselves. Against the Buddhist, for whom love is not the ground of things, it is the account in which love is the first word and the last (1 John 4:8; 1 Corinthians 13:8). Its claim is reach, not count: it explains A1–A7 together, including what the best rivals leave out or must take as given: forgiveness when no one on earth is left to give it; a ground for the rule that one person may not be spent for others' totals, which many of the best rivals share and rest on a standing they take as given; and, as the Church teaches and this book argues, though not every reader grants it, why that line has no exceptions and is not even her own to cross; and a longing no one on earth can answer. It does not remove all mystery; it is one mystery, and a mystery that explains. And it is Someone, not less than personal, which is what guilt's request to be forgiven, forgiveness itself, and communion suggest.

**On the other side of the scale is real weight, and it is heavy.** Children suffer horribly. Animals suffered for hundreds of millions of years before any human chose anything, and pain in nature falls roughly where survival would put it, not where justice would. In the world God made, one creature's good often comes through another's loss: the wolf lives by the fawn, and the parasite lives by the child; if our nature is a wisdom and not an accident (W2), so is theirs, and that too is on this side of the scale. Honest seekers look for God and do not find Him. And the Catholic faith adds a weight of its own. It teaches that hell is real and eternal (CCC 1035): a person can refuse God forever by his own free choice (CCC 1033). God predestines no one to that refusal (CCC 1037) and desires all to be saved (1 Timothy 2:4), and He holds no one to account beyond what he knew, or could and should have known, and freely chose: fear, wounds and what others did to him can lessen or even remove his responsibility (CCC 1735, 1859–1860). The Church sees in this the seriousness of freedom: God does not force His love on anyone able to refuse it (Chapter 4), and a refusal made with full knowledge and consent and held to the end becomes final, as the angels' choice became final, through no defect in His mercy (CCC 393, 1033, 1857). That death fixes the will is the Church's teaching (CCC 393, 1021), not something this case shows, and so hell stays on this side of the scale. And two rivals bear this weight more easily than we do. A world with no one behind it would put pain where survival puts it, which is where we find it. The Buddhist never said that being born is good; he prizes a human birth as rare, but as the chance to be free of birth, so a world full of suffering is what he expects. We say that it is good that she exists, and so we must answer for the child. Free will (Chapter 1) answers much of the evil we do to one another: it is ours, not His. Why He did not keep the first man from sinning, as by grace He kept Mary free of every sin her whole life long (CCC 491–493), the Church answers, with St. Leo the Great, only with what He brought from it, and gave to the very ones who fell: Christ's grace gave us more than the devil's envy took away (CCC 412). Free will also bears on why God did not simply begin with heaven, where the saints are free and cannot sin. He can give the highest good as pure gift (CCC 1250), and the Church teaches that He gives it to the baptized child who dies before she can choose (Benedict XII, *Benedictus Deus*; CCC 1023). But He also willed creatures who can choose to give their love back of their own accord, in a life where it could be refused, before it is confirmed forever (Chapter 1; Chapter 4). That is a good, not a necessity; whether it is worth this world's cost is part of the weighing, not settled by it. It does not answer the fawn in the forest fire or the child with cancer. For those the Church gives a setting, not an explanation: God made a world still on its way to its perfection, and gave His creatures the dignity of acting on their own and being causes for each other, so they can fail and harm (CCC 306, 310). He wills the order of the whole, never the loss for its own sake (ST I q.19 a.9), and within that order He governs each person for her own sake (*Summa contra Gentiles* III.112). Beyond that she claims no explanation; only Christian faith as a whole answers it (CCC 309). Faith gives the certainty that God would not permit an evil unless He brought a good from that very evil (CCC 324), though the evil never thereby becomes good (CCC 312); and we will know His ways fully only at the end (CCC 314).
Three things keep that weight from overturning the case, though they do not make it light. *First*, evil has nothing of its own: it is always a good thing harmed (Chapter 1). That does not make pain less real; it says what pain is the loss of. There can be a Supreme Good but not a supreme Evil (ST I q.49 a.3): every choice of evil reaches for a lesser good the chooser lacks, and a will that lacks nothing has nothing to grasp at. So the world's darkness is not evidence of a dark god. It does not by itself rule out a source that is simply indifferent, or a god beyond good and evil who makes and breaks out of sheer fullness. Those are answered only by the whole case: neither explains a debt that outlives every creditor on earth, nor a worth that does not follow ability, nor a longing to hear from Someone that it is good that *you* exist. A yes said to whatever happens is said to no one; it must say yes to what was done to her as well as to her, and so it cannot take her side. God says no to what was done to her, and yes to her (Wisdom 1:13; CCC 413). *Second*, God's permission is not a trade, and two answers must be kept apart here. *Why* He permits is, so far as reason can see, the setting above: a world of real causes, and we are among them; faith adds that He permits no evil from which He will not draw a good, though the evil never earns that good and never becomes good itself (CCC 311, 312, 324, 412). God is at work in every cause (CCC 308), but the evil in what a free creature does is his own: God gives the act its being, never its defect, and is in no way, directly or indirectly, the cause of moral evil; He permits it (CCC 311; ST I-II q.79 a.2; Chapter 11). The harms that come in a world of real causes He does not will for their own sake, but only as they come with the order He wills; in willing that order, Aquinas says, He wills some things to be naturally corrupted (ST I q.19 a.9; q.49 a.2). Among animals that order does give one creature's loss to another's life: the lion lives by the slaying of other animals (ST I q.22 a.2 ad 2). A person it never gives away (*Summa contra Gentiles* III.112). The parasite takes from the child; God does not give her to it. The hands that pull a child from the fire are part of what He made real, so His permission never excuses ours (CCC 2269). *What* He brings from the evil is a further thing: for a person who suffers, the good the Church hopes for is a good offered to her; God never spends anyone as a mere means against her own good. That is not to deny that suffering can bear fruit for others. Christ's did: He laid down His life of His own accord (John 10:18), as a ransom for many (Mark 10:45), and the Father raised Him; and whoever joins her suffering to His shares in that fruitfulness (Colossians 1:24; *Salvifici Doloris* 19, 24). But what Christ gave was given, not taken, and it was not lost. We know enough of goodness to know what a good God would value, which is why this weight is real; what we would not expect to see, if the good is finished only in eternal life and in a creation set free (Romans 8:21; CCC 314, 324, 1046–1047), is how each particular evil is answered. So for one weighing by reason alone, not seeing God's reasons is weak evidence that He has none. It is not no evidence: the pattern of pain in nature still weighs. The Church does not say whether the fawn will live again; she says creation itself waits to be set free (Romans 8:19–22). For the fawn that hope is thinner than it is for the child, and this book does not pretend otherwise. *Third*, the God weighed here is not a designer watching from a distance. He entered suffering Himself: the eternal Son became man and suffered in His own flesh on the Cross. God, St. Bernard said, cannot suffer, but He can suffer with (*Spe Salvi* 39). Christian faith professes that He will raise the dead (CCC 998), and hopes in Him to wipe away every tear (Revelation 21:4; *Salvifici Doloris* 15, 25). This is not a further proof; it is what the God being weighed actually is. The question is not whether a distant designer fits this world, but whether a God who suffers with it and will not leave it in the ground does. That does not explain why. It means that, if He is, the last word over the suffering child is not the suffering. Not every account without a Creator is without hope: the Buddhist hopes that every being, this child too, will be freed from suffering, and the Confucian hopes for a world in which the widow, the orphan and the disabled are all cared for. The Christian hope is more particular: that this child, herself, body and all, will be raised and made whole, and will know the One who never forgot her. That is not evidence, but it is where the accounts part.

**Conclusion.** Weighing both sides, the best explanation of love as we actually find it is a Lawgiver who is Love. Evil weighs against that conclusion, heavily; in this book's judgment it does not overturn it. An honest reader may weigh it differently, and should still weigh it; the Church does not presume that he is at fault, and leaves the judgment of every heart to God (CCC 847, 1861). The Church teaches that arguments of this kind can bring a person to certainty, and that God can be known with certainty from the things He has made by the natural light of reason (CCC 31, 36; First Vatican Council, *Dei Filius* ch. 2). This book offers the case for weighing, not because certainty is out of reach, but because each reader must reach it with his own reason, and many of us find the way hard (CCC 37). Certainty of this kind comes, as St. John Henry Newman saw, from many signs converging in one mind, not from any one of them compelling every mind. It can predispose to faith (CCC 35) but does not replace it, and it cannot compel the one who gives up A4. To him it says only: look at the newborn whom instinct passed by, the one the ancient world left on the hillside, and ask which you are surer of: that she, weak and not yet able to know herself, was wronged when she was left there, and wronged no less than a strong newborn left beside her, or a grown man, however gifted, put out there helpless to die; or any argument that says she was not, or that it was a lesser wrong, or that you cannot know it. If she was wronged no less than either, her worth never followed her strength or her mind; and then, this book answers, it follows what she is, and she is that as fully as you are. What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it.
