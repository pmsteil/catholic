# A moral argument for God (for Chapter 12 of a Catholic book)

**Form:** a converging case (CCC 31). Things we take to be true (A), witnesses that point beyond them (W), and an honest comparison of where each account stops (C). Reason's witness: the Church teaches that arguments of this kind (CCC 33) can bring a person to certainty (CCC 31, 36), and that they can predispose one to faith (CCC 35), but they do not replace faith.

**A. What we take to be true.** These are offered as truths: not as reports of feelings people happen to share, nor as true only by the lights of one community so far. What still seems true to us after honest reflection, including honest reflection on how we came to believe it, we have reason to trust until something more certain shows it false, or shows that its truth plays no part in the best account of why we hold it. (For the one who says these truths are real but need no Mind, W2 and C take up the claim; for the one who says nothing binds, A4 and the conclusion do, and there the case ends in a weighing each reader must make, not in a proof that compels him: such arguments are "converging and convincing," not proofs of the kind the natural sciences give, though the Church teaches that they "allow us to attain certainty about the truth," CCC 31.) None is beyond denial; C takes up those who deny them, and shows what each denial costs.
- **A1. Some things are really wrong, for everyone.** Torturing a child for fun, betraying a friend for gain, enslaving the weak. Not merely disliked: wrong even if every culture approved.
- **A2. Doing them knowingly and freely leaves us guilty: not merely mistaken, and not merely flawed.** A wrong sum needs correcting; a flawed character needs reforming; a wrong done to a person needs forgiving. Making the world worse and wronging someone are not the same. If I promise my dying friend to keep a secret she trusted to me, and after her death tell it for a laugh to a stranger in a far city who never knew her and will never repeat it, nothing in the experience of anyone on earth is worse, and still she was wronged. Had the same stranger found it in a letter blown into the street, her wish that it stay hidden would be just as frustrated, and no one would have wronged her. Guilt faces a person: I did this to *her*. Shame that looks only at itself says I am less; guilt says I wronged her; only guilt asks her pardon. And many wrongs are done by many hands, through customs and systems no one of us chose. They are still done to *her*, and whoever takes part in them willingly, approves them, or fails to hinder them when he is bound to, shares in the wrong (CCC 1868); the Church calls such systems structures of sin, the expression and effect of personal sins (CCC 1869).
- **A3. No one can make the gravest wrongs right.** A friend can release a promise as a gift. But no one's consent, given beforehand or after, could make his own enslavement, or a child's torture, right. The one we wronged can forgive us; that heals the bond, but it does not make the wrong not wrong. No one else on earth can give her own pardon in her place (the absolution the Church gives forgives the sin before God, W1(1); it does not stand in for her pardon): not her friends, not a nation by vote, not the whole human race. And when she is gone, the wrong does not go with her: we still owe her something we can no longer fully pay her.
- **A4. Every human being has the same dignity, whatever he can or cannot do.** The newborn, the dying, the severely disabled, the enemy: equal, prior to anyone's recognition, owed care and not only spared harm, never to be used as a mere means, not for the many and not for the few; never, if innocent, to be directly and deliberately killed by any of us for others' sake, nor "for his own good" without his asking (nor, the Church teaches, even at it); and not lost when life grows hard, nor even by the worst of crimes (*Dignitas Infinita* 1, 15, 34; CCC 2261, 2267). (That these lines hold however vast the numbers and against his own asking, the Church teaches, A6; so too that the death penalty is inadmissible, CCC 2267. Not all who hold the rest of A share them; W1(2) says why a death the Lord of life decrees is not the killing the line forbids, and why one who obeyed His command would act by His right, never by his own.) Equal worth is not equal duty: we owe more to our own parents and children than to strangers, but no one is worth less. Nor is it equal holiness: the saints are higher than I am in how well they love, never in the worth that is theirs by being human (DI 7). Our instincts favor our own, the healthy and the beautiful, and what is before our eyes; where instinct fails, for the infant no one is drawn to hold, we learn whether what we meant was worth or only attraction. Reason can see it (DI 1), and culture and the reasoning that holds us to consistency can carry regard outward; yet history shows how late it came and how easily it is lost (cf. CCC 37–38; *Dignitas Infinita* 10–13, 33). Seneca, who wrote that slaves are men, wrote as calmly that weak and deformed newborns are drowned (*Letters* 47.1; *On Anger* I.15.2), and Christians too were slow to see what slavery denied. So the belief's history does not decide between the accounts; its weight lies in how sure we are of it (see the conclusion).
- **A5. What we can do, and what we want, do not by themselves settle what we ought to do.** We are capable of truth and lies, justice and injustice, mercy and cruelty, giving and grasping, and we carry good desires and disordered ones. Listing what we can do, or what we want, never by itself tells us which to choose. On reflection we find certain goods good and to be pursued, and find that good is to be done and evil avoided. Aquinas, who called this the first precept of law, names among them: life; the union of man and woman and the raising of children; knowing the truth, the truth about God included; and living in society with others (ST I-II q.94 a.2). Whether that means our nature carries its own ends, as Aristotle held, and whether Someone gave it those ends, is what W must weigh.
- **A6. Real good for another always asks something of us who give it** (time, attention, comfort, sometimes far more). And some things must never be done: not to save ourselves, and not to buy a better outcome for many. A surgeon may not kill one healthy patient to give his organs to five, even if no one would ever know; a city may not secretly hang an innocent man to calm a riot; no prisoner may be experimented on to death, though many would be saved. Almost everyone sees these at once. Some say this certainty is only an alarm built to sound at harm planned as a means; but to call the alarm false one must already know that the difference it tracks does not matter, which is the very question; and the impartial benevolence that would spend the one, which its defenders say evolution would never have built, has a history too, in sympathy learned among kin and widened by consistency. A history settles neither. Fewer hold the line when the stakes grow vast (Mencius held it against the gain of the whole realm, *Mencius* 2A2), and many who would never hang the innocent man would bomb a city to end a war. Fewer still hold that a man's own request or consent could never make it right to kill him (the Buddhist monastic rule is one witness, Vinaya, third pārājika): if he may lay down his life for others, as a firefighter does (John 15:13), why may he not ask for death? The Church holds the line at every number (*Evangelium Vitae* 57; *Veritatis Splendor* 52, 80; CCC 2314), and even against a man's own request or consent (CCC 2277, 2280–2282, 2295; *Evangelium Vitae* 65–66; *Samaritanus Bonus* III, V.1): a man may lay down his life for another, or for the truth (*Veritatis Splendor* 50), and accept the death that comes, even at the hands of those who wrongly kill him: so Christ, who laid down His life of His own accord (John 10:18; CCC 609), and so St. Maximilian Kolbe, who offered himself in a condemned man's place; but a man may not aim at his own death, as an end or as a means, by his own hand or another's. (Grave psychological disturbance, anguish, or fear can lessen the responsibility of one who takes his own life, and the Church does not despair of his salvation but prays for him: CCC 2282–2283.) W1(2) explains these lines on this book's premises; the Church teaches that they belong to the natural law that reason itself can know (*Evangelium Vitae* 57, 65), though in fact they are not shared ones: many who hold the rest of A would let a dying man ask for death, or a volunteer give his life to an experiment. Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent. Weighing is not a license: "the acceptance by human society of murderous famines, without efforts to remedy them, is a scandalous injustice and a grave offense" (CCC 2269). The absolute can cost lives, even many. Without it, no innocent is safe, in principle, from being spent. The Church's rule is that one may never do evil so that good may come of it (CCC 1789).
- **A7. We are bound to the truth.** We may not believe against the evidence because a falsehood would comfort us, even if no one would ever know; and we may not stop looking because we fear what we will find. "Follow the evidence" is not advice for those who happen to want truth. It is something every reasoning mind ought to do (CCC 2104). It binds believer and unbeliever alike, though it does not ask the believer to hold his faith in suspense (*Dei Filius* ch. 3; see the conclusion).

**W. Witnesses**
- **W1. Wronging points to her, and beyond her to Someone.** The Catholic philosopher Elizabeth Anscombe saw that our words "obligation," "duty" and "morally wrong" came to us from faith in a divine law, and she asked whether they keep their sense without it. She did not answer that question with God; she proposed setting the words aside. The best accounts without God keep them, and keep a great deal: justice is owed to another, a wrong must be set right, and no vote can make cruelty good. On one point every account that keeps these words in the sense Anscombe meant agrees: the wrong is wrong of necessity, not up to anyone. We see it first in what it does to the child, and those who have never heard of a revealed law can see it (Romans 2:14–15): it is not wrong merely because it is forbidden. Not even God makes cruelty right; He cannot, because He is the Good (Chapter 3). And the Church adds that, because His love made her precious, every such wrong is also, and before all else, an offense against Him, a rupture of communion with Him (CCC 1440, 1850). It is wrong because of what it does to her, and so against the law that is His own wisdom; and it offends Him not as a second injury beside hers, for nothing harms God (Job 35:6–8), but as His love refused in her, and so as a turning away from Him. He is offended by us, St. Thomas says, only because we act against our good (*Summa contra Gentiles* III.122). So the fact that no one can release us does not decide between the accounts; nor does the fact that guilt is owed, for the nearest rivals, the realist, the Kantian, the contractualist, the Aristotelian and the Buddhist among them, answer that pardon must be asked of the one we wronged and amends made to her, and this book agrees: of her, truly and in her own right, and not of God alone (A2). We part at the grave, and over what gives her the claim at all. Two witnesses take up those two questions.
  (1) *The debt still owed.* A wrong done to a person needs forgiving, and the pardon that heals the bond between us is hers to give. When she has died, no one on earth can give it in her place, and still we owe her (A3). There are three ways to take that. Some deny it: the debt died with her, and what is left is remorse, a wound that understanding may quiet, as Spinoza taught of repentance, or that a forgetting of the whole ledger of praise and blame may dissolve, as Zhuangzi taught, but that will never be healed by the one it was done to. Or the debt outlives her, owed to her as she was, and the living repay what they can: they keep her memory, honor her wishes, care for those she loved. That is the road of many philosophers without God, who hold that the dead can be wronged as they were and that the wrong stays fixed forever; and it is the road of Xunzi, who taught that the cultivated man keeps the rites for the dead as the human way of completing a bond, without holding that the dead hear. Or she is still someone, and the debt can one day be received by her. The second road is noble, and the Church walks much of it. But on that road, what the living pay, she is not there to take up or answer; what can never be repaid, no one ever releases; and many of the dead have no one left to remember them by name. Guilt does not only mourn her. It asks to be forgiven by her, and only someone can forgive. The Buddhist walks a form of the third road without a Keeper: the dead are born again, and can receive what the living dedicate to them; but the one born again is neither the same as she was nor another; whether she and the one who wronged her meet again depends on their deeds, and no one keeps her for the meeting; and the wish to be forgiven by her as a self that abides in its own right rests on the view of self the path dissolves.
  The Catholic answer begins with God, who alone forgives sins (CCC 1441), through the Cross of His Son (CCC 613). He does so through His Church: first in Baptism, and for sins after Baptism ordinarily in the Sacrament of Reconciliation (CCC 977, 1263, 1442, 1484); and when He forgives, He takes away the whole guilt of the sin, without waiting on her pardon, so that no guilt is left unforgiven. Yet that does not cancel what we owe her: every offense against justice and truth "entails the duty of reparation, even if its author has been forgiven" (CCC 2487; 2412), so we must still repair what we can; and since absolution "does not remedy all the disorders sin has caused" (CCC 1459), for sins after Baptism we must still make satisfaction, which is made through Christ (CCC 1460, 1472–1473; Trent, Session XIV, can. 12); and God, who forgives the sin as an offense against Him, does not take her place in pardoning the wrong done to her: that pardon is hers to give (Matthew 6:12). Nor has she ceased to be. The soul does not perish at death (CCC 366); she lives to God (Luke 20:38), who forgets no one (Isaiah 49:15); and on the last day He will raise her body too (CCC 998, 1001). Death does not cut the bond: the living can still reach the dead in love, with prayer and even with a request for pardon, and, as Benedict XVI says, "it is never too late to touch the heart of another, nor is it ever in vain" (*Spe Salvi* 48; CCC 958). And no one dies with no one left to speak for her: at every Mass the Church prays for the dead, including, in one of her prayers, those whose faith God alone has known (Roman Missal, Eucharistic Prayer IV). He did not keep her from what was done to her; that weight is on the other side of the scale. What He keeps is her claim, and her, so that what was done to her is not the last word over her. How the last reconciliation between a victim and the one who wronged her is completed, the Church does not spell out. She says that grace "does not make wrong into right," and that the wrongdoer does not sit at the eternal banquet beside his victim without distinction, as though nothing had happened (*Spe Salvi* 44); if he refuses to the end, what he owes her is never received, on this road as on the second. And Benedict XVI, taking up the view of some recent theologians, proposes that the fire which both burns and saves is Christ Himself: before His gaze "all falsehood melts away," and His touch heals, in the purification after death, whoever has died open to Him, the guilty too (*Spe Salvi* 47; CCC 1030–1031). Reason cannot prove that hope, though it can come to know that she has a spiritual soul (CCC 33), which does not perish at death (CCC 366); and it can see that guilt asks for what it could have only if she is still someone, herself, and that this hope holds it open: to be forgiven by her. That guilt asks proves nothing, for a mind made for living bonds would ask whether or not she hears; what he owes her is the same on the second road and the third, and the roads part over whether she is there to receive it. So this, like the longing in W3, is a clue: it weighs as much as the reader finds that a debt truly owed to her is one she must be able, one day, to receive. (It weighs little for a reader who holds that "owed" names what a people's laws, or the rules best for all, assign, or who will say "truly owed" and still hold that a debt can be owed to one who will never receive it; and it does not reach the error theorist, for whom "owed" in the full sense is in error of the living and the dead alike; A1, A4 and A7 must.) A truth can be true; only a person can forgive.
  (2) *A worth no deed confers and no order wants.* The victim's claim is truly her own. She did not make it, cannot sign it away (A3), and it belongs as fully to the newborn who has done nothing to earn it as to the man who has done much. Being human explains why the newborn belongs. The best accounts without God also explain much of why she may not be killed for others: the wrong would be done to *her*; each person must be answered one by one, and no total answers her; the five who need her organs have no claim on her life; and doing harm is not the same as failing to help, which is Philippa Foot's own answer to the magistrate and the mob. What they say is true as far as it goes, though many let the line bend when the numbers grow vast (A6); and few say why it is not even hers to waive. Kant did, from the humanity in her own person, a worth beyond price, which she cannot release because her free reason binds her as a natural being; and he counted the newborn a person (C, Reason's own law). The Theravāda Buddhist does too, from what every being shares, that each holds life dear as I hold mine (Dhammapada 129–130; SN 55.7), and because the will to end a life is rooted in aversion, no sum makes it wholesome, and it takes from her the rare birth in which she could be freed: a real reason, not a count, but drawn from the act and from her chance, not from a worth she was given. That she can suffer, or holds her life dear, explains why harming her matters; by itself it does not explain the line, for others can always suffer more. A law or an order gives a nature to every member of a kind; it does not want any one of them. If her worth lies in what she is, then it is a gift exactly as far as her being is a gift, and here the moral question and the question of creation meet. Our love answers to a worth already there; God's love gives it, by giving her being (ST I q.20 a.2). He wills greater goods to some than to others (ST I q.20 a.3), but the good that grounds her dignity He gives every human being alike: a rational nature, whose dignity reason can see (DI 1) in every human being, whatever her power to understand or choose (DI 24, 53), and which faith knows is made in His image (Genesis 1:27), and with it a call to communion with Him, a gift no nature could claim (*Humani Generis* 26), in which the Church finds the root reason for human dignity (GS 19; CCC 27). Neither the nature nor the call is added later, and this dignity, the dignity of what she is, cannot be lost, not even by her own refusal, though the dignity of a good life can be (DI 7, 15, 22). The human person is the only creature on earth God willed for itself (GS 24): not for the species, not for the total, not for us (so too Aquinas, *Summa contra Gentiles* III.112–113). So her life is not ours to spend, not even for many, and, as the Church holds, not hers to dispose of, even when it has grown hard: it is held in trust from the One who alone is Lord of life and remains its sovereign Master (CCC 2258, 2280). She may refuse burdensome or disproportionate treatment and accept relief from pain that may shorten her days, but the ordinary care owed to the sick, food and water included, may not be withdrawn while it nourishes her without harm or intolerable suffering (CCC 2278–2279; *Samaritanus Bonus* V.3). What the trust forbids is that her death be the thing aimed at, for that turns against the good of her own life even when she asks it, against the just love of herself as well as against the love of God, as the Church says of taking one's own life (CCC 2281). That is why, on this account, the line has no exceptions for any of us, and it rests on two grounds: her own worth, which no total can make it right to aim against, for to aim at her death for others is to treat one willed for her own sake as existing for them; and the trust in which her life is held, so that no one of us, and no number of us, has any right of his own to take it. Her death foreseen and not aimed at may be weighed; aimed at, it may not be, even when she would die whatever is done, as when mother and unborn child will both die unless the child is killed (*Evangelium Vitae* 58, 62; Holy Office, 1884 and 1889; Pius XII, Address to the Family Front, 27 November 1951, who adds that treatment her life urgently needs independently of her pregnancy, by which the child's death follows, in no way willed or intended, is another matter). That is the line's hardest cost, and there it rests most on the trust; there many who hold her worth as firmly, and draw the line without the difference between aiming and foreseeing, part from this book. He who gives life its being and remains its Lord does no one wrong when He calls a life home; we, who neither give it nor own it, could only take it. That right is never ours: "no one can under any circumstance claim for himself the right directly to destroy an innocent human being" (CCC 2258). (Defending the innocent against an unjust aggressor is another matter: CCC 2263–2265.) Scripture tells of wars in which Israel was told to destroy whole peoples, children too (1 Samuel 15:3). St. Thomas holds that the Lord of life could so command without injustice. The precepts of the Decalogue "admit of no dispensation whatever" (ST I-II q.100 a.8), and this is none: by His sentence all die, the righteous with the sinners, on account of the sin of our first parent, so a death He decrees is not the undue killing the precept forbids, and one who carried out such a command would be "the executor of that sentence by Divine authority," acting by God's right and not by any right of his own (ST I-II q.94 a.5 ad 2; q.100 a.8 ad 3; II-II q.64 a.6 ad 1). This book holds with him, as Catholic theologians may, though the Church has not defined it: it is the principle just stated. God does not make a wrong right by willing it (W1), and He, who willed her for her own sake, uses no one merely as a means (GS 24). The Church holds those pages inspired, teaching "solidly, faithfully and without error that truth which God wanted put into sacred writings for the sake of salvation" (*Dei Verbum* 11), asks that what their human authors meant be sought through the literary forms of their age (*Dei Verbum* 12), and asks that they be read in the light of Christ, as part of a revelation given in stages to a people God was still teaching, which describes "acts of violence and massacre, without explicitly denouncing the immorality of such things" (*Dei Verbum* 15; *Verbum Domini* 41–42); she has not thereby rejected what St. Thomas says of God's right. No one may now claim such a command, whatever voice he says he heard, for no private revelation can surpass or correct the Revelation of which Christ is the fulfillment (CCC 67), and Christ laid down His life and took no one's. This has a cost: Kant holds that a death God commanded would still be the killing the line forbids (C, Reason's own law), and so, in binding every agent alike, do realists such as Frances Kamm and Thomas Nagel (though most such realists let vast numbers bend it, and both let a dying man's own request bend it), Mencius, who would not believe the pages of his revered *Documents* that told of a humane king's war floating pestles in blood (*Mencius* 7B3), and the Way (C, The Way). This book answers only that the One who holds that right gave her being, willed her for her own sake, and does not lose her in death (W1(1)); the wars stay on the other side of the scale. All share one origin and one calling (GS 29); an equal call, which does not wait on what anyone can do, explains an equal worth, and measured against one end that is God Himself, the genius and the newborn are for the same thing. Not every child is wanted by those who conceived her; every child was wanted by the One who made her, before anyone saw her (cf. Jeremiah 1:5; CCC 2270).
  This Lawgiver is not a commander who picks rules. A will that invents values can command, but it cannot bind; even a promise binds only under a law no promiser invented. That there is a *her* at all, made for an end, is His gift, and what we are, as He made us, is good, our desires included; their disorder is not His work but the wound of sin (CCC 405; ST I q.95 a.1). He is the author of the law (*Veritatis Splendor* 36, 40), not as a will that invents it, but because it is His own wisdom: He chose to make creatures who can be wronged, established what they are for in the very natures He created, and so fixed what love must answer to, not by decree but by what He is, for He is Himself the Good He asks of us. Our share in that law is reason's own light, not a foreign rule (*Veritatis Splendor* 41); its fullness is the New Law, "the grace of the Holy Spirit given to the faithful through faith in Christ" (CCC 1965–1966). The law binds because He is good; the offense against Him can be forgiven because He is Someone; and the one we wronged is kept, because He forgets no one. And the Judge lets Himself be asked (Genesis 18:23–32).
- **W2. The law and the mind that reads it point to one Source.** Begin with truth (A7). Evolution explains well why our senses, and the bodily powers our reasoning works through, mostly get the world right: a creature that misreads cliffs and lions does not last. (Not the mind itself: the spiritual soul is created immediately by God, CCC 366; *Humani Generis* 36; the argument here does not assume it.) It does not explain why we *ought* to believe what is true when a falsehood would serve us better (C, Nothing really binds). Then ask about our nature. The Church and most of her best rivals agree that what is good for a creature depends on what it is, and that much of the natural law is fitted to our kind. So the question is not whether our nature could have been otherwise, but whether it is an accident, an order that needs no mind behind it, or a wisdom. Darwin imagined rational creatures raised like hive bees who would think it a sacred duty to kill their brothers. If our nature is an accident, it still aims at its own good, as every living kind does; but that good is the good of the kind that happened to arise, and another kind could have found its good in what we call cruelty. Yet the core of the law is not fitted to our kind only: gratuitous cruelty, betrayal, treating a person as a thing would be wrong for any creature that can reason and suffer. If so, there are eternal truths about what is good and what we owe, and minds can know them. The Church places such truths in God's own wisdom, where every possible nature is first known (ST I q.15; q.16 a.7; I-II q.91 a.1–2; *Veritatis Splendor* 43). The best rivals give two answers. One says the order is a fact that needs no mind: the features that make a deed wrong (pain, trust, betrayal) are the very ones survival taught us to notice, and reason, holding itself to consistency, carries the judgment wherever those features are, as it carries a proof. The other says mind and law belong to one nature, so that a rational animal simply reads its own form. Both are real answers. What they leave is the fact they rest on: that minds built, on their account, for survival, or the form of one rational animal, grasp necessary truths at all, of logic and number as well as of right, and that the starting points survival rewarded were truly good. The realist answers that survival and reason explain that fit, and that only the necessary truths at its bottom need nothing further, as this book says of God; this book holds that one Source of law and mind explains it more fully, in logic and number as in right. That is a judgment about where explanation may stop, and the rivals do not share it. The debunker presses from the other side: logic paid its way in survival by being true, a belief that kin must be cared for paid its way either way, and the step from "wrong for us" to "wrong for any mind" is made by consistency, carrying only our attitude. Against him the case rests on two things, and neither settles it: A7, for he asks us to give up beliefs because the evidence requires it, and that requirement, if it binds only those who want the truth, does not bind one who fears where it leads; and A4, whose weight against him lies in how much surer the reader is that the infant no one held was wronged than of any premise in his argument (see the conclusion). Our conscience fails where self-interest pulls (the slave, the stranger, the infant who cannot repay), as the Church expects, for disordered self-love is the root of sin (ST I-II q.77 a.4; CCC 1850), though a world with no one behind it predicts it too, and more exactly (see the section on evil). Yet conscience also corrects itself, pushed by the wronged themselves, who would not be ignored, by sympathy, interest and force, and by reason holding itself to consistency with goods it already sees; but consistency can shrink regard as well as widen it, and that history alone does not decide. Nor does one Source require that every people agree: Aquinas held that the first principles of the law are known to all, while its further conclusions can be darkened by passion, bad custom or bad habit (ST I-II q.94 a.4, a.6; CCC 1960). If truth and life have one Source (John 14:6), the will to truth cannot finally lead away from life. (Those for whom moral words voice refined feeling, Kantians, for whom the law is the form of a reasoning will, and pragmatists, for whom the norms of inquiry come with the practice of giving reasons, need no meeting of law and mind; theirs is the question in C. Wang Yangming and Mou Zongsan, for whom the heart itself is the law, and Spinoza, for whom law and mind are one in God or Nature, agree with this witness and part from this book in W1 and W3; Laozi grants one Source and denies that it can be named or laid down as a law, *Daodejing* 1, 25.) W1 and W3 give the reasons to think that Source is Someone and not something, and not less than personal: knowing and loving, and willing each of us for her own sake, are in Him more truly than in us.
- **W3. Communion is a clue that points to One who is Love.** Only a person can love us; a law, a force, an order or an idea cannot. We long to be known and loved wholly, and no one on earth can do it, because no one knows the whole of us, not even ourselves; even if we are made of our relationships, the longing remains, to be received whole, across every role and every year, by someone who will not die. Science can trace the paths this longing travels in us: attachment, a mind that pictures being seen, the shaken judge within who appeals, as Adam Smith saw, to the all-seeing Judge (*Theory of Moral Sentiments* III.2.33); that tells us how we long, not whether there is Someone to long for, and the longing alone settles nothing. Sartre went further: we are a lack that longs to be its own ground, which is to long to be God, and so the longing must fail, and the wish to be loved with it. That is true of the longing that grasps, the wish to be like God without God (Genesis 3:5; CCC 398), and of a love that asks a rival freedom to found us; it is not true of the longing to be given being, and told that it is good that we exist, by One who is no rival beside us. Part of our longing grasps, and both the Buddhist and the Catholic say grasping love must die; the Buddhist keeps a wholesome desire for the path until it reaches its end, and the Catholic adds that desire itself, eros too, is not to be killed but healed (*Deus Caritas Est* 3–5, answering Nietzsche's charge that Christianity poisoned eros). We part over what is left. The Buddhist says what is left is wisdom that no longer clings to a self, and with it boundless kindness, compassion, joy in another's good, and an even mind that holds every being alike. The Catholic says all of that is real, though the self that stops clinging is not dissolved (CCC 366), and that beneath it is love's first word to another, in Josef Pieper's phrase: "it is good that you exist." Love does not say that for a while only. Nietzsche heard that wish for always too, and answered it without a Person: say yes to your life so wholly that you would will it all again forever; but that yes is said by me, not to me, and it must be said to what was done to her as well. Zhuangzi answers with an easy heart that it is good to be human now and good to be changed into whatever comes next, and that to demand to stay oneself is to be metal leaping up in the forge. But the Christian hope is not more of the same years, nor a refusal of change, for "we shall all be changed" (1 Corinthians 15:51). It is that the one we love will go on being herself, body and all, made whole (CCC 1025, 999). The risen Christ kept His wounds (John 20:27): what a finite love lived through is not erased but kept, and healed. A longing does not prove its answer, but it shows what answer would fit: a Person who said that word to each of us first, who knows us wholly, and who loved us first (1 John 4:19). Spinoza objected that whoever truly loves God cannot want God to love him back, for that would be to want God changed. But to love is to will another's good (ST I q.20 a.2), and God wills ours without gaining anything from us; to long for His love is to want to be wanted by One who lacks nothing. If the highest good knows and loves no one, or knows each of us only as a truth it holds, and loves us only in loving itself, wanting none of us, then love, the best thing in us, is answered in the end by no one besides ourselves. That is not evidence, for we may not believe a thing because it comforts (A7); but it is where that account stops.
  This witness raises the hardest question: if such a Person loves us, why do many honest seekers not find Him? Some of the most disciplined seekers in history, the Buddhist contemplatives, looked inward for a lifetime and report a peace beyond the self rather than a Person; and the Neo-Confucians, who for centuries searched things and their own hearts, found a principle rather than a Person. The Church rejects nothing that is true and holy in their way, even as she proclaims Christ, in whom all may find the fullness of religious life (*Nostra Aetate* 2). Her own mystics speak of a darkness on the way to God too, though theirs is the night of faith before Someone; each tradition can read the other's summit as a stage on its own road, and for this weighing neither reading is evidence. The Church does not presume that such seekers are hiding from Him (CCC 847), though she knows that any of us can (CCC 29). He makes Himself known through the world and the conscience (CCC 32–33), fully in Christ (*Dei Verbum* 4), and, for most of us first, through persons: a mother's love before a creed, a neighbor's mercy before a name (cf. Matthew 25:37–40); and He has entrusted us with making Him known to one another (CCC 848). He does not withhold what is needed for salvation from anyone who, through no fault of his own, has not yet come to an explicit knowledge of Him and, by grace, tries to live a good life (*Lumen Gentium* 16; CCC 1260); when such a person, moved by grace, turns from a wrong out of love for the good, this book hopes he is already turning toward the One who forgives. A child is loved long before he can say who loves him. That is part of an answer, not a full answer to why Love would choose this way; and this book's own definition makes the question harder, for a love that binds into communion wills to be known back, for our good and not from any lack of its own. For many honest seekers the conscious knowing comes late, and for many not in this life at all. Against the objection in its strongest form, that a perfectly loving God would let no one open to Him go without that knowing now, this book denies only the *now*, on the Church's teaching: the choice made in this life (CCC 1021) can be made before the name is known, for the Spirit offers every person, in a manner known only to God, the possibility of sharing in Christ's Paschal Mystery (GS 22); God can lead those who through no fault of their own do not know the Gospel "to that faith without which it is impossible to please him" (CCC 848), and for those who are saved the knowing is completed at the end. For the infant no one holds, there is no mother's love to carry His love to her; yet she is not forgotten by Him (Isaiah 49:15). The Church holds that Baptism is necessary for salvation, and that God, who bound salvation to it, is not Himself bound by His sacraments; so if she dies unbaptized, the Church entrusts her to His mercy and hopes that there is a way of salvation for her, and all the more urgently asks that no child be kept from Baptism (CCC 1257, 1261). Revelation gives the Lover's name: God is Love (1 John 4:8), Himself "an eternal exchange of love, Father, Son and Holy Spirit" (CCC 221).

**C. An honest comparison.** Every account stops somewhere, God's included. Each rival below *accommodates* much of A, often in its own words; the question is what each *explains* and what it must give up. Each is stated as its best defenders state it. No rival's origins are used against it that we would not let it use against ours.
- **Nothing really binds** (error theory, often joined to an evolutionary account of conscience and of religion; others, such as Sharon Street, use the same argument to show that our values bind us only from within, so that a consistent person who began from other values could have reason to do what we call cruel) is consistent and borrows nothing; nor does it tell us to stop caring, for Mackie urged that we go on making morality as a human device. With one cause it explains why we feel bound, guilty and watched. Its price: torturing a child for fun is not really wrong (A1); the newborn, like each of us, has no worth except what we are built and taught to give, and we are least built to give it to the infant no one is drawn to hold (A4); and "follow the evidence" binds only as the aim built into believing, or as advice for those who happen to want truth, so that no one, in the full sense, ought to keep looking when he fears what he will find, or to care whether its own case is true (A7). Its ablest defenders accept that cost or argue that it is none.
- **Feeling, refined** (Hume, Adam Smith, and today's quasi-realists: moral words voice our attitudes, made informed, consistent and sympathetic) can say nearly everything in A in its own voice, even "her worth does not depend on us" and "my guilt is owed to her." Its defenders will say, in their own voice, that the heart which holds she is worth as much as you sees truly, and would see truly if no heart held it; and each of us judges from where he stands. So the difference is not who must judge, nor what first makes cruelty wrong, which both find in what it does to her, nor whether a heart refined as far as any could be might still be wrong about her, which its ablest defenders say it can grant; it is whether the truth about her has any home beyond what is done to her. On ours it is held first in the Mind that made her, by which any heart might be found wrong; its defenders call that further claim idle. Its founders were honest about their costs: Hume admitted that if the heart of the "sensible knave," who cheats when it pays, does not rebel, no answer will satisfy him. No argument compels such a man, ours included; the difference is what he is missing: on Hume's answer, a feeling and its peace; on the quasi-realist's, a good that is there whether he knows it or not; on ours, Someone too. And at the grave, joined as it usually is to a view on which nothing of her remains, it leaves no one to forgive.
- **Our best words, widening** (pragmatism, above all Richard Rorty; and nearby the relativism of Gilbert Harman and David Wong, for whom moral truth is relative to a morality, though for Wong human nature rules some moralities out) does not deny A; it declines to ask what makes A true, holding that no answer would do work our practices of giving reasons do not already do. Moral progress, it holds, is the widening of "we," won more by stories than by proofs, and the Church grants that the recognition of equal dignity grew slowly (A4, W2). Asked why "we" should widen rather than shrink, it answers from within: because we are people who count cruelty the worst thing we do; to one who is not, it has nothing to say, and this case cannot compel him either. The difference lies elsewhere: it will say, from within, that the slave was wronged whether or not anyone said so, and decline to say that anything makes that true, our words included; on ours, her claim holds first in the Mind that made her. The pragmatist asks what difference that makes. William James's own test answers: looking back, the two may come to the same; looking forward, one promises that what is good is kept and the other does not (*Pragmatism* III). That shows the question is not idle, not that the promise is true (A7); it is what W1(1) and W3 weigh.
- **Reason's own law** (Kant) explains universality, and its "never merely as a means" stands with A4 and A6: humanity has a worth beyond price, which admits no equivalent and so cannot be traded even for many (*Groundwork* 4:434–435). Kant held that no one, not even God, is the author of the moral law; each rational will must regard itself as giving the law to itself, because the law is the very form of a free will (*Groundwork* 4:431, 4:436; *Metaphysics of Morals* 6:227). This book holds that God is its author, not as a will that invents it but as the Good whose wisdom it is (W1; *Veritatis Splendor* 36, 40, 43). Then the question of W2 returns in Kant's form: why there are beings at all whose own law it is. As rational faith Kant gave an answer near this book's: a being under moral law is the final end of creation, which requires a moral Author of the world (*Critique of Judgment* §§84, 86–87), but he held it for practical purposes only and as no ground of any duty (§87; *Critique of Practical Reason* 5:125–126): near this book in what it says, far in what it claims. He counted the child a person from the act that brought her into the world (*Metaphysics of Morals* 6:280); those of his readers who ground her dignity in the rational nature she has by kind must say why that kind carries such worth before reason is ever used, a question this book faces too, and answers that her nature is made in God's image and carries a call that does not wait on her powers (GS 19; W1(2)). Kant made a forgiving spirit a duty and held that the dead can be wronged (*Metaphysics of Morals* 6:460–461, 6:295–296); that the one we wronged can forgive once she is dead, his principles leave to rational faith in the soul's enduring, which he grounded on another need, endless progress toward holiness (*Critique of Practical Reason* 5:122–124), and which his secular heirs set aside. On one point Kant parts from this book: whether a death God commands is still the killing the line forbids. He held that not even God may use a rational being merely as a means (*Critique of Practical Reason* 5:131), and this book agrees (GS 24); and he held that Abraham should have answered the voice that bade him kill his son that he was sure he must not, and not sure the voice was God's (*Conflict of the Faculties* 7:63). This book agrees with him about every voice that now claims such a command (CCC 67), and parts from him about a command truly God's, and so about Abraham, whose obedience Scripture praises (Hebrews 11:17–19; ST I-II q.100 a.8 ad 3): with St. Thomas it holds that the Lord of life does no wrong in calling any life home, and that one who obeyed would act by His right, not his own (W1(2)). That is a cost against Kant, and this book pays it openly.
- **What we owe each other** (contractualism: a wrong is what principles no one could reasonably reject would forbid, and so what could not be justified to each person it touches, above all the one it is done to) explains why a wrong is done to someone, why we answer to the one we wronged, and why no total can justify spending one person for others, since each must be answered one by one. It reaches the abandoned child as surely as the wanted one, for it asks what could be justified to *her*, not whether anyone chooses to speak for her. But it stops at two things. Why being born one of us puts her among those to whom we must answer, its best defenders explain by a relation: she is born of us and of our kind, and others may speak for her as trustees; why that relation, and not her powers, brings her within that circle, it takes as given. And why answering to each one is what we owe each other, it explains by the worth of standing with each person in mutual recognition, which it takes as given, or, with Stephen Darwall, by the standing every demand presupposes in the one addressed, which reaches only those who can be addressed. These it holds as truths that simply hold, and there it meets the realist's question (W2). When the one we wronged is gone, it can say she is still owed, and, with Darwall, that the standing to forgive is hers alone; joined, as it usually is, to a view on which nothing of her remains, it cannot say that she can ever forgive.
- **Care between persons** (the ethics of care: Nel Noddings, Virginia Held, Joan Tronto, Eva Feder Kittay) begins where this book's hardest cases begin, with the newborn and the one who holds her. It sees what accounts built for equals who bargain miss: that each of us began, and many will end, wholly in others' hands; that many wrongs betray a trust (A2; Annette Baier); and that carers need care too (Kittay's *doulia*; CCC 2208–2209). With Kittay it says why worth does not follow ability: each of us is some mother's child (A4). It reaches even the infant no one holds: she is still her mother's child and one of us, and, with Tronto, a people that leaves her on the hillside has failed a care it owed her together. Its best defenders argue that no one's standing can rest on powers some never have and all of us lack for a time; what they take as given, as every account takes something, is that dependency calls for care, and that care has the worth it has. And of the infant no one wanted, it can say that she ought to have been wanted, not that anyone did want her. This book's answer has the shape of care's first act, attention to this one for her own sake: the One who made her wanted her before anyone saw her (W1(2)); not of its next, for He did not take her up from the hillside in this life, and that is weighed in the section on evil.
- **Well-being, counted impartially** (utilitarianism) counts every pain equally, refuses to let the stranger count for less, and is right that consequences matter; the Church weighs them too, as secondary elements, which can make neither good nor right an act that is in itself evil (CCC 1754). It has often spoken up for those whom others passed by, as Mill did for the slave and for women, and Singer for the animals and the distant poor. Its best defenders can say why we should hold firm rules against killing the innocent, and even live by them day to day as if they had no exceptions (Hare's two levels). But it explains wronging only by worsening: even its rule form, which holds an act wrong when it breaks the rules best for all to accept, though it does better this time (Brad Hooker), grounds those rules, and so her claim, in well-being counted impartially, with some priority to the worst off. Counting experiences, it finds no wrong in the telling itself, though it condemns the man disposed to tell, for no one feels the betrayal (A2); counting wishes, hers among them, it can call the telling wrong, but her claim still weighs as one part of the total, not as a claim no total can override. It makes every person, in principle, someone who may be deliberately killed for better totals: case by case in its act form, and in its rule form when the numbers grow large enough to call on its rule to prevent disaster (A4, A6). It can say that it is good that you exist, because your life goes well; but it values your good, not you, and at most holds irreplaceable one who wants to go on living, which the newborn cannot (W3).
- **The flourishing of our kind** (secular Aristotelian) sees the most: what we need, why virtue is good for us, why justice is owed to another and a wrong must be repaid, why one may not be killed for others, and, in the hands of its best modern defenders, why the weak still belong (though not in Aristotle's own, who would have had deformed infants left unreared, *Politics* VII.16). But it must say whether our nature, and the good it carries, is an accident, or an order that needs nothing behind it; its best defenders answer that a living form acts for an end without deliberating, and that there is no place to stand outside the life of reason from which to ask (W2). Each answer is a stopping point. Its best defenders hold too that the child who will never reason has your nature, and has it whole, for a nature is not had more or less (Aristotle, *Categories* 5), so that what she lacks is a power, not what she is. But on its account the same form is the measure by which a life is judged sound or defective; that what she is owed does not fall short with her powers, it holds by the reach of justice and charity, taken as given. Where we part is over whether her being was given and willed, and over her end: on its account the end her form is for is a life of reason well lived, an activity each must achieve, which she will never reach, though others can give her much of what a human life holds; the end the Church names surpasses every human power and comes as a free gift (CCC 1722), so strength and mind set no one's end, though for the child who dies unbaptized the Church can only hope that it is given (CCC 1261; W1(2)). That is a fair standoff, over gift and hope, and over whether a form that grades lives can give an equal worth. When the one we wronged is gone, it has no one left to receive what we owe. And some of its best defenders hold that when a person's life has stopped being a good to him, he may ask to have it ended and be helped, since justice guards a life for the sake of the one who lives it; the Church answers that life is always a good (*Evangelium Vitae* 34), and that he holds it in trust rather than owning it (CCC 2258, 2280; a truth the Church holds reason can reach, though not a shared premise, A6).
- **Necessary truths that belong to no one** (godless realism) stand with us against relativism and matter alone. Its best defenders gather those truths under a few deep principles, and say the truths need nothing behind them: their necessity lives in the natures of pain, persons and promises themselves, or, as Parfit held, needs no dwelling at all, as the truths of logic need none. Every account has a first principle, ours included. Where we part is where persons are: the debt to the dead, which on this account can be honored but never released (W1); why worth follows kind and not ability, which some of its defenders ground in the essence of the kind, much as we ground it in what she is, and many of its ablest, who ground standing in capacities, deny for some, as Jeff McMahan does, while we add why there is such a kind at all, and what it is for (W1(2)); and whether the fit between minds built, on its account, for survival and truths that hold for every mind, in logic and number as in right, needs explaining beyond the truths' necessity and reason's reach (W2). It says the law holds of itself. We say it holds first in a Mind, the same Mind that made the natures it is about and the minds that read it.
- **An impersonal sacred order** (Heaven's principle as the Neo-Confucians, above all Zhu Xi, understood it) is among the strongest rivals a book about love can face. It explains a great deal (A1, A3, A5, A6, W2). It holds that every person, however clouded in body or mind, carries the whole of Heaven's principle, as the one moon shines whole in ten thousand rivers, so that worth follows nature, not capacity (A4). Its sages say the heart of Heaven and Earth is to give life, and Zhu Xi called that heart humaneness; Zhang Zai's *Western Inscription* calls Heaven his father and Earth his mother, all people his brothers and sisters, and the disabled, the orphaned and the widowed his kin in distress. But its principle, Zhu Xi said, has no feeling or intention and makes no plans; Heaven and Earth have a mind, he granted, or an ox might give birth to a horse, but it does not deliberate. So it can say that each one holds the whole nature, as whole in the infant no one holds as in the sage, and that whoever sees her rightly answers to it; not that *this* one is wanted for her own sake by One who sees her when no one does. Heaven and Earth give life to things; God wanted *you*. Its teachers know well that a wrong is done to a person, a father, a friend, a stranger (A2); but the order itself cannot forgive, and it cannot know or love anyone (W3). Confucius spoke of Heaven as the one who gave him his virtue and who alone knew him (*Analects* 7.23, 14.35); that Heaven, the Heaven of the Zhou that gave and withdrew its mandate, was nearer a Someone, and Zhu Xi read such sayings by his rule that Heaven is principle. The modern Confucian Mou Zongsan would refuse the word "impersonal": for him the ground is a living moral heart, the same in all of us, that is itself the infinite and knows each thing as it is in itself; but it knows as my own deepest heart, not as another who turns to me, and no one at all can release what I did to another or keep the one I lost. Such an order is free of the problem of evil only if it intends no one in particular. The more such an order cares, the more it faces our question, as the historian Sima Qian did when he set the good men who starved beside the robber who lived long, against the old saying that the Way of Heaven has no favorites and is always with the good (*Daodejing* 79), and asked whether the Way of Heaven is just.
- **The Way that gives and does not claim** (Laozi and Zhuangzi) holds that Heaven and Earth are not humane: the Way gives life to each thing and does not possess it, and benevolence and righteousness appear when the Way is lost (*Daodejing* 5, 18). It sees much of A: the cruelty of rulers, the newborn as the image of whole virtue, a sage who abandons no one, is good to the good and to the not-good alike, and holds compassion as his first treasure (A1, A4; *Daodejing* 27, 49, 55, 67), and that to kill in the Executioner's place is to cut in the master carpenter's place (A6; *Daodejing* 74). It meets a grievance by not keeping the ledger: when a great grievance is reconciled, it says, some grievance remains, so the sage holds the creditor's tally and does not press for payment, and answers resentment with virtue (*Daodejing* 79, 63), and Zhuangzi would forget both praise and blame; it does not forbid asking pardon, but pardon is not what settles a wrong (A2, A3), and it lets the dead be transformed (W3). Where we say God wanted *you*, it says the highest giving does not want. Nor, though she is His, does God possess her for any gain of His own, for He lacks nothing: He willed her for her own sake and gave her life to her keeping (GS 24; CCC 2280). But He remains its Lord (CCC 2280), and there the Way parts from Him most plainly: it rears and does not rule (*Daodejing* 10, 51). And since the Way commands no one, and so never sends anyone to kill in the Executioner's place, on that point it, like Kant, parts from this book (W1(2)), though elsewhere it keeps no rule, and its sage takes up weapons when he cannot avoid it (*Daodejing* 31). We part too over whether the Giver knows her, and whether she is kept or let go.
- **God, or Nature** (Spinoza) keeps God's name and denies Him a will that chooses: all things follow from one infinite, necessary being as a triangle's properties follow from the triangle. It sees much: law and mind are one order of one God (W2); the free man will not break faith even to save his life (A6, A7); the world's pain needs no excuse, for no one intended it. But it parts from us where persons are. Cruelty is bad for every human by reason, but nothing is a sin or a wrong done to someone except under a people's laws (A2); repentance is cured by understanding that the deed had to happen, not by her pardon (A2); something of each mind is eternal, but it remembers nothing (W1); a mind's perfection is its power, so the child whose body can do little has a mind whose eternal part is small (A4); and its God, in whom each of us is known eternally, loves men in loving Himself. Aquinas too roots God's love of creatures in His love of His own goodness (ST I q.19 a.2), but his God also wills each person for her own sake (*Summa contra Gentiles* III.112–113); Spinoza's has a will only as the necessary working of its intellect, and chooses no one (W3).
- **The Buddha's Dhamma** (karma, and beyond it the unconditioned; for the Madhyamaka, emptiness) is the most searching account of the moral life without a Creator. It explains why intention matters most, why real good costs, why, in its monastic rule and in the Theravāda, the line against killing a human holds at every number and even at his asking (A6, W1(2); some Mahāyāna teachers allow a bodhisattva, out of compassion, to kill one about to kill many), and why we must follow the truth (A7). It holds with A2 that we must ask pardon of the one we wronged, though it counts remorse that lingers past amends as a hindrance to be let go: the Buddha sent a monk who had insulted a layman to ask his pardon (Vinaya, Cullavagga I). Its ground for compassion is among the most beautiful ever offered: hold every being in a boundless heart, as a mother guards her only child with her life (Sn 1.8). It never taught that conditioned existence is good in itself. Birth itself, the Buddha taught, is suffering, as is all that is conditioned, and a human birth is prized as rare because it is the chance to be free of birth. No one decides the fruit, the Buddhist says; it simply ripens, and a ripening is owed to no one and cannot forgive (W1(1)). No one, the Buddha taught, can purify another (Dhammapada 165); the Catholic says that One can, and does (1 John 1:7). Its highest good is an insight each must reach, which some will never reach in this life (AN 8.29), though on its account the chance returns in lives to come; the Catholic's highest good is also a seeing, but a seeing given, which God can give to the infant and to the one who will never reason (CCC 1028, 1250, 1261). Some of its teachers say every being has the nature of a buddha, so that worth follows nature, not capacity, and every being is measured against one end, awakening, which does not wait on what she can do now; but a nature held whole by all, like the moon in ten thousand rivers, is not by itself a call to this one; and where the Pure Land teaches that Amida's vow calls each being and gives her an end she could not reach, he too, like every buddha who knows and loves, loves beings already there: none of them gave anyone being. For the Buddhist, compassion is the seed and the fruit of the path; for the Catholic, love is also the root of the world. Here the two part honestly, over what is left when grasping dies, and over whether it is good that we exist at all.
- **History and class** (Marx and Engels, and their heirs) holds that moral codes answer to how a society produces its life and often serve those who rule, and that the gravest harms are done by systems, whose agents Marx would not hold responsible for relations whose creatures they remain. Some of this the Church grants (W2), though not that those agents are excused, and she runs the explanation the other way (A2; CCC 1868–1869). But where it says what anyone is owed, it divides. If justice is only what fits each age, the slave of antiquity was dominated and exploited, which Marx, as Allen Wood reads him, condemned in the name of freedom, not of justice, but she was not wronged in any sense that says what she was owed (A1, A2); if, with its analytic heirs, the deepest principles of justice hold in every age, it meets the realist's question (W2). Need reaches the newborn, and asks most for those who can give least; and Marx, who refused to measure persons by any one standard, counts her whole and not as a worker, but would not say why she counts as much as you: right, he held, can never be higher than the economic order of its age (*Critique of the Gotha Programme* I), and Engels judged any demand for equality beyond the abolition of classes absurd (*Anti-Dühring* I.10; A4). Where it spent the innocent for history's goal, it gave up A6; its humane heirs restored a line against that, though few hold it at every number.
- **Power and rank** (Nietzsche) is the most honest rival, and it explains a great deal: why guilt grew so large, how moral feeling has changed across history, and why equal worth spread where Christianity spread (the Church, not sharing his explanation, traces how slowly the idea grew: *Dignitas Infinita* 10–13, 16). It stays consistent by giving up A1 through A5 as claims on everyone, and the second half of A6, openly, and asks the hardest question of all: why should truth matter more than life? Nietzsche kept his own honesty, which he called the virtue his kind could not get away from, but he denied that it binds every mind. So the case puts a choice. Either truthfulness binds every reasoning mind, and then something binds every mind by what a mind is, which is the question of W2 (A7); or it binds only those who hold it, and then his unmasking can turn our own honesty against us, as he meant it to, but it cannot show that anyone ought to keep that honesty, his own included. Whether honest inquiry, which he held must turn next on Christian morality, ends there or at the Lawgiver is the question of the whole case, which this dilemma does not settle. His genealogy of equal worth is not a single story: where he found no resentment, as in the Buddha, he traced universal pity to weariness with life, and he read the love of enemies as weakness renamed forgiveness (*Antichrist* 20–22; *Genealogy* I.7, I.14). But by this case's own rule (A), the story of how a belief arose defeats it only if its truth plays no part in the best account of why we hold it. So the genealogy defeats A4 only if its story, and not God's making Himself known, best accounts for the belief, which is the question this case asks; nor can this book use that history to vindicate A4; and Nietzsche himself held that this morality stands or falls with faith in God (*Twilight of the Idols*, "Skirmishes" 5). And worth that follows nature, not strength, was taught outside Christianity too (the sacred order; the Way; the Dhamma). Nor need he praise cruelty: he can despise the man who tortures a child as base. What he cannot say is that the child was wronged. To his hardest question this book answers that truth and life have one Source, so that honesty may cost a life but can never finally be the enemy of life; on his account it might be, which is why he held that without art our honesty would be unbearable (*Gay Science* 107).
- **Freedom that makes its values** (Sartre and Simone de Beauvoir, with Camus near them) shares more of this case than it seems: Sartre and Beauvoir condemned the torture of prisoners in Algeria whatever their country approved (A1). It even grants what the secular Aristotelian denies: only a Creator, Sartre held, could give us a nature we are *for*, so without God values are made, not found (A5). Its best answer to A4 is that freedom has no degrees, and that while there is no human nature there is a human condition shared by all; Beauvoir counts the child a freedom not yet taken up, so the newborn on the hillside is wronged as fully as you. Its price: one who never will be, or never again will be, conscious has only the worth our freedom gives her (A4); its masters let the line bend for liberation (A6); and what binds one freedom to will another's, as Beauvoir held it must, is made by no freedom and binds every freedom before it chooses, as a law binds; Sartre granted that a morality's form is universal though its content is chosen, and whence that form binds is the question put to Kant (C, Reason's own law; A7, W1). Camus, who found in revolt a value common to all and was led to suspect a human nature after all, stood nearer to us. The rebel can take her side; he cannot keep her.
- **A God who is Love** is also a stopping point, but not, the Church holds, a brute one: He is the One whose very being is to be (Exodus 3:14; CCC 213; ST I q.3 a.4), who needs nothing outside Himself to explain Him. The realist says the same of his necessary truths; the difference is not fewer first principles but one source for the law, the minds that read it and the beings it is about, which he connects through survival and reason rather than through a common source (W2). Against the sacred order, the nameless Way and the God of Spinoza, which also stop at one, it is the one that wills each of us for her own sake, freely and from no need, to share His goodness (GS 24; ST I q.19 a.3; q.44 a.4; CCC 293, 295), a claim hell makes hardest to see (below), and is Someone besides ourselves, though His name is "at once a name revealed and something like the refusal of a name" (CCC 206). Against the ethics of care, it is the one relation no one can deny her, though she may refuse the communion it offers; against the Buddhist, the account in which love is the first word (1 John 4:8, 19). Its claim is reach, not count: one source for what the best rivals ground in several places or take as given: why there is at all a kind whose worth does not follow ability, each one willed for her own sake and, as faith adds, offered an end against which that worth is equal (W1(2)); and why law and mind fit (W2). That these ask for a source at all is a judgment the rivals do not share. Guilt's request to be forgiven by the one who is gone, and a longing no one on earth can answer, it answers but does not explain better than its rivals: clues of fit, not proofs (W1, W3). It does not remove all mystery; it is one mystery, and a mystery that explains; and it is Someone, which is what forgiveness and communion suggest.

**On the other side of the scale is real weight, and it is heavy.** Children suffer horribly. Animals suffered for hundreds of millions of years before any human chose anything, and pain in nature falls roughly where survival would put it, not where justice would. So, roughly, does conscience (W2). In the world God made, one creature's good often comes through another's loss: the wolf lives by the fawn, and the parasite lives by the child; if our nature is a wisdom and not an accident (W2), so is theirs, and that too is on this side of the scale. Many who do not resist Him never come to know Him by name in this life, and whether one does depends far more on where and to whom one was born than on how honestly one sought. And the Catholic faith adds a weight of its own. It teaches that hell is real and eternal, that those who die in mortal sin suffer its punishments, "eternal fire," and that its chief punishment is eternal separation from God (CCC 1035): a person can refuse God forever by his own free choice (CCC 1033), and the Church gives this teaching as an urgent call to conversion (CCC 1036). God desires all to be saved (1 Timothy 2:4), and He holds no one to account beyond what he knew, or could and should have known, and freely chose (CCC 1735, 1791, 1859–1860). God does not force His love on anyone able to refuse it (Chapter 4); He permits a refusal because He respects the freedom He gave (CCC 311), and a refusal made with full knowledge and consent and held to the end becomes final, as the angels' choice became final, through no defect in His mercy (CCC 393, 1033, 1857). But freedom does not finish the answer. God kept Mary from every sin by grace without forcing her yes (CCC 490–494), so His grace can bring a free will to its yes without overriding it. How it does so, and why one will is won and another refuses it to the end, the Church does not explain; she lets her theologians differ (Paul V, decree of 5 September 1607), within limits she has defined: grace does not take away the will's power to refuse it (Council of Trent, Session VI, ch. 5, can. 4); God commands nothing impossible (ch. 11); He predestines no one to evil (can. 17), nor anyone to hell, for which "a willful turning away from God (a mortal sin) is necessary, and persistence in it until the end" (CCC 1037). That death ends the time for accepting or rejecting grace is her teaching too (CCC 393, 1021), not something this case shows. So hell stays on this side of the scale, and it is the heaviest thing there, for here the principle this book holds with the Church (GS 24) is hardest for us to see. It says that God wills each of us for her own sake; of one man Christ said that it would have been better for him had he not been born (Matthew 26:24). The one who is lost is not spent for anyone's gain; he excludes himself. Yet St. Thomas, who holds that God governs each person for her own sake, holds too that in permitting some to be lost He shows His justice, as in the saved His mercy, and so His goodness in the whole (*Summa contra Gentiles* III.112–113; ST I q.23 a.5 ad 3). How that permission accords with His having willed the one who refuses for his own sake, reason cannot show, and the Church does not say. She prays that no one be lost, for with God all things are possible (CCC 1058, 1037), and in hope prays for all to be saved (CCC 1821); she does not declare of any human being that he is lost, for without special divine revelation it is not granted to us to know which human beings are (John Paul II, General Audience, 28 July 1999, 4). For the one who refuses to the end, this book holds its claim on the Church's word that God predestines no one to hell (CCC 1037), prays with her that none be lost, and bears this weight. The wars of 1 Samuel 15 stay there too, and weigh more than an unanswered why: W1(2) holds with St. Thomas that the Lord of life could command them without injustice, but reason can see only that He does no wrong in calling a life home, not why He would call these lives home by these hands; and a reader weighing by reason alone may be surer that those infants were wronged than of any argument that they were not (see the conclusion): for him it is a cost against the case, not only a mystery; for the believer, faith is the more certain (CCC 157): these pages are inspired and teach without error what God willed for our salvation, and, whatever the right reading of their literary form (*Dei Verbum* 12), God does no injustice (W1(2)). Some rivals bear this whole weight more easily than we do. The Buddhist, who never taught that conditioned existence is good in itself, expects a world full of suffering, and since no one made the round and no state in it lasts forever, no one must answer for the child. Nietzsche did not merely expect great suffering but prized it as the discipline behind every human greatness; what he pays for that is the yes answered below. A Way that is not humane gives life and lets each thing go, and owes no one an answer; nor does Spinoza's God, which intends no one. The realist for whom these truths need no Mind bears it as easily: what was done to her was wrong on his account as on ours, and no one permitted it; what he cannot offer her is repair. We say that it is good that she exists, and that Someone willed her for her own sake, and so we who say it must give an account of the child, the account we would ask of anyone who loved her and could have spared her. Free will (Chapter 1) answers whose the evil we do to one another is: it is ours, not His (CCC 311); that He respects the freedom He gave, which the Church gives as one reason (CCC 311), does not by itself answer why He permits it, since His grace can, as with Mary, bring a free will to its yes. Why He did not keep the first man from sinning, the Church answers, with St. Leo the Great, only with what He brought from it: Christ's grace gave us more than the devil's envy took away (CCC 412). Nor did God simply begin with heaven, though He gives it as pure gift to the baptized child who dies before she can choose (CCC 1250, 1023; Benedict XII, *Benedictus Deus*, 1336); He also willed creatures who can give their love back of their own accord, in a life where it could be refused (Chapters 1, 4). That is a good, not a necessity; whether it is worth this world's cost is, for reason, part of the weighing, not settled by it; and Ivan Karamazov, who would hand back his ticket even if the harmony bought with one child's tears were real, refuses the weighing itself, even should the child herself forgive. It does not answer the fawn in the forest fire or the child with cancer. For those the Church gives a setting, not an explanation: God made a world still on its way to its perfection, and gave His creatures the dignity of acting on their own and being causes for each other, so they can fail and harm (CCC 306, 310). He wills the order of the whole, never the loss for its own sake (ST I q.19 a.9). Beyond that she claims no explanation; only Christian faith as a whole answers it (CCC 309). Faith gives the certainty that God would not permit an evil unless He brought a good from that very evil (CCC 324), though the evil never thereby becomes good (CCC 312); and we will know His ways fully only at the end (CCC 314).
Three things bear on that weight, though none makes it light, and only the second lessens it as evidence. *First*, evil has nothing of its own: it is always a good thing harmed (Chapter 1). That does not make pain less real; it says that pain is the felt loss of some good. There can be a Supreme Good but not a supreme Evil (ST I q.49 a.3): every choice of evil reaches for a lesser good the chooser lacks, and a will that lacks nothing has nothing to grasp at. So the world's darkness is not evidence of a dark god. That rests on what evil is; a reader who holds that malice could be as full and real as love must weigh a dark god against the whole case, and for him much of the case cuts both ways. Nor does it by itself rule out a source that is indifferent, or one from whose fullness the ruin follows with the rest, as Spinoza held, or a god beyond good and evil who makes and breaks out of sheer fullness. Those are answered only by the whole case: chiefly by a worth that is each one's own and not to be spent for others, which this book holds is wanted for her own sake (W1(2)); against a source with no mind at all, though not against Spinoza's, by the law and the mind that reads it (W2); the debt to the dead asks that persons endure, and the longing to hear that it is good that *you* exist asks for One who gave us being, but they are clues, for a dark god or an indifferent world would leave them just as we find them (W1(1), W3). A yes said to whatever happens, or a calm that understands it had to happen, is said to no one; it may fight for her while she lives, but toward what was done to her it can only will it again or understand that it had to be, and so it cannot take her side against the past. God says no to what was done to her, and yes to her (Wisdom 1:13; CCC 413). *Second*, God's permission is not a trade in which she is spent for someone else's gain. The Church does say, with St. Thomas, that God permits evil in order to draw forth some greater good (CCC 412), and from that very evil, though the evil never earns that good and never becomes good itself (CCC 312, 324). This book's claim is narrower: whatever good is drawn, she is not spent as a mere means for it, and the good hoped for is offered to her as well (GS 22; for the child who dies unbaptized, the Church hopes that it is given: CCC 1261). That is said of those who suffer; of one who refuses God to the end, it can be said only that the good was offered him, and that is the weight of hell, above. The evil in what a free creature does is his own: God gives the act its being, never its defect, and is in no way the cause of moral evil; He permits it (CCC 311; ST I-II q.79 a.2). Among animals the order He wills gives one creature's loss to another's life: the lion lives by the slaying of other animals (ST I q.22 a.2 ad 2; q.48 a.2 ad 3; q.49 a.2), though St. Thomas speaks there of death, not of long agony. A person that order never gives away for another's sake: the parasite takes from the child, but she was not made for it (GS 24). What we see is the same as with the fawn; the difference is in what He wills, and in the loss of the first harmony, through which visible creation became alien and hostile to man (CCC 376, 400, 1008). That is a teaching, not something we see; the parasite, and the pain of the ages before man, stay on this side of the scale. The hands that pull a child from the fire are part of what He made real, so His permission never excuses ours (CCC 2269). The best rivals ask of Him what they ask of us, whether it could be justified to her, and reason may fairly put that question, though God is answerable to no creature's court (Job 38:1–4); an order of the whole cannot answer it by itself, nor could a good paid her afterward; only a good brought from that very evil (CCC 324), and given to her herself (this book's claim), so that what was done to her is answered and not merely outweighed, could, and that is a hope, not a proof. We know enough of goodness, though only by analogy (CCC 43), to know much of what a good God would value, which is why this weight is real; what we would not expect to see, if the good is finished only in eternal life and in a creation set free (Romans 8:21; CCC 314, 1046–1047), is how each particular evil is repaired. So for one weighing by reason alone, not seeing how each evil is repaired is weak evidence that it is not; it is no evidence that each was needed. Whether a world of real causes needs this much pain is that question, and there the evidence is not weak: the pattern of pain in nature still weighs, and so does the child who dies of disease before she can choose, or who lives a whole life in pain without the power to choose, whom the good of her own freedom does not serve. The Church does not say whether the fawn will live again; she says creation itself waits to be set free (Romans 8:19–22). For the fawn that hope is thinner than it is for the child, and this book does not pretend otherwise. *Third*, the God weighed here is not a designer watching from a distance. He entered suffering Himself: the eternal Son became man and suffered in His own flesh on the Cross. God, St. Bernard said, cannot suffer, but He can suffer with (*Spe Salvi* 39). Christian faith professes that He will raise the dead (CCC 998), and hopes in Him to wipe away every tear (Revelation 21:4; *Salvifici Doloris* 15, 25). This is not a further proof, and it does not explain why; it is what the God being weighed actually is, and it means that, if He is, the last word over the suffering child is not the suffering. Accounts without a Creator have hopes too: the Buddhist hopes that every being, this child too, and the fawn, will be freed from suffering; the Confucian hopes for a world in which the widow, the orphan and the disabled are all cared for; the Marxist works for a world in which no one is spent for another's gain; and the pragmatist hopes, with John Dewey, that the world can be made better by our effort, though nothing promises that it will. The last three are hopes for those who come after; to the children already spent they can offer memory and rites, not repair. Some of the Marxists' own heirs saw it: true justice, Adorno held, would require that even what is irrevocably past be undone, which, Benedict XVI observes, would mean a resurrection of the dead, and the question of justice, he adds, is the strongest argument for eternal life (*Spe Salvi* 42–43). The Christian hope is more particular: that this child, herself, body and all, will be raised and made whole, and will know the One who never forgot her. That is not evidence, but it is where the accounts part. One thing more belongs here, as an invitation, not a reply. Whoever protests against God on the suffering child's behalf does not refute himself; his protest is this weight at its strongest. But it stands on the ground this case starts from: that what was done to her is really wrong, whoever approves it, that no one on earth can make it right, and that she was worth as much as anyone, though she could give nothing back (A1–A4). This book invites him to follow that protest to its source, and to ask what makes it true.

**Conclusion.** Weighing both sides, this book judges that the best explanation of love as we actually find it is a Lawgiver who is Love. Evil weighs against that conclusion, heavily; in this book's judgment it does not overturn it. (This is reason's weighing, offered to every reader. The believer's own assent does not wait on it: he believes because of the authority of God who reveals, and faith is more certain than all human knowledge, CCC 156–157, and there can never be any real discrepancy between faith and reason, CCC 159.) That the law, the minds that read it and the persons it is about have one Source is a judgment about where explanation may stop (W2); that this Source is Love, willing each of us for her own sake, rests on W1(2) and two clues (W1(1), W3), and there the scale is thinnest, for there evil, God's hiddenness, hell and the wars of 1 Samuel 15 weigh, on us and not on the impersonal rivals. Against the realist who holds that these truths are real and need no Mind, the case is closest: this chapter adds reach and fit, not a refutation, and evil is no evidence against him, for on his account no one permitted it; this chapter alone does not settle the weighing against him. An honest reader may weigh it differently, and may turn the question on us: which is he surer of, that no good could justify a loving and almighty God in letting this child die so, or that such a God exists? The section on evil says how far this book can answer that. He should still weigh it; the Church does not presume that he is at fault, and leaves the judgment of every heart to God (*Lumen Gentium* 16; CCC 847, 1861). The Church teaches that arguments of this kind can bring a person to certainty, and that God can be known with certainty from the things He has made by the natural light of reason (CCC 31, 36; First Vatican Council, *Dei Filius* ch. 2 and can. 2.1). This case claims less, and each reader must reach certainty with his own reason, as many of us find hard (CCC 37). Certainty of this kind comes, as St. John Henry Newman saw (*An Essay in Aid of a Grammar of Assent*, ch. 8–9), from many signs converging in one mind, not from any one of them compelling every mind. It cannot compel the one who gives up A4. To him it says only: look at the newborn whom instinct passed by, the one the ancient world left on the hillside, and ask which you are surer of: that she, weak and not yet able to know herself, was wronged when she was left there, before anyone said so, and wronged no less than a strong newborn left beside her, or a grown man, however gifted, put out there helpless to die, however differently one counts what each of them lost; or any argument that says she was not, or that it was a lesser wrong, or that you cannot know it. If she was wronged no less than either, her worth never followed her strength, or her mind, or how much she can feel or want, or how much good her life would have held, nor how much any of us cared for her, for none of us did; and then, this book answers, it follows what she is and what she is made for (W1(2)), and she is that as fully as you are. What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it.
