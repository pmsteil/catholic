# Blind review: the Buddhist school (Theravāda and Madhyamaka)

## 1. School and voice

I speak for the Buddha's Dhamma as the Pāli Canon and Buddhaghosa's commentaries give it (Theravāda), and as Nāgārjuna and Śāntideva work it out (Madhyamaka), with modern scholars (Peter Harvey, Damien Keown, Jay Garfield, Mark Siderits, Charles Goodman) as interpreters. I hold that there is no creator, that persons are conventional realities without an abiding self (anattā), that the moral order is an impersonal lawfulness (dhammaniyāma, kamma), and that the highest good is nibbāna, the ending of craving. I am a non-theist who agrees with much of A, so my attack is strongest where the text says what the Dhamma *lacks*. All paraphrases of Buddhist texts and scholars are mine; mark "verify."

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** Not justice as what is owed but harm to beings who, like me, want to live and do not want to suffer: the self-comparison of SN 55.7 and Dhp 129–130, set out in the five precepts and the reciprocal duties of DN 31 (Sigālovāda). Keown builds rights language from those duties (verify). → **Holds** for "do not harm"; **borrows** when it speaks of what anyone is *owed* in the strong sense, a concept the Pāli sources barely have.

**2. A hard dilemma, resolved.** The Vinaya case (Vin III.79ff., verify): monks moved by compassion who praised death to a sick monk in agony, who then died, were held to have committed the third pārājika. Principles: analyse the intention (cetanā); killing always arises with aversion (dosa), even "kind" killing, because it pushes away the suffering by pushing away the being; the agent's mind is what the act shapes; the remedy is care (Vin I.301: whoever would tend me, let him tend the sick), palliative presence, and mindfulness of dying. → **Holds** on its own terms. It uses truthfulness (sacca), compassion (karuṇā) and giving (dāna), but not as the book defines them, bound in covenant; nothing is borrowed.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** Theravāda: every being fears pain and holds life dear (Dhp 129–130); a human birth is rare and precious (Dhp 182) because it is the opening to liberation; the Vinaya treats killing a human as pārājika from the first moment of consciousness in the womb (Vin III.73, verify), so the *rule* protects all humans equally. Madhyamaka: Śāntideva's equalizing of self and other (BCA 8.90–103, verify). Since suffering has no owner, I have no ground to prefer mine to yours, so every being's suffering is to be removed. → **Holds** for equal *consideration* and equal *protection under the rule*. **Breaks** on equal *worth*: "dignity" is not a Pāli category, and Buddhaghosa grades the weight of a killing by the victim's virtue among humans and by the animal's size (DN-a on DN 1, verify). Śāntideva's equality also leans toward totals, and Mahāyāna allows the compassionate killing of the one about to kill many. I partly deny A4 as the book means it.

**4. Ultimate good and guiding principles.** Nibbāna, the unconditioned peace, the highest happiness (Dhp 204), reached by the Noble Eightfold Path (virtue, concentration, wisdom) and lived as the four boundless states (loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, equanimity). For the infant or the severely disabled, the path continues in lives to come. → **Holds** coherently. Without rebirth (secular Buddhism) it **breaks**: the infant who dies has no path left.

## 3. Debate highlights

**E1. Guilt as debt.** *Attack:* A2's guilt-versus-shame distinction is a juridical and theistic construction. Buddhist moral psychology rests on moral shame and moral dread (hiri-ottappa, the "guardians of the world," AN 2.9), and treats lingering remorse (kukkucca) as a hindrance. The secret-letter case is explained by the teller's intention alone. *Defense:* The text already concedes the hindrance point (Dhamma row). The Church also rejects morbid guilt: contrition is sorrow plus a resolution, aimed at return, not self-punishment (CCC 1451). And the Dhamma's own practice confirms A2: the Buddha required the monk Sudhamma to ask the householder Citta's pardon (Cullavagga I), and confession is "accepted" by the one wronged. A wrong faces a person. *Rejoinder:* Granted. Intention explains why the teller did wrong. It does not by itself explain why the pardon belongs to *Citta*, and the Vinaya assumes that it does. **Held.**

**E2. A receiver without a Keeper.** *Attack:* W1(1) argues that guilt asks for a receiver. The Dhamma supplies one with no God: her continuum goes on, and kamma brings the two together again (the Kāḷī story, Dhp-a on v. 5). And "herself" assumes the strict identity that Parfit and Siderits show is not what matters. *Defense:* The text names this road exactly ("another form of the third road without a Keeper") and claims only a clue. Its difference is precise: the one reborn is neither the same nor another (Milindapañha), and nothing guarantees the meeting. Moreover the Catholic soul is not the self the Buddha refuted in SN 22.59. It is not self-made, not self-sustaining, and not in its own control. It is a creature held in being moment by moment. *Rejoinder:* Even so, a numerically identical subsistent soul is what we call eternalism (sassatavāda). We hold that wanting her *as herself* is the clinging. That is a standoff over the self, not a defect in W1(1), whose weight the text leaves to the reader. **Held** (parity).

**E3. The line without a worth-giver.** *Attack:* W1(2) suggests that only a worth God gives explains why the line holds at every number and even at her asking. The Theravāda holds that line more widely than the Church, from conception and at every number, with no giver: killing is always rooted in aversion. And it extends the precept to every sentient being. *Defense:* The text grants this in so many words ("a real reason, not a count, but drawn from the act and from his chance, not from a worth he was given"). Its claim is narrower: the Buddhist reason sits in the *agent's* mind and the victim's *opportunity*, not in *her* worth. Your own commentary shows the price. Without a given worth, the gravity of the killing follows the victim's virtue (Buddhaghosa), which is exactly "worth follows ability" moved into a moral register. *Rejoinder:* I concede the gradation and will not hide it. But the rule (pārājika) does not grade, and the rule is what protects her. **Held.** The text describes my school accurately, and the gradation is evidence for its thesis.

**E4. Ownerless suffering.** *Attack (Śāntideva):* Equality falls out of emptiness more simply than out of God. Pains have no owner, so nothing licenses partiality. That grounds radical equality across all beings with one principle. *Defense:* It grounds equal concern for *suffering*, not inviolability. Goodman reads Śāntideva as a consequentialist of character (verify), and Mahāyāna permits killing the one to save the many. That is the text's own warning: "others can always suffer more." *Rejoinder:* The Theravāda combines the two: the intention-analysis forbids killing, while self-other equality governs kindness. That works, but it is two sources, which is the text's "reach" point (C, God who is Love). **Held**, though the text should name Śāntideva, since he is the best case for its claim.

**E5. The Source cannot be a cause.** *Attack (Vasubandhu AKBh 2; Dharmakīrti PV 2; Śāntideva BCA 9.119–126, verify):* A permanent, single, self-sufficient Source either produces all its effects at once or depends on further conditions, and then it is not the one Source. So C's "not a brute stopping point" and W2's "one Source of law and mind" are unearned. Nāgārjuna adds that nothing that functions has intrinsic nature (MMK 15, 24.18–19). *Defense:* The argument treats God as a cause acting by necessity of nature, within time. Aquinas's God acts by will, not natural necessity (ST I q.19 a.3–4). He wills from eternity that each effect come at its time (ST I q.19 a.7; q.46 a.1, verify). He gives being rather than changing what is there, so a succession of effects needs no succession in Him. The text claims a converging judgment, not a proof. And the Madhyamaka has its own stopping point: beginningless dependent arising, and a lawfulness that stands "whether Tathāgatas arise or not" (SN 12.20). *Rejoinder:* If God freely wills X and is identical with His willing, either the contingency sits in God or His love of her is "a relation of reason" in Him (ST I q.13 a.7), which seems to thin out "God wanted *you*." *Counter:* His knowledge and love of each one is real and identical with His essence (ST I q.14 a.11; q.20 a.2–3). What is contingent is the effect, not a change in God. This is a live dispute in natural theology as a whole. It is not specific to the moral argument as written, which reports the Church's view. **Held** (standoff at parity).

**E6. The weight of evil, and the best explanation.** *Attack (Bhūridatta and Mahābodhi Jātakas, J 543, J 528, verify):* If a lord shapes the world, he is stained by its suffering. The Dhamma explains suffering without remainder: it is a characteristic of all that is conditioned. By the text's own admission ("Some rivals bear this whole weight more easily"), the Dhamma explains the *whole* evidence, A plus evil, better. So the conclusion's "best explanation" fails. *Defense:* The text weighs; it does not compel. It concedes that the Buddhist bears evil more easily, and names the price: on the Dhamma, no one wills her and it was never said that her being born is good. The conclusion says "this book judges" and invites the reader to weigh otherwise (CCC 847). Kamma also does not explain everything, as the Buddha himself says (SN 36.21). *Rejoinder:* The price is real, and we pay it gladly. That is a choice about what is left when grasping dies, which the text names as the honest parting. **Held** (parity).

**E7. "No one gives that end."** *Attack:* The Dhamma row says that buddha-nature "is not a call to this one, and no one gives that end or keeps her as herself in it." That is false of Pure Land Buddhism. Amitābha's eighteenth vow is addressed to each being who calls; in Shinran, even entrusting faith (shinjin) is given by Amida's other-power; and Shinran said, in effect, that the vow was for him alone (Tannishō, postscript, verify). *Defense:* Amida gives no one being, does not create, and receives beings already there. The text's final difference ("they gave no one being... love is also the root of the world") stands. *Rejoinder:* Agreed. But the sentence as written overstates. **Held with repair** (§6, R3).

**E8. Born and good.** *Attack:* "It never said that being born is good" makes the Dhamma sound life-hating. *Defense:* The text says at once that a human birth is "prized as rare because it is the chance to be free of birth," which is precise (Dhp 182; SN 56.47). *Rejoinder:* Accurate. Loving-kindness wishes that beings be well. It does not say that it is good that they *are*, because becoming (bhava) is what craving clings to. **Held.**

## 4. Ledger

- **A heading:** Holds. The rule for genealogies (a story of origins defeats a belief only if it would have produced it whether or not true) is one a Buddhist accepts and is bound by. No Buddhist position is misstated among the denials.
- **A1:** Holds. Torturing a child for fun is unwholesome for anyone, whatever a culture approves; kamma does not consult votes.
- **A2:** Holds. The Buddhist explains the secret case by intention but grants that the pardon belongs to the one wronged (Cullavagga I).
- **A3:** Holds. The fruit of a deed stands even after confession is accepted (DN 2), which the text later uses accurately.
- **A4:** Holds as a stated truth. My school dissents in part (graded gravity; "dignity" is not its category). That is a cost on my side, not a defect in the text.
- **A5:** Holds. Dhp 183 (avoid evil, cultivate good, purify the mind) is an independent witness to the first principle.
- **A6:** Holds with repair. The third pārājika citation is right: its origin story is monks who asked to be killed. Minor: Theravāda practice is not uniform (Mahāvaṃsa 25, verify), and the canon has suicide cases the Buddha did not blame (SN 35.87 Channa; SN 22.87 Vakkali; Keown contests the reading, verify). The text's claim concerns another person killing at one's asking, so it stands.
- **A7:** Holds. A strong ally (MN 61: there is no evil that one unashamed of a deliberate lie would not do). Minor: Mahāyāna skilful means (the Lotus parable of the burning house) lets a well-meant deception count as no lie.
- **W1 frame:** Holds. "The Buddhist among them" answers that pardon is asked of the one wronged: accurate.
- **W1(1):** Holds. The Buddhist form of the third road is described exactly (petas receiving merit, Tirokuḍḍa; Kāḷī; neither the same nor another). It is properly a clue, and the self question is a parity standoff.
- **W1(2):** Holds with repair. The Theravāda reason is described fairly. Pronoun slip ("takes from *him*" where the victim is "her"). It would be stronger if it named Śāntideva as the case that confirms "others can always suffer more."
- **W1 closing:** Holds. It is about what kind of Lawgiver; nothing in it misstates or presumes against the Buddhist.
- **W2:** Holds. The Buddhist answer is the first rival the text names: the order needs no mind (dhammaniyāma, SN 12.20). Where explanation may stop is honestly left as a judgment.
- **W3:** Holds. The Buddhist goal is stated rightly: wholesome desire for the path (chanda), the four boundless states, "grasping must die." Hiddenness is handled at parity. It could name the Brahmajāla's genealogy of theism (DN 1), held to the same rule for genealogies.
- **C, Nothing binds:** Holds; no Buddhist objection.
- **C, Feeling refined:** Holds; no Buddhist objection.
- **C, Our best words:** Holds; no Buddhist objection.
- **C, Reason's own law:** Holds; the cost against Kant is paid openly.
- **C, What we owe each other:** Holds.
- **C, Care between persons:** Holds.
- **C, Utilitarianism:** Holds; Keown's Buddhist critique of consequentialism agrees.
- **C, Secular Aristotelian:** Holds.
- **C, Godless realism:** Holds.
- **C, Impersonal sacred order:** Holds. Minor: the moon in ten thousand waters is a Chan image (Yongjia Xuanjue's *Song of Enlightenment*, verify) that Zhu Xi borrowed.
- **C, The Way:** Holds.
- **C, Spinoza:** Holds.
- **C, The Buddha's Dhamma:** Holds with repair. Its citations are accurate (Cullavagga I; DN 2; Sn 1.8; SN 36.21; MN 130; Dhp 165; AN 8.29). Two overstatements: "no one gives that end... not a call to this one" (false of Pure Land), and the comparison of "this life" with eternity omits the Buddhist's lives to come.
- **C, History and class:** Holds.
- **C, Nietzsche:** Holds. His reading of the Buddha (*Antichrist* 20–22) is reported accurately.
- **C, Sartre:** Holds.
- **C, A God who is Love:** Holds. "Not a brute one" is reported as the Church's view; against Madhyamaka it is a parity standoff (E5).
- **Evil, paragraph 1:** Holds with repair. "No being is lost in it forever" is loose: saṃsāra is without discoverable beginning and many beings go on wandering. What is true is that no state lasts forever.
- **Evil, paragraph 2 ("Three things"):** Holds. Minor: the Abhidhamma counts greed, hatred and delusion as real mental factors, not privations, but the text conditions its claim ("a reader who holds that malice could be as full and real..."), and that view leads to no god rather than a dark one.
- **Conclusion:** Holds. A Buddhist answers yes, she was wronged: pārājika is pārājika, from the womb. He hesitates only at "no less" (Buddhaghosa's grading), which is his cost. The claim is hedged below CCC 31, as it should be.

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**As written: NONE.**

**Minor items**
1. *Pure Land overgeneralization* (Dhamma row): "no one gives that end or keeps her as herself in it" and "not a call to this one" are false of Pure Land Buddhism (Amida's vow; Shinran's other-power). The true difference, that buddhas gave no one being, survives.
2. *Asymmetric comparison* (Dhamma row): "some will never reach in this life" is set against God's gift in eternity. On its own account, the Buddhist's chance returns in later lives.
3. *"No being is lost in it forever"* (evil section): loose. Better: no state in it lasts forever, and no one must answer for the round.
4. *Pronoun* (W1(2)): "it takes from him the rare birth in which he could be freed." The victim is "her" throughout.
5. *Uniformity* (A6, Dhamma row): Theravāda doctrine holds the line, but its chronicles do not always (Mahāvaṃsa 25), and canonical suicide cases (Channa, Vakkali, Godhika) complicate "even at his asking" if it were extended to one's own hand. The text does not extend it, so a footnote would do.

**Misstatements of my school:** only item 1 above, plus the slight implicit asymmetry in item 2. Everything else about the Dhamma is accurate, often unusually so.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Self against no-self: whether guilt's request needs her *as herself* (E2).
- Whether a permanent single Source can coherently be a cause, and whether every account must stop somewhere (E5).
- Whether it is good that we exist, or only that we be freed (E8, W3).
- Whose summit is the stage on whose road: contemplative reports of peace beyond self against the night of faith (W3).
- The final weighing of A plus evil (E6).

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1 (W1(2), pronoun).** Old: "and it takes from him the rare birth in which he could be freed:" → New: "and it takes from her the rare birth in which she could be freed:"

**R2 (W1(2), naming the Mahāyāna equality case; optional).** Old: "That she can suffer, or holds her life dear, explains why harming her matters; by itself it does not explain the line, for others can always suffer more." → New: "That she can suffer, or holds her life dear, explains why harming her matters; by itself it does not explain the line, for others can always suffer more. Śāntideva drew the boldest equality from it: since no pain has an owner, I have no ground to prefer mine to yours; and his heirs allowed the bodhisattva to kill the one who would kill many."

**R3 (Dhamma row, Pure Land).** Old: "but a nature held whole by all, like the moon in ten thousand rivers, is not a call to this one, and no one gives that end or keeps her as herself in it." → New: "but a nature held whole by all, like the moon in ten thousand rivers, is not by itself a call to this one; and where the Pure Land teaches that Amida's vow calls each being and gives her the end she could not reach, the one who calls her did not give her being."

**R4 (Dhamma row, lives to come).** Old: "Its highest good is an insight each must reach, which some will never reach in this life (AN 8.29);" → New: "Its highest good is an insight each must reach, which some will never reach in this life (AN 8.29), though on its account the chance returns in lives to come;"

**R5 (evil section).** Old: "since no one made the round and no being is lost in it forever, no one must answer for the child." → New: "since no one made the round and no state in it lasts forever, no one must answer for the child."

**R6 (A6, footnote; optional).** After "(the Buddhist monastic rule is one witness, Vinaya, third pārājika)" add: "though its canon also tells of monks who took their own lives whom the Buddha did not blame, a reading its scholars dispute"

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**New insights**
- **Dhp 183 for A5.** The verse summing up the teaching of the buddhas (avoid evil, cultivate the good, purify the mind) is a non-theist witness to Aquinas's first principle of practical reason, independent of Aristotle.
- **Buddhaghosa's grading confirms W1(2).** The most rigorous non-theist line against killing (pārājika from conception, at any number, at any request) still, in its commentaries, grades the weight of a killing by the victim's virtue. Holding the line equally is not the same as grounding an equal worth. The text could use this, stated fairly, as evidence that equal worth needs more than an equal rule.
- **SN 12.20 for W2.** "Whether Tathāgatas arise or not, this lawfulness stands" (paraphrase) is the Buddhist's own form of "the order needs no mind." Citing it would show the text met the rival's best formulation.
- **Brahmajāla (DN 1) as a genealogy of theism.** The Buddha's story of the first-born Brahmā who mistakes himself for creator is a debunking genealogy. The text's rule for genealogies answers it exactly as it answers Nietzsche, and naming it would show that the rule cuts both ways.
- **The Vinaya case of compassionate killing** (monks who praised death to a suffering monk) is a 2,400-year-old witness for A6's hardest claim, that compassion and consent do not make it right. It may be stronger than the bare citation of the third pārājika.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "a real reason, not a count, but drawn from the act and from his chance, not from a worth he was given." (Fair, and exactly right.)
- "a ripening is owed to no one and cannot forgive"
- "Where its buddhas know and love, they love beings already there; they gave no one being."
- "both say grasping must die, and they part over what is left, and over whether it is good that we exist at all."
- "each tradition can read the other's summit as a stage on its own road, and neither reading is evidence."
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."

## 8. Score

**8.5 / 10.** The text describes the Dhamma with rare accuracy, concedes what it should (we bear evil more easily; our line is real), and parts from us at the true junctions (self, creation, whether existence is good). It needs only small repairs, chiefly the Pure Land overgeneralization. **Honest ceiling for any theistic case against my school: about 9**, because the decisive disputes (no-self, the coherence of a single uncaused Source, whether it is good that we are) are standoffs at parity. No moral argument can make a Buddhist who holds the line on his own grounds give it up.
