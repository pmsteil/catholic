# Blind review: secular neo-Aristotelian / virtue ethics

## 1. School and voice

I speak for the secular revival of Aristotelian ethical naturalism. It began with Anscombe's 1958 advice to drop the "law conception" of *ought* and talk instead about what is unjust, untruthful or cruel. Foot (*Natural Goodness*), Thompson (*Life and Action*; "What is it to wrong someone?"), Hursthouse (*On Virtue Ethics*), McDowell ("Two Sorts of Naturalism"; *Mind and World*) and Nussbaum (*The Fragility of Goodness*; "Transcending Humanity"; *Frontiers of Justice*) developed it. On our view, goodness and defect in a living thing are set by its life-form. Human beings are social, rational animals, and the virtues are what that life-form needs. Practical wisdom judges particular cases from inside a formed second nature, and there is no standpoint outside the life of reason from which to validate it. The good life is finite, vulnerable to fortune, and worth having anyway. We are not debunkers or error theorists. We hold most of A, and we deny that any of it needs a Mind behind it. Positions are paraphrased throughout. Attributions to secular thinkers marked "verify" should be checked against the source before use.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** Justice is a virtue of the will: a settled disposition to give each person what is due (Foot; Aristotle *NE* V). What is due comes from the relational ("bipolar") structure that holds among members of a rational, social life-form, where A owes something to B (Thompson's account of wronging, verify). Trust, fidelity, not injuring, and fair shares are what such creatures need from one another to live well at all. **Holds.** It takes the life-form and its relational structure as basic. That is a stopping point, not a borrowing.

**2. A hard dilemma.** A competent patient is dying in pain he cannot bear. His life has stopped being a good to him, and he asks his physician to help him die. On Foot's line in "Euthanasia" (1977, verify): justice guards a life for the sake of the person living it, so he may waive the protection. Charity aims at his good, and here that may favour helping him. Non-voluntary killing is ruled out by justice, and whether to legalize the practice is a separate, prudential question. Hursthouse adds that where every option is bad, the virtuous agent still acts, carries a "remainder" of regret, and the life is marred. We use honesty, justice, charity and courage, but in Aristotle's sense, which is older than the book's definitions, not the covenantal sense. **Coherent.** It **breaks** against the book at exactly the point the book marks as its own claim (A6): we do not hold the line against a person's own request. We also have no decision procedure, only practical wisdom, and we accept that some dilemmas are tragic.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** Every human being bears the rational life-form *whole*. A substance's nature is not had more or less (Aristotle, *Categories* 5, 3b33–4a9), so the newborn, the dying, the severely disabled and the enemy are all inside the circle of justice as fully as the philosopher. Their unequal flourishing does not make their standing unequal. **Holds** for the newborn, the disabled and the enemy. Aristotle's own exclusions (natural slaves, *Politics* I; the deformed infant, *Politics* VII.16) we disown as bad practical wisdom about our own form, much as A4 admits Christians were slow on slavery. **Breaks** in three places:
- "Unconditional" fails against the person's own request.
- Some of us, drawing the line of a human life at its capabilities, deny that permanent unconsciousness is still a human life (Nussbaum, verify).
- *Why* bearing this form gives a worth that no total outweighs is, for us, a stopping point.

**4. End of flourishing.** The end is eudaimonia: rational activity in accordance with virtue over a complete life, with friends, in a political community. Nussbaum's central capabilities spell out its material conditions. The good is internal to human life, and it ends with that life. **Coherent.** It **breaks** at two points. For the child who will never reason, we can name her share in human goods (being held, comfort, relationship), but nothing in our account offers her the end her form is for. For the victim who is dead, we have no repair, only memory.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1: equal worth and the end (C, "The flourishing of our kind").**
- *Attack.* The row says that, by our end, the child who will never reason "falls short of the end as you need not, so her equal worth must come from justice, taken as given, and not from the end." That is a non sequitur, and it is not what our best defenders say. We ground her equal standing in her *form*, which she has whole. Falling short of the end affects the life she can live, not what she is. The text draws this same line for itself: the dignity of what she is versus the dignity of a good life (DI 7, 15, 22), and "the saints are higher than I am in how well they love, never in what they are worth" (A4). The godless-realism row even credits realists with grounding worth "in the essence of the kind, much as we ground it in what she is." The school whose whole theory is the essence of the kind gets the harsher verdict.
- *Defense.* On the Catholic view the end is a *call*. It does not wait on her powers and is offered to her as a gift (CCC 1722), so she is "for" something she can actually be given. On the Aristotelian view she is for an end she cannot reach, a defective specimen of her form. Her equality then rests on the form alone, and the form by itself does not say why a frustrated instance counts equally.
- *Rejoinder.* The Church also calls her lack a privation of a due good (Chapter 1's account of evil), so "defect" talk is shared. The end is equally *normative* for every bearer on both views. Its *attainment* is unequal on both, and for the unbaptized child who dies the Church only *hopes* it is given (CCC 1261). Attainability grounds worth on neither view. What really differs is whether the end is offered to her, and that is a difference in **hope**, not in the ground of worth.
- **Failed as written; Held with repair** (Repair R1). This is the main finding.

**Exchange 2: "no order wants any one of them" (W1(2)).**
- *Attack.* Worth does not need to be wanted. The text rejects care ethics because it can only say the unwanted infant "ought to have been wanted," and it says our love "answers to a worth already there." Then "wanted by God" cannot be what makes her worth hers; at most it explains why she exists. If her worth lies in what she is, the Aristotelian already has it.
- *Defense.* The text does not equate worth with being wanted. It says worth follows what she is, and that her being is a gift. "Willed for itself" (GS 24) does real work: it explains why the individual is not *for* the species or the total. Aristotle's own biology makes individuals the means by which a kind shares in the eternal through reproduction (*De Anima* II.4, 415a26–b7). Without a will, a form is a pattern, and its instances serve its life-cycle.
- *Rejoinder.* For a *rational* life-form, Foot and Thompson hold that the good in question is the individual's own life, not the perpetuation of the kind. A creature that can wrong and be wronged exists as an end in that relational structure. We take this as part of the rational form, a stopping point. The text grants stopping points everywhere else.
- **Held with repair.** The witness stands as a claim about *why there is* such a kind and why each member exists for her own sake. It does not show a gap in *her worth* on our view (Repair R2). The *De Anima* point is a real gain for the book against Aristotle himself, though not against his modern heirs.

**Exchange 3: "accident, or order, or wisdom" and Darwin's bees (W2).**
- *Attack.* "Accident" mixes up contingent origin with lack of real normativity. Natural goodness is not fitness (Foot, *Natural Goodness* ch. 2): Aristotelian categoricals describe a form's way of life, not what is selected for. Darwin's bees are harmless. Rational bees would have bee-goods, and the formal virtues of any rational life-form (justice, honesty, practical wisdom) would hold for them too. The "core of the law" that holds for any reasoning, suffering creature is a truth about rational life as such, and that needs no Mind. Asking why the form of a rational animal grasps necessary truths is like asking why reason reasons. It asks for a view from sideways-on, and there is none (McDowell).
- *Defense.* The text already grants both answers as "real answers" and calls its own position "a judgment about where explanation may stop, and the rivals do not share it." What it leaves standing is *one* fact: that there is a form whose starting points are truly good and which reads truths that hold for every mind.
- *Rejoinder.* That is accurate and fair.
- **Held.** An honest standoff at parity.

**Exchange 4: inalienability and euthanasia (A6; W1(2); the end of the C row).**
- *Attack.* The trust argument (life "held in trust" from its "Lord") needs a Lord. Without one, the line does not hold against the person's own request, and Foot's reasoning stands. The row's reply ("his life does not stop being a good to him") is an assertion without a reason.
- *Defense.* Life is not only the condition of a person's other goods. It is itself a good of the one who lives it, the object of the first natural inclination (ST I-II q.94 a.2). Aiming at its end turns against "the just love of self" (CCC 2281), which is a natural-law argument, not one from revelation. And the text says openly that these are "this book's claims," argued "on its own premises, not shared ones."
- *Rejoinder.* Whether life is a basic good or an enabling one is exactly what Foot disputes. It is an honest standoff, and the text labels it as one.
- **Held** (the text admits the limit). Repair R6 adds the reason, so the row does not read as a bare assertion.

**Exchange 5: the debt to the dead and hylomorphism (W1(1)).**
- *Attack.* (a) On the hylomorphism that Aquinas shares with us, a form of a living body does not survive apart from it, and Aquinas himself wrote "anima mea non est ego" (*Super I Cor.* 15, lect. 2, verify). So "she is still someone" between death and resurrection strains against the book's own philosophy. (b) More basically, an obligation can be truly owed without being dischargeable. Irreparable wrong and a lasting remainder of regret are part of the moral truth (Hursthouse; Nussbaum), so guilt's wish for her pardon does not point beyond itself.
- *Defense.* (a) The Church teaches that after death a spiritual element continues and subsists, with consciousness and will, so that the "human self" subsists without its body until the resurrection (CDF, *Letter on Certain Questions concerning Eschatology*, 1979). That is exactly what the text claims. (b) The text offers W1(1) only as a clue that "weighs as much as the reader finds that a debt truly owed to her is one she must be able, one day, to receive." It also admits that on its own road what the unrepentant owes "is never received."
- *Rejoinder.* (a) is answered on Catholic terms, and secular Aristotelians simply deny survival. (b) is a principled reason to give the clue little weight, which the text allows.
- **Held** (as a clue). Repair R6b makes the C row name the remainder view.

**Exchange 6: the longing and friendship with a god (W3).**
- *Attack.* Two Aristotelian objections to the claim that the answer "fits." First, Aristotle held that where one party is as far above the other as a god, friendship lapses (*NE* VIII.7, 1158b33–1159a5), so communion with God is the one love the longing cannot get. Second, Nussbaum argues that much of what we love in a human life we love *because* it is finite, so the wish to be kept forever is divided against itself ("Transcending Humanity," verify).
- *Defense.* Aquinas answered Aristotle directly: charity is friendship founded on God communicating His own happiness to us (ST II-II q.23 a.1), and Christ crosses the distance ("I have called you friends," John 15:15, RSV-CE). Against Nussbaum, the text already says the hope "is not more of the same years," that "we shall all be changed," and that the risen Christ kept His wounds. What a finite love lived through is kept, not erased.
- *Rejoinder.* Both answers are good, but the text does not raise either objection, and the "fit" claim reads as unopposed when it is contested.
- **Held with repair** (Repair R7). Minor.

**Exchange 7: evil and practical wisdom (the section on evil, "Second").**
- *Attack.* The text says "we know enough of goodness to know what a good God would value," and then that not seeing how each evil is answered is "weak evidence." A person of practical wisdom judges from particulars. If a human guardian permitted a hundredth of this, we would not suspend judgment.
- *Defense.* The text separates the *particular* answer (where our ignorance is to be expected if the good is finished in eternal life) from the *pattern*, where it concedes "the evidence is not weak." It also keeps both Job 38 and the claim that "reason may fairly put that question."
- *Rejoinder.* The distinction is fair, and the concession is real.
- **Held.** One omission: the Aristotelian, who expects no one to have intended the world, also carries this weight more easily, as tragedy (Repair R8).

**Exchange 8: Anscombe's "sense" (W1 frame).**
- *Attack.* The text glosses "the sense Anscombe meant" as "wrong of necessity, not up to anyone." Her sense was the *law* sense, which she thought had no content without a lawgiver. Her alternative was our school.
- *Defense.* The gloss names what every rival that keeps the words must keep, and the text reports accurately that she did not answer the question with God.
- *Rejoinder.* That is fair, but the text leaves out that a whole tradition took her advice. Saying so is more accurate and helps the book: it can then show the three places where that tradition, by its own admission, stops (the dead, the inalienable, why each person is willed for herself).
- **Held with repair** (Repair R4). Minor.

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**Serious: the C row misstates the Aristotelian ground of equal worth, and the comparison against my school depends on it.** The row says the child who will never reason "falls short of the end as you need not, so her equal worth must come from justice, taken as given, and not from the end."
- *Valid.* The best neo-Aristotelian grounds equal standing in the *form*, which is had whole, not in reaching the end. Falling short of the end bears only on the life she lives.
- *Premises at least as plausible as the text's.* The text draws the identical line for itself (ontological dignity versus the dignity of a good life, DI 7, 15, 22; "never in what they are worth"). It credits the realist with grounding worth "in the essence of the kind, much as we ground it in what she is."
- *No answer at parity in the text as written.* The defense that "she can be given her end" changes the *hope*, not the *ground*, and for the unbaptized dying infant the Church only hopes (CCC 1261).

So the text overstates its advantage over my school at the place where it presses hardest, the equality of the child who will never reason. This does not overturn the case. The honest difference that remains, whether the form was *given and willed* and whether the end is *offered* to her, is real and is at parity. But the row must be rewritten (R1, with R2 and the "A God who is Love" adjustment).

No Fatal objection stands.

**Minor**
- W1 frame parenthetical: "though every sin offends God, A7" reads as if A7 established that every sin offends God (R3).
- The Anscombe gloss leaves out that her proposal *is* the neo-Aristotelian programme (R4).
- A5: "the truth about God included" is accurate to Aquinas but will read to a non-theist as loaded unless glossed (R5).
- C row: the euthanasia reply is a bare assertion; give the reason (R6). "No one left to receive" should name the remainder view (R6b).
- W3: Aristotle's denial that one can be friends with a god (*NE* VIII.7) and Nussbaum's defence of loving finitude go unanswered, though the text has the materials to answer them (R7).
- Evil: the Aristotelian is missing from the list of rivals who bear the weight more easily (R8).
- Conclusion: the closing appeal is shared ground with us and the realist, and the text should say where the parting actually lies (R9).
- "A God who is Love": "why there is a kind whose worth does not follow ability" can be read as claiming that rivals lack such a kind; clarify it as *why there is* one.

**Misstatements of my school**
- The equal-worth charge in the C row, as above.
- Otherwise the text is accurate: Foot on the magistrate and the mob; Foot on euthanasia; Aristotle on deformed infants; both of our answers on the "accident" question; our place among those who say pardon must be asked of the victim.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
1. Where explanation may stop: our rational form and its relational structure, or one Source of law and mind (W2).
2. Whether life is a basic good of the one who lives it or an enabling good, which decides euthanasia at a person's request (A6 / W1(2)).
3. Whether a truly owed debt must one day be receivable, or whether irreparable wrong is part of moral truth (W1(1)).
4. Whether the individual rational creature's being for herself needs a will behind it (W1(2)).
5. Whether the longing to be kept fits a Person or is divided by our love of finitude (W3).

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1 (C, "The flourishing of our kind").**
- Old: "Its best defenders can say that the child who will never reason has your nature and is measured by your end, and is owed our care and our justice; but by that measure she falls short of the end as you need not, so her equal worth must come from justice, taken as given, and not from the end. The end the Church names surpasses every human power and comes as a free gift (CCC 1722), so strength and mind set no one's end (W1(2))."
- New: "Its best defenders can say that the child who will never reason has your nature, and has it whole, for a nature is not had by degrees; that she is measured by your end; and that she is owed our care and our justice. So her equal worth follows her form, much as this book says it follows what she is, and what falls short is the life she can live, not what she is. Where we part is over why there is such a form at all (W2), and over the end itself: on its account she will never reach the end her form is for, and no one offers it to her. The end the Church names surpasses every human power and comes as a free gift (CCC 1722), so strength and mind set no one's end, though for the child who dies unbaptized the Church can only hope that it is given (CCC 1261; W1(2))."

**R2 (W1(2)).**
- Old: "If her worth lies in what she is, then it is a gift exactly as far as her being is a gift, and here the moral question and the question of creation meet."
- New: "If her worth lies in what she is, then it is a gift exactly as far as her being is a gift, and here the moral question and the question of creation meet. (The Aristotelian and the realist agree that her worth lies in what she is; they deny only that her being is a gift. So the difference here is not in how much she is worth, but in whether anyone willed her, and whether she is for herself or for her kind, as Aristotle held that each living thing shares in what lasts only through its offspring, *De Anima* II.4.)"

**R3 (W1 frame, closing parenthetical).**
- Old: "(Not every *ought* wrongs another human person when it is broken, though every sin offends God, A7; these two rest on the wrongs done to a person.)"
- New: "(Not every *ought* wrongs another human person when it is broken: the duty to the truth (A7) is one that need not, though every sin offends God. These two rest on the wrongs done to a person.)"

**R4 (W1 frame, Anscombe).**
- Old: "She did not answer that question with God; she proposed setting the words aside."
- New: "She did not answer that question with God; she proposed setting the words aside and speaking instead of what is unjust, untruthful or cruel, which is the road the modern Aristotelians took."

**R5 (A5).**
- Old: "knowing the truth, the truth about God included;"
- New: "knowing the truth, including, he adds, the truth about God, which the unbeliever too may seek, if only to learn whether there is a God;"

**R6 (C, "The flourishing of our kind," last sentence).**
- Old: "the Church answers that his life does not stop being a good to him, and that he holds it in trust rather than owning it (CCC 2258, 2280; this book's claim, A6)."
- New: "the Church answers that his life does not stop being a good to him, for life is not only the condition of his other goods but is itself a good of the one who lives it, the first his nature inclines him to keep (ST I-II q.94 a.2; CCC 2281), and that he holds it in trust rather than owning it (CCC 2258, 2280; this book's claim, A6)."

**R6b (same row).**
- Old: "When the one we wronged is gone, it has no one left to receive what we owe."
- New: "When the one we wronged is gone, it has no one left to receive what we owe, and many of its defenders hold that such a debt is rightly carried, unpaid, as lasting regret (W1(1))."

**R7 (W3, after "The risen Christ kept His wounds (John 20:27): what a finite love lived through is not erased but kept, and healed.")**
- Insert: "Two Aristotelian objections meet the longing here: that much of what we love in a human life we love because it ends, and that between a man and a god, as Aristotle held, the distance is too great for friendship (*Nicomachean Ethics* VIII.7). To the first, the hope is not the end of change but the keeping of the one who changed; to the second, St. Thomas answers that charity is a friendship God founds by sharing His own happiness with us (ST II-II q.23 a.1), and Christ crossed the distance: "I have called you friends" (John 15:15)."

**R8 (Evil, "Some rivals bear this whole weight more easily than we do.")**
- Insert after the Nietzsche sentence: "The Aristotelian, who expects no one to have intended the world, can call what befalls her tragic and owe no one an account; what he cannot offer her is anything beyond the life she had."

**R9 (Conclusion).**
- Old: "and then, this book answers, it follows what she is, and she is that as fully as you are."
- New: "and then it follows what she is, and she is that as fully as you are. Many who do not believe in God say so too; this book adds that what she is was given to her, by One who willed her for her own sake (W1(2))."

- Old: "why there is a kind whose worth does not follow ability and an end, given to each, against which that worth is equal (W1(2))"

## 7. New insights; lines worth keeping

**New insights**
- **Aristotle's own point that substance does not admit of more and less (*Categories* 5)** gives the book a classical ally for "the dignity of what she is." The book should claim it and then mark where it parts from us (gift, willing, end offered), not deny that we have it.
- **The book could present my school as the rival that took Anscombe's advice.** That framing makes the comparison exact. By its own admission the tradition stops in three places: the dead (no one to receive), the inalienable (no reason the line holds against request), and the person for her own sake (a stopping point in the rational form). These are W1(1) and W1(2) already, now aimed precisely.
- **Aristotle's biology (*De Anima* II.4: individuals share in what lasts through their offspring) is the sharpest secular text for W1(2)'s "not for the species."** GS 24 reads as a direct answer to it. It wins against Aristotle; against Foot and Thompson it reaches parity.
- **Thompson's account of wronging** is a strong secular confirmation of A2: wronging is relational and cannot be reduced to making the world worse.
- **Aristotle's denial that one can be friends with a god (*NE* VIII.7) and Aquinas's reply (ST II-II q.23 a.1)** are a ready-made bridge to Chapter 10 on communion. A love that binds into communion must cross a distance that Aristotle thought could not be crossed.
- **Nussbaum's case for loving finitude** is the strongest secular objection to W3, and the text already has the right reply (the wounds kept). It just needs to say what it is replying to.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent."
- "Shame that looks only at itself says I am less; guilt says I wronged her; only guilt asks her pardon."
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."
- "Equal worth is not equal duty … Nor is it equal holiness." It is also the text's best tool against its own overstatement in the Aristotelian row.
- "The rebel can take her side; he cannot keep her."

## 8. Score

**7 / 10.** The text is honest about every stopping point, but against my school it claims one decisive advantage on equal worth that it does not have. With R1 and R2 the score rises to 8, because what remains (the dead, the inalienable, whether a Giver willed her) is real and fairly argued.

**Honest ceiling for any theistic case against this school: 8 / 10.** The deepest parting is over where explanation may stop, and argument cannot settle that.
