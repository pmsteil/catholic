# Blind review: the non-naturalist secular moral realist

## 1. School and voice

I speak for robust, non-naturalist, non-theist moral realism: Parfit's non-metaphysical cognitivism, Enoch's robust realism and his "third-factor" reply to debunkers, Shafer-Landau's and Wielenberg's brute necessary ethical facts, and Huemer's intuitionism with phenomenal conservatism. I add Fine on essence and grounding, the theorists of directed duties (Darwall, Wallace, Thompson), Kamm and Nagel on deontological constraints and inviolability, and McMahan (and Kagan) on the moral status of infants and the severely cognitively impaired. We agree with the book against error theory, relativism and reductive naturalism. We deny only that the necessary moral truths need to be held in, or come from, a Mind. Of all the rivals we are the nearest, and so the hardest place for the argument to find an advantage. (All paraphrases of these thinkers below are mine; verify.)

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** Justice is giving each what she is owed. What she is owed is fixed by necessary, non-natural normative truths about persons. A being with a point of view, interests and (actual or kind-typical) rational agency has claims against others: directed duties in Thompson's "bipolar" sense, and Wallace's relational normativity. These claims are not derived from totals or agreements. **Verdict: holds.** It is a first principle taken as necessary and needing nothing behind it. It does not borrow, but the significance of personhood is a brute stopping point, as the text says (C, "Necessary truths").

**2. A hard dilemma.** Take conjoined twins of the Jodie-and-Mary kind (England, 2000). Mary lives only through Jodie's heart and lungs, and without separation both will die. My principles: (a) Mary has no claim to the use of Jodie's body (Thomson's violinist); (b) the constraint is against killing as a means, not against every death foreseen (Kamm's Principle of Permissible Harm, paraphrased; verify); (c) separation takes away from Mary only what she had no right to, so it is permissible and arguably owed to Jodie. Mercy and self-gift as the book defines them play no role; "owed" plays the role justice plays everywhere. **Verdict: holds.** One soft spot I admit: my school has no principled account of when catastrophic numbers override a constraint. Nagel conceded that this may leave real moral dilemmas, and threshold deontologists set the threshold by intuition.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** Here my school divides, and I must be honest about it. (a) McMahan and Kagan deny full equality. Below the threshold of personhood, the wrong of killing is weighted by time-relative interests (McMahan), or the being has a high but lesser "modal person" status (Kagan). On that view the newborn is wronged less than the grown man. (b) Kind-essence realists (Liao's genetic basis for moral agency; Fine-style essence) say equal status lies in the essence of the human kind, so the infant, the dying and the severely disabled are equal. The enemy keeps that status, because desert changes what treatment is fitting, not what she is. **Verdict:** (a) breaks A4 openly. (b) holds, but it takes as brute why kind rather than capacity carries the status. This is the one place where the text has a real advantage over us (see 3, E4).

**4. Ultimate flourishing.** An objective list: knowledge, achievement, deep personal relationships, pleasure, virtue and the appreciation of beauty, pursued within the claims of others and with impartial concern. There is no single final end, only a plural set of goods, the best life being one rich in them and lived rightly toward others. **Verdict: holds as an account of a good life. It breaks on two points.** It has no one end against which the genius and the newborn who dies within the hour are "for the same thing," and the longing to be known and kept (W3) is answered by no one. The first point feeds back into challenge 3.

## 3. Debate highlights

**E1. The A heading sends the realist to the wrong place.**
*Attack.* The parenthesis in A says that W2 answers anyone who holds that the truths are real but need no Mind, by taking up "the claim that evolution, or a living nature's own form, has shown this." Realists do not claim that evolution shows anything about whether the truths need a Mind. We hold that necessary truths need nothing behind them, as the truths of logic need nothing (Parfit). We use evolution only to explain our access to them. The heading runs together the realist and the naturalist or Aristotelian.
*Defense.* The C row "Necessary truths that belong to no one" states the realist exactly as we state ourselves ("needs no dwelling at all"), and W2 also covers "a fact that needs no mind."
*Rejoinder.* Then the heading should match the C row. **Held with repair** (R1).

**E2. The fit between law and mind (W2): this is my strongest ground.**
*Attack.* W2 says: "The realist holds that this fit needs no further explanation." That misstates us. Enoch, Wielenberg and Shafer-Landau each give an explanation, a "third factor." Survival and reproduction aimed creatures like us, by and large, at things that are in fact good: avoiding pain, caring for the young, keeping faith with partners. Reason, holding itself to consistency, then carried the judgment further. Wielenberg adds that the same faculties that cause our belief in rights are what give us the rights. Only the necessary truths at the bottom of that explanation get no further explanation. That is exactly the status the book gives God's nature. The explanations therefore stop at parity. What is more, the observed pattern of moral knowledge is predicted at least as well by our account as by design: warm toward kin, late toward the slave and the stranger, and corrected slowly by consistency. The text itself concedes that "a world with no one behind it predicts it too."
*Defense.* Theism gives a common-cause explanation, and it covers logic and number as well as right: one Mind is the source of the truths and of the minds that read them. The third factor uses a normative premise (that what survival rewarded is good) and a contingent history. Darwin's bees show that history could have tuned us otherwise. So on realism some luck remains, and theism removes it. The text also does not pretend to compel: it calls this "a judgment about where explanation may stop, and the rivals do not share it."
*Rejoinder.* The luck is bounded. The goods that selection rewarded are robustly good across nearby histories, and the bees are a remote possibility. Theism removes the luck only by adding a hypothesis whose probability the text itself lowers in the section on evil. And using a normative premise inside the explanation is no more circular than the theist using God's goodness inside his. The two positions are at parity. The text says so in W2, but then counts W2 as "reach" in "A God who is Love" and does not class it among the clues in the conclusion. **Held, as an honest standoff, with repair** (R2, R6). As written the text calls W2 a judgment; it should not lean on it more heavily elsewhere.

**E3. Grounding the moral truths in God only relocates them (Morriston and Wielenberg, paraphrased; verify).**
*Attack.* "Not even God makes cruelty right ... because He is the Good." Then why is kindness good? If the answer is "because God's nature is kind," the explanation runs backwards, since we call God good because kindness is good. If kindness is good in itself, God is not the ground. In either case the theist keeps necessary normative facts he does not explain: that God's nature is the standard, and that a Creator has authority.
*Defense.* On the Thomist view (divine simplicity) goodness is not a property God has. God is subsistent Goodness, and every creaturely good is a participation in it. The realist also stops at necessity. The text claims neither fewer first principles nor the removal of brute fact: "not fewer first principles but one source," "one mystery, and a mystery that explains."
*Rejoinder.* Granted. The whole dispute between the book and my school then comes down to one question: is the Good personal? The text's reasons for "Someone" are W1(1) and W3, which it calls clues, and W1(2)'s "willed for its own sake." **Held.** The text concedes exactly what it should.

**E4. Equal dignity: call or nature (W1(2)). This is the text's real edge.**
*Attack.* The text grounds equal worth in "an equal call ... measured against one end that is God Himself." Either that call is natural, belonging to a nature in God's image that "reason can see" (DI 1), or it is supernatural, "a gift no nature could claim" (*Humani Generis* 26). If it is natural, my kind-essence realism is at parity: we too ground worth in what she is. If it is supernatural, reason cannot see it, the argument imports revealed content into what is meant to be a reasoned explanation, and dignity looks conferred rather than intrinsic.
*Defense.* The text holds both, in order. The image, a rational nature, is visible to reason and is the dignity reason recognizes. Reason can also see that such a nature is made for truth and good without limit, and so is ordered to God as its last end (CCC 27, 1718; ST I-II q.2 a.8). Revelation discloses the gratuitous fulfillment of that end and names it the "root reason" (GS 19). A converging case may weigh the specific hypothesis it actually believes. Most importantly, the realist kind-essence view must still say why kind rather than capacity confers status, and it takes that as given. The call gives a reason: worth is measured by the end, and the end is equal and does not wait on powers.
*Rejoinder.* "Worth is measured by one's end" is itself a normative principle taken as given. But it is one principle, and it does explain why the worth is equal, where my essence view only asserts equality. My school's other wing (McMahan, Kagan) gives equality up, and the conclusion's test ("which are you surer of ... wronged no less") presses that wing hard. Huemer's own method, phenomenal conservatism, tells us to trust the seeming. **Held.** It needs a repair for clarity only (R4): say which part reason sees and which part Revelation adds.

**E5. 1 Samuel 15 and inviolability: what actually protects her?**
*Attack.* W1 says the wrong is "wrong because of what it does to her." But W1(2) lets a divine command make it not unjust for a soldier to kill infants. What is done to her (terror, the sword, the loss of earthly life) is the same in either case. So on the book's account the absolute line is not grounded in her worth alone, but also in a jurisdictional fact: no human holds the right. Secular deontologists who ground the constraint in the victim's status as inviolable (Kamm; Nagel's victim-centered account of constraints; paraphrased, verify) protect her against every agent whatever. The text credits only Kant with that firmer protection.
*Defense.* The text says both things deliberately: "her own worth, which no total outweighs, and that no number of others has any right of their own to take her life." The act also differs with the agent. One who gives being and can give it back "calls a life home," while we "could only take it." The cost is paid openly and the wars stay "on the other side of the scale."
*Rejoinder.* That is coherent and honest. But by the text's own measure, secular inviolability realists protect her against every agent more firmly than the book does, so they deserve the credit it gives Kant. Fairness also requires adding that most of them let consent, or vast numbers, bend the line, which the book does not. **Held with repair** (R3).

**E6. Skeptical theism cuts both ways (Wielenberg's asymmetry worry, paraphrased; verify).**
*Attack.* The section on evil says that not seeing how each evil is answered is "weak evidence that it is not." If our view of God's reasons is that limited, then the text's predictions of fit, about what a loving God would do (keep her, will her for her own sake, not spend her), are weakened to the same degree.
*Defense.* The text does not plead general ignorance of God's reasons. It says "we know enough of goodness to know what a good God would value." Its claim is specific: the answer lies in a domain (eternal life) that cannot be observed from here, so its absence from view is expected. It also keeps the predictions about the dead and about longing as clues, and it says outright that the pattern of pain in nature "still weighs" and that "the evidence is not weak."
*Rejoinder.* That is fair. The auxiliary hypothesis of an eschatological answer comes from the same theism whose probability is being weighed, and being so specific lowers its prior. The text's line, "only the second lessens it as evidence," is the right place to stand. **Held.**

**E7. Must a debt be receivable to be owed? (W1(1))**
*Attack.* No principle says that what is owed must be receivable. The dead can be wronged as the people they were before death, which the text grants. The text's own case of the wrongdoer who refuses to the end gives a debt owed and never received.
*Defense.* The text's premise is weaker: the creditor must be *able* to receive the debt, not that it will be received. The witness is offered as a clue whose weight the reader sets ("it weighs as much as the reader finds ...").
*Rejoinder.* The weight is set honestly. By my lights it is small, since the good news that she is there is not evidence that she is. **Held as a clue**, which is what the text claims.

**E8. The conclusion's test does not separate us.**
*Attack.* I pass the conclusion's test without God. I am surer that the infant on the hillside was wronged no less, and I conclude that her worth "follows what she is." That is my view.
*Defense.* The text knows this. The test is addressed to "the one who gives up A4," and the case against the realist rests on W1(2) and W2, not on the test.
*Rejoinder.* Agreed. **Held.**

## 4. Ledger

- **A heading (the "offered as truths" principle and the list of deniers): Holds with repair.** The epistemic principle is phenomenal conservatism with a debunking clause, and my school accepts it. Its routing of the realist to "evolution ... has shown this" misstates us (R1). The phrase "whether or not it was true" is a poor condition for necessary truths, since the counterfactual is counterpossible; an explanatory-connection condition is better (R5). Mackie, Rorty, Singer and McMahan are fairly stated.
- **A1: Holds.** My school asserts it.
- **A2: Holds.** Wronging versus worsening, and the secret-and-the-letter case, are exactly the directed-duty point (Thompson, Wallace). It is well made.
- **A3: Holds.** Inalienability against gravest wrongs, and forgiveness that does not unmake the wrong: we agree.
- **A4: Holds.** It is stated as a truth whose full form some of my school deny. The parts that belong only to the book are fenced off properly.
- **A5: Holds.** Is and ought are kept apart. The question of ends is rightly left for W.
- **A6: Holds.** It is fair to the threshold and consent positions held by most of my school. The point about the alarm that tracks means versus side effect is fair both ways (the fairness rule is kept). The book's own claims are marked as such.
- **A7: Holds.** Robust epistemic normativity is our own companions-in-guilt argument (Cuneo). It counts against error theory, not against us.
- **W1 frame: Holds.** The Euthyphro answer is clean. The text grants that the realist agrees pardon is owed to the victim, which is accurate.
- **W1(1): Holds as a clue.** The realist's second road is stated fairly. The text classes the witness rightly ("a clue, not a proof").
- **W1(2): Holds with repair.** Its real edge over my school is the explanation of equal worth by an equal end (E4). It should say which part reason sees (R4) and should extend the Kant concession to secular inviolability theorists (R3).
- **W1 closing ("This Lawgiver is not a commander"): Holds.** It answers voluntarism. "A will that invents values can command, but it cannot bind" is a point realists share.
- **W2: Holds with repair.** It is an honest standoff, but its sentence about what the realist holds misstates us (R2). Its use of logic and number makes it partly an argument from reason, which is legitimate in a converging case.
- **W3 (longing; hiddenness): Holds.** It is rightly classed as a clue, and the hiddenness paragraph is candid. No claim is made against my school beyond fit.
- **C, Nothing really binds: Holds.** It is fair to Mackie and Street.
- **C, Feeling, refined: Holds.** It is fair to the quasi-realist and locates the difference correctly.
- **C, Our best words, widening: Holds.**
- **C, Reason's own law: Holds.** The cost against Kant is paid honestly. Its Kant citations are accurate as far as I can check (6:280, 5:131, 7:63, 6:337).
- **C, What we owe each other: Holds.** The limit of Darwall's second-personal standing for infants is fairly stated.
- **C, Care between persons: Holds.**

- **C, Necessary truths that belong to no one (my school): Holds.** It is the fairest statement of us in the text: the necessity "lives in the natures" (Fine), or "needs no dwelling at all" (Parfit), and "every account has a first principle, ours included." It could add the third-factor explanation and Kagan's modal personism, but nothing in it is false.

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text as written

**As written: NONE.**

No objection from my school is Fatal or Serious. On every point where the book and non-naturalist realism truly part (grounding, the fit, whether the Good is personal), the text either calls the matter a judgment the rivals do not share, or calls it a clue. Where it claims an edge (an equal end that explains equal worth; one source for law, minds and beings), the edge is real, and modest.

**Minor items:**
1. W2 misstates the realist as holding that the fit "needs no further explanation." We explain it with the third factor and stop only at necessary truths (R2).
2. The parenthesis in the A heading says realists claim evolution "has shown" that the truths need no Mind. We do not (R1).
3. The Kant concession on inviolability (W1(2); "A God who is Love") omits the secular realists who ground the line in the victim's own inviolable status (R3).
4. "A God who is Love" lists W2's fit as reach without noting that it is the book's judgment, unlike the careful wording in W2 itself (R6).
5. The debunking condition "whether or not it was true" is ill-suited to necessary truths (R5).
6. In W1(2) it is unclear which part of the equal-worth ground reason sees and which part Revelation adds (R4).

**Misstatements of my school:** items 1 and 2 above. Everything else, including the C row, is accurate, and the C row is very good.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**
- Whether necessary moral truths need a personal source, or nothing (E3).
- Whether the remaining luck in the fit between evolved minds and necessary truths calls for a common cause (E2).
- Whether a debt owed to the dead implies a creditor still able to receive it (E7).
- Whether being willed "for her own sake" adds anything to the grounding of worth, beyond giving assurance about how God will treat her.

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1 (A heading).**
Old: "(For the one who says these truths are real but need no Mind, W2 takes up the claim that evolution, or a living nature's own form, has shown this;"
New: "(For the one who says these truths are real but need no Mind, because necessary truths need nothing behind them, or because evolution, or a living nature's own form, accounts for our grasp of them, W2 takes up that claim;"

**R2 (W2).**
Old: "The realist holds that this fit needs no further explanation; this book holds that one Source of law and mind explains it, in logic and number as in right."
New: "The realist answers that the fit is explained: survival aimed us, by and large, at what is in fact good, and reason carried the judgment further; only the necessary truths at the bottom of that explanation, he holds, need nothing further, as this book says of God. This book holds that one Source of law and mind explains the fit more fully, in logic and number as in right, and leaves nothing to the luck of which goods survival happened to reward."

**R3 (W1(2) and "A God who is Love").**
Old (W1(2)): "This has a cost: Kant protects her here more firmly than this book does (C, Reason's own law)."
New: "This has a cost: Kant, and the secular realists who ground the line in her own inviolable status, protect her here more firmly than this book does (C, Reason's own law), though most of those realists let her consent, or vast numbers, bend it."
Old ("A God who is Love"): "and Kant holds it against every will, God's included, more firmly than this book (W1(2))."
New: "and Kant, with the realists who ground it in her own inviolable status, holds it against every will more firmly than this book (W1(2))."

**R4 (W1(2)).**
Old: "All share one origin and one calling (GS 29); an equal call, which does not wait on what anyone can do, explains an equal worth,"
New: "All share one origin and one calling (GS 29). Reason can see that a rational nature is made for truth and good without limit, and so for God (CCC 27, 1718); Revelation adds that He calls each one to share His own life. An equal call, which does not wait on what anyone can do, explains an equal worth,"

**R5 (A heading, and the same condition in the Power and rank row).**
Old (A): "or shows that the best account of what produced the belief would have produced it whether or not it was true."
New: "or shows that the best account of what produced the belief would have produced it with no connection to whether it was true."
Old (Power and rank): "defeats it only if that story would have produced it whether or not it was true;"
New: "defeats it only if that story would have produced it with no connection to whether it was true;"

**R6 ("A God who is Love").**
Old: "and why law and mind fit (W2)."
New: "and why law and mind fit (W2), where the realist judges that survival, reason and necessary truths suffice, and this book judges that they do not."

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights:**
- The text's epistemic rule in A is our own phenomenal conservatism (Huemer). The book can say outright that on method it stands with the realist against the debunker. That strengthens A4 and A7 at no cost.
- Against my school the whole case comes down to one sentence the book nearly writes: *the realist and this book agree about what is true and that it is necessary; they part over whether the Good is Someone.* Saying so plainly in the C row would make the comparison sharper and more honest at the same time.
- Kagan's "modal personism" is the strongest secular attempt to reach the severely disabled without kind-essence. It still gives up full equality. Naming it would show the reader that equal worth is precisely the point where secular realism strains, and why the "equal end" (W1(2)) is the book's real contribution.
- Cuneo's companions-in-guilt argument (epistemic and moral normativity stand or fall together) is the best academic form of A7 against the error theorist. The realist shares it, so it is a common weapon, not a theistic one.

**Lines worth keeping:**
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."
- "Every account has a first principle, ours included."
- "Its claim is reach, not count."
- "Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent."
- "Equal worth is not equal duty."
- "A law or an order gives a nature to every member of a kind; it does not want any one of them."
- "A will that invents values can command, but it cannot bind."
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it."

## 8. Score

**8 / 10.** Against my school the text is fair, accurately stated (with two small misstatements), and honest about its costs. It claims a real but modest edge on one point, why worth is equal, and calls the rest judgment or clue, which is exactly right. With R1 to R6 it would reach 9.

**Honest ceiling.** For a theistic case against non-naturalist realism, about 9 on robustness. On persuasive force the ceiling is a modest cumulative edge, never compulsion. The two views share necessary moral truths and differ only over whether those truths have a personal source, and reason can weigh that difference but cannot settle it.
