# Blind review: the ethics of care

## 1. School and voice

I speak for the ethics of care: Carol Gilligan's "different voice," Nel Noddings on caring as a relation completed in the one cared for, Annette Baier on trust, Sara Ruddick on the preservative love that is the first demand of mothering, Joan Tronto on care as a practice in phases and as a shared political work, Virginia Held on care as the most basic moral value and persons as relational, and Eva Feder Kittay, who wrote as the mother of a profoundly disabled daughter, Sesha, about dependency, *doulia* and the equality of being "some mother's child." I am a non-theist. The newborn and the severely disabled are not hard cases for my school. They are where we start. I will test the text where it claims to do better for them than we do. (All paraphrases of these thinkers below: verify.)

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

1. **Starting point for justice.** We start from the fact of dependency. Every person begins wholly in others' hands, and many end that way, so what anyone is owed begins with need met inside a relation. A need for care creates a responsibility in whoever is placed to meet it (Noddings's "I must," Tronto's responsibility). Those who give care are owed care in turn (Kittay's *doulia*). Justice is the fair sharing of caring work across a society (Tronto; Held puts justice inside care). **Verdict:** this holds for near relations and for the dependent. For the distant stranger it borrows the scope that justice frameworks supply, and Held admits this openly.
2. **A hard dilemma (concrete).** A daughter cares for her disabled son and for her mother, who has advanced dementia. She is breaking down. Should she move her mother into residential care? Our principles are attentiveness to what each person actually needs; responsibility as it actually falls; competence; responsiveness to how the care is received (Tronto's four phases); keeping both relations alive; and *doulia*, under which the carer's own collapse counts as a moral failure and not as a virtue. We would move the mother, keep visiting, and press the community to share the load. **Verdict:** this is mostly coherent. It borrows truthfulness, since attentiveness means seeing truly, and justice, in how the care is allocated. It refuses the book's "sacrifice" wherever sacrifice means the carer giving herself up. That is a real disagreement, not a borrowing (see section 7).
3. **What grounds equal, unconditional dignity.** Kittay's answer: each of us is some mother's child. Equality rests on connection, not on capacity. Dependency is universal and no one becomes a person except through others' care, so standing cannot rest on powers some people never have. **Verdict:** this holds for the newborn and the severely disabled, which is our strongest ground. It is strained for the enemy and the distant. At bottom it takes the worth of care, and the claim that dependency calls for care, as basic. The text's charge (C, care row) is right on that point.
4. **Ending point for flourishing.** What is ultimately good for humans is to live inside relations of good care, caring and being cared for, in a society organized so that needs are met and carers are not used up (Noddings's ethical ideal; Held; Tronto's caring democracy). There is no end beyond the relations themselves. **Verdict:** this is coherent. It breaks only where the text says it breaks: at the grave, where we can honor the dead but cannot keep them. That is a standoff we accept with open eyes. It is not a contradiction.

## 3. Debate highlights

**E1. "Care's shape."**
*Attack.* The care row says: "This book's answer has care's shape: the One who made her wanted her before anyone saw her." Care is a practice before it is a feeling (Tronto, Held). It has phases: attending, taking responsibility, giving care, and the cared-for's response. The God weighed here attends to the hillside infant and wills her good. But by the text's own account He gives her no care in this life ("He did not save her from the hillside"). By our measure that is caring-about without caregiving. Ruddick's preservative love is the first demand of care, and it is not met.
*Defense.* The text claims the shape and not the whole practice. It sends the failure to rescue to the evil section and does not hide it. God's love is creative, not responsive: it gives her being and worth (ST I q.20 a.2), so it comes before every human act of care rather than competing with them. He also gives care through persons ("a mother's love before a creed"; W3), and He keeps her beyond death.
*Rejoinder.* Then say that. The row borrows care's word for a claim about her origin and destiny. That is a different and real claim, but it is not care's shape in this life.
**Held with repair** (section 6, R1).

**E2. "Takes as given."**
*Attack.* The row says care's best defenders "take as given" why relation rather than powers gives full standing. That understates Kittay. She argues for it: every capacity-based equality fails, because capacities vary and some people lack them for life; dependency is universal; personhood itself is made in relations of care. So connection is the only ground that every human being has.
*Defense.* An argument by elimination plus a universal fact still ends somewhere. Kittay must still take it as basic that dependency calls for care, and that care is worth what it is. The text says the same of itself ("A God who is Love is also a stopping point") and claims reach, not fewer starting points.
*Rejoinder.* Agreed. This is parity, honestly stated, provided the row reports our argument before naming our stopping point.
**Held with repair** (R2). This is a standoff at parity, not a defect.

**E3. The hillside test in the conclusion.**
*Attack.* (a) The test separates people who deny A4 (Singer, McMahan) from people who hold it. It does not separate theism from care. Care passes it: she was wronged no less, because her worth follows what she is, a human child born into and owed human care. (b) The conclusion says her worth never followed "how much anyone cared for her, for no one did." On the text's own view, Someone did (W1(2): "every child was wanted by the One who made her"). The sentence contradicts the book, and it also blunts the book's jab at us ("not that anyone did want her").
*Defense.* (a) The text already limits the test to "the one who gives up A4," and its last step is marked "this book answers," not "it follows." (b) The text distinguishes two kinds of love: human love answers to a worth already there, while God's love gives that worth (W1(2); ST I q.20 a.2). "No one" in the conclusion means no human being.
*Rejoinder.* (a) Accepted. (b) Then the text should say "none of us." One word removes the contradiction.
**Held with repair** (R3).

**E4. The unbaptized hillside infant: "held by Him."**
*Attack.* The infant in the book's central example, the one the ancient world left on the hillside, was unbaptized. W3 says flatly that "she is held by Him." The next sentence says only that the Church hopes for her salvation (CCC 1257, 1261). The International Theological Commission (2007, non-magisterial) speaks of serious grounds for hope and leaves limbo open as a possible theological opinion. There is also an irony here that my school cannot let pass. The ordinary economy of salvation ties the infant's highest good to whether some human carer brought her to Baptism. That is exactly the dependence on human care that the book says God's wanting rises above.
*Defense.* God has bound salvation to Baptism but is not bound by His sacraments (CCC 1257). She is held in being, in His regard and in His providence in every case, and she is not lost in death (CCC 366). The text states the cost itself and, with the Church, calls it a summons to mission. On the text's view, her dignity does not wait on her salvation: DI 53 says each person, whatever his vulnerabilities, receives dignity from the sole fact of being willed and loved by God.
*Rejoinder.* The defense is sound. "Held" is the overreach: in care's language it means being taken up, and the book can promise that for her only as a hope. The irony about dependence on human care stands as an insight, not as a refutation, because the text names the cost.
**Held with repair** (R4).

**E5. The severely disabled: the image of God by proxy?**
*Attack.* The Catholic tradition places the image of God in intellect and will (CCC 1705). For Sesha that means her dignity comes from powers she will never use, held through membership in a kind. Kittay found that species-norm move suspect: it makes the disabled person a lesser instance of a rational kind. The book's definition of love says communion "wants to be known back." Read as knowing someone propositionally, that seems to leave her out in this life.
*Defense.* The text deliberately does not ground worth in powers held by kind. It grounds it in "a call that does not wait on her powers" (W1(2), C Kant row), and says "the genius and the newborn are for the same thing." CCC 357 grounds the image in being, not in exercise: "not just something, but someone." DI 24 says recognizing dignity cannot depend on a judgment about the person's ability to understand and act freely. DI 53 says the person with a disability receives dignity from being willed and loved by God. The end is a seeing that is given, and God can give it to one who will never reason. Canon law treats a person who never attains the use of reason as an infant in regard to Baptism (CIC can. 852 §2).
*Rejoinder.* That answer is stronger than the text currently cites, and it is fully consistent with Kittay. The text should cite DI 24 and 53 and can. 852 §2. From Noddings it should take the point that a caring relation is completed by the cared-for's response, which may be a gaze or a reaching, so "known back" need not mean known in words.
**Held with repair** (R5, R6).

**E6. 1 Samuel 15 and preservative love.**
*Attack.* The book's main evidence is the newborn's inviolable worth. Yet the book chooses to hold with Aquinas that the Lord of life could, without injustice, command that children be put to the sword, and it calls Him her "sovereign Master." Whatever "wanted her for her own sake" means, it cannot mean what any carer means, since no carer could order the child's death. The ground the book offers for her inviolability is revocable by the One who grounds it.
*Defense.* The text holds only the principle that God could so command. It gives no human being a right of his own over innocent life. It closes the door now (CCC 67), and Christ "took no one's" life. It does not lose her in death, so being called home is not, for her, a loss before God. And it pays the cost openly: "Kant protects her here more firmly than this book does." In practice, for every actual newborn, the book's line against human killing is firmer than most care ethicists' lines, because we generally resist absolutes.
*Rejoinder.* Honest and priced. But the price is paid in the Kant row and in W1(2), not in the care row, which is where it bites hardest: a care ethicist reading "the One who made her wanted her" should meet the cost in the same place.
**Held as a declared cost**, with the link added in R1. This is a standoff, not a defect.

**E7. Sacrifice and the carer.**
*Attack.* Gilligan showed how the ideal of the selfless woman served as a moral trap. The book's definition binds love to "sacrifice." A6 says real good "always asks something of the giver." W1(2) forbids withdrawing ordinary care, food and water included. All of that falls in practice on carers, mostly women, often alone. Where in the text is the carer's claim?
*Defense.* The care row itself cites *doulia* together with CCC 2208–2209: the family should care for the young, the old, the sick and the handicapped, and society must help and defend families. Catholic teaching does not require disproportionate means (CCC 2278). Rightly ordered love of self is a duty. A6's "asks something" is not "asks everything."
*Rejoinder.* The doctrine is sound, but it sits in the care row, while the duty sits in W1(2) with no one assigned to carry it. Name the shared carrying at the point where the duty is laid down.
**Held with repair** (R7).

**E8. Method: absolutes and stripped-down cases (A6).**
*Attack.* Gilligan and Held: transplant and riot cases, stripped of history and relation, are the justice orientation's tools. The "line at every number" belongs to that voice.
*Defense.* Care also sees the transplant case at once, and Baier explains why: the surgeon betrays the trust the patient placed in him and the trust on which medicine depends. The absolute protects most firmly the people least able to speak for themselves, who are care's own constituency. Under a rule that allows exceptions at large numbers, it is the dependent who get spent.
*Rejoinder.* Conceded for the newborn and the disabled. On end-of-life cases, where a person asks to die, we prefer responsiveness to the particular person over a rule. The text marks those claims as its own and not shared.
**Held.** Standoff at parity. (Fairness rule: I do not use the genealogy "rule morality is a masculine construct" against A6, because I would not let the text answer that care is simply the role assigned to women.)

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**"As written: NONE."**

Each of my strongest attacks (E1, E4, E6) is met by something the text already discloses or a distinction it already draws (creative versus responsive love; hope rather than promise; the declared cost against Kant). What remains is wording.

**Minor:**
- Conclusion, "nor how much anyone cared for her, for no one did," contradicts W1(2), where every child was wanted by the One who made her (R3).
- W3, "yet she is held by Him," says more than the Church's stated hope supports for the unbaptized infant (R4).
- Care row, "This book's answer has care's shape": true of care's first act, attention to this one for her own sake, not of caregiving in this life. The 1 Samuel 15 cost is paid elsewhere but not here (R1).
- W1(2) lays down the duty of ordinary care without naming who carries it (R7).
- W1(2) and the C Kant row: the strongest magisterial support for the severely disabled (DI 24, 53; CIC 852 §2) is not cited (R5).
- Evil section: the severely disabled life lived wholly without the power to choose is not named among those whom the free-will good does not serve (R8).

**Misstatements of my school (minor):**
- "a wrong is a betrayal of trust": Baier holds that many wrongs betray a trust and that trust is morally central, not that every wrong is such a betrayal.
- "its best defenders take as given": Kittay argues for connection-based equality from the universality of dependency and the failure of capacity-based grounds. What she takes as basic is narrower: that dependency calls for care, and the worth of care.
- The row lists Noddings with Kittay and Tronto as all reaching "the infant no one holds." Noddings's account grounds obligation in actual or potential relations, so it reaches her less easily. This over-credits us. It is not unfair.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**
- The final ground: we stop at the worth of care and the claim dependency makes; the text stops at a God whose love gives worth. Each takes something as basic. The text's claim is reach, which it rightly does not overstate.
- The grave: care honors the dead and cannot keep them. The text's third road is a clue, as it says.
- End of life: care prefers responsiveness to the particular person over absolutes. The text marks its line as its own.
- 1 Samuel 15: a declared cost.

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1 (C, Care between persons).**
Old: "This book's answer has care's shape: the One who made her wanted her before anyone saw her (W1(2)); that He did not save her from the hillside is weighed in the section on evil."
New: "This book's answer has the shape of care's first act, attention to this one for her own sake: the One who made her wanted her before anyone saw her (W1(2)). It does not have the shape of care's next act: He did not take her up from the hillside, and the Lord of life, this book holds, does no wrong in calling a life home (W1(2)). That is weighed in the section on evil, and what this book offers her there is not that she was taken up but that she is kept."

**R2 (C, Care between persons).**
Old: "Where it stops is where the contractualist stops: why that relation, and not her powers, gives her full and equal standing, its best defenders take as given, as they take the worth of care itself."
New: "Its best defenders argue that no one's standing can rest on powers some never have, since each of us began wholly dependent and became a person only through others' care. Where it stops is near where the contractualist stops: that dependency calls for care, and that care has the worth it has, they take as given, as every account takes something."

**R2b (same row).**
Old: "that a wrong is a betrayal of trust (A2)"
New: "that many wrongs betray a trust (A2; Annette Baier)"

**R3 (Conclusion).**
Old: "nor how much anyone cared for her, for no one did;"
New: "nor how much any of us cared for her, for none of us did;"

**R4 (W3, hiddenness).**
Old: "For the infant no one holds, there is no mother's love to carry His love to her; yet she is held by Him."
New: "For the infant no one holds, there is no mother's love to carry His love to her; yet she is not unwanted: He keeps her in being, wills her for her own sake, and does not lose her in death."

**R5 (W1(2)).**
Old: "a nature made in His image, which reason can see (DI 1),"
New: "a nature made in His image, which reason can see (DI 1), and which does not wait on her ability to understand or choose (DI 24; for the person with a disability, DI 53),"
And in the C Buddha row, after "(CCC 1250, 1261)", add: "; the Church counts one who never attains the use of reason as an infant in this (CIC can. 852 §2)".

**R6 (W3, hiddenness).**
Old: "A child is loved long before he can say who loves him."
New: "A child is loved long before he can say who loves him, and loves back, in a gaze or a reaching, long before he can say whom he loves."

**R7 (W1(2)).**
Old: "may not be withdrawn while it nourishes her without harm or intolerable suffering (CCC 2278–2279; *Samaritanus Bonus* V.3)."
New: "may not be withdrawn while it nourishes her without harm or intolerable suffering (CCC 2278–2279; *Samaritanus Bonus* V.3); and that care is owed her by all of us, not by one exhausted pair of hands alone, for those who care must be cared for too (CCC 2208–2209)."

**R8 (Evil, paragraph 2, under *Second*).**
Old: "and so does the child who dies of disease before she can choose, whom the good of her own freedom does not serve,"
New: "and so does the child who dies of disease before she can choose, and the one who lives a whole life without the power to choose, whose pain, where she suffers it, the good of her own freedom does not serve either (her life is not the weight; her suffering is),"

## 7. New insights; lines worth keeping

**Insights for the book**
- **Care is completed in the one cared for** (Noddings). The definition's "communion" need not mean knowing in words. A smile, a gaze or a reaching completes it. This answers in advance the charge that a love that "wants to be known back" leaves out Sesha or the infant (R6).
- **"Life-giving" disciplines "sacrifice."** On the book's own definition, a self-gift that destroys the carer's ability to go on giving fails the definition's "life-giving communion." Saying this guards the word "sacrifice" against the use Gilligan exposed, and it is fully Catholic (CCC 2208–2209; rightly ordered love of self). Chapter 9 may be the place for it.
- **The dependency irony.** The Church ties the unbaptized infant's ordinary path to her highest good to a human carer's act, and hopes beyond it. The book can make this a strength: God has made us the carriers of His care (CCC 848), so the hillside is a failure of the very hands He entrusted her to. This sharpens the evil section's line that His permission never excuses ours.
- **Absolutes protect the dependent.** Against my school's distrust of rules, the book's best reply is the one care should hear: when exceptions are allowed at large numbers, the person spent is almost always the one who cannot speak. The text implies this ("Without it, no innocent is safe"). It could say it of the newborn and the disabled by name.
- **DI 53 is the book's best sentence for the disabled.** It already says what W1(2) argues.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "Equal worth is not equal duty: we owe more to our own parents and children than to strangers, but no one is worth less."
- "Where instinct fails, for the infant no one is drawn to hold, we learn whether what we meant was worth or only attraction."
- "Measured against one end that is God Himself, the genius and the newborn are for the same thing."
- "Not every child is wanted by those who conceived her; every child was wanted by the One who made her, before anyone saw her."
- "Against the ethics of care, it is the one relation no one can refuse her."
- "The hands that pull a child from the fire are part of what He made real, so His permission never excuses ours."
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it."

## 8. Score

**8 / 10.** The text states my school accurately and generously, takes the newborn and the severely disabled as the center of its case, prices its hardest cost (1 Samuel 15) openly, and leaves only wording that overreaches in care's own vocabulary ("care's shape," "held," "no one did"). All of it is repairable without changing the argument.

**Honest ceiling for any theistic case against care ethics: 9.** Care's final ground, the worth of care known from inside the practice, is a stopping point at parity with the theist's. The best a theistic case can show is greater reach: why there is a kind whose worth does not follow ability, and why she is kept. It cannot show that care borrows its central claim.
