# Blind review: the UTILITARIAN school

## 1. School and voice

I speak for the utilitarian tradition at its strongest: Sidgwick's *Methods of Ethics* (rational benevolence as a self-evident axiom: from the point of view of the universe, no one's good counts for more than another's), Hare's two-level theory (*Moral Thinking*, 1981), Smart's unflinching act-utilitarianism, Hooker's rule-consequentialism (*Ideal Code, Real World*, 2000), and above all Katarzyna de Lazari-Radek and Peter Singer's *The Point of View of the Universe* (2014). That book is objectivist, non-naturalist and hedonist. It answers the evolutionary debunker *selectively*: it discounts the partial and deontological intuitions that survival would have produced whether or not they were true, and keeps impartial benevolence, which survival would not have taught. So I am no error theorist. I hold that morality is objective, that reason can know it, and that what it says is: make the world go as well as possible for every being that can fare well or badly, each counted equally. I do not need God for that. My quarrel with the text is over whether A2, A4 and A6, in their strongest forms, are data at all.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** Equal consideration of interests: each counts for one and none for more than one. What anyone is owed is that her well-being be weighed equally with everyone's, plus whatever rights and duties are fixed by the rules and dispositions whose general acceptance does most good (Hare's intuitive level; Hooker's code, which adds some priority for the worst off). *Holds, coherent, borrows nothing.* It **breaks** with "owed to her in her own right" as a basic fact. I deny that such a basic fact exists and explain the practice of directed claims by its usefulness. That is a bullet I bite, not a contradiction.

**2. A hard dilemma.** Pandemic triage: one ventilator, two patients. A is 35 with an 80% chance of surviving on it; B is 75 with 25%. I allocate by expected well-being: survival probability times the quality and length of life to be saved. That gives the ventilator to A. At the intuitive level I keep rules that protect trust: an open protocol, no covert decisions, honest disclosure to B's family, full palliative care for B, and no removing a patient who is already doing well. I justify each of these by its effects on welfare, not by truth, mercy or self-gift as the book defines them. *Holds.* It **strains** where the critics press hardest: quality-weighting can discount the disabled. I answer with a rule against disability discrimination, justified by error and trust costs. A disability advocate will say, fairly, that this protection is contingent.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** I **deny** it in the book's strong sense, that every human life has equal worth whatever its capacities. I affirm something I think is cleaner. Every sentient being's interests count equally, so the newborn's pain counts exactly as much as the professor's. The dying, the severely disabled and the enemy lose none of their claim to consideration. What differs is what each stands to lose, because how bad a death is depends on the future it ends. *Coherent, not borrowed.* It **breaks** with A4 as the text states it, openly. I can endorse "equal human dignity" as the best public rule, but only at the second level.

**4. The final good.** Well-being. On the hedonist view (Sidgwick; de Lazari-Radek and Singer) it is desirable consciousness; on Hooker's objective list it is pleasure, knowledge, friendship, achievement and autonomy. The guiding principle is rational benevolence, maximized in expectation and lived through two-level practice. *Holds.* It is **thinnest** at two points. First, Sidgwick's own "dualism of practical reason": he could not show from reason alone why my own good should give way to the universal good, and he thought the conflict could be closed only by something like a moral government of the world, which he could not prove (Methods, Concluding Chapter; verify). De Lazari-Radek and Singer close it by debunking egoism, and that move is disputed. Second, the final good is impersonal. In the end there is no one whose good is kept.

## 3. Debate highlights

**E1. The secret told to the stranger (A2; utilitarian row of C).**
*Attack.* The row says that, counting experiences, utilitarianism "finds nothing wrong" in the telling. That overstates our view. Hare and the hedonist both condemn the teller's disposition, and both endorse the intuitive-level rule that condemns the act. The letter-in-the-street contrast is also captured without directed duties: utilitarianism judges acts against their alternatives, and the wind has none. On a preference view, telling frustrates *her* preference, so the reason against telling is hers.
*Reply.* The row already grants that "counting wishes, it can blame the teller." Its point is narrower and accurate. Even where utilitarianism condemns the telling, her claim weighs as one input to the total, not as something owed to her. The two cases differ in that someone wronged her, not only in that someone could have done otherwise.
*Rejoinder.* Granted: directedness is exactly what we deny is basic, and the text describes that correctly. But "finds nothing wrong" is false of two-level utilitarianism. What the hedonist finds is no critical-level wrong in the isolated act.
*Verdict: Held with repair* (wording, §6 R4).

**E2. The debunking asymmetry against A6.**
*Attack.* This is our strongest weapon (Greene, "The Secret Joke of Kant's Soul" and *Moral Tribes*; Singer, "Ethics and Intuitions," 2005; de Lazari-Radek and Singer 2012 and 2014; all verify). The certainty that the surgeon may not kill one to save five comes from an alarm that sounds at harm planned as a means. That alarm would sound whether or not the means/side-effect difference matters morally. Impartial benevolence toward distant strangers is the one thing survival would not have taught. The text's own rule in A says a belief is defeated when "the best account of what produced the belief would have produced it whether or not it was true." The text's reply in A6 is that "an alarm that tracks that difference would sound just so if the difference were real." That misapplies its own rule, which asks whether the alarm would sound *whether or not* the difference is real.
*Reply.* The rest of A6 holds. (a) The debunker needs a normative premise: that the feature the alarm tracks, intending harm as a means, is morally irrelevant. That premise is the very point at issue (Berker, "The Normative Insignificance of Neuroscience," 2009; verify). (b) The text's cases (a secret hanging, prisoner experiments) are judged at leisure and at a distance. No personal-force alarm is sounding in them. (c) The argument comes back on its user (Kahane, "Evolutionary Debunking Arguments," 2011; verify). Impartial benevolence rests on the evaluative belief that suffering is bad, which is Street's own paradigm of a belief evolution would have produced. (d) Empirically, the so-called "utilitarian" verdict in sacrificial dilemmas tracks low empathy and antisocial traits, not impartial concern (Kahane et al., *Cognition* 2015; verify). (e) The fairness rule binds me. If I debunk the deontological intuition by its origin, I must let the text debunk benevolence by its origin. A6 already says "a history settles neither."
*Rejoinder.* The hedonist answers (c) by saying that pain's badness is known by acquaintance, in having it. The theist can answer in kind that the victim's worth is seen when we look at her ("We see it first in what it does to the child," W1). Parity. But the sentence in A6 is still the weak point of the text's reply and should state the real answer, (a) and (c).
*Verdict: Held with repair* (§6 R1).

**E3. "Her own worth, which no total outweighs" (W1(2)) and the side-effect asymmetry.**
*Attack.* The text holds that "side effects may be weighed" (A6). So the Church lets a total outweigh her life whenever her death is foreseen and not intended: proportionality in just war (CCC 2309, 2314), double effect (CCC 2263). Her worth is identical in the intended and the foreseen case. So her worth cannot be what explains why one case is absolute and the other is weighed. This is the paradox of deontology (Scheffler, *The Rejection of Consequentialism*; Nozick on the "utilitarianism of rights"; verify): if her inviolability matters so much, why may I not minimize the number of inviolable people who are violated?
*Reply.* The text has the resources, even if this sentence does not use them. A few lines earlier W1(2) says the human person is willed "for itself (GS 24): not for the species, not for the total, not for us." To aim at her death for others treats as existing for them someone who exists for her own sake. A death foreseen and not aimed at does not subordinate her to anyone. This is Warren Quinn's grounding of double effect in respect for persons ("Actions, Intentions, and Consequences," 1989; verify), and it fits the Thomist account of the moral object (VS 78–80). Kamm's inviolability argument meets the paradox: a world in which no one may be spent gives everyone, including the five, a higher status (verify).
*Rejoinder.* That answer is at parity, and it is a good one. But the sentence as written says "no total outweighs," and on the text's own view that is false of foreseen deaths. The reply works only if the sentence is reworded to say what it means.
*Verdict: Held with repair* (§6 R2).

**E4. Escalation: the vital conflict, where the line protects no one.**
*Attack.* Take the obstetric conflict where mother and child will both die unless the child is killed directly. The Holy Office answered that craniotomy may not safely be taught as licit (1884, 1889; verify). The USCCB doctrine committee judged the 2009 Phoenix case a direct abortion (2010; verify). Here the absolute imposes a death and saves no one. The child dies whatever is done. W1(2) says "what protects her is her own worth." In this case nothing protects her, so the stated rationale does no work.
*Reply.* The Church's line is not, at bottom, a rule for protecting outcomes. It forbids the will to aim at an innocent's death, because that turns the agent against the good of her life, however short. The text says this itself about the dying patient: "what the trust forbids is that her death be the thing aimed at, for that turns against the good of her own life" (W1(2); CCC 2281). Treatment aimed at the mother's own pathology, with the child's death foreseen, can fall under double effect. The tradition also debates how the act should be described (Rhonheimer, *Vital Conflicts in Medical Ethics*, 2009, a non-magisterial proposal; verify). And the text already concedes that "the absolute can cost lives, even many."
*Rejoinder.* I grant the reply is consistent: the line guards the shape of the will, not only her survival. But then the text should say so plainly. It should also put this case, a death with no beneficiary, on the scale beside "the absolute can cost lives." It is the purest form of the cost, and the reader will meet it from us first.
*Verdict: Held with repair* (§6 R2). It is a real cost, and the text's own premises meet it at parity.

**E5. The argument from marginal cases against A4 and the conclusion.**
*Attack.* A4 tests worth against attraction: "where instinct fails, for the infant no one is drawn to hold, we learn whether what we meant was worth or only attraction." Apply that test to species. Instinct favors our own kind. The strong A4 holds that an anencephalic infant has worth beyond price, while a chimpanzee who knows, plans and fears more has much less. That is exactly what favoritism toward our kind would produce whether or not it is true, so by the text's own rule in A it is debunked. The conclusion's question, "which are you surer of," is fair. My answer: I am surer that leaving her on the hillside is gravely wrong than of any argument against that. I am not surer that she was wronged "no less than a grown man, however differently one counts what each of them lost" than I am that how bad a killing is depends on what it takes. McMahan, no utilitarian, agrees with me.
*Reply.* The text never grounds worth in membership of a biological species. It grounds worth in "what she is," a rational nature had by kind, together with a call to communion that "does not wait on what anyone can do" (W1(2)). That is a principled line, not a prejudice, and it is not available to the naturalist. The Church does not deny that animals matter: causing them to suffer or die needlessly is contrary to human dignity (CCC 2416–2418). Most of all, the text claims no more than a weighing of certainties. It "cannot compel the one who gives up A4," and A4's "weight lies in how sure we are of it."
*Rejoinder.* That is an honest standoff, and the text has earned it by being modest. But the conclusion's list ("her strength, or her mind, or how much she can feel or want") leaves out my ground, the good her life would have held, so it looks as if every naturalist ground has been considered when mine has not. And A4 never faces the animal comparison. A utilitarian reader will raise it on page one.
*Verdict: Held* (standoff at parity). Two additions are recommended (§6 R7, R8).

**E6. Lying and A7 (the utilitarian row of C).**
*Attack.* The row says utilitarianism "must in principle favor the lie (A7)" and sets this against CCC 2485. But A7 is about *belief* and *inquiry*, not about lying to others. The two have been run together. Worse, the cost the row charges us with, lying to the murderer at the door, is one most non-Christian readers count as a cost to the Church's view, not to ours. The brief forbids premises with "Christian-only demands." This is one, presented as our cost.
*Reply.* CCC 2489 does let the truth be withheld from someone with no right to it, by silence or discretion, so the Church's absolute is less harsh than it sounds. The sentence is true as a description: we do favor the beneficial lie in principle.
*Rejoinder.* The description is true. Its placement is the fault: it is tied to the wrong item in A, and it charges as a shared cost something the book elsewhere labels as its own claim (A6's practice).
*Verdict: Held with repair* (§6 R5).

**E7. W2 and the utilitarian rationalist.**
*Attack.* W2 considers two realist answers and the debunker. It then says that what they all leave unexplained is "that the starting points survival rewarded were truly good." Our school does not rest on those starting points. We debunk most of them. We keep only two things: the felt badness of pain, known by having it, and reason's own seeing of impartiality. Reason came as a by-product of a mind selected for general reasoning, like the ability to do sums (de Lazari-Radek and Singer 2014, ch. 7; Parfit; verify). That is an explanation, not a refusal to explain.
*Reply.* The question W2 asks still stands: why do minds built for survival grasp necessary truths, in logic, number and morality, at all? "By-product" names the fact; it does not make it less surprising. And Sidgwick's dualism is on the text's side. The most careful utilitarian found that practical reason gives two final ends and could not show that they agree without something like a moral order of the world. That is W2's "this law holds together," and it came from our own founder.
*Rejoinder.* Parity on the first point. On Sidgwick, I concede the point and recommend the text use it.
*Verdict: Held with repair* (§6 R10; §7).

**E8. Theodicy as a consequentialist justification (evil section).**
*Attack.* CCC 324 and 412 say God permits evil to draw a greater good. That is a consequentialist justification for God. Meanwhile the text forbids us to reason that way, and condemns human acceptance of famines without remedy as a grave offense (CCC 2269). By the text's own standard, a bystander who could stop the famine at no cost and does not is guilty. Add hundreds of millions of years of animal pain, which the text admits "falls roughly where survival would put it."
*Reply.* (a) Permitting is not doing. Weighing side effects is something the text allows to humans too ("side effects may be weighed"). God wills the order and never the loss for its own sake (ST I q.19 a.9). So the structure is consistent with A6. (b) The text refuses aggregation in God's case as well: "she is not spent as a mere means for it, and the good hoped for is offered to her as well." That is not a utilitarian sum. It is a promise that the victim's own good will be restored. (c) The text says the positive duty binds us ("His permission never excuses ours"), admits that God "is answerable to no creature's court" is no proof, and calls the evidence from the pattern of pain "not weak."
*Rejoinder.* The text has not hidden the weight, so I cannot charge it with hiding anything. My verdict on the overall weighing differs from its verdict. That is a standoff the text itself foresees ("an honest reader may weigh it differently").
*Verdict: Held* (standoff at parity on the overall weighing).

## 4. Ledger

- **A heading (form, the rule for trusting beliefs, the list of deniers):** Holds. The debunking rule is the right one, and I use it. "Singer denies parts of A2 through A7" and "McMahan, no utilitarian" are accurate.
- **A1:** Holds. Utilitarians affirm it: torturing a child for fun is wrong whoever approves, because of what it does to her.
- **A2:** Holds as a contested datum. We deny that wronging is basic and not reducible to worsening; the secret case is a fair test, and we bite it. Standoff.
- **A3:** Holds. We largely agree. Mill denies that a man may sell himself into slavery (*On Liberty* V), a useful non-theist witness (§6 R9).
- **A4:** Holds with repair. Its strong form is the real battleground and is fairly offered as "this book's claim" where it goes beyond the shared core. It should face the animal comparison (R7).
- **A5:** Holds. The hedonist agrees that capacity and desire do not settle the ought. That pain is bad is a normative truth, not a want.
- **A6:** Holds with repair. The "alarm" sentence misapplies the text's own rule (R1). Otherwise the section is candid: it brackets the book's claims and names the absolute's cost.
- **A7:** Holds. We treat the evidence norm as constitutive of belief, plus a policy with very high utility, not a categorical moral duty. Standoff.
- **W1 frame (Anscombe, wrong of necessity, offense against God):** Holds. Anscombe is accurately described. SCG III.122 is correctly used to block "a second injury beside hers."
- **W1(1) (the debt still owed):** Holds as a clue. We take the first road (hedonist) or the second (Hare's preferences of the dead). The text does not claim more than fit.
- **W1(2) (a worth no deed confers):** Holds with repair. The sentence "which no total outweighs" contradicts the text's own allowance for weighed side effects (R2). The wars of 1 Samuel 15 are paid for openly. "That she can suffer ... does not explain the line, for others can always suffer more" is accurate against us.
- **W1 closing (not a commander who picks rules):** Holds. "Even a promise binds only under a law no promiser invented" is something utilitarians accept.
- **W2:** Holds with repair. It omits the third realist answer, utilitarian rationalism with reason as a by-product (R10). Its central question survives at parity.
- **W3 (communion; replaceability):** Holds as a clue. The replaceability charge is accurate for the total view.
- **W3 second paragraph (hiddenness):** Holds. It is candid ("part of an answer"). CCC 1257/1261 and GS 22 are used accurately.
- **C heading:** Holds.

The repairs: Hooker's code is not simply "the total," since he gives priority to the worst off (R3); "finds nothing wrong" overstates (R4); the lying/A7 mismatch and the Christian-only cost (R5); it leaves out the school's objectivist metaethics and selective debunking (R6).

- **Evil, rivals bear it more easily:** Holds. Utilitarians also expect a world indifferent to justice.
- **Evil, free will, Mary, Leo, and "setting, not explanation":** Holds. Its admission that free will does not explain permission (since God can bring a free will to its yes) is honest.
- **Evil, First (privation):** Holds at parity. Hedonists hold that pain is a positive bad, not a felt loss (consider neuropathic pain with no damage). The text uses privation only against a dark god and says it does not rule out indifference.

- **Evil, the protester's invitation:** Holds. A utilitarian protester stands on A1 and on suffering's badness, not on A2–A4. The invitation reaches him less than it claims, but it does not misstate him.
- **Conclusion:** Holds with repair. The exposure test is the right test and is put without overclaiming. Its list of grounds leaves out ours (R8).

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**As written: NONE.**

I found no Fatal or Serious objection. Each of the strongest utilitarian attacks (selective debunking of A6, the paradox of deontology, the vital conflict, marginal cases, the consequentialist theodicy) either meets premises the text has already declined to impose on non-Christians, or meets an answer the text's own resources give at parity.

**Minor items**
1. The A6 "alarm" sentence answers the debunker below parity and misapplies the text's own rule from A (R1).
2. "Which no total outweighs" in W1(2) is false on the text's own allowance for weighing side effects. The answer is available elsewhere in W1(2) (GS 24) and should be put here (R2).
3. The vital conflict (a death aimed at, with no beneficiary) is the purest form of the absolute's cost and is not named (R2).
4. In the utilitarian row, lying is tied to A7, which concerns belief, and a Christian-only absolute against lying is charged to us as a cost (R5).
5. A4 never faces the animal comparison that the text's own "worth or only attraction" test invites (R7).
6. The conclusion's list of eliminated grounds leaves out the good her life would have held (R8).
7. W2 leaves out the by-product answer of the utilitarian rationalist (R10).

**Misstatements of my school**
- "Counting experiences, it finds nothing wrong": two-level hedonists condemn the disposition and endorse the rule. What they deny is a critical-level wrong in the isolated act (R4).
- Hooker grounds the code in impartially weighted well-being with some priority to the worst off, not in "the total" without qualification (R3).
- The row describes our normative theory but not our metaethics. The leading utilitarians today are objectivists who use debunking selectively (R6). This is an omission rather than an error.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Whether wronging reduces to worsening (A2).
- Whether A4's strong form, equal worth of lives whatever the capacities, outweighs equal consideration of interests. This is the conclusion's own question.
- Whether the means/side-effect distinction is real or an artifact of an alarm.
- Whether reason's grasp of necessary truths needs a Source beyond the by-product story (W2).
- Whether pain is a privation or a positive bad.
- The overall weighing of evil, above all animal pain across the ages, against the case.

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1 (A6).**
Old: "Some say this certainty is only an alarm built to sound at harm planned as a means; but an alarm that tracks that difference would sound just so if the difference were real, and the regard for the many that would spend the one has a history too, in sympathy learned among kin. A history settles neither."
New: "Some say this certainty is only an alarm built to sound at harm planned as a means, which would sound whether or not the difference mattered, while the impartial regard that would spend the one for the many is reason's own. But that story defeats the certainty only if the difference does not matter, which is the very question; the cases above are judged at leisure, at a distance and in secret, where no alarm of the hands is sounding; and impartial regard rests on the conviction that suffering is bad, which survival taught us as surely as it taught us to recoil from the knife. A history settles neither."

**R2 (W1(2)).**
Old: "That is why, on this account, the line has no exceptions for any of us: what protects her is her own worth, which no total outweighs, and that no number of others has any right of their own to take her life."
New: "That is why, on this account, the line has no exceptions for any of us: what protects her is her own worth, which no total can make it right to aim against, for to aim at her death for others is to treat as existing for them one who was willed for her own sake; and no number of others has any right of their own to take her life. Her death foreseen and not aimed at may be weighed (A6), for that does not make her a means. Her death aimed at may not, even where she will die whatever is done and her death would save another; treatment aimed at the mother's own illness is another matter. That is the line's hardest cost, and this book pays it."

**R3 (C, utilitarian row).**
Old: "grounds those rules, and so her claim, in the total."
New: "grounds those rules, and so her claim, in the well-being they bring, counted impartially, even where it gives some priority to the worst off."

**R4 (C, utilitarian row).**
Old: "Counting experiences, it finds nothing wrong in the secret told to a stranger who will never repeat it, for no one feels it (A2); counting wishes, it can blame the teller, but her claim is still the total's, not hers in her own right."
New: "Counting experiences, it finds no wrong in the telling itself, though it condemns the man disposed to tell, for no one feels the betrayal (A2); counting wishes, hers among them, it can call the telling wrong, but her claim still weighs as one part of the total, not as hers in her own right."

**R5 (C, utilitarian row).**
Old: "And it makes truthfulness a servant of the total: where a lie would do more good, it must in principle favor the lie (A7); the Church, which allows a truth to be withheld from one with no right to it (CCC 2489), condemns the lie by its very nature (CCC 2485)."
New: "And it makes the duty to follow the evidence a servant of the total: where a comforting falsehood would do more good, it cannot say in principle why one ought still to believe the truth, though it holds that a policy of believing on the evidence does so much good that the exception will almost never arise (A7). So too with the lie: it must in principle favor the lie that does more good, where the Church, which allows a truth to be withheld from one with no right to it (CCC 2489), condemns the lie by its very nature (CCC 2485); that is this book's claim, not a shared one."

**R6 (C, utilitarian row, add at the end).**
New: "Its ablest recent defenders, Katarzyna de Lazari-Radek and Peter Singer, are no error theorists: with Sidgwick they hold that impartial benevolence is a truth reason sees, and they turn the debunker's argument on A4 and A6, as instinct for our own and an alarm at harm done by our hands, while sparing impartial benevolence, which survival would not have taught. A6 answers that argument; and they must say why reason's seeing that suffering is bad is less shaped by survival than the instincts they discount, which is W2's question in their form."

**R7 (A4, after "we learn whether what we meant was worth or only attraction.").**
New, inserted: "The same test cuts at us: instinct favors our own kind too, and some ask why the infant who will never reason should count for more than an animal that knows and fears more than she does. This book answers that her worth was never set by what she knows or fears, in her or in the animal, whose suffering also matters and may not be caused needlessly (CCC 2416–2418); it follows what she is and what she is for (W1(2)). That answer comes from the case; it is not one of its premises."

**R8 (Conclusion).**
Old: "her worth never followed her strength, or her mind, or how much she can feel or want, nor how much anyone cared for her, for no one did;"
New: "her worth never followed her strength, or her mind, or how much she can feel or want, or how much good her life would have held, nor how much anyone cared for her, for no one did;"

**R9 (A3, after "beforehand or after.").**
New, inserted: "Even Mill, who would let a man do almost anything with his own life, held that he may not sell himself into slavery (*On Liberty* V)."

**R10 (W2).**
Old: "The best rivals give two answers."
New: "The best rivals give three answers."
Insert, after the sentence describing the second answer: "A third, the utilitarian rationalist's, trusts few of the starting points survival tuned: only the felt badness of pain, known in having it, and reason's own seeing that no one's good counts more than another's, a power that came, like the power to do sums, with a mind built to reason in general."

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **Sidgwick is a witness for W2.** The greatest utilitarian found that reason gives two final ends, my own good and the good of all. He could not show that they agree without something like a moral order of the world, which he did not think reason could prove (Methods, Concluding Chapter; verify the exact formulation before quoting). This is the strongest non-theist confirmation of W2's "this law holds together," and it comes from the rival who most wants to do without God.
- **The real utilitarian challenge is epistemic, not normative.** It is a contest of certainties: impartial benevolence plus selective debunking against the strong A4 and A6. The text's conclusion already frames the contest correctly. R1 and R6 would make the text meet it by name.
- **The evil section's refusal to aggregate is the deepest answer to us.** "Only what is offered to her can [answer]" holds God to the same anti-aggregative standard A6 holds us to. That consistency is rare and should be kept in any condensation.
- **Mill on voluntary slavery** gives A3 a secular witness from inside my own school.
