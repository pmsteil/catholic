# Blind review: the atheist existentialist school

## 1. School and voice

I speak for atheist existentialism, mostly in Simone de Beauvoir's careful version (*The Ethics of Ambiguity*) and Albert Camus's (*The Rebel*, *The Plague*), with Jean-Paul Sartre's ontology underneath (*Being and Nothingness*, *Existentialism Is a Humanism*). These are the starting points I bring. Existence comes before essence. No God conceived a human nature, so there is a human condition but no human nature. Values come into the world through freedom, which keeps them going by choosing them. The person who treats values as hard objects already sitting in the world (the "serious man") is in bad faith. The universe answers no appeal, which is the absurd. Camus's rebel says no to it and finds solidarity in saying no. I accept the cost this view admits openly, that nothing keeps the dead, and I don't accept the text's claim that it costs more than that. I attack the text's starting points as well as its steps. All paraphrases below are mine; each one marked "verify" should be checked against the source.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** Freedom is the source of every value. If I will my own freedom honestly, I must also will the freedom of others. My projects get their meaning only when other freedoms take them up, and a freedom that wills slaves contradicts itself (Beauvoir, *Ethics of Ambiguity* II–III, verify). What anyone is owed is not to be shut into immanence, meaning not to be made a thing whose future is closed off. Oppression is the central injustice. **Verdict: this holds as a structure, but it borrows.** The rule that freedom must will freedom was made by no one's free choice. It binds anyone who wills honestly, and the demand to be honest (to avoid bad faith) is A7 under another name.

**2. A hard dilemma.** My case is Camus's *Les Justes*: Kaliayev refuses to throw the bomb at the Grand Duke because the Duke's young nephew and niece are in the carriage. The principle is that rebellion draws its value from a solidarity that killing the innocent would deny. Rebellion has a limit (Camus's *mesure*). A rebel who does kill has to pay with his own life, so his act never becomes a calculated exchange. **Verdict: this holds for Camus but breaks across the school as a whole.** Sartre, in his preface to Fanon, and Beauvoir, in *Ethics of Ambiguity* III, allowed the line to bend for liberation. Even Camus's limit amounts to "no one may be spent, and whoever spends someone owes himself", which is close to the book's own justice and self-gift.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** My school rejects "dignity" if that means a property belonging to a nature. It grants instead the equal ontological freedom of every consciousness, since freedom does not come in degrees. The newborn, the dying who are still conscious, the severely disabled and the enemy are each a whole freedom, which is why torture is the paradigm crime: it is an attempt to make a freedom confess itself a thing. **Verdict: this holds for every conscious human being and breaks at the margin.** A person who never will be conscious, or never again will be, gets standing only through other people's fidelity. On that point the text states my cost accurately.

**4. Ultimate end of flourishing.** No end is given. The ideal is authenticity: lucidly taking up my freedom and my situation, willing freedom in concrete projects that open the future for myself and for others, and Camus's revolt lived "without appeal", with measure and solidarity. **Verdict: this is coherent and borrows nothing, at a cost the school pays openly.** The longing to be one's own ground fails, so Sartre's man is a "useless passion" (B&N IV, verify), and the dead are given up to the living.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1. The method of A, attacked as the spirit of seriousness.**
*Attack.* A treats what "still seems true after honest reflection" as evidence. But values seem to be found out there in the world exactly because of the spirit of seriousness, the bad faith that hides from us that we are what sustains them (B&N, conclusion; Beauvoir, *Ethics of Ambiguity* II, verify). By A's own defeater clause, that account would produce the seeming whether or not values are found.
*Defense.* The clause works the same way for both sides. The anguish in which values seem to rest on my freedom is also a seeming, and it is less stable than the seeming that the tortured child is wronged. Sartre couldn't live out his own account. He wrote about Alleg's *La Question* and the torture in Algeria as an outrage he had found, not as a project he had chosen. A1's clause "even if every culture approved" is also one Sartre accepts, since a culture's approval makes nothing right on his view either.
*Rejoinder.* This is fair. What I dispute is only that values are found, and the A heading assigns Sartre exactly that denial. **Held.**

**Exchange 2. The hinge: Sartre's paper-knife.**
*Attack.* The text uses Sartre's conditional, that a nature we are for would need a God who conceived it (*Existentialism Is a Humanism*, verify), against the secular Aristotelian. But the conditional cuts the other way too. A being with a nature conceived beforehand is like the paper-knife: it exists *for* something, which makes it an instrument, and a freedom made for an end is a tool with good manners.
*Defense.* The end is not a function served for someone else. The human person is "the only creature on earth which God willed for itself" (GS 24). Its end is its own good, and God is offended only when we act against that good (SCG III.122, cited in W1). Freedom reaches its fulfilment in that end rather than being cancelled by it (CCC 1730–1733; VS 41 on participated theonomy). The text even keeps the possibility of refusing that end, which an instrument doesn't have.
*Rejoinder.* "Willed for itself, and for an end that is its own good" holds together. The paper-knife analogy depends on the end belonging to the maker, and here it doesn't. **Held.**

**Exchange 3. "A will that invents values can command, but it cannot bind."**
*Attack.* This line in the W1 closing paragraph is asserted, not argued. Engagement binds. By committing myself I become the project, and the pledge in Sartre's *Critique of Dialectical Reason*, sworn among a group, creates real obligation where there was none (verify).
*Defense.* Sartre's own analysis grants the text's point. The pledged group holds together through Terror, which is each member's right to kill the traitor. Sartre presents the pledge as a freedom defending itself against its own later freedom (verify). A bond that one freedom made, and another free act could unmake, holds by force or fear. That is the difference between commanding and binding. Even a promise binds only because breaking it wrongs the person it was made to, and no promiser made that wrongness.
*Rejoinder.* My school's best text on the subject agrees with the line. **Held.**

**Exchange 4. The real crux: is Beauvoir's "must" a law or a condition?**
*Attack.* The C row says that what binds one freedom to will another's "is made by no freedom, and so binds as a law binds, which is what this account set out to deny". That misstates us. Sartre explicitly allowed that the content of a morality varies while a certain form of it is universal (*Existentialism Is a Humanism*, verify). What the school denied was values given *a priori* in an intelligible heaven, and content given beforehand. It never denied the formal structure of freedom among other freedoms. A condition is not a law.
*Defense.* Then name the condition. A form that every freedom finds already in place, which it didn't make and can't honestly refuse, and whose violation Sartre condemned in moral language (he called those who flee their freedom "cowards", and those who think their existence necessary *salauds*), binds the way a law binds, whatever it is called. Sartre labelled his verdict on bad faith a logical judgment, but it carries the force of condemnation. Its source is W2's question: why is there a binding form at all that belongs to what a freedom is?
*Rejoinder.* The substance holds and my school borrows here. A form that was found, binds, and applies universally is what the text says it is. The text's words "which is what this account set out to deny" still overstate the school's denial, which was aimed at *a priori* content. **Held with repair** (see §6.1).

**Exchange 5. The newborn on the hillside: "what she is", or "what she will be"?**
*Attack.* The conclusion argues that if the weak newborn was wronged no less than the strong one, then her worth "follows what she is". There is a third ground that is neither capacity nor nature. She is a freedom, a for-itself whose being is its future, and freedom has no degrees. Beauvoir counts the child a freedom not yet taken up. So the weak and the strong newborn, and the grown man, are wronged equally, and no Mind is needed.
*Defense.* Then the existentialist has granted the conclusion's inference, since "a consciousness, a freedom" is what she *is*. The conclusion is addressed to "the one who gives up A4", and on the conscious newborn you haven't given it up. The case separates from you elsewhere, and the C row says where. For the infant who will never be conscious, you have only "the worth our freedom gives her". For every infant, her standing depends on others' willing her freedom, which is the binding form from Exchange 4.
*Rejoinder.* I agree. The hillside test doesn't divide the book from Beauvoir. The never-conscious case and the question of whether value is found do divide us, and the text puts its weight on both correctly. I also note a symmetry. On the text's own account, her worth is given too: "God's love gives it, by giving her being" (W1(2)). The difference between us is not given versus found. It is given with her being by the Creator, before anyone responds, versus conferred afterwards by fellow creatures. The C row's "our" carries that difference exactly. **Held.**

**Exchange 6. Sartre's dilemma against creation, and the Look against W3.**
*Attack.* W1(2) says her worth "is a gift exactly as far as her being is a gift". Sartre's introduction to *Being and Nothingness* argues that a created being is caught in a dilemma. If it stays within the creating subjectivity, it is not really a being in its own right. If it stands over against God, it supports itself and owes Him nothing (verify). W3 runs into the same problem. A Person who "knows us wholly" is the Look raised to the absolute. Sartre treats God as the concept of the Other pushed to its limit (B&N III, verify), and being seen wholly means being fixed as a thing. The longing W3 relies on is therefore either contradictory or a longing for one's own objectification.
*Defense.* Both horns treat God and the creature as two beings on one plane, competing for the same causal room. Thomist participation rejects that picture. Creation is not a change made in something already there. It is the creature's continuing dependence for its own *esse*, and that dependence gives the creature real being, real causality and real freedom (CCC 308; "In him we live and move and have our being", Acts 17:28). When God moves the will He does not make its acts unwilling. He is the cause of their being willed (ST I q.83 a.1 ad 3), and "the grace of Christ is not in the slightest way a rival of our freedom" (CCC 1742). W3 already says this compactly: "One who is no rival beside us".
*Rejoinder.* That is a full answer at the level of metaphysics, and the dispute ends at whether being is univocal. That is a standoff at parity, not a defect. The text's phrase "no rival beside us" carries more than a lay reader will see in it, so a clause would help. **Held, with an optional repair** (see §6.3).

**Exchange 7. Camus's revolt: the account owed to the child.**
*Attack.* The evil section sets its own standard: "the account we would ask of anyone who loved her and could have spared her." Then it admits it can give "a setting, not an explanation" (CCC 309–314). Under its own standard the theist account fails. Rieux at the boy's deathbed refuses to love a creation in which children are tortured, and that refusal is a moral verdict, not a probability estimate. Even if God has reasons, a person may refuse a harmony bought this way.
*Defense.* The text claims neither that it meets the standard nor that the verdict is wrong. It names Ivan, gives his verdict its weight, and calls the pattern of natural pain evidence that is "not weak". It argues only that our failing to see how *each* evil is answered is weak evidence if the good is finished in eternity, and it limits even that. Then it adds what neither Rieux nor Ivan can offer the child: if God exists, the last word over her is not the suffering. The "invitation" also observes that the rebel's protest rests on A1–A4, and doesn't claim that this refutes him.
*Rejoinder.* Camus would say the protest's source is the solidarity found in revolt, with nothing behind it. Whether that source is enough is the realist's question from W2, and the text concedes that this is "a judgment about where explanation may stop". The text doesn't overclaim, and neither side can compel the other. **Held** (standoff at parity).

**Exchange 8. The frame: "the case ends in a choice, not a proof."**
*Attack.* That admission is my thesis. If the decisive step is the reader's choice of what he is "surer of", then value rests on choice in the end.
*Defense.* Choosing which judgment to trust is an act of weighing evidence, not an act of making value. A juror who decides he is surer of the witness than of the alibi has not created the fact. The conclusion's last sentences state this distinction outright: what rises in the reader is "your answer to" her worth, not its source.
*Rejoinder.* The distinction is real, and I can't show that the reader's certainty about the hillside is invented rather than found without assuming my own conclusion. **Held** (parity).

## 4. Ledger

- **A heading:** Holds. The epistemic rule is offered openly with a defeater clause that applies to both sides. "Ends in a choice, not a proof" is honest. Sartre's denial (that any of these is found rather than made by freedom) is stated accurately.
- **A1:** Holds. My school asserts it in its own voice (Algeria) and accepts the "even if every culture approved" clause. It disputes only that the wrong is *found*.
- **A2:** Holds. The secret-told-to-a-stranger case works for existentialism too, since betraying her wrongs her past freedom, which is now in the hands of the living (B&N IV on death, verify).
- **A3:** Holds. Beauvoir agrees that no freedom can consent itself into slavery. A free act that abdicates freedom is bad faith, not a release.
- **A4:** Holds as a claim. My school matches it for every conscious human being and breaks for the never-conscious. The text's separation of shared claims from the book's own claims is exact.
- **A5:** Holds. Sartre agrees strongly that facts and desires don't settle choice (his student's dilemma). The Aquinas list is offered as something found on reflection, not as compelling, and Sartre's denial is answered fairly in C.
- **A6:** Holds. The school divides here: Camus holds the line, while Sartre and Beauvoir let it bend. C reports this accurately.
- **A7:** Holds. It is the text's best weapon against my school, because the existentialist critique of bad faith needs lucidity to be owed.
- **W1 frame:** Holds. Anscombe ("Modern Moral Philosophy", 1958) is represented correctly. The Euthyphro handling avoids voluntarism.
- **W1(1):** Holds as a clue. My school takes the first or second road. Sartre's version, in which the fact is fixed but its meaning stays at the mercy of the living, is a variant of the second road that the text doesn't need to name.
- **W1(2):** Holds. The cost of 1 Samuel 15 is paid openly, and the debt to Kant is admitted. CCC 67 on present voices agrees with Sartre's own Abraham in *Existentialism Is a Humanism* (who proves it was an angel?).
- **W1 closing ("This Lawgiver is not a commander"):** Holds. "A will that invents values can command, but it cannot bind" survives Sartre's own account of the pledge (Exchange 3).
- **W2:** Holds as a standoff at parity. This isn't a strong field for my school. Sartre's dilemma against creation affects "one Source" but is answered by participation (Exchange 6).
- **W3 (the longing):** Holds. Sartre's "useless passion" is represented accurately and answered precisely: the grasping longing must fail, the receiving one need not. The answer is compressed; see the optional repair (§6.3).
- **W3 (hiddenness):** Holds. It is honest ("part of an answer"). The existentialist reads the silence as evidence, and the text gives that real weight.
- **C, Nothing binds:** Holds. Mackie's project of inventing morality is stated fairly.
- **C, Feeling refined:** Holds. Hume's sensible knave is reported fairly. No objection from my side.
- **C, Our best words:** Holds. Rorty is treated fairly, and the use of James's test is fair.
- **C, Reason's own law:** Holds. Conceding that Kant protects her more firmly than this book does is exemplary.
- **C, What we owe each other:** Holds. No objection from my side.
- **C, Care between persons:** Holds. Its stopping point is the same as Beauvoir's at the margin.
- **C, Well-being counted impartially:** Holds. No objection from my side.
- **C, Flourishing of our kind:** Holds. It could use Sartre's conditional as a wedge (§7).
- **C, Godless realism:** Holds. The parity is acknowledged honestly.
- **C, Impersonal sacred order:** Holds. No objection from my side.
- **C, The Way:** Holds. No objection from my side.
- **C, Spinoza:** Holds. No objection from my side.
- **C, the Dhamma:** Holds. No objection from my side.
- **C, History and class:** Holds. Its line that Marx condemned exploitation in the name of freedom rather than justice is accurate, and fits Sartre's later Marxism too.
- **C, Power and rank:** Holds. The dilemma over whether honesty binds every mind or only those who hold it is the same one that catches Sartre.
- **C, Freedom that makes its values:** Holds with repair. Its substance is accurate: Algeria, the conditional "only a Creator", freedom without degrees, the human condition, Beauvoir's child, the never-conscious, the bending of the line, and the unmade "must". Two overstatements: "which is what this account set out to deny" (§6.1), and "Camus, who held that revolt finds a nature common to all" (§6.2).
- **C, A God who is Love:** Holds. "Reach, not count" is the right claim. "One mystery, and a mystery that explains" is offered as a judgment, not as a proof.
- **Evil, paragraph 1 ("On the other side of the scale"):** Holds. It is honest about hell, 1 Samuel 15, the Mary case and Ivan. Camus would add that refusal is a moral act and not only a weight, and the invitation partly does this.
- **Evil, First (privation):** Holds. It is limited correctly ("Nor does it by itself rule out..."). Sartre would say nothingness is real in the for-itself, but that doesn't touch the narrower claim that there is no supreme Evil.
- **Evil, Second (no trade):** Holds. The skeptical step is weighted correctly ("weak evidence"), and the pattern of pain "still weighs". The parasite and the fawn are left on the scale.
- **Evil, Third (God who suffers with):** Holds. "This is not a further proof" is correct, and it is fair to Rieux and Paneloux.
- **Evil, Invitation:** Holds. "An invitation, not a reply". The rebel's protest does stand on A1–A4, and the text doesn't claim more.
- **Conclusion:** Holds. Its hillside test doesn't separate the book from Beauvoir for the conscious newborn (Exchange 5), but it is addressed to the one who gives up A4, and the C row carries the existentialist difference.

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**As written: NONE.**

(No Fatal or Serious objection from my school survives. Against the text's own standard, every attack I could bring ends in a reply at least to parity, or in a cost the text has already put on its own side of the scale.)

**Minor items**
- C, Freedom row: "which is what this account set out to deny" overstates the school's denial. Sartre and Beauvoir denied values given *a priori* and content given beforehand, and Sartre explicitly granted a universal form. The substantive charge (an unmade form that binds every freedom) survives the correction. Repair in §6.1.
- C, Freedom row: "Camus, who held that revolt finds a nature common to all". In the introduction to *The Rebel*, Camus says that analysing rebellion leads at least to the *suspicion* of a human nature, against the assumptions of his contemporaries. The verb "held" is too strong (verify). Repair in §6.2.
- W3: "One who is no rival beside us" is the right answer to Sartre's Look, but a reader who doesn't know Thomist causality won't see it. Optional repair in §6.3.

**Misstatements of my school:** only the two Minor items above. The rest is accurate and, unusually, charitable: the Algeria record, the conditional "only a Creator could give us a nature", freedom without degrees, the human condition, Beauvoir's child, the bending of the line for liberation, the useless passion of the longing to be God, and Rieux.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Whether value is found or made. The text's method treats firm seemings as evidence, and Sartre's phenomenology of anguish treats that firmness as seriousness. Each side's defeater test applies to the other.
- Sartre's dilemma against creation versus Thomist participation, which comes down to univocity versus analogy of being.
- Whether the form "freedom must will freedom" needs a source (the text) or is simply what a freedom is (my school). This is W2's question in my vocabulary, and the text concedes it ends in a judgment about where explanation may stop.
- Camus's moral refusal of any harmony bought with a child's tears, against the Christian hope that the last word over her is not the suffering. Neither side can compel the other, and the text says so.

## 6. Proposed repairs

**6.1. C, Freedom that makes its values (required; fixes a misstatement)**

Old:
> and what binds one freedom to will another's, as Beauvoir held it must, is made by no freedom, and so binds as a law binds, which is what this account set out to deny (A7, W1).

New:
> and what binds one freedom to will another's, as Beauvoir held it must, is made by no freedom, and binds every freedom before it chooses, as a law binds. Sartre granted that while the content of a morality is chosen, its form is universal; whence a form binds every freedom by what a freedom is is the question of W2 (A7, W1).

**6.2. C, same row (required; fixes a misstatement)**

Old:
> Camus, who held that revolt finds a nature common to all, stood nearer to us.

New:
> Camus, who found in revolt a value common to all, and was led by it to suspect that there is a human nature after all, stood nearer to us.

**6.3. W3 (optional; makes the answer to Sartre's Look visible)**

Old:
> it is not true of the longing to be given being, and told that it is good that we exist, by One who is no rival beside us.

New:
> it is not true of the longing to be given being, and told that it is good that we exist, by One who is no rival beside us, whose knowing does not fix us as a thing, as another creature's gaze would, because it gives the very freedom it knows (ST I q.83 a.1 ad 3; CCC 1742).

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **Sartre's conditional can be used against the secular Aristotelian.** Sartre, no friend of faith, held that a nature we are *for* needs someone who conceived it. The Flourishing row could cite him in one clause where it asks whether our nature is "an accident, or an order that needs nothing behind it". The atheist most serious about the question agreed with the book on what the question turns on.
- **The real line between the book and Beauvoir.** It is not found versus given. On the book's own account, worth is given: "God's love gives it, by giving her being." The line runs between worth given *with her being, before anyone responds*, and worth conferred *afterwards by fellow creatures*. Saying this plainly in the chapter would stop the existentialist from replying "you too say worth is bestowed", and it would sharpen W1(2)'s line "Our love answers to a worth already there; God's love gives it."
- **The hillside test doesn't divide the book from existentialism, and the book can afford to say so.** For the conscious newborn, Beauvoir agrees she is wronged as fully as a grown man. The cases that divide are the infant who will never be conscious, and the question of where the binding form comes from. A condensed chapter should aim its existentialist paragraph there, not at the strong-versus-weak newborn.
- **Sartre's own words work against him on A7.** He called his judgment on bad faith logical, not moral, yet he called those who flee their freedom cowards and *salauds*. That is evidence, from inside the school, that lucidity is owed and not merely advised.
- **Sartre's account of the pledge supports the W1 closing paragraph.** A bond made by freedom and held together by Terror shows the gap between commanding and binding (verify the *Critique* reference before using it).
- **Sartre's Abraham agrees with the book on present voices.** In *Existentialism Is a Humanism* Sartre asks who could prove the voice was an angel's. That stands with Kant and with CCC 67, and could be noted beside Kant's Abraham in the Reason's own law row.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "The rebel can take her side; he cannot keep her."
- "A will that invents values can command, but it cannot bind."
- "It even grants the hinge: only a Creator, Sartre held, could give us a nature we are *for*."
- "That is true of the longing that grasps... it is not true of the longing to be given being."
- "but that yes is said by me, not to me" (aimed at Nietzsche, and it reaches Camus's Sisyphus too).
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it."

## 8. Score

**8/10.** The text states my school accurately and charitably, finds its real crux (an unmade "must" that binds every freedom, and the never-conscious margin), and claims only a judgment of weighing that no existentialist can show to be invented without assuming his own conclusion. With the two small repairs to the Freedom row it would be **8.5**. **Honest ceiling for any theistic case against my school: 9.** The decisive question, whether any value is found rather than made, can be settled only by the reader's weighing, and the text's own conclusion rightly refuses to claim more than that.
