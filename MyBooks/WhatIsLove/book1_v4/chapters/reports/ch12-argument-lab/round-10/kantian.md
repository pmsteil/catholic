# Kantian review: "A moral argument for God" (Ch. 12, *The Lawgiver Is Love*)

## 1. School and voice

I speak for the Kantian school: Kant himself, read through Christine Korsgaard (constructivism, the normativity of agency), Allen Wood (humanity as the fundamental value), Barbara Herman (moral judgment and salience), Onora O'Neill (the authority of reason, practical judgment) and Thomas Hill (dignity, humanity, self-regarding duties). One fact has to come first. Kant was not an atheist. He held *rational faith* in God and immortality. But he denied that morality rests on God, that moral facts are evidence for God, or that any theoretical proof of God succeeds. I argue as a secular Kantian, which is how most of his modern readers stand. Wherever Kant himself gives the theist ground, I say so rather than hide it. On my view the moral law is the law that a free rational will gives itself, because it is the form of free willing as such. Its authority comes from reason's self-legislation, not from any being, divine or natural.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**(1) Starting point for justice.** The Universal Principle of Right says an action is right if it can coexist with everyone's freedom under a universal law (*Metaphysics of Morals* 6:230). From it comes the single innate right: freedom from another's constraining choice, which includes innate equality and being one's own master. Every human being has it by virtue of humanity (6:237). What anyone is owed follows from that right and from the Formula of Humanity: treat humanity never merely as a means, always also as an end (*Groundwork* 4:429). **Holds**, with a weak point. The value of humanity is established by Kant's regress argument (G 4:428–429, as Korsgaard reconstructs it), and that argument is disputed even among Kantians. Our first principle is a first principle, just as the book's is.

**(2) A hard dilemma.** A Gestapo officer at the door asks whether Jews are hidden in my house. Kant himself held that I may not lie even here, though I may refuse to answer (*On a Supposed Right to Lie*, 8:425–430). Korsgaard argues the maxim of lying to someone who is himself deceiving passes the universal-law test, and that under non-ideal conditions the Formula of Humanity works as a goal to pursue, not a limit to obey mechanically (*The Right to Lie*, 1986, verify). My resolution follows Korsgaard: lie, because protecting the hunted is a duty of right and of respect. It uses respect for rational agency and the principle of right. It does not use the book's covenantal definitions of truth, justice, mercy and self-gift. **Holds** without borrowing. Two honest notes. Kant and Korsgaard split on this case. And the Catholic position, which condemns the lie by its nature but allows withholding truth from someone with no right to it (CCC 2485, 2489), sits nearer to Kant than Korsgaard does. A second note: Kantian ethics is thin on mercy and self-gift. Beneficence is a wide duty that need not make the giver needy (6:454), and that thinness is a real cost.

**(3) Equal, unconditional dignity.** Dignity belongs to humanity, the capacity for setting ends and in the end for autonomy. It is "beyond all price" and admits no equivalent (G 4:434–435). So it has no degrees: no one has more of it than another. It is not lost by crime (6:463). The enemy keeps it. Kant counted the newborn a person and argued that parents may not treat her as their product or property (6:280–281). The dying keep it. **Holds** for the newborn, the dying and the enemy. **Strains, and borrows something close to the book's own move**, for the human being who will never reason. Our options are three. (a) Ground it in the rational *kind* (Patrick Kain and others, verify). That is a natural-kind essentialism very near the book's "what she is." (b) Respect the potentialities and fragments of rational nature (Wood, verify). That puts equality at risk. (c) Broaden being an end in itself to every creature with a good of its own (Korsgaard, *Fellow Creatures*, 2018). That keeps unconditional regard but loosens the specifically human, equal standing.

**(4) Ultimate good and guiding principles.** The only unconditional good is a good will (G 4:393). The complete good is the *highest good*: virtue, which is worthiness to be happy, joined with happiness in proportion to it, in a kingdom of ends. Kant held that we must believe this possible, and therefore postulate God and immortality (*Critique of Practical Reason* 5:110–132). Secular heirs recast it as a this-worldly social ideal (Andrews Reath, verify), or drop it as a separate duty. **Coherent, but at this point the secular Kantian departs from Kant**, who judged that without God the highest good is unreachable and the law would direct us toward an imaginary end (CPrR 5:114, verify). Here my founder sides with the theist, and I record it.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1. The paradox of method against A5.**
*Attack.* A5 lists goods that reflection finds (Aquinas's inclinations) as though the moral good were known first and the law derived from it. Kant's "paradox of method" (CPrR 5:62–64) runs the other way. The concept of the morally good must be fixed *after and through* the law, or ethics becomes heteronomous, a technique for reaching ends that nature supplies.
*Defense.* A5 does not say the list grounds the law. It says what we can do and want does not settle what we ought to do, which Kant affirms. Its first principle, "good is to be done and evil avoided," is formal, as Kant's law is. And it leaves the deeper question to W: "whether that means our nature carries its own ends... is what W must weigh." The goods named are goods. A Kantian need not deny they are goods, only that they are unconditional ones.
*Rejoinder.* Granted. A5 offers truths a Kantian can sign: the is–ought gap, and a list of real but conditional goods. The ordering dispute is real but correctly deferred. **Held.**

**Exchange 2. Autonomy against the Lawgiver (W1 closing paragraph).**
*Attack.* Kant's dilemma against theological ethics (G 4:443) has two horns. Either we recognize God's will as good because we already know the good, in which case the law binds by its own content and God adds nothing to its binding force. Or we take the law from His will as such, in which case obligation rests on power and fear. The text's sentence "The law binds because He is good" invites the dilemma.
*Defense.* The text sees the dilemma and takes neither horn. God "did not choose whether cruelty is evil, for goodness is His nature," and the law is "His own wisdom," not His choice. Our share in it is "reason's own light, not a foreign rule" (*Veritatis Splendor* 41: participated theonomy). The wrong is wrong "because of what it does to her." So binding force reaches us through our reason, and God is the law's source, not its sanction. Kant himself defined religion as recognizing all duties as divine commands. He meant not arbitrary ordinances of an alien will, but laws essential to every free will in itself (CPrR 5:129; *Religion* 6:153–154, verify). That is very close to VS 41.
*Rejoinder.* The defense is correct. The convergence at CPrR 5:129 is real and worth citing. The disagreement left is not about what binds or how. It is about whether the law's necessity *resides* in one Mind or *is* the form of every rational will, and that is a standoff at parity. Even so, the sentence "The law binds because He is good" reads as the first horn. It should say that the law binds through reason because it is true, and is true because it is His wisdom. **Held with repair** (R1).

**Exchange 3. The fit of law and mind (W2; C, Reason's own law).**
*Attack.* W2's main puzzle, that minds built for survival grasp necessary truths, has no grip on Kant. On transcendental idealism the necessity of logic and mathematics comes from the mind's own forms. On constructivism the moral law is constitutive of agency. Law and mind do not "fit"; the law *is* the form of the mind. The text concedes this ("need no meeting of law and mind"), and then asks only "why there are beings at all whose own law it is." That is not a moral question. It is the cosmological question, and the moral argument inherits every weakness Kant found in the cosmological proof.
*Defense.* Kant felt the question himself. He held that we cannot understand how freedom is possible (G 4:459–461). In the third *Critique* he argued that a being under moral laws is the final end of creation, and that we must think of a moral Author of the world (§§84, 86–87). Kant also never closed the gap between nature and freedom without a regulative idea of design. The question is not idle. It is the one Kant left open, and he answered it in the book's direction.
*Rejoinder.* The fact holds, but the text **understates the epistemic status of Kant's answer**. Kant gave it for practical purposes only. He said it is no proof for the doubter, only something a person must assume if he wants to think consistently in morality (CJ §87, 5:450 note, verify), and expressly no ground of obligation (CPrR 5:125–126; *Religion* 6:3). "An answer near this book's" is fair about the content and misleading about the claim. The remaining dispute, over where explanation may stop, is a standoff at parity, and W2 rightly says so. **Held with repair** (R2).

**Exchange 4. Equal dignity at the edge (W1(2); C, Reason's own law).**
*Attack.* The text says Kant's ground, "like any ground laid in her powers of mind, must stretch to reach her." This misdescribes humanity as empirical powers. Kantian dignity belongs to the person as *homo noumenon*. It has no degrees by definition, and Kant ascribed it to the newborn as a practical idea, not by inspecting her capacities (6:280). Worse for the book, the Catholic ground is laid in mind too. The image of God consists in a spiritual soul with intellect and free will (CCC 1703–1705), had by nature, not by use. If "powers of mind" stretches for Kant, it stretches for the Church. And Kant has his own version of the call: the *vocation* (*Bestimmung*) of humanity, the same for every human being and not graded by talent.
*Defense.* The C row already concedes parity on kind: "a question this book faces too." Its claimed advantage is narrower. The Kantian vocation is self-given and has to be taken up by one's own reason, while the Catholic call is given to each individual by Another. It "does not wait on her powers," it can be completed as gift (CCC 1250, 1261), and it individualizes: she is "willed for itself" (GS 24), not only a bearer of a valuable kind.
*Rejoinder.* That asymmetry is real for the one human who will never reason. Kant's vocation cannot be completed for her in this life. My postulate of immortality is for the moral progress of agents, not for her, and my secular heirs have no postulate at all. Note, though, that the supernatural call (*Humani Generis* 26) is not something reason sees. Reason sees the image, which is rational nature, so for reason alone the two accounts are at parity. The edge goes to the theist only as a hypothesis that includes the call. The text says enough to hold that line. The W1(2) phrase about "powers of mind" should still be fixed, since it misstates my school and cuts against the book's own ground. **Held** (repair R3 for the misstatement).

**Exchange 5. The debt to the dead (W1(1)).**
*Attack.* Kant gives the second road something the text does not credit. The dead can be wronged as *homo noumenon*, the person thought apart from physical existence (6:295–296). Guilt's healing, for Kant, is not the victim's pardon but the *revolution of disposition*: the new person takes up the old one's debt, and the debt of sin is "the most personal" and cannot be carried by anyone else (*Religion* 6:72, paraphrase, verify). So guilt has a rational answer that does not require her to be there.
*Defense.* The text agrees that the debt is personal and cannot be transferred, which is why God "does not take her place in giving the pardon that is hers to give." Conversion heals the wrongdoer's will, not the *bond*, and guilt asks for the bond: "only someone can forgive." And Kant's own 6:295, the person who can still be wronged in abstraction from bodily existence, leans toward the third road: if her claim survives her death, the question whether *she* does comes back.
*Rejoinder.* At 6:295 the noumenal abstraction is juridical, not a theory of survival. The secular Kantian walks the second road with open eyes. But I grant the text's real point, and the text claims only a "clue, not a proof." Kant offers the book a better category than "clue." From "I truly owe her, so she must be able to receive it," practical reason may *postulate* the condition of a duty's fulfilment, as Kant did for the highest good. That is neither theoretical evidence nor feeling. **Held** (insight in section 7).

**Exchange 6. Evil and the symmetry of ignorance (Evil, "Second").**
*Attack.* Kant's essay on the failure of every philosophical theodicy (1791, 8:255–271) concludes that theoretical reason can neither justify God nor convict Him. The text uses half of that: "not seeing how each evil is answered is weak evidence that it is not." By symmetry, if our ignorance of the divine standpoint weakens evil as evidence, it equally weakens moral goodness and fit as evidence for God. And the text's privation thesis ("evil has nothing of its own") runs into Kant's view that moral evil is a *real opposition*: in his notation, vice is −a, not merely the absence 0 (*Religion* 6:22–23 note).
*Defense.* The text does not generalize the skepticism. It says "we know enough of goodness to know what a good God would value, which is why this weight is real." It confines the ignorance to the *how* of each particular answer, and it keeps the general pattern of pain as strong evidence ("there the evidence is not weak"). So it does not shield itself from evil while counting goodness. On privation: Kant also denies that a human can have a *diabolical* will that chooses evil as evil (6:35–37). Evil, for Kant, is the inversion of the order of incentives, putting self-love above the law (6:36). That is choosing a lesser good over a higher one, as Chapter 1 says. Kant's −a concerns *imputability*, not ontology. On the dark god he is the book's ally.
*Rejoinder.* Conceded on both counts. The skepticism is local and the general weight is kept, which is honest. And the "Second" claim, that whatever good is drawn "she is not spent as a mere means for it, and the good hoped for is offered to her as well," is exactly the structure of my own formula: never merely as a means, always also as an end (CPrR 5:131, which applies the formula even to God). By its own wording the text meets Kant's standard. **Held.**

**Exchange 7, the strongest. God's right over innocent life (W1(2); C, Kant row; Evil ¶1; Conclusion).**
*Attack.* The book holds with St. Thomas that God could command the killing of the Amalekite children without injustice, and that the human hand would act by God's right (ST I-II q.94 a.5 ad 2). Three Kantian objections follow. (a) *Epistemic.* Kant's Abraham argument (*Conflict* 7:63; *Religion* 6:187) is not tied to the closure of public revelation. At every time, no human can be as sure a voice is God's as he is sure that killing an innocent child is wrong. That applied to Saul as much as it applies to us. The book's reason why no one may *now* claim such a command (CCC 67) is internal to faith. (b) *Metaphysical.* Giving being to a free being creates duties toward her, not dominion over her life. Kant argued that parents may not destroy their child as their product, because one cannot make a free being one's handiwork (6:280–281). (c) *Internal.* The conclusion's master test is: which are you surer of, that the child was wronged, or "any argument that says she was not"? By that test the reader should be surer that the Amalekite infant was wronged by the sword than of St. Thomas's argument that she was not. The book applies its own test everywhere except here.
*Defense.* (a) The book concedes the epistemic point for every present claimant, and concedes that Kant "protects her here more firmly than this book does." It says so in four places (W1(2), the Kant row, the God row, and the evil section). (b) The analogy with parents fails. Parents are secondary causes who neither give being from nothing nor hold it in being. God sustains her existence at every moment and "can give it back" (resurrection, CCC 998), so His calling her home does not destroy what He made. Nor does He use her *merely* as a means, since He wills her for her own sake and keeps her (GS 24; *Summa contra Gentiles* III.112). That satisfies Kant's own "not merely, always also as an end." (c) The master test asks whether her worth follows her strength. St. Thomas does not deny the Amalekite child's equal worth. All are equally under the Creator's dominion (ST I-II q.94 a.5 ad 2: by God's command death may justly come to the guilty and the innocent alike).
*Rejoinder.* (b) is a real answer at parity. Creation from nothing and the power to give life back are disanalogies Kant's parent argument does not reach. But (a) and (c) stand, partly. The book's evil section describes this weight as an unanswered *why* ("not why He would call these lives home by these hands"). For a reader weighing by reason alone, it is more than that. It is a *clash* with A4 at full strength. Reason can see, at most, that the Giver of life wrongs no one by natural death. Reason cannot see that a man could rightly be the hand. The book holds that on the authority of Scripture as St. Thomas read it, which is faith's ground, not reason's. The text pays the cost openly but describes it too lightly, and it does not notice that its own concluding test points at it. **Held with repair** (R4). The cost stays on the scale. The description is what has to change.

- **A2.** Holds. Kant agrees. The secret-telling case is a wrong against her right, independent of anyone's experience, and Kant holds the dead can be wronged (6:295–296).
- **A3.** Holds. Kant agrees: a contract to slavery is void (6:283, 6:330). Kant also limits a sovereign's pardon to crimes against himself (6:337), which supports "no one else... in her place."
- **A4.** Holds. Its scope is fully shared by Kant except the death penalty, where Kant is exactly the case the text describes (R8). Its strength as a datum is what Exchange 7 turns back on the text.

- **A6.** Holds. Kant is the strongest secular witness for every case listed, and for the line against consent (6:422–423). Kant should be named alongside the Vinaya (R7).
- **A7.** Holds. Kant agrees: truthfulness and inquiry as duties of every rational being. The Kantian locates their binding force in reason's own law, which the text acknowledges.
- **W1 frame (Anscombe, necessity, offense against God, Job 35, SCG III.122).** Holds. Lists Kantians among those who hold that pardon is owed to her, which is accurate. The offense against God is placed after, not beside, the wrong to her, which closes off the heteronomy worry.

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**As written: NONE.**

No Fatal or Serious objection stands. The large Kantian disagreements are standoffs at parity, and the text says so in its own words: whether the law's necessity resides in a Mind or *is* the form of rational will; whether the existence of rational beings needs explaining; whether moral data are theoretical evidence at all. The largest Kantian cost, God's right over innocent life, is already put on the scale and conceded to Kant in four places.

**Minor items**
1. **The 1 Sam 15 cost is described too lightly.** This is the most important minor item, close to the line. The evil section calls it an unanswered "why," but for a reader weighing by reason alone it is a clash with A4. The text's own concluding test points at it, and the instrument doctrine is held on faith, not reason (R4). It stays Minor because the cost is already priced and conceded. What is wrong is the description, not the weighing.
2. **"The law binds because He is good" reads as the first horn of Kant's dilemma** (G 4:443) when the surrounding text plainly means participated theonomy (R1).
3. **The God row's claim of reach does not register Kant's own single source**, rational nature, for law, mind and beings, and so overstates its advantage over Kant (R6).
4. **A6 and A4 leave out Kant where he is the best witness**: against killing at a person's request (6:422–423), and as one who held that no crime destroys dignity yet still required the death penalty (6:333, 6:463) (R7, R8).

**Misstatements of my school**
- W1(2): "his ground, like any ground laid in her powers of mind, must stretch to reach her." Kantian humanity is not a set of empirical powers. Dignity has no degrees by definition, and Kant ascribed personhood to the newborn as a practical idea (6:280). The real strain is the human who will never reason, and that is the Kant row's (correct) framing, not this one (R3).
- C, Kant row: "as rational faith he gave an answer near this book's." Near in content, but Kant held it valid for practical purposes only: no proof to the doubter, no ground of any duty (CJ §87; CPrR 5:125–126; *Religion* 6:3). The omission makes Kant look like a fellow traveler in the inference to the best explanation, which he was not (R2).

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Whether the necessity of the law is the form of every rational will or resides in one Mind (W1 closing, W2).
- Whether "why there are rational beings at all" needs an answer beyond natural history (W2; Kant row).
- The human who will never reason: both accounts ground her in kind as far as reason goes. The book's added call is part of its hypothesis, which is legitimate but not visible to reason alone (W1(2)).
- Whether creation from nothing and the power to restore life give God a right that parents and states lack (W1(2) against MM 6:280–281).
- Whether guilt is healed by the wrongdoer's conversion or only by the victim's pardon (W1(1)).

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1 (W1 closing).**
Old: "The law binds because He is good; the offense against Him can be forgiven because He is Someone;"
New: "The law binds us through our own reason because it is true, and it is true because it is His wisdom and He is good; the offense against Him can be forgiven because He is Someone;"

**R2 (C, Reason's own law).**
Old: "a being under moral law is the final end of creation, which requires a moral Author of the world (*Critique of Judgment* §§84, 86–87)."
New: "a being under moral law is the final end of creation, which requires a moral Author of the world (*Critique of Judgment* §§84, 86–87). But he held it for practical purposes only, as no proof for the doubter and no ground of any duty, for morality needs no idea of a being above us in order to know its duty (*Critique of Practical Reason* 5:125–126; *Religion* 6:3). It is near this book's answer in what it says, and far from it in what it claims."

**R3 (W1(2)).**
Old: "he counted the newborn a person, though his ground, like any ground laid in her powers of mind, must stretch to reach her."
New: "he counted the newborn a person, and held that a worth beyond price has no degrees; but his heirs must still say why a human being who will never reason shares it, a question this book faces too (C, Reason's own law)."

**R4 (Evil ¶1).**
Old: "The wars of 1 Samuel 15 stay there too: W1(2) holds with St. Thomas that the Lord of life could command them without injustice, but reason can see only that He does no wrong in calling a life home, not why He would call these lives home by these hands."
New: "The wars of 1 Samuel 15 stay there too, and they weigh more than an unanswered why. W1(2) holds with St. Thomas that the Lord of life could command them without injustice; but reason can see only that He does no wrong in calling a life home, not that a man could rightly be the hand. A reader who weighs by reason alone is as sure that the infant of Amalek was wronged by the sword as that the infant on the hillside was wronged (see the conclusion). This book holds otherwise on the word of Scripture as St. Thomas read it, not on reason; for that reader it is a cost against the case, not only a mystery within it."
(An option for the author, not a repair: if he wants a lighter cost, the Church's reading of such pages in the light of Christ, as revelation given in stages (*Dei Verbum* 15; *Verbum Domini* 42; and the Pontifical Biblical Commission's 2014 *The Inspiration and Truth of Sacred Scripture*, verify), may leave room not to hold the command as reported. That is his decision. R4 works with the text's present position.)

**R5 (C, Reason's own law, closing; optional tightening).**
Old: "That is a cost against Kant, and this book pays it openly."
New: "That is a cost against Kant, and this book pays it openly; by this case's own test in the conclusion, it is not a small one."

**R6 (C, A God who is Love).**
Old: "which he connects through survival and reason rather than through a common source (W2)."
New: "which he connects through survival and reason rather than through a common source (W2). Kant too finds one source for all three, rational nature itself, whose law it is and which it is about; but that source reaches the human who will never reason only by kind, and does not say why there is rational nature at all (C, Reason's own law)."

**R7 (A6).**
Old: "(the Buddhist monastic rule is one witness, Vinaya, third pārājika)"
New: "(the Buddhist monastic rule is one witness, Vinaya, third pārājika; Kant, who held that a man may not dispose of his own person even to escape his pain, is another, *Metaphysics of Morals* 6:422–423)"

**R8 (A4).**
Old: "which many who hold that no crime destroys a man's dignity do not conclude."
New: "which many who hold that no crime destroys a man's dignity, Kant among them, do not conclude (*Metaphysics of Morals* 6:333, 6:463)."

**R9 (W3).**

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **Kant's definition of religion is the book's anti-voluntarism in his own words.** He described religion as recognizing duties as divine commands, not as arbitrary ordinances of an alien will but as laws essential to every free will (CPrR 5:129; *Religion* 6:153–154, paraphrase, verify). Set beside VS 41, it shows that the strongest secular-rationalist ethics and Catholic participated theonomy agree on *how* the law binds, and differ only on *where its necessity resides*. That narrows the dispute honestly, and in the book's favor.
- **"Not merely, always also as an end" applies even to God, and the book meets it.** CPrR 5:131 says not even God may use a rational being *merely* as a means. The evil section's claim, that whatever good is drawn she is not spent as a mere means and the good hoped for is offered to her as well, is exactly that standard. Saying so turns Kant from an accuser into a witness on that point.
- **A third category for W1(1) and W3.** Kant's *practical postulate*, which holds that if I truly owe it, the condition of its being received may be assumed, is neither theoretical evidence nor mere feeling. It describes what the book is doing with "a debt truly owed to her is one she must be able, one day, to receive" more precisely than "clue" does, and it fits CCC 35 (reason predisposes; it does not replace faith).
- **Kant against the dark god.** Kant denies that a human can have a diabolical will, one that chooses evil as evil (*Religion* 6:35–37), and analyses evil as an inversion of the order of incentives (6:36). That is a non-Thomist witness for Chapter 1's account of sin as choosing a lesser good over the greater, and for the evil section's "a will that lacks nothing has nothing to grasp at."
- **Kant's "authentic theodicy."** In the 1791 essay, Job, who will not flatter God, is approved, and the friends who defend God with arguments are rebuked (8:265–267, verify; Job 42:7). The book's closing invitation to the protester already has this shape. Citing Job 42:7 would bring the Church's own Scripture to the point Kant made.
- **Kant's casuistry on self-sacrifice** (Curtius leaping into the chasm to save Rome, 6:423) is the same question A6 raises with the firefighter. Kant leaves it open while forbidding aiming at one's own death. That supports the Church's distinction between laying down one's life and aiming at death.
