# Blind review: Pragmatist / anti-representationalist / relativist school

## 1. School and voice

I speak for the pragmatist family: Richard Rorty's anti-representationalism above all, with Dewey's ethics of inquiry and growth, James's pragmatic test and his meliorism, and nearby the relativism of Gilbert Harman (moral judgments relative to the implicit agreements of a group) and David Wong (pluralistic relativism: more than one adequate morality, with human nature and the needs of cooperation ruling some out). Our quarrel is less with the text's moral verdicts, most of which we share, than with its picture of what a moral truth is: a truth that something must *make* true and that a mind must *grasp*, so that the best explanation of the making and the grasping can be weighed. We attack that starting point as well as the steps. We do not say "true for us." We say the child was wronged, and that nothing makes that true, our words included. (All paraphrases of thinkers below are mine; verify.)

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** Rorty: justice is a larger loyalty, the loyalty we owe kin and neighbors widened in conversation and imagination until it takes in the stranger (Rorty, "Justice as a Larger Loyalty," verify). What anyone is owed is what our widest loyalty, made consistent by our own best reasons, commits us to give. Harman adds that duties not to harm come out stronger than duties to aid because conventions are struck by bargaining between the strong and the weak (verify). **Verdict: coherent, and it borrows.** It has no principle prior to an inherited practice. Its content (the stranger counts, cruelty is worst) is inherited, in good part from Christianity. Rorty says as much and does not mind.

**2. A hard dilemma.** Take the Manchester conjoined twins (Re A, Court of Appeal, 2000; verify). Separating them would save Jodie and certainly end Mary's life; without surgery both would die within months. The Catholic parents refused, and Catholic moralists were divided on whether the operation was a direct killing (verify). The Deweyan method is dramatic rehearsal: imagine each course through to its consequences for everyone touched, including what each verdict would do to medicine, law and the standing of parents, and choose the course that best answers all the demands in play. I would side with the court and separate, while treating "never deliberately kill the innocent" as a rule so entrenched it is almost never open to revision. **Verdict: coherent, and it does not borrow the book's definitions.** It uses ordinary honesty, fairness and care, not the book's truth, justice, mercy and self-gift. But it **breaks with A6**: the line is a very strong rule, not an exceptionless one, and I give it up openly.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** Rorty: nothing grounds it, and asking for a ground adds no force to the commitment. We hold, as the best thing our moral tradition has produced, that the newborn, the dying, the severely disabled and the enemy are each fully one of us, and that she had that standing before anyone said so. That last sentence is true, said in our language, which is the only place anything is said. Wong: equal dignity of the never-conscious belongs to some adequate moralities and need not belong to all. **Verdict: the Rortian answer is coherent but borrows.** It keeps the conclusion with no premise that reaches the infant no one is drawn to hold. The only resource it has is to say that we have committed ourselves to reaching her. The Wongian answer **breaks** the universal form of A4. Harman's breaks A1 for agents outside our agreements.

**4. Ending point for flourishing.** Dewey: growth itself is the only moral end, and there is no fixed final end. James: satisfy as many demands as can be satisfied while frustrating as few as possible ("The Moral Philosopher and the Moral Life," verify). Rorty: a world with less cruelty and more room for self-creation. **Verdict: coherent as guiding principles, but it breaks on "ultimately."** We deny there is an ultimate good, and that is our position, not an evasion. "Growth" strains for the dying and for the one who will never reason. There, as in challenge 3, we fall back on solidarity: an extension of commitment rather than a measure of her good.

## 3. Debate highlights

**E1. The explanatory demand itself (C preface, W2, conclusion).**
*Attack.* The whole case is an inference to the best explanation of moral truths: who or what "makes" A true. For us that is the wrong question. A1–A4 are moves within our practice of giving reasons. They need no truthmaker, and "held first in the Mind that made her" adds nothing to the reasons we already have against torturing a child. An explanation that changes no reason we give is idle.
*Defense.* Every account stops somewhere (C preface). Declining to ask is itself a stopping point, and the text says so: the pragmatist "declines to ask what makes A true." The text also gives a pragmatist's own reason to think the difference is not idle. By James's test (*Pragmatism* III), the hypotheses differ looking forward: one promises that what is good is kept, the other does not. And the text claims no compulsion (conclusion; "this case cannot compel him either").
*Rejoinder.* Granted: this is a standoff over where explanation stops, and the text concedes it is one. But then the text must not describe the difference as "independent of any words." Rorty grants that sense already: the slave was wronged whether or not anyone said so. The only difference that survives is James's forward-looking one, which is a difference in hope. **Held with repair** (R5).

**E2. How the text states my school (A heading, W1 frame, W2 parenthetical, C row).**
*Attack.* Three times the text gives pragmatism a thesis it rejects. (a) The A heading and C row make Rorty a constructivist, for whom "our widening 'we'" or "our going on speaking" is what *makes* the claims true. Rorty refuses the anti-realist side of the realism debate as firmly as the realist side: nothing makes true sentences true, our speaking included. (b) The W2 parenthetical files pragmatists under "those for whom moral words voice refined feeling." Dewey attacked emotivism directly (*Theory of Valuation*, verify); pragmatists deny the split between feeling and cognition that expressivism needs. (c) It then excuses pragmatists from W2 ("need no meeting of law and mind") when we have a definite answer to W2, given in E3.
*Defense.* The phrases can be read charitably: "our going on speaking this way" as shorthand for practices of justification. And the C row elsewhere states the view fairly ("does not deny A"; "answers from within").
*Rejoinder.* The charitable reading does not rescue (b), which is an outright misfiling. **Held with repair** (R1, R2, R3, R5). None of this damages an inference, but a book that promises to state each rival "as its best defenders state it" should state this one that way.

**E3. A7 and the W2 dilemma: what binds a reasoner?**
*Attack.* The text's dilemma (Power and rank row, W2) says truthfulness either binds every reasoning mind by what a mind is, which raises W2's question, or binds only those who want it. I take the first horn without the theism. Following the evidence is constitutive of believing and asserting at all: to assert is to take on a commitment answerable to the reasons of others (Sellars, Brandom; Peirce's case for the method of science in "The Fixation of Belief"; verify). The norm comes with the practice. No separate law exists for the mind to fit, so nothing needs explaining.
*Defense.* (i) A constitutive norm binds within the game. A7 forbids something the game does not forbid: quietly declining to look, for fear, at a question no one else will ever ask. (ii) If the practice binds every reasoner by what reasoning is, the question returns in a new form: why is there a kind of animal whose practice is answerable to truth in logic and number, with results that converge across every culture? W2 calls the naturalist answers "real answers" and claims only a judgment about where explanation may stop.
*Rejoinder.* On (ii), the pragmatist denies there are "necessary truths" in the sense of objects grasped. Necessity is the standing we give rules we have made indispensable (Quine, Wittgenstein; verify). On (i), the defense is right, and it lands on Rorty. In "Religious Faith, Intellectual Responsibility, and Romance" he sides with James against Clifford: responsibility to evidence is owed to those with whom we share projects, and a privatized belief may be kept for the comfort it gives (verify). So the pragmatist does pay A7's "even if no one would ever know." The constitutive reply does not escape the dilemma; it relocates the cost. **Held**, standoff at parity on (ii), and a point to the text on (i). The text should record that cost in the C row (R5).

**E4. Moral progress in W2.**
*Attack.* W2 says conscience corrects itself against interest, and that "what moves those who hear them is reason holding itself to consistency ... the law of truth again." The history does not show that. Rorty argues that the widening of "we" came by sentimental education: stories, contact, the testimony of the wronged. Appiah's study of how dueling, foot-binding and the Atlantic slave trade ended gives honor and identity a large role (verify). A world of sympathetic animals with no Source behind it predicts self-correction as well as it predicts failure.
*Defense.* W2 already grants that "the push has often come from the wronged themselves." Testimony is the story Rorty means. And the great arguments were often consistency arguments: Douglass's Fourth of July speech convicted the nation by its own declared principles (verify). Sympathy itself widens when the hearer sees a likeness he already counts, which is consistency at work.
*Rejoinder.* Then sympathy and consistency work together, and calling the whole process "the law of truth again" claims more than the history shows. **Held with repair** (R4). The witness survives once the sentence names both causes.

**E5. The hillside test and the relativist (A1, A4, conclusion).**
*Attack.* Harman: one can say that exposing the infant was evil and the Romans were wrong to live so, but "they wronged her," "they ought not," presumes they were party to our agreements. Wong: equal standing for the never-conscious belongs to some adequate moralities, not all. And the conclusion's Moorean test ("which are you surer of") is open to me as well: I am surer that some moral disagreement is deep and faultless than that one morality binds every people.
*Defense.* The test is aimed at a particular, not at a general thesis: this infant, left out to die. That is where Moorean confidence is strongest, and the relativist must say plainly that she was not wronged by them. The text lists exactly that price and does not pretend the test compels. Where disagreement *is* deep among reasonable people (A6's line at every number and against a man's own request; the death penalty), the text says outright that these are "this book's claims, not shared ones."
*Rejoinder.* Fair. The relativist pays A1's "for everyone" and A4's universality openly. The text's selectivity is honest selectivity: it applies the test only where it claims general assent. **Held.**

**E6. The debt to the dead as a captive picture (W1(1)).**
*Attack.* "Owed," "release," "receive," "repay": a forensic ledger has been laid over grief and remorse, a picture that holds us captive (Wittgenstein's phrase). Moral repair is the restoration, among the living, of trust in shared norms and in each other (Margaret Urban Walker, *Moral Repair*, verify). Guilt that "asks to be forgiven by her" is a phenomenology formed by Christian practice.
*Defense.* The fairness rule cuts here. If calling the ledger a "picture" is a genealogy, the text may answer that repair "among the living" is the picture a hope without the dead would produce. More to the point, the text never rests on the ledger. It calls this witness "a clue, not a proof." It concedes that a mind made for living bonds would ask "whether or not she hears," and that what is owed is the same on the second road and the third. And A2's "I did this to *her*" is something Rorty asserts.
*Rejoinder.* Conceded: a standoff at parity, and the text weighs the clue as lightly as I would. **Held.**

**E7. James's test, and James (C row, evil section).**
*Attack.* James himself called the guaranteed eternal order a "moral holiday" for the tender-minded and preferred meliorism, a world where salvation is possible and not promised, with real losses and real losers (*Pragmatism* VIII, verify). And James was no atheist: he held an over-belief in a wider, finite divine order (*Varieties*, postscript; *A Pluralistic Universe*; verify). The evil section's "the pragmatist hopes, with William James ... though nothing promises" presents him as a hope without God.
*Defense.* The text uses James's test only to show that the difference is not idle, and it calls the forward-looking difference a clue that weighs as hope, not evidence (W3 closing: "That is not evidence ... (A7)"). It does not claim James's endorsement.
*Rejoinder.* The use holds. The attribution in the evil section should name Dewey, the cleanly non-theist pragmatist. **Held with repair** (R6).

**E8. Fairness: Darwin's bees (W2).**
*Attack.* "Minds could have been tuned otherwise" is an argument from contingent origins. Would the text let me turn it back, and say minds tuned by belief in a Lawgiver could have been tuned otherwise too?
*Defense.* W2 does not use the bees to debunk. It uses them to ask for an explanation of the fit, and it lets the debunker press from the other side, conceding that "neither settles it." The A heading applies one rule to both sides: an origin defeats a belief only if it would have produced the belief whether or not it was true. The Nietzsche row applies that rule symmetrically.
*Rejoinder.* None needed. The rule is applied evenly. **Held.**

## 4. Ledger

- **A heading** — Holds with repair. The trust-until-defeated rule is our own Neurath's-boat starting point, and the debunking clause works with the cautionary use of "true," which Rorty keeps. The roll call misstates Rorty as holding that the "we" makes the claims true, and omits his denial of A7's strong form (R1).
- **A1** — Holds against Rorty, who asserts it in his own voice. Against Harman it is an honest standoff: he denies the "for everyone" in the sense that would say the outsider had reason to refrain.
- **A2** — Holds. Rorty and Dewey both grant that wronging differs from worsening. The secret-telling case is well chosen and needs no theism.
- **A3** — Holds. We agree that no vote makes cruelty right, and we say so from within.
- **A4** — Holds as a claim we assert. Its grounding is the point at issue, and the text puts the weight where it belongs: on how sure we are, not on the belief's history. The Seneca example is apt and fair.
- **A5** — Holds with repair. Dewey grants that the desired is not yet the desirable. But the closing alternative (ends carried by nature, or given by Someone) omits Dewey's third option, ends formed and tested in inquiry (R7).
- **A6** — Holds as a statement of claims, honestly labeled as the book's own. I break with the absolute and say so. The paragraph on what total-counting accounts can and cannot say is accurate.
- **A7** — Holds, and is the text's best lever against my school. Rorty and James deny its "even if no one would ever know." Note that "against the evidence" (rather than Clifford's "on insufficient evidence") leaves James's will-to-believe untouched, so the premise stays modest.
- **W1 frame** — Holds with repair. The Anscombe framing is excellent. The parenthetical on the pragmatist ("it is ours") half-misstates Rorty, who is the truest heir of Anscombe's own proposal: he lets the law-sense of "obligation" go (R2).
- **W1(1), three roads** — Holds as a clue. The second road is where we stand, and the text describes it fairly. What it says the second road lacks is true, and we pay that cost.
- **W1(1), Catholic answer** — Holds. It is internal doctrine offered as a hope, explicitly not a proof. The closing parenthetical omits a pragmatist reading of "owed" (a normative standing within practice), but nothing turns on that.
- **W1(2)** — Holds. Against my school the claim "her worth is a gift exactly as far as her being is a gift" is a conditional we cannot fault. We simply decline its antecedent's demand for a ground. The call to communion sits in the explanation, not in a premise, so it imposes no Christian-only demand.
- **W1 closing (Lawgiver not a commander)** — Holds. The anti-voluntarism is coherent, and it answers the pragmatist's suspicion that "law" means "command."
- **W2** — Holds with repair. Its main claim is offered honestly as "a judgment about where explanation may stop." It omits the anti-representationalist answer and misfiles pragmatists as expressivists (R3), and it claims more than the history of moral progress shows (R4).
- **W3** — Holds. "That is not evidence ... (A7)" is exactly right, and nothing in it is turned against us unfairly.
- **C: Nothing really binds** — Holds. Fair to Mackie and Street.
- **C: Feeling, refined** — Holds. Fair to the quasi-realist. Since pragmatists are not expressivists, this row is not ours.
- **C: Our best words, widening** — Holds with repair. Its tone is fair and its James test is well placed. But it states the difference in a form Rorty grants already, names no A-cost for Rorty (A7) or Harman (A1), and cross-references W3 where A4/W2 is meant (R5).
- **C: Reason's own law** — Holds. Not our school; the costs are paid openly.
- **C: What we owe each other** — Holds. Its "takes as given" is the same move we make, so it is fairly put.
- **C: Care between persons** — Holds. "Ought to have been wanted, not that anyone did want her" is a precise and fair stopping point.
- **C: Well-being, counted impartially** — Holds.
- **C: The flourishing of our kind** — Holds. The same diagnosis applies to Dewey's growth (see §2.4).
- **C: Godless realism** — Holds.
- **C: Impersonal sacred order** — Holds (outside my competence; I see no unfairness).
- **C: The Way** — Holds (same caveat).
- **C: God, or Nature** — Holds.
- **C: The Buddha's Dhamma** — Holds (same caveat).
- **C: History and class** — Holds. The split between Marx's "freedom, not justice" and his analytic heirs is fairly drawn.
- **C: Power and rank** — Holds. The dilemma on truthfulness is the text's strongest single passage against all naturalisms, mine included (E3).
- **C: Freedom that makes its values** — Holds.
- **C: A God who is Love** — Holds. "Reach, not count" is the right claim, and its admission that the clues "answer but do not explain better" is exactly the concession we would demand.
- **Evil, paragraph 1 ("On the other side")** — Holds. It is unusually honest. Nothing here needs our help.
- **Evil, "First"** — Holds. It says plainly what the privation account does not rule out.
- **Evil, "Second"** — Holds. The skeptical-theist step is stated modestly ("weak evidence"), and the text keeps the pattern of natural pain as strong evidence. That is the right split.
- **Evil, "Third" and the hopes** — Holds with repair. The pragmatist hope should be credited to Dewey, not James (R6). "To the children already spent they can offer memory, not repair" is true of us, and we accept it.

- **Conclusion** — Holds. It claims less than the Church permits and says it cannot compel. The final test is aimed where Moorean confidence is strongest. "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth" is the right last word against a sentimentalist reading. Against Rorty, it states a commitment we share and a ground we decline.

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**As written: NONE.**

Every attack my school can press on the starting points (E1, E3) ends in a standoff at parity, and the text already concedes those standoffs in its own words. Every attack on the steps (E2, E4, E7) is repaired by a wording change that leaves the inferences standing.

**Minor items**
1. W2's claim that what moves hearers toward the wronged is "reason holding itself to consistency ... the law of truth again" says more than the history shows. Sympathy and narrative work alongside it (R4).
2. The C row for pragmatism names no A-cost for Rorty or Harman, though both pay one (A7; A1). Naming them strengthens the text (R5).
3. A cross-reference in the C row, "the Church grants much of that history (W3)," points to the wrong witness. A4 and W2 are meant (R5).
4. A5's two-way alternative leaves out Dewey's ends-in-view (R7).

**Misstatements of my school**
1. A heading: "Rorty ... that anything beyond our widening 'we' and its reasons makes them true" implies that, for Rorty, the "we" makes them true. He denies that anything makes them true (R1).
2. W1 frame: "for the pragmatist ... it is ours" half-states him. He would sooner say that "necessity" adds nothing to the reasons. He also takes Anscombe's own course and drops the law-sense (R2).
3. W2 parenthetical: "Those for whom moral words voice refined feeling, the pragmatists" is a misfiling. Pragmatists are not expressivists (R3).
4. C row: "on its account nothing beyond our going on speaking this way makes her claim hold" is the same constructivist reading as (1) (R5).
5. Evil section: "the pragmatist hopes, with William James" presents James as a hope without God. He held a theistic over-belief (R6).

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Whether moral truths need a ground or truthmaker at all (E1).
- Whether there is a "fit" between mind and necessary truth to explain, or only rules we have made indispensable (E3 ii).
- Whether equal dignity of the never-conscious needs more than our commitment to it (§2.3; conclusion).
- Whether guilt's address to the dead is evidence of anything (E6). The text already says it is a clue only.
- A6's exceptionless line, which I reject and the text labels as its own claim.

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1 (A heading).**
Old: `Rorty, who asserts most of them, that anything beyond our widening "we" and its reasons makes them true;`
New: `Rorty, who asserts most of them, that their truth needs, or could have, any ground beyond the reasons we give one another for them, or that a belief kept private, in which no one else has a stake, must answer to the evidence (A7);`

**R2 (W1 frame).**
Old: `(Some keep the words and let that sense go: for the pragmatist the wrong is not up to any one of us, but it is ours; for the relativist it is wrong relative to a morality. C weighs what that costs.)`
New: `(Some keep the words and let that sense go: the pragmatist holds that the wrong is not up to any of us, and that calling it necessary adds nothing to our reasons against it, and Rorty takes the very course Anscombe proposed, letting the law-sense of "obligation" go; for the relativist it is wrong relative to a morality. C weighs what that costs.)`

**R3 (W2 parenthetical).**
Old: `(Those for whom moral words voice refined feeling, the pragmatists, and those who with Kant make the law the form of a reasoning will need no meeting of law and mind; theirs is the question in C.`
New: `(Those for whom moral words voice refined feeling, those who with Kant make the law the form of a reasoning will, and the pragmatists, who hold that the norms of inquiry come with the practice of giving reasons and that "grasping necessary truths" is a picture of mind they decline, need no meeting of law and mind; theirs is the question in C.`

**R4 (W2, moral progress).**
Old: `what moves those who hear them is reason holding itself to consistency with goods it already sees: the law of truth again.`
New: `what moves those who hear them is sympathy widened by their stories and, with it, reason holding itself to consistency with goods it already sees: in that part, the law of truth again.`

**R5 (C, Our best words, widening).**
(a) Old: `and the Church grants much of that history (W3).`
New: `and the Church grants much of that history (A4, W2).`
(b) Old: `to one who is not, it has nothing to say, and this case cannot compel him either. The difference lies elsewhere: on its account nothing beyond our going on speaking this way makes her claim hold; on ours, it holds independently of any words, first in the Mind that made her.`
New: `to one who is not, it has nothing to say, and this case cannot compel him either. Its costs are elsewhere. For Harman, to say that those who exposed the child wronged her presumes they shared our agreements; one may call the deed evil, not say that they had reason to refrain (A1). And Rorty, with James, holds that a belief answers to the evidence only where others have a stake in it, so that a comforting belief kept private may be kept (A7). The difference lies further still. It will say, in its own words, that she was wronged whether or not anyone ever said so; what it denies is that anything makes that true, our words included. On ours, her claim holds first in the Mind that made her.`

**R6 (Evil section, "Third").**
Old: `and the pragmatist hopes, with William James, that the world can be made better by our effort, though nothing promises that it will.`
New: `and the pragmatist hopes, with John Dewey, that the world can be made better by our effort, though nothing promises that it will.`

**R7 (A5).**
Old: `Whether that means our nature carries its own ends, as Aristotle held, and whether Someone gave it those ends, is what W must weigh.`
New: `Whether that means our nature carries its own ends, as Aristotle held, or only that inquiry forms and tests ends-in-view, as Dewey held, and whether Someone gave our nature its ends, is what W must weigh.`

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **Rorty is Anscombe's truest secular heir.** He does what she proposed: he drops the law-conception of "obligation" while keeping the verdicts. Saying so sharpens W1's frame. The best accounts that keep the word "obligation" in her sense need a law, and the clearest atheist who sees this gives the sense up rather than the God.
- **A7 is the lever against my school, and the text underuses it.** Rorty's privatized-belief view and James's will-to-believe are where pragmatism visibly pays A7. Naming that cost in the C row (R5) makes the Nietzsche dilemma bite on pragmatists as well, and fairly.
- **The pragmatist's real question is "what difference does it make?"** The text's best answer is already in hand: James's own forward-looking test. Keep it, and keep calling it a difference in hope, not evidence. That restraint is what makes it unanswerable.
- **On moral progress, Douglass is a better witness than consistency in the abstract.** An argument from a people's own declared principles, voiced by the wronged himself, combines testimony and consistency in one act and fits R4 exactly (verify for any quotation).
- **Dewey's "growth" as the end of life** fails at the same place the secular Aristotelian's end fails: the dying and the never-reasoning. That is a natural one-line addition wherever the book contrasts ends that wait on powers with an end that does not.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."
- "Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent."
- "to one who is not, it has nothing to say, and this case cannot compel him either."
- "looking forward, one promises that what is good is kept and the other does not."
- "The rebel can take her side; he cannot keep her."
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it."

## 8. Score

**7.5 / 10 as written; 8 with the repairs.** The case never claims to compel the pragmatist, concedes every real standoff in its own words, and keeps its verdicts where we share them. Its faults against my school are misstatements of the view, not failed inferences.

**Honest ceiling for any theistic case against my school: about 8.** Against an anti-representationalist, a theistic argument can at most show that refusing the explanatory question is costly (A7, the dead, the infant no one held) and that the theist's hope makes a forward-looking difference. It can never show that the refusal is irrational, because the refusal rejects the very demand the argument answers.
