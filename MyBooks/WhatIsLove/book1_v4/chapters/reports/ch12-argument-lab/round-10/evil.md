# Blind review: the problem of evil and divine hiddenness

## 1. School and voice

I argue as the atheist who says *a good God would never allow this*. My materials are the logical problem (Mackie), the evidential problem (Rowe's fawn, Draper's hypothesis of indifference), divine hiddenness (Schellenberg, and Maitzen's point about who gets to know God depending on where one is born), Stephen Law's evil-god symmetry, Marilyn Adams's horrendous evils and Thomas Talbott's case against eternal hell, Morriston and Wielenberg on the divine-command killings, and Ivan Karamazov's refusal. My ethics is godless robust realism (Wielenberg, Parfit, Enoch, all verify). I need real moral truths, because my argument says what a perfectly good being would not permit. So I do not deny A1–A4. I deny that a Person stands behind them, and the reason is the world those truths judge. My best case is not that the text is careless. It is that the text's distinctive claim, that God wanted *her*, is the claim the hillside infant tests.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

1. **Starting point for justice.** Persons have basic moral claims grounded in what they are: subjects of a life, who can be harmed, betrayed and spent. These are necessary ethical facts, and the "making" relation between natural features and moral status is a brute necessity (Wielenberg, verify). What anyone is owed is what respects those claims. **Holds**, and it stops at a brute necessity. That puts it at parity with the text's own stopping point ("every account has a first principle, ours included").

2. **A hard dilemma.** Take conjoined twins: one twin's heart and lungs keep both alive, and both will die within months unless they are separated, and separation kills the weaker twin (the Manchester case, 2000). My principles: (a) no innocent may be killed as a means; (b) no one has a claim on another person's body for life support (Thomson); (c) where (a) and (b) conflict, ask whether the death is aimed at or is the removal of support that was never owed. My verdict: separate, because the weaker twin's death follows from withdrawing support, not from her being used. **Holds without borrowing the book's definitions**, though it does use "owed," and the text will ask what grounds that. Note that the Catholic bishops of England opposed separation (verify). So my principles reach a verdict the Church did not, and I do not pretend otherwise.

3. **Equal, unconditional dignity.** I ground it in kind-membership: being a member of the human kind, whose nature is rational, not in present capacity. The text concedes this is "much as we ground it in what she is." **Holds, but partly borrows.** I take as given that kind-nature confers *equal* standing regardless of degree, and I cannot say what the kind is *for*. I deny it needs a "for." At the edges (anencephaly) some in my school bend, and I record that as a break for them, not for me.

4. **Ultimate flourishing.** An objective list: knowledge, love, friendship, achievement, pleasure, virtue. The highest is a finite life lived in loving relationships. There is no last word beyond death. **Coherent, and it breaks at one point the text names exactly.** On my view the hillside infant is never repaired, the debt to her is never received, and the dead are honored, never answered. I pay that cost. But that unrepaired condition is not evidence against me. It is what my argument observes.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1: the logical problem (Mackie).**
*Attack.* An omnipotent, perfectly good being would eliminate evil as far as it can. Evil exists. And the text itself removes the free-will escape: "God kept Mary from every sin by grace without forcing her yes … so He can bring a free creature to say yes without overriding her."
*Defense.* The logical problem needs only a *possible* morally sufficient reason (Plantinga). The text supplies one that does not depend on God being unable to win a free yes: "He also willed creatures who can give their love back of their own accord, in a life where it could be refused." A world where love is offered, can be refused, and can be returned is a distinct good. It is "a good, not a necessity," and no contradiction follows.
*Rejoinder.* Mary's love was freely given and never refused, so the good of freely returned love does not require any actual refusal. What remains is the good of a real *risk*. That is enough to block a strict contradiction, and I grant the logical form is answered. But the text has knowingly moved the whole weight to the evidential problem.
**Held.** The text already says "freedom does not finish the answer."

**Exchange 2: Rowe's fawn and the hillside infant.**
*Attack.* There are evils no good we know of requires. The text names the good it hopes for, eternal life, and in the same paragraph says God "can give the highest good as pure gift" to the child who dies before choosing (CCC 1250). So the known good does not need the evil. What justifies it must be some good we do not know, which is skeptical theism. Skeptical theism cuts the root of A: if we cannot judge what a good being would allow, we cannot trust "almost everyone sees these at once."
*Defense.* The text does not use unknown goods to justify anything. It separates two questions. On whether each evil will be *answered*, our blindness is expected if the good is finished only in eternal life. On whether a world of real causes *needs this much pain*, it says "the evidence is not weak." It also keeps "we know enough of goodness to know what a good God would value," which is why the weight is real. That is not CORNEA-style skepticism. It concedes Rowe's point where Rowe is strongest.
*Rejoinder.* Agreed, and that is the honest position. The only defect is the word "answered," which can be read as "justified." See repair R6.
**Held** (as a concession), with a minor repair.

**Exchange 3: Maitzen, ordinary morality implies atheism.**
*Attack.* If God permits no evil unless He draws good from that very evil (CCC 324), and the good is "offered to her as well," then every victim's suffering is, all things considered, not bad for her. Then my reason to stop it, that it is bad for her on balance, is gone. Theism dissolves the duty to rescue.
*Defense.* The duty to rescue was never a forecast of outcomes. Charity binds me to my neighbor's good as it is now. Evil "never becomes a good" (CCC 312), so a harm God will one day heal is still a real harm, and a person may not inflict it or allow it when bound to prevent it. The text says so: "His permission never excuses ours (CCC 2269)." The comparison is the certainty of heaven, which licenses no one to kill a baptized infant.
*Rejoinder.* That works for a deontology, and the book is one. The text asserts it in one clause but never says *why* the hope of repair does not discount the duty. One sentence would close the gap.
**Held with repair** (R5).

**Exchange 4: Stephen Law's evil god.**
*Attack.* Every theodicy has a mirror. An evil god gives creatures a moral sense so they can suffer guilt, a longing to be wholly loved that is never answered, a debt to the dead they can never pay, and a hiddenness that makes seekers despair. W1(1) and W3 describe exactly what an evil designer would build. The text says these "fit a loving God better." They do not. What we observe of them in this life fits malice as well as love.
*Defense.* The text's answer is metaphysical, not a symmetry claim. Evil is a privation, there is no supreme evil (ST I q.49 a.3), and "a will that lacks nothing has nothing to grasp at." It also states the cost openly: "a reader who holds that malice could be as full and real as love must weigh a dark god against the whole case." The longing and the debt are offered as "clues of fit, not evidence."
*Rejoinder.* The privation premise is a real premise, and Law's reply that it begs the question is answered only at parity. I accept that standoff. But "fit a loving God better" claims more than "clues of fit." The longing would be *answered* only by a loving God; what we *see* of it fits both.
**Held with repair** (R3).

**Exchange 5: hell (Adams, Talbott). The strongest exchange.**
*Attack.* Put four of the text's own sentences side by side.
1. Each person's dignity "cannot be lost, not even by her own refusal" (A4).
2. God "governs each person for her own sake," "never spends her as a mere means against her own good," and His first word to each of us is "it is good that you exist" (W1(2), W3, C).
3. God "can bring a free creature to say yes without overriding her" (Mary).
4. Hell is real and eternal, and some may refuse to the end.

For a person lost forever, existence is an infinite evil to him. Christ says of one man, "It would have been better for that man if he had not been born" (Matthew 26:24). If God could have won that man's yes without overriding him (3), then permitting his final ruin cannot have been for *his* sake (2). It was for nothing, or for the whole. Aquinas's own account of reprobation explains it by the whole: God shows His goodness by mercy in some and by justice in others, and the perfection of the universe calls for both (ST I q.23 a.5 ad 3, paraphrase, verify). That is the whole-over-part logic the book denies for persons everywhere else. Adams's test is that a good God must be good *to each* person, so that each one's life is a great good to her on the whole. Eternal loss fails it.
*Defense.* Distinguish God's antecedent will, which wills every person's salvation (1 Timothy 2:4), from His consequent will, which respects a refusal held to the end (ST I q.19 a.6 ad 1). "God predestines no one to go to hell" (CCC 1037), and no one is lost except by his own willful turning away. Mary's preservation was a singular privilege. Nothing in it shows that God could so win every will, in every world He might make, without losing some other good: Molinists hold that some creatures would refuse under any grace (verify). Existence stays a good even to the lost, and Augustine argued that even the wretched do not wish not to be (*City of God* XI.27, paraphrase, verify). The Church prays that no one be lost (CCC 1058), and she has never declared that any particular human being is.
*Rejoinder.* Each move is a real one. The text as written blocks the Molinist move, because its Mary sentence states the general capacity ("He can bring a free creature to say yes") as the reason freedom does not finish the answer. The Molinist escape also costs something: if God creates someone He knows will refuse under every grace, for the sake of a world that contains others, that person is spent for the whole. Augustine's point weighs little against Matthew 26:24. The CCC 1058 hope is the strongest reply, and it is not in the text. As written, the text concedes hell's weight ("the heaviest thing there") but never says that its own claim "God governs each person for her own sake" is the claim hell puts in doubt, and its Mary sentence supplies Talbott's key premise.
**Failed as written. Held with repair** (R1, R1b) to an honest standoff.

**Exchange 6: the divine-command killings (Morriston, Wielenberg).**
*Attack.* "Kill both man and woman, infant and suckling" (1 Samuel 15:3). The text holds with Aquinas that God could command this without injustice. Three consequences follow.
1. The absolute in A4/A6 is not grounded in her worth alone. It depends on who holds the right, and the text grants this ("what protects her is her own worth … and that no number of others has any right of their own").
2. The book's own method turns against it. The conclusion asks the reader which he is surer of: that the infant was wronged, or any argument that she was not. Applied here, the reader is far surer that soldiers who cut down suckling infants did wrong than that an ancient narrative reports a real divine command. By the book's own Moorean rule, he should conclude the command was not God's.
3. The narrative's stated ground is what Amalek did generations earlier (1 Samuel 15:2), and the law God gave Israel says: "The son shall not suffer for the iniquity of the father" (Ezekiel 18:20).

*Defense.* Aquinas's ground is not punishment of the infants. It is that the death of nature comes to all, guilty and innocent, by God's decree on account of original sin, so He may take any life at any time without injustice (ST I-II q.94 a.5 ad 2). The reader's certainty is about men killing *by their own right*, and the text never said the line binds the Lord of life. "No one may now claim such a command" (CCC 67). Dei Verbum 15 and Verbum Domini 42 put these texts in a revelation given in stages. The text names the cost openly ("Kant protects her here more firmly than this book does") and leaves the wars "on the other side of the scale."
*Rejoinder.* The Moorean argument does not go away. The reader's certainty about the infants under the sword does not feel like a certainty about human jurisdiction. It feels like a certainty about *her*. A Catholic reading is open that the text does not take: that the herem passages give Israel's understanding at a stage God was still correcting, not a report of a command God actually gave. The Church permits that reading, and the book's own method favors it (verify the PBC's 2014 *Inspiration and Truth of Sacred Scripture* on violent texts). The text chooses the harder road, which is its right, and it pays.
**Held, at a heavy cost openly paid.** An honest standoff, not a defect.

**Exchange 7: hiddenness and geography (Schellenberg, Maitzen).**
*Attack.* A perfectly loving God would make sure that anyone not resisting Him can, at any time, be in a conscious relationship with Him. Such people exist. Whether one knows God by name tracks birthplace, which the text concedes. The text's own definition, a love that "wants to be known back," makes the premise stronger.
*Defense.* The text denies only the *now*. A real yes can be given before the Name is known: the Spirit offers every person a way to share in Christ's Paschal Mystery "in a manner known only to God" (GS 22; LG 16). God's love comes first through persons, a mother's love before a creed. The knowing is completed at the end.
*Rejoinder.* Schellenberg's point is that every period without conscious relationship is a loss to the creature, and the text gives no reason for the delay. It says so itself: "part of an answer, not a full answer."
**Held** as a conceded standoff.

**Exchange 8: Ivan, and the "invitation."**
*Attack.* The invitation says my protest stands on A1–A4 and asks me to follow it to its source. My protest needs A1–A4 only as strong as godless realism makes them. Showing that I am a moral realist is a point for God only if realism needs God, and the text calls that question (W2) "a judgment about where explanation may stop." Ivan also does not "weigh it otherwise." He refuses the harmony *even if it is true*.
*Defense.* The text calls it "an invitation, not a reply," and lets the protest stand as "this weight at its strongest." Its point is narrower: the protester wants her kept, and on his own view no one keeps her. "The rebel … takes her side; he cannot keep her."
*Rejoinder.* Granted as an invitation, and that is fair. But Ivan is misdescribed.
**Held**, with a minor repair (R2).

**Exchange 9: where the surplus sits.**
*Attack.* The text grants that godless realism grounds equal worth "much as we ground it in what she is." What theism adds is that Someone *wanted* her. That added claim is what the hillside infant tests. Realism accommodates A4 and pays nothing on the evil side. Theism's distinctive surplus explains nothing observable that realism cannot, and it alone is made improbable by the pattern of pain. So on the text's own concessions, the evidence tips toward me.
*Defense.* The surplus is not only "she was wanted." It includes why there is a kind whose worth does not follow ability, what that kind is for, and why minds built for survival grasp necessary truths of logic, number and right (W2). One Source for all of these is a real explanatory gain, by unification ("reach, not count"). The text never claims more than a judgment ("this book judges") and says an honest reader may weigh it differently.
*Rejoinder.* That is a standoff at W2. For a reader at parity on W2, evil decides it. The conclusion asserts the balance but never names what carries it against a weight it calls "heavy" and "not weak."
**Held with repair** (R8). This is a standoff, not a defect, once the conclusion says what it rests on.

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**Serious: the lost, and the claim that God wills each person for her own sake.** (Exchange 5.)
The text holds that God governs each person for her own sake and never spends anyone against her own good. It holds that a person's dignity cannot be lost. It holds that God "can bring a free creature to say yes without overriding her." And it holds that some may be lost forever. For such a person existence is an infinite evil (Matthew 26:24). With the Mary premise stated generally, the text has no reason left why God permits that loss for that person's sake. The only candidate reasons serve the whole, which the text forbids for persons everywhere else. The text puts hell "on the scale" but never says that its own universal claim is the claim in doubt. Two things are missing: the hope the Church actually holds out (CCC 1058) and a qualification on the Mary sentence. With R1 and R1b this becomes an honest standoff, the same as hiddenness.

No Fatal objection stands.

**Minor.**
- "Fit a loving God better" (Evil, First) claims evidential fit that the text elsewhere disclaims. A dark god fits what we observe of the longing and the unpaid debt just as well (R3).
- "Yet she is held by Him" (W3) states as fact what the next sentence holds only in hope (R4).
- The duty-to-rescue clause asserts but does not explain why the hope of repair does not discount the duty (Maitzen) (R5).
- "How each particular evil is answered" can be read as "justified," which would be pure skeptical theism. The text means "repaired" (R6).
- The conclusion asserts a balance against a weight it calls heavy without naming what holds it (R8).

**Misstatements of my school.**
- Ivan Karamazov does not "weigh it otherwise." He refuses the weighing: even if the harmony is real and the account balances, he returns the ticket. That is a moral refusal, not a different estimate, and no weighing can meet it (R2).
- Otherwise the text represents my school accurately and often more strongly than its critics usually do. That includes Maitzen's birthplace point, Draper's pattern argument, Talbott's lever (via Mary), Law's dark god, and Rowe's fawn.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects).**
- The privation thesis against Law's evil god.
- W2, where explanation may stop: a Source versus brute necessity plus third-factor fit.
- The divine-command killings. Aquinas's principle is coherent, and the cost against Kant is openly paid. The book's own Moorean method favors the reading on which the command was Israel's, not God's, and that remains a pressure, not a refutation.
- Hiddenness: "denies only the now," with an admittedly partial reason.
- The natural-evil pattern. The text concedes the evidence "is not weak."
- Ivan's refusal, once it is correctly described: nothing in reason answers it, and the text's reply, what is offered to the child herself, is a hope it labels as a hope.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact old → new)

**R1 (Evil, first paragraph).**
Old: "why one will is won by His grace and another refuses it to the end, the Church does not explain, and she lets her theologians differ over it, holding only that no one is lost except by his own willful turning away from God, held to the end (CCC 1037)."
New: "why one will is won by His grace and another refuses it to the end, the Church does not explain, and she lets her theologians differ over it, holding only that no one is lost except by his own willful turning away from God, held to the end (CCC 1037). Here the objection is at its strongest. One who is lost keeps a dignity that cannot be lost (A4), and of one man Christ said, 'It would have been better for that man if he had not been born' (Matthew 26:24). If the One who could have won that man's yes did not, reason cannot show how governing him to that end was for his own sake, and this book does not claim to. The Church has never declared that any particular human being is lost; she prays that no one be (CCC 1058), for with God all things are possible (Matthew 19:26). This book's claim that God wills each of us for her own sake is held, for the one who refuses to the end, in that hope and under this weight."

**R1b (Evil, Second).**
Old: "This book's claim is narrower: whatever good is drawn, she is not spent as a mere means for it, and the good hoped for is offered to her as well."
New: "This book's claim is narrower: whatever good is drawn, she is not spent as a mere means for it, and the good hoped for is offered to her as well. That is said of those who suffer; of one who refuses God to the end, it can be said only that the good was offered him, and that is the weight of hell, above."

**R2 (Ivan, Evil, first paragraph).**
Old: "and Ivan Karamazov, who would hand back his ticket to any harmony bought with one child's tears, weighs it otherwise."
New: "and Ivan Karamazov does not weigh it at all: even if the harmony were real and the account came out even, he would hand back his ticket rather than accept a harmony bought with one child's tears. His is a refusal, not a calculation, and no weighing answers it; only what is offered to the child herself could (see the second point below)."

**R3 (Evil, First).**
Old: "the debt to the dead and the longing to hear that it is good that *you* exist fit a loving God better, but as clues (W1(1), W3)."
New: "the debt to the dead and the longing to hear that it is good that *you* exist could be answered only by a loving God, but they are clues, not evidence: what we see of them in this life, a debt unpaid and a longing unanswered, a dark god or an indifferent world would leave just as we find it (W1(1), W3)."

**R4 (W3, hiddenness paragraph).**
Old: "For the infant no one holds, there is no mother's love to carry His love to her; yet she is held by Him."
New: "For the infant no one holds, there is no mother's love to carry His love to her; yet He who made her does not forget her (Isaiah 49:15)."

**R5 (Evil, Second).**
Old: "The hands that pull a child from the fire are part of what He made real, so His permission never excuses ours (CCC 2269)."
New: "The hands that pull a child from the fire are part of what He made real, so His permission never excuses ours (CCC 2269). Nor does the hope that He will repair what we fail to prevent lessen what we owe her now: we are bound to her good as it is today, not to a forecast of how her story ends, and a harm He will one day heal is still a harm we were bound to stop (CCC 312)."

**R6 (Evil, Second).**
Old: "what we would not expect to see, if the good is finished only in eternal life and in a creation set free (Romans 8:21; CCC 314, 1046–1047), is how each particular evil is answered. So for one weighing by reason alone, not seeing how each evil is answered is weak evidence that it is not."
New: "what we would not expect to see, if the good is finished only in eternal life and in a creation set free (Romans 8:21; CCC 314, 1046–1047), is how each particular evil is repaired. So for one weighing by reason alone, not seeing how each evil is repaired is weak evidence that it is not; it is no evidence that each one was needed, and that is the next question."

**R7 (Evil, the invitation).**
Old: "Whoever protests against God on the suffering child's behalf does not refute himself; his protest is this weight at its strongest."
New: "Whoever protests against God on the suffering child's behalf does not refute himself; his protest is this weight at its strongest. Scripture keeps such protest: after Job had cried out against his sufferings, the Lord said to the friends who had defended Him, 'you have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has' (Job 42:7)."

**R8 (Conclusion).**
Old: "Evil weighs against that conclusion, heavily; in this book's judgment it does not overturn it."
New: "Evil weighs against that conclusion, heavily; in this book's judgment it does not overturn it. What holds the conclusion up against that weight is chiefly W1(2) and W2: that a worth no total outweighs is best explained as a gift of being, and that law and mind fit because they have one Source. A reader who judges, with the godless realist, that those truths need nothing behind them will find that evil tips the scale the other way, and this book does not presume that he is at fault (CCC 847)."
(Note: say "not at fault," not "not in error." The latter would contradict *Dei Filius* ch. 2 / CCC 36.)

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights.**
- **The weight and the worth are the same fact.** The more the book proves that the hillside infant is worth as much as anyone, the heavier its problem of evil becomes. The text half-sees this ("so we who say it must give an account of the child"). Saying it outright would make the chapter's honesty its argument: the believer does not escape the problem of evil by believing in equal worth, he takes it on.
- **Every rival that grounds A4 without a Person pays nothing on the evil side.** That is the real price of theism's explanatory surplus, and the chapter should name it as the trade the reader is weighing.
- **The book's defense of God against "spending persons" rests on two supports.** One is jurisdiction (the Lord of life). The other is defeat for each person (Adams): the good is offered to her. The second is unavailable for the lost and thin for animals. Naming those two supports explicitly would show the reader exactly where the defense stands and where it cannot reach.
- **Job 42:7 gives the protester scriptural standing.** It balances the Job 38 citation, which on its own reads as a refusal to hear.
- **Ivan's refusal is not answered by weighing.** It can be met only by what is offered to the child herself, which is the text's own best move (the Second point, Spe Salvi 44 and 47). Pointing Ivan there is stronger than calling him a different weigher.

**Lines worth keeping.**
- "We say that it is good that she exists, and that Someone willed her for her own sake, and so we who say it must give an account of the child, the account we would ask of anyone who loved her and could have spared her."

## 8. Score

**7/10.** The text concedes nearly every point my school presses at full strength, answers the logical problem, and keeps the evidential problem at an honest standoff. One Serious defect remains as written: the claim that God wills each person for her own sake sits unqualified beside eternal loss and a God who, by the text's own Mary premise, could have won every free yes. R1 and R1b repair it to a standoff.

**My honest ceiling for any theistic case against my school: 8/10.** Reason can block the logical problem and keep the evidential problem at parity. It cannot make the pattern of natural pain, or the infant who dies before she can choose, into evidence for a loving God. The best a theistic case can do is what this one nearly does: carry that weight openly and set against it a judgment the reader may share or not.
