# Blind review: the Confucian school

## 1. School and voice

I speak for the Ru tradition across its main forms: Confucius and Mencius (humaneness, *ren*, rooted in family affection and extended outward; a nature that is good), Xunzi (a nature that must be shaped, rites as the human way, a Heaven that does not answer prayers), Zhu Xi (one principle, *li*, whole in every thing; the "heart of Heaven and Earth" that gives life), Wang Yangming (the heart is principle; innate knowing, *liangzhi*; humaneness that forms one body with all things), and Mou Zongsan (an infinite moral heart that knows things as they are in themselves), with modern readers such as Tu Weiming (the person as a center of relationships), P. J. Ivanhoe, Stephen Angle, Irene Bloom and Joseph Chan on dignity and rights. We are not theists in the book's sense. We revere Heaven, and the oldest strata of our texts speak of Heaven almost as Someone, but our mature tradition locates the moral law in a nature conferred by Heaven and realized in human relations and rites. We argue in good faith: the text has studied us, and I judge it on what it says.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** Righteousness (*yi*), what is fitting, grows from the heart that is ashamed of wrong and recoils from it, one of the four beginnings all humans have (*Mencius* 2A6, 6A6; verify). What anyone is owed is set by humaneness extended outward through the five relations, by reciprocity (do not impose on others what you would not want, *Analects* 15.24), and by a floor owed to everyone: no innocent may be killed, and the widow, widower, orphan and childless come first in government (*Mencius* 1B5). Xunzi adds that rites allot goods so that desire and resources fit. **Holds.** It is coherent and borrows nothing. Our idiom is "fitting" rather than "owed in her own right," but Mencius makes the floor non-negotiable.

**2. A hard dilemma.** Shun is emperor and his father kills a man (*Mencius* 7A35). The judge Gao Yao must arrest him, and Shun may not obstruct the law his office upholds. Shun gives up the throne "like a worn sandal," carries his father off to the coast, and lives there content. The principles are filial humaneness as the root, rightness to the office, and discretionary weighing (*quan*) that lets standards yield to their purpose, as when one grasps a drowning sister-in-law's hand although the rites forbid touching (*Mencius* 4A17). **Holds with strain.** The virtues are our own, defined in our own terms. Their overlap with truth, justice, mercy and self-gift is convergence (Romans 2:14–15 would predict it), not borrowing. The strain is real, though: the victim's family gets no justice from Shun's flight, and modern Confucians themselves argue over whether this is family-centered partiality that corrupts justice (Liu Qingping and his critics; verify).

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** Every human has the four beginnings and can become a Yao or a Shun (*Mencius* 6B2). Everyone has within himself a nobility that Heaven confers, which no one else gives and so no one else can take away; Mencius contrasts it with the rank a minister grants and can revoke (6A16–17; verify). For Zhu Xi, principle is whole in every person however clouded his endowment of qi, like the one moon whole in ten thousand rivers. That reaches the newborn and the severely disabled by what they are. **Holds for the newborn, the dying and the disabled. Strains for the enemy and the criminal.** Mencius says the difference between humans and beasts is slight and that ordinary people lose it (4B19). He says the tyrant Zhou had become "a mere fellow," so killing him was not regicide (1B8). The tradition kept capital punishment and graded ranks. What we hold is that the *nature* survives every crime (Ox Mountain's nature is not its bareness, 6A8), but we have no ground that the realized dignity survives. We have no "unconditional" dignity in the modern sense without reconstruction (Bloom, Chan, Angle; verify).

**4. Ultimate good.** Humaneness fully realized: sagehood, fulfilling one's own nature, then others', then the nature of things, and so "forming a triad with Heaven and Earth" (*Zhongyong* 22). It means forming one body with all things (Cheng Hao; Wang Yangming's *Inquiry on the Great Learning*). It is lived in family, rites and government, and survives death through virtue, deeds and words that endure (the "three imperishables," *Zuozhuan*, Duke Xiang 24; verify), and through one's descendants. **Coherent, but it breaks at two points.** First, sagehood is an attainment, and the infant who dies or the person who will never reflect cannot attain it; her nature is fulfilled, if at all, through others' care. Second, for the dead nothing is kept as herself: Zhang Zai's qi disperses back to the Great Void, and the rites honor without reaching.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1. The moon in ten thousand rivers (C, impersonal sacred order).**
*Attack.* The image is misused. The moon is *whole* in each river, not divided among them. So five rivers hold no more moon than one, and no total can outweigh one person: our metaphysics gives A6's refusal to aggregate the same ground the book gets from "willed for her own sake." "The moon never knew which river it was" is the author's gloss. It is not Zhu Xi's point, and it makes us sound more indifferent than we are.
*Defense.* The row already grants our ground for A4 ("worth follows nature, not capacity"), and W1(2) puts the line in "her own worth, which no total outweighs," which is the same form. The row's distinctive claim concerns something else: whether *this* one, the individual rather than the instance of a kind, is wanted, and whether she is kept. A principle whole in each still does not turn toward any one of them.
*Rejoinder.* Granted on wanting and keeping, which are W1(1) and W3, and the text calls both clues. On grounding, the two accounts are at parity, and the gloss should be softened.
**Held with repair** (repair 2).

**Exchange 2. Does Zhu Xi's Heaven have a heart?**
*Attack.* "Has no intention" is too flat. Zhu Xi taught both sides. Principle has no feelings or plans. Yet Heaven and Earth do have a heart, whose work is to give life, and without it a cow could give birth to a horse (*Zhuzi yulei* 1; verify). Humaneness in us *is* that heart.
*Defense.* The row reports both sides: the heart that gives life, which Zhu Xi called humaneness, and principle without intention. A heart that gives life without planning still wants no one in particular.
*Rejoinder.* Fair. The text reports both, and the dispute is over the gloss "lets each one go," which is the author's and not Zhu Xi's.
**Held with repair.**

**Exchange 3. Heaven sees as the people see (escalation on the same row).**
*Attack.* Mencius quotes the *Documents*: Heaven sees as the people see and hears as the people hear (5A5). Heaven knows her, and wants her, through every human heart that rightly responds to her, and the child at the well moves *anyone* (2A6). We need no Someone beyond the human web, because the web is Heaven's heart at work.
*Defense.* Then take the book's hard case, the infant no one holds. No eye sees her, so on 5A5 Heaven does not see her. Mencius's child at the well is seen. The infant left on the hillside is the one whom no heart answered, and Seneca shows that cultures can train the heart not to answer. On the book's account she is wanted "before anyone saw her" (Jeremiah 1:5) by One whose seeing does not wait on ours.
*Rejoinder.* None at parity. Our best reply is that she *ought* to have been seen, and that her nature is whole although unseen, which is the care ethicist's answer. This is the clearest point where the theistic account reaches further than ours, and the row should say so in our own terms.
**Held** (the text holds; my attack fails here).

**Exchange 4. The debt to the dead (W1(1)).**
*Attack.* Confucius sacrificed "as if" the spirits were present (*Analects* 3.12) and would not ask about death before knowing life (11.12). Xunzi made the rites the human way, not dealings with ghosts. The demand that the debt "reach her" and be released by her is a juridical and Augustinian frame that you need not impose. Worse, your own A2 example holds that the dead friend was wronged although nothing reached her. If a wrong can reach the dead without her presence, why can honor and repair not do the same?
*Defense.* Because the two are not symmetric. Being wronged asks nothing of her, but being forgiven asks her to act, and "only someone can forgive." The text does not claim this as proof. It "weighs as much as the reader finds that a debt truly owed to her is one she must be able, one day, to receive," and it states the second road (Xunzi's) fairly.
*Rejoinder.* The asymmetry is real, and we accept it. We deny only that guilt must be released for the moral life to be whole: the cultivated man carries shame, repairs what he can, and continues the bond in rite. This is a standoff at parity, and the text weighs it as one.
**Held.**

**Exchange 5. W2 and the heart that is principle.**
*Attack.* For Wang Yangming the heart *is* principle. There is no fit between law and mind to explain, because they were never two. For Zhu Xi one principle is whole in every nature and every mind ("one principle, many manifestations"), which gives one Source of law, mind and natures with no Person. So W2 cannot favor a personal Source over ours.
*Defense.* W2 claims only one Source and hands the question of personhood to W1 and W3. Its parenthetical says Wang and Mou "agree with this witness."
*Rejoinder.* Then against us the personal conclusion rests on two clues plus the wanting of W1(2). The text is honest about that. One wording slips: Wang and Mou do not find a Source *behind* law and mind but their identity in the heart, and Zhu Xi, the closest fit for "one Source," goes unnamed.
**Held with repair** (repair 3).

**Exchange 6. Mencius and the slaughter in the canon (W1(2), 1 Samuel 15).**
*Attack.* Mencius read in his own revered *Documents* that King Wu's conquest left blood enough to float pestles. He said that believing all of the *Documents* is worse than having none, kept two or three slips of that chapter, and held that the most humane cannot have shed such blood (7B3; verify). A Confucian under a revered canon protects the innocent more firmly than this book does. The book contrasts Heaven, which "gives life," with God, who "wanted *you*," yet only the personal Lord is said to have the right to command a child's death. Our impersonal order never commands anyone's death.
*Defense.* The book states the principle: He who gives being and can give it back does no wrong in calling a life home. No one now may claim such a command (CCC 67), Christ took no one's life, and the One who holds that right does not lose her in death. The book pays this cost openly against Kant. The Church cannot do as Mencius did and deny the inspiration of those pages (*Dei Verbum* 11), but she asks that they be read in the light of Christ (*Dei Verbum* 15; *Verbum Domini* 42).
*Rejoinder.* The defense holds as internal coherence, and the cost is conceded. But it is conceded only against Kant. Mencius is a second, and in one way closer, witness: he faced the same problem in a revered text and chose the innocent over the text. The comparison is incomplete without him.
**Held with repair** (repair 4).

**Exchange 7. The end, given or achieved (C, A God who is Love).**
*Attack.* The "reach" claim is empty against us. Principle is one source for the kind, its worth and its end, which is sagehood open to all.
*Defense.* Sagehood is an attainment. The infant who dies, or the person who will never reflect, falls short of your end as you need not, the same point the text presses against the Aristotelian and the Buddhist. The Church's end "surpasses every human power and comes as a free gift" (CCC 1722), which God can give the infant (CCC 1250, 1261). Her end, not only her nature, does not wait on her powers.
*Rejoinder.* *Zhongyong* 22 has the sage fulfill others' natures, so her nature is fulfilled through others' humaneness. But then it is fulfilled by their achievement, not given to her, and if no one cares for her it is not fulfilled at all.
**Failed** (my attack). The text's claim holds, although the Confucian row does not state this point in our terms.

**Exchange 8. Shame and guilt (A2).**
*Attack.* "Shame that looks only at itself... only guilt asks her pardon" invites the old charge that shame cultures cannot face the victim. Mencius made the heart ashamed of wrong the beginning of righteousness. It looks outward at the wrong done, and in our histories it moves men to beg pardon in person, as Lian Po did, carrying thorns to Lin Xiangru (*Shiji* 81; verify).
*Defense.* The text says "shame that looks only at itself," which is a qualified kind, not shame as such.
*Rejoinder.* True. The sentence is defensible as written, but a reader will hear the stereotype. A half-sentence would prevent that.
**Held with repair** (Minor).

## 4. Ledger

- **A heading (form, epistemic rule, deniers):** Holds. The trust-after-reflection rule is one Mencius would accept (the office of the heart is to reflect, 6A15). No Confucian is misplaced among the deniers.
- **A1:** Holds. We hold it without qualification (Mencius 2A2).
- **A2:** Holds with repair. The wronged/worsened distinction and the secret example stand. Add a half-sentence so Confucian shame is not read as self-regarding (Exchange 8).
- **A3:** Holds. We agree that no vote and no third party can pardon in the victim's place.
- **A4:** Holds. Its content is ours by nature, and the text already distinguishes equal worth from equal duty, which answers our graded love. Mencius 6A16–17 would be a strong convergent witness for "prior to anyone's recognition."
- **A5:** Holds. Xunzi supports it more strongly than anyone: what we want never settles what we ought.
- **A6:** Holds with repair. The surgeon, the city and the prisoner cases stand. "Mencius did" slightly overstates 2A2, whose stake is gaining the realm, the greatest prize, rather than saving a stated number of lives (repair 1). The book's own claims about consent are clearly marked as its own.
- **A7:** Holds. Sincerity (*cheng*) is "the way of Heaven" (*Zhongyong* 20) and binds every mind. Analects 13.18 concerns concealment, not belief against evidence.
- **W1 frame:** Holds. The Euthyphro answer is clean, and the claim that the wrong is first of all to her is one we share.
- **W1(1):** Holds. Xunzi's road is stated fairly and the ancestral rites without caricature. The weight is correctly called a clue, and the asymmetry argument (only someone can forgive) answers our strongest rejoinder at parity (Exchange 4).
- **W1(2):** Holds with repair. Its grounding of dignity is at parity with ours, and the text does not claim more. The 1 Samuel 15 cost is admitted against Kant but should also be admitted against Mencius 7B3 (Exchange 6).
- **W1 closing ("This Lawgiver is not a commander"):** Holds. The rejection of voluntarism is coherent, and "the law is His own wisdom" is the nearest any theism comes to our principle while remaining Someone.
- **W2:** Holds with repair. It is honest that it favors one Source, not a Person, but the Wang/Mou parenthetical blurs identity with source and leaves out Zhu Xi (repair 3).
- **W3 (longing):** Holds. It is offered as a clue. We contest that the longing is universal (Confucius was content that Heaven knew him, 14.35, and would not ask about death, 11.12), but the text claims only "fit."
- **W3 (hidden God, Neo-Confucian seekers):** Holds. "Found a principle rather than a Person" is fair, and the text does not presume fault (CCC 847).
- **C intro:** Holds. The fairness rule is stated and kept.
- **C Nothing binds:** Holds.
- **C Feeling refined:** Holds.
- **C Best words widening:** Holds.
- **C Reason's own law:** Holds.
- **C What we owe each other:** Holds.
- **C Care between persons:** Holds. It is the account nearest ours on the infant, and its stopping point is ours too.
- **C Well-being counted impartially:** Holds.
- **C Flourishing of our kind:** Holds. Its argument about the end applies to us as well (Exchange 7).
- **C Godless realism:** Holds.
- **C Impersonal sacred order:** Holds with repair. Its sources are accurate (moon image; heart of Heaven and Earth as humaneness; *Western Inscription*; *Analects* 7.23, 14.35; Mou; Sima Qian on Bo Yi against *Daodejing* 79). The gloss "lets each one go / the moon never knew" understates Zhu Xi's heart that gives life and omits Mencius 5A5. The decisive point, the infant whom no one sees, should be stated in our terms (repair 2).
- **C The Way that gives and does not claim:** Holds.
- **C God, or Nature:** Holds.
- **C The Buddha's Dhamma:** Holds.
- **C History and class:** Holds.
- **C Power and rank:** Holds. "Worth that follows nature was taught outside Christianity too" fairly credits us.
- **C Freedom that makes its values:** Holds.
- **C A God who is Love:** Holds. Its claim is reach, and it holds against us at the given end and the unseen infant, while correctly calling the rest clues.
- **Evil, para 1 (the weight):** Holds. It is honest, and hell and 1 Samuel 15 are left on the scale. It does not claim our Heaven escapes the problem.
- **Evil, para 2, First (privation):** Holds. Its limit, that privation does not rule out an indifferent source, is stated.
- **Evil, para 2, Second (not spent):** Holds. Its distinction between ordering and giving a person away is coherent, and the parasite is left on the scale.
- **Evil, para 2, Third (God suffers with):** Holds with repair (Minor). The Confucian *datong* hope is grouped with the others, but "the last two are hopes for those who come after" leaves unclear whether ours is one too. It is, and to the dead we offer rite and memory, not repair (repair 5).
- **Evil, invitation (the protester):** Holds.
- **Conclusion:** Holds. The hillside test does not discriminate against us, since we too say she was wronged by what she is, but the text never claims it does, and "it follows what she is" is our view as well. The claim is properly limited (clues, heavy evil, no compulsion).

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**As written: NONE.**

No Fatal or Serious objection stands. Every point where the Confucian account matches or out-protects the book (grounding equal dignity by nature, refusing to aggregate, one Source of law and mind, Mencius on the canon's slaughter) is either already conceded by the text or concerns a cost the text pays openly against another rival. The text's discriminating claims against us rest on wanting, keeping and forgiving by the dead, which it explicitly calls clues, and on the given end and the unseen infant, where it does out-reach us.

**Minor:**
1. A6, "Mencius did," overstates 2A2 slightly: the stake is gaining the realm, not a stated number of lives (repair 1).
2. C sacred order: "lets each one go / the moon never knew which river it was" is the author's gloss, presented next to Zhu Xi's words, and understates the heart that gives life. Mencius 5A5 is missing, and including it would actually sharpen the book's case (repair 2).
3. W2 parenthetical: it treats Wang and Mou as finding a "Source" when they find identity in the heart, and it leaves out Zhu Xi (repair 3).
4. W1(2): the cost of 1 Samuel 15 is admitted against Kant only, though Mencius 7B3 is an equal or closer witness (repair 4).
5. A2: the shame/guilt sentence invites the shame-culture stereotype (repair 6).
6. Evil, Third: "the last two" is ambiguous about the Confucian hope (repair 5).

**Misstatements of my school:** None of substance. All the citations I checked (Mencius 2A2, Xunzi 19 on rites, the moon image, the heart of Heaven and Earth as humaneness, the *Western Inscription*, *Analects* 7.23 and 14.35, Mou's infinite heart, Sima Qian against *Daodejing* 79, the *datong* hope) are fairly paraphrased. Minor items 2 and 3 are imprecisions, not misstatements.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**
- Whether a debt to the dead must be receivable by her (W1(1)): we say it is honored, the book says it is to be received. The text weighs this correctly.
- Whether worth needs a giver who intends this individual, or only a conferred nature: on grounding A4 and A6 we are at parity, and the difference lies in wanting and keeping.
- One Source, personal or not (W2): the text concedes that W2 does not decide this.
- Whether the longing to be known wholly by one who will not die is universal (W3).
- The problem of evil: our Heaven bears it more lightly because it intends less. The text says as much of the nearby rivals.

## 6. Proposed repairs

1. **A6.**
 Old: "Fewer hold the line when the numbers grow vast (Mencius did: none of the sages, he said, would kill one innocent man even to win the whole realm, *Mencius* 2A2), and"
 New: "Fewer hold the line when the numbers grow vast (Mencius held it against the greatest prize there is: none of the sages, he said, would do one unrighteous deed or kill one innocent man even to gain the whole realm, *Mencius* 2A2; Xunzi says the same of the humane man, *Xunzi* 11), and"

2. **C, impersonal sacred order.**
 Old: "But its principle, Zhu Xi said, has no intention: it keeps each kind true to itself and lets each one go. It can say that each one holds the whole nature, not that *this* one is wanted for her own sake; when one river is drained, the moon never knew which river it was."
 New: "But its principle, Zhu Xi said, has no feeling or intention and makes no plans; Heaven and Earth have a heart to give life, yet bring things to completion without deliberating. Its sages add, from the *Documents*, that Heaven sees as the people see and hears as the people hear (*Mencius* 5A5). So it can say that each one holds the whole nature, and that Heaven sees her through every eye that sees her rightly; not that *this* one is wanted for her own sake by One who sees her when no one does. For the infant no one holds, there is no eye through which that Heaven sees her."

3. **W2 parenthetical.**
 Old: "Those who find the one Source in the heart itself, as Wang Yangming and Mou Zongsan did, or in God or Nature, as Spinoza did, agree with this witness and part from this book in W1 and W3;"
 New: "Those for whom law and mind are one in their root, as for Zhu Xi one principle is whole in every nature and every mind, or for whom the heart itself is the law, as for Wang Yangming and Mou Zongsan, so that there is no fit to explain, or who find both in God or Nature, as Spinoza did, agree with this witness and part from this book in W1 and W3;"

4. **W1(2), the cost.**
 Old: "This has a cost: Kant protects her here more firmly than this book does (C, Reason's own law)."
 New: "This has a cost: Kant protects her here more firmly than this book does (C, Reason's own law), and so does Mencius, who, reading in his own revered *Documents* that the most humane king's conquest left blood enough to float pestles, kept two or three slips of that chapter and not the rest, for, he said, the most humane has no enemy in the realm (*Mencius* 7B3)."

5. **Evil, Third.**
 Old: "The last two are hopes for those who come after; to the children already spent they can offer memory, not repair."
 New: "These are hopes for those who come after; to the children already spent they can offer memory, and the Confucian his rites, not repair."

6. **A2.**
 Old: "Shame that looks only at itself says I am less; guilt says I wronged her; only guilt asks her pardon."
 New: "Shame that looks only at itself says I am less; guilt says I wronged her; only guilt asks her pardon. (Not all shame looks only at itself: Mencius counted the heart ashamed of wrong the beginning of righteousness, *Mencius* 2A6, and that shame too goes to her.)"

## 7. New insights; lines worth keeping

**New insights for the book:**
- **Mencius 6A16–17 as a witness for A4.** The nobility Heaven confers is in everyone, and what another man ennobles that man can debase, but not this. It is a pre-Christian, non-theist statement of dignity "prior to anyone's recognition," and it strengthens the book's convergence claim (CCC 31).
- **The unseen infant is the book's sharpest point against us, and it should be stated against us by name.** Our Heaven sees through the people's eyes (5A5). The infant no one holds is seen by no one, and only a Someone whose seeing does not wait on ours reaches her. This ties the Confucian row to the conclusion's hillside test.
- **The given end.** The Aristotelian argument (an end each must achieve) applies to Confucian sagehood too, and the Confucian row could say so. *Zhongyong* 22, where others fulfill her nature, is our best reply and shows our limit.
- **Mencius 7B3 alongside Kant.** He is a witness from within a revered canon, and closer to the Catholic situation than Kant is.
- **Whole in each, so no aggregation.** "One principle, many manifestations" gives a non-theist reason for A6 that is structurally the book's own: five hold no more of the whole than one. The book could credit it as convergence.
- **Body received from parents** (*Classic of Filial Piety* 1: one does not dare harm it). This is a non-Christian witness to life "held in trust" (CCC 2280). Its limit should be noted: the same tradition honored giving one's life to complete humaneness (*Analects* 15.9; *Mencius* 6A10) and sometimes praised loyalist and widow suicide, which crosses the book's line.
- **Mencius 4A17, the sister-in-law** (rites yield to save a life). It is a clean example for Chapter 11 on prudence: a standard serves its purpose.

**Lines worth keeping:**
- "Heaven and Earth give life to things; God wanted *you*."
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."
- "Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent."
- "The rebel can take her side; he cannot keep her."
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it." (Mencius would sign this: the heart that rises is the beginning of humaneness, responding to a nature, not creating it.)

## 8. Score

**8 / 10.** The text knows us, cites us fairly, concedes parity where we have it (worth by nature, one Source, the second road for the dead), and wins honestly where it can: the infant no one sees, and an end given rather than achieved. What remains are small precisions and one cost it already pays against Kant.

**Honest ceiling for any theistic case against the Confucian school: 8.5.** On A1–A6 and W2 we reach parity on our own ground, so any theistic case against us must rest on the personal items (wanting, keeping, forgiving, the given end). Two of these can only be clues, and two can be decisive only for a reader who grants the hard case's weight.
