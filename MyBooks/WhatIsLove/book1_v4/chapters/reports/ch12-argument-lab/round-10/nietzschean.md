# Blind review: the Nietzschean school

## 1. School and voice

I argue as Nietzsche, read through his best analytic interpreters: Brian Leiter (Nietzsche as a naturalist and an anti-realist about value, attacking "morality in the pejorative sense" because of what it does to higher human types), Bernard Reginster (will to power as the will to overcome resistance; nihilism and the affirmation of life), John Richardson (values as the aims of drives, given a Darwinian reading), and Maudemarie Clark (the mature Nietzsche's commitment to truth and empirical method, with the earlier "falsification thesis" dropped). On this view there are no moral facts. "Good/evil" is a slave revaluation, born of ressentiment, of the older "good/bad". Equal worth is a priestly and herd ideal that harms the rare. The question that goes deepest is not "is God real?" but "what is the value of truth, and of morality, for life?" Every paraphrase of Nietzsche or his commentators below is mine; verify before using.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** Justice begins as exchange and settlement between parties of roughly equal power (HH 92; GM II.8, verify). In its highest form it is the strong person's ability to keep an impartial eye even toward someone who injured him (GM II.11). What anyone is owed follows from rank and compact: equality for equals, inequality for unequals (TI "Skirmishes" 48, paraphrase). **Holds** as a coherent account and borrows nothing. It **breaks**, by design, for anyone who is no party to exchange, the newborn first of all. She is owed nothing except what flows from the generosity of the strong.

**2. A hard dilemma.** A young composer of real gifts has a severely disabled younger brother. He can either give up his work to care for the brother full-time or leave and arrange paid care. My principles are: love of the farthest over love of the neighbor (Z I "On Love of the Neighbor"); pity is a feeling that depresses, not a virtue (A 7); "become who you are"; giving should come from abundance, not self-loss (Z I "On the Gift-Giving Virtue"); and the exceptional person has a duty to treat the mediocre and the weak more tenderly than he treats himself (A 57). Resolution: if he really is a creator, he goes. He arranges good care and gives generously from his abundance, but he does not sacrifice his task to pity. If he is not a creator, herd morality is the right morality for him, and he stays. **Partly borrows**: his decisive standard is honesty about himself, which Nietzsche admits is a Christian conscience turned on itself (GS 357; GM III.27). **Strains** at the self-assessment: "am I a creator?" is exactly where self-deception thrives, and the only test Nietzsche offers is the finished work, which comes too late for the decision.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** I deny it, openly (BGE 257, 258, 62; A 43). Worth is a matter of rank: what a life expresses and makes possible, and whether it ascends or declines. Care for the newborn, the dying and the disabled can be grounded in the overflow and duty of the strong (A 57), not in a claim the weak hold. **Holds** coherently. **Breaks** against A4, which is the price I pay knowingly.

**4. Ultimate flourishing.** There is no good that is ultimate for "humans" as such; goods are relative to type (Leiter's "type-facts"). For a higher type, flourishing means organizing one's drives into one style (GS 290), creating values, growing through resistance and suffering (BGE 225; Reginster), and saying yes to one's life so completely that one would will its eternal return (GS 341; EH "Clever" 10). **Holds** as an ideal. **Breaks** in two places. First, the step from "all life wills power" to "power is what is valuable" is a gap the literature has not closed (Leiter against Reginster and Richardson, verify). Second, it offers nothing to the infant who dies or to the forgotten dead. It does not try to.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1. The Moorean lever (A4, conclusion).**
- *Attack.* The conclusion's whole force comes from asking which you are surer of: that the infant on the hillside was wronged no less than the strong one, or any argument that says she was not. But that certainty is the very thing a genealogy explains. Two thousand years of training produced the feeling, and a feeling of certainty is not evidence (TI "Four Great Errors"; A 50).
- *Defense.* The text concedes the history (A4: "how late it came and how easily it is lost"; Seneca). It holds that "a history settles neither," because the regard for the many, and your own sense of rank, have histories too (A6; fairness rule in C). It also does not claim to compel: "it cannot compel the one who gives up A4."
- *Rejoinder.* There is an asymmetry. A genealogy damages a value that claims to hold unconditionally for everyone. It barely touches mine, because I claim only perspectival authority for it. That asymmetry, however, comes from what each side claims, not from an unfair method, so the fairness rule is respected. The text never needed the genealogy to fail. It needs only that the reader, after honest reflection that includes the genealogy, still finds A4 true, and it says that this ends in a choice.
- **Held**, as an honest standoff at parity.

**Exchange 2. "Only by assuming the answer" (C, Power and rank).**
- *Attack.* By the rule the text itself states in A, a genealogy defeats a belief if "the best account of what produced the belief would have produced it whether or not it was true." Ressentiment is insensitive to truth. My genealogy therefore does not have to assume that God is absent. It only has to be the best history. Even if God existed, Christianity could still have arisen from ressentiment, and in that case a true belief would not be "answering to what is there."
- *Defense.* For a Catholic, the rival history is God making Himself known through Israel and Christ. Which story is "the best account" of how the belief arose cannot be settled without settling whether He exists and spoke. So the genealogy wins only if naturalism about that history wins, and that is the question the case asks.
- *Rejoinder.* Granted. But the sentence as written ("only by assuming the answer") is looser than this, and the same point cuts the other way: the text cannot use A4's history to support A4 either. The text says so in A4, but not here.
- **Held with repair** (Section 6, R1).

**Exchange 3. The value of truth (A7; C, Power and rank).**
- *Attack.* The text's dilemma assumes that truthfulness must either bind every mind or bind none. I take the second option. Honesty is the virtue my kind cannot get away from (BGE 227). It is a value of rank, not a law. And the unconditional will to truth is the last form of the ascetic ideal (GM III.24–27).
- *Defense.* The text grants that the dilemma "does not settle" the case. It also adds a point Nietzsche himself made: he saw that the godless still take their fire from the old faith that God is truth, and he asked what truthfulness could mean once that faith was gone (GS 344; GM III.24). On that point Nietzsche agrees with A7's hinge: unconditional truthfulness sits uneasily in a godless world. That is the text's claim in W2, in his own words.
- *Rejoinder.* Yes. I keep honesty as a discipline, not as a debt. But the text has not yet used my own testimony, and it should.
- **Held** (strengthened by R3).

**Exchange 4. "A will that invents values … cannot bind" (W1 closing).**
- *Attack.* The sovereign individual earns the right to make promises and keeps his word even against misfortune (GM II.2). The legislating philosopher creates values (BGE 211). Self-made law binds the man strong enough to keep it.
- *Defense.* "Bind" is being used in two senses. In Nietzsche's sense it means constancy of will, a fact about character. The text means normative binding, an "ought" the will does not set for itself. Nietzsche denies that the second exists. He does not show that the first produces it. And the "morality of custom" that bred the promise-keeping animal (GM II.2) is precisely "a law no promiser invented."
- *Rejoinder.* Fair. The sentence argues against voluntarism, and it holds there. Against me it reaches only as far as the reader still wants a normative "ought."
- **Held.**

**Exchange 5. Amor fati and the past (W3; evil section, First).**
- *Attack.* You say a yes to everything that happens "cannot take her side against the past." But your felix culpa does what my "thus I willed it" does (Z II "On Redemption"). God permits the evil in order to draw a greater good from it (CCC 412), and so gives the past a new meaning from what came of it. Your God says yes to the whole that contains what was done to her.
- *Defense.* There are two real differences. First, God permits what He does not will; the evil is never willed and never becomes good (CCC 311, 312, 324). Second, the good drawn from it is offered to her as well, and she is raised (Spe Salvi 44, 47; CCC 998). Nietzsche's yes wills the evil's return and gives her nothing. It is said by the one who affirms, not to her.
- *Rejoinder.* For the unbaptized infant, the good offered to her remains a hope, and the text says so ("a hope, not a proof").
- **Held.** The text concedes exactly as much as is true.

**Exchange 6. A rank ethic at the summit (W1(2); 1 Samuel 15; A4).**
- *Attack.* No creature may spend the innocent, yet the Lord of life may command that whole peoples be killed, infants included. The stated reason in Scripture is to punish what Amalek did (1 Sam 15:2): the infants die for others' deeds. Your equality turns out to be the equality of subjects before a master, a leveling under the priest's God (A 43). And A4 states the line in the passive voice ("never … to be deliberately killed for others' sake") without saying "by any of us."
- *Defense.* God's prerogative rests on His being the Creator, not on power. He gives being, can give it back, and loses no one (W1(1)). The text pays this cost openly against Kant, and it bars any present-day claim to such a command (CCC 67). The norm applied to God is "never as a mere means against her own good," and the good He wills for her is not cancelled by the death He permits or decrees.
- *Rejoinder.* The defense is coherent within its frame, and the cost is declared. But A4's scope should match W1(2), or a hostile reader will find an apparent contradiction.
- **Held with repair** (R5).

**Exchange 7. Life-denial and eros (W3).**
- *Attack.* Christianity gave Eros poison to drink (BGE 168). It says No to this life and calls the No God (A 18).
- *Defense.* Benedict XVI answers this charge by name (Deus Caritas Est 3–5): eros is to be healed, not killed. The text's first word is Pieper's "it is good that you exist," and its hope is that the body is raised and the wounds are kept.
- *Rejoinder.* That is a real answer, and the text should say whom it is answering. Eternal hell remains (CCC 1035), and the text itself calls it the heaviest weight it carries.
- **Held** (R6 adds the attribution).

**Exchange 8. Necessary truths and fit (W2).**
- *Attack.* There is no "fit" to explain. The earlier Nietzsche held that logic and number are useful simplifications that survival bred into us (GS 111; BGE 4).
- *Defense.* Clark shows that the mature Nietzsche drops that thesis. Without it, the falsification view undermines itself, including the genealogy that depends on it. W2 also presents its explanation only as "a judgment about where explanation may stop."
- **Held**, as a standoff.

## 4. Ledger

- **A heading.** Holds with repair. The epistemic principle is fair and symmetric. "Nietzsche denies A1 through A4" should say "as holding for everyone" (R2). The rest of the list of denials is accurate.
- **A1.** Holds as a premise the reader may accept. I deny it openly (TI "Improvers" 1: there are no moral facts). The text records that denial.
- **A2.** Holds. The secret-told-to-a-stranger case is strong. Note that Nietzsche keeps duties and wronging among equals (BGE 260; GM II.2), so the dying friend could be wronged on his terms too. What he denies is guilt as a universal moral fact and any claim the weak hold on everyone.
- **A3.** Holds as a premise. I deny it, and the text records that.
- **A4.** Holds with repair. It is honest about the history (Seneca; slowness to see). Its scope sentence needs "by any of us" to match W1(2) (R5).
- **A5.** Holds. On Richardson's reading, Nietzsche makes the aims of drives the standard of value, but even then "what we want" settles only a type's good, not the "ought" A5 means. The text records the right denial: that A5 holds alike for everyone.
- **A6.** Holds. Nietzsche denies the second half (BGE 258). He would also recast the first half as giving from abundance rather than cost, which is a minor nuance and not a defect.
- **A7.** Holds as a premise. It is the place where Nietzsche is most open to pressure: by his own account (GS 344) his unconditional honesty is an inheritance from faith.
- **W1 frame.** Holds. The account of Anscombe is accurate. Good care is taken to keep "wrong because of what it does to her" ahead of "offense against God."
- **W1(1).** Holds. It is presented as a clue, and it explicitly grants that "guilt asks proves nothing." It cites the GM II.19–20 genealogy of debt to ancestors and gods accurately and answers it at the right strength.
- **W1(2).** Holds with repair. The Kant and Theravāda contrasts are strong. The 1 Samuel 15 cost is declared. The scope of A4 should be matched (R5).
- **W1 closing.** Holds against voluntarism. Against Nietzschean self-legislation it reaches only the normative sense of "bind" (Exchange 4).
- **W2.** Holds as a judgment about where explanation stops, honestly labeled as such. The line "If truth and life have one Source, the will to truth cannot finally lead away from life" is the text's real answer to my hardest question. It is conditional, as it should be.
- **W3.** Holds. The eternal-recurrence passage is accurate and fair. It would be stronger with the Deus Caritas Est 3 attribution (R6).
- **W3, second paragraph (hiddenness).** Holds, and goes beyond my school's concern. Nothing Nietzschean presses here except A 47's charge that "God" is a pretext, which the text's restraint already disarms.
- **C intro.** Holds. The fairness rule is stated and kept.

- **C, Well-being, counted impartially.** Holds. Nietzsche would add his own contempt for the "greatest number" (BGE 228).

It is accurate and fair in its main lines: the "most honest rival"; BGE 227 on honesty; GM III.27; A 20–22 and GM I.7, I.14; TI "Skirmishes" 5; GS 107; and "he can despise the man who tortures a child as base. "Nietzsche … prized it as the discipline behind every human greatness" is accurate for BGE 225. Strictly, he prized the suffering of those who grow from it; he did not prize the sick child's suffering. That is minor.

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**As written: NONE.**

The text never claims to refute my school. It claims to show what my school costs (A1–A4, the second half of A6, universal truthfulness, any reconciliation with the dead) and to let the reader decide whether to pay. That claim is true, and I pay the cost knowingly.

**Minor items**
1. C, Power and rank: "the genealogy can defeat A4 only by assuming the answer" is looser than the text's own rule. The genealogy needs only to be the best history, which means the question is whether revelation or ressentiment produced the belief (R1).
2. A4: "never, if innocent, to be deliberately killed for others' sake" does not say "by any of us." W1(2)'s Thomist position on 1 Samuel 15, where the stated reason is punishment of a people for its deed, makes the passive voice look like a contradiction (R5).
3. W3 cites Deus Caritas Est 5 without noting that DCE 3 answers Nietzsche's charge about eros by name (R6).

**Misstatements of my school (all small)**
- "Nietzsche denies A1 through A4" (A heading; C row). He denies them *as holding for everyone*. He keeps duties among equals (BGE 260), justice among parties of comparable power (GM II.8, II.11), and the promise-keeping of the sovereign individual (GM II.2). The dying friend in A2 could be wronged on his terms; the infant could not.
- "Chapter 1 must meet his denial of free will." This is right, but he also denied the *unfree* will (BGE 21), and what matters for this book is his denial of the responsibility that guilt assumes (TI "Four Great Errors" 7–8).
- "Prized [suffering] as the discipline behind every human greatness." Accurate (BGE 225), though it applies to the suffering of those it forms, not to every suffering.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Moorean confidence in A4 against the genealogy of that confidence (Exchange 1).
- Whether truthfulness binds every mind or only those of a certain rank (Exchange 3).
- Where explanation of the fit between mind and necessary truths may stop (Exchange 8).
- God's prerogative over life as a Creator's right, or as a sovereign's rank (Exchange 6).

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1. C, Power and rank.**
Old: "So the genealogy can defeat A4 only by assuming the answer to the question this case asks;"
New: "So the genealogy can defeat A4 only if its story, and not God's making Himself known, is the best account of how the belief arose; that is the question this case asks, and this book cannot use the same history to vindicate A4 without assuming its own answer either;"

**R2a. A heading.**
Old: "Nietzsche denies A1 through A4, the second half of A6,"
New: "Nietzsche denies that A1 through A4 hold for everyone, and denies the second half of A6,"

**R2b. C, Power and rank.**
Old: "It stays consistent by giving up A1 through A4, and the second half of A6, openly,"
New: "It stays consistent by giving up A1 through A4 as claims on everyone, and the second half of A6, openly (he keeps duties among equals and the word of the man who can answer for himself, *Beyond Good and Evil* 260, *Genealogy* II.2, but not a claim the weakest holds on all),"

**R3. C, Power and rank.**
Old: "Nietzsche kept his own honesty, which he called the virtue his kind could not get away from, but he denied that it binds every mind."
New: "Nietzsche kept his own honesty, which he called the virtue his kind could not get away from, but he denied that it binds every mind; and he saw that the unconditional will to truth, which his own unmasking used, still drew its fire from the old faith that God is truth, and asked what it could mean once that faith was gone (*Gay Science* 344; *Genealogy* III.24)."

**R4. C, Power and rank.**
Old: "Chapter 1 must meet his denial of free will."
New: "Chapter 1 must meet his denial of free will, and of the responsibility that guilt assumes (*Twilight*, "The Four Great Errors" 7–8)."

**R5. A4.**
Old: "never, if innocent, to be deliberately killed for others' sake,"
New: "never, if innocent, to be deliberately killed by any of us for others' sake,"

**R6. W3.**
Old: "and the Catholic adds that desire itself, eros too, is not to be killed but healed (*Deus Caritas Est* 5)."
New: "and the Catholic adds that desire itself, eros too, is not to be killed but healed (*Deus Caritas Est* 5, answering Nietzsche's charge that Christianity poisoned eros, 3)."

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**New insights**
- **Nietzsche is the book's best witness on its hinge.** He held that this morality stands or falls with faith in God (TI "Skirmishes" 5), that unconditional truthfulness is a residue of the faith that God is truth (GS 344), and that a "death of God" without revaluing values is self-deception. The secular egalitarian is therefore caught between Nietzsche and the Church, and the book can say so. Nietzsche's choice is the honest alternative to its own, and the chapter could make that pairing explicit.
- **Duty without claim.** A 57 says the exceptional person has a *duty* to treat the weak tenderly. That is the clearest way to show what A4 adds: a duty that belongs to the strong, grounded in who they are, as opposed to a claim that belongs to her. The text's line "he can despise the man who tortures a child as base" could point to it.
- **Senselessness, not suffering.** Nietzsche held that what makes suffering unbearable is its lack of meaning (GM II.7; III.28). The evil section's honesty, "a setting, not an explanation," is exactly the right stance toward this critique: the book does not manufacture meaning for the child; it hopes that she is kept.
- **Felix culpa versus "thus I willed it."** The structural parallel between the two is real, and the difference is real too: permitting versus willing, and a good offered to her versus a good for the one who affirms. The book could state that contrast in one sentence in Evil, First.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "Nor need he praise cruelty: he can despise the man who tortures a child as base. What he cannot say is that the child was wronged."
- "but that yes is said by me, not to me, and it must be said to what was done to her as well."
- "Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent."
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."
- "Heaven and Earth give life to things; God wanted *you*."
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it."

## 8. Score

**8/10.** The text states my school accurately, keeps its own fairness rule, claims only what it shows (that denying A1–A4 is the price of my position), and openly leaves the final step as a choice, which is the most any moral argument can honestly win against Nietzsche. **Honest ceiling for any theistic case against my school: about 8.** A school that openly gives up A1–A4 and the universal authority of truth cannot be compelled from moral premises. A theistic case could rise above that only by evidence that is not moral, such as historical claims about the Resurrection, and my school would contest that on other grounds.
