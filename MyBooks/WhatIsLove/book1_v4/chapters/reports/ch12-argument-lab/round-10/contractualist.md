# Blind review: the contractualist / second-personal school

## 1. School and voice

I speak for the view that the part of morality concerned with wronging is the part concerned with what we owe each other. An act is wrong when any principle for the general regulation of conduct that no one could reasonably reject would forbid it (T. M. Scanlon, *What We Owe to Each Other*). Directed obligations, claims and the reactive attitudes are second-personal: they presuppose the standing of persons to address demands to one another and to be answerable (Stephen Darwall, *The Second-Person Standpoint*; R. Jay Wallace, *Responsibility and the Moral Sentiments* and *The Moral Nexus*). Behind both stands Rawls's idea of fair terms of cooperation among free and equal persons. On metaethics the Scanlonian wing is a realist about reasons that needs no metaphysical backing (*Being Realistic about Reasons*, verify), and the Rawlsian wing is a constructivist that declines to call its principles "true." I am a non-theist. I think this school is the nearest secular rival to the text on A2, A3 and A6, and I argue here at full strength.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** Each person has standing to ask for a justification of how she is treated. What anyone is owed is whatever principles require that no one could reasonably reject. They are tested by the generic reasons attached to each standpoint, comparing the strongest individual complaints, and they never add lesser harms across persons to outweigh one person's grave harm (the individualist restriction). The motive is the value of living with others on terms of mutual recognition. → **Holds.** It is coherent and borrows nothing theistic. It rests on a first principle (that value, together with facts about reasons), which I defend by reflective equilibrium. That is the same method the text uses in the A preamble, so this is at parity with the text's own stopping point.

**2. A hard dilemma.** Take the Transmitter Room case (Scanlon, verify). Jones is pinned under equipment and getting painful shocks. Saving him means cutting a broadcast enjoyed by millions for fifteen minutes. We must save him, because no viewer's complaint comes near his, and complaints do not add up across persons. A harder case is a competent, dying patient who asks for help to die. On one side are the generic reasons of the suffering patient (control over one's life, relief). On the other are the generic reasons of the vulnerable (pressure, being seen as a burden). Many in my school conclude that a narrow permission with strong safeguards cannot reasonably be rejected. → **Holds without borrowing** the book's covenantal definitions of truth, justice, mercy or sacrifice. I use only the bare concept of what is owed to each. But the method assumes that every standpoint counts equally, which is challenge 3. On the second case my school reaches the opposite verdict from the Church, and the text correctly labels that line as "this book's claim, not shared."

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** Rawls rests equality on a capacity for moral personality held as a "range property," which almost everyone meets above a minimum (TJ §77, verify). Scanlon rests it on the capacity for judgment-sensitive attitudes. Principles are justified to the standpoint of whole lives. That reaches the newborn, who stands at the start of a person's life, and the dying, who remain the person they were. The enemy never loses standing, because his punishment must itself be justifiable to him. The case I struggle with is the person severely and permanently cognitively disabled from birth. For her my school offers a relation (born of human parents and of our kind) and trustees who speak for her (WWO ch. 4, verify). Darwall's grounding of dignity in second-personal authority does not reach her in her own right. → **Holds for most people. At the hardest case it holds only by a relation taken as basic, so it strains but does not break.** I do not deny her dignity. I cannot explain it from my own materials better than the text says I can. The text describes this fairly.

**4. Ultimate human flourishing.** My school is deliberately a theory of the right, not of the final good. It is pluralist about goods. For Scanlon, enjoyment, friendship, achievement and understanding are goods, and well-being is no master value. For Rawls there are rational plans of life, the Aristotelian Principle, and social union. The guiding ideal is a life of worthwhile activities lived among others on terms each could accept. → **Coherent, but it declines the question rather than answering it.** It has nothing to say to the longing of W3 or to the dead person who cannot forgive (W1(1)). That is a real limit of scope, not a contradiction. It borrows nothing.

## 3. Debate highlights

**E1. The text has conceded the directed structure.**
*Attack.* The C row grants that contractualism explains why a wrong is done *to* someone, why we answer to the person we wronged, and why no total justifies spending one person. So A2, A3 and the first half of A6 are not evidence for God over my school. The moral argument must live on what is left over.
*Defense.* The text agrees and builds on that agreement. W1 says that "the fact that no one can release us does not decide between the accounts; nor does the fact that guilt is owed," and it names the contractualist among the "nearest rivals." It claims only that the accounts part "at the grave, and over what gives her the claim at all."
*Verdict.* **Held.** The text does not overclaim here.

**E2. "Takes as given" is not a defect.**
*Attack.* The C row says my school "takes as given" the relation that gives the infant standing and the value of mutual recognition. Every account takes something as given. The text's own epistemology (A preamble: what still seems true after honest reflection) is reflective equilibrium, which is my method. I defend the value of mutual recognition exactly as the text defends A.
*Defense.* The text concedes the general point: "Every account has a first principle, ours included" (godless realism row), and "Its claim is reach, not count" (God row). It is not saying my school has an ungrounded premise and it has none. It is saying one source grounds what I ground in several places.
*Rejoinder.* Then the count question becomes a parsimony question. "One source" is a single posit with very rich content: a necessary, personal, perfectly good mind whose wisdom contains the moral law. Packing many primitives into one posit is not obviously simpler.
*Reply.* Classical theism holds that God is simple (ST I q.3 a.7). The complexity lies in our concepts of Him, not in Him. The text also refuses to cash this out as fewer primitives. It speaks of "one mystery, and a mystery that explains" and calls W2 "a judgment about where explanation may stop, and the rivals do not share it."
*Verdict.* **Held**, as an honest standoff that the text itself names.

**E3. The fit between minds and necessary truths.**
*Attack.* As a reasons realist I hold that grasping normative truths is just reason at work, as in logic and arithmetic. The "third factor" reply (Enoch, Wielenberg, verify) explains the fit. Survival rewarded attention to pain, trust and kin, and those features really are reason-giving. So the fit has a natural explanation plus a normative one, and needs no Source.
*Defense.* W2 states this reply in its own words: "the features that make a deed wrong (pain, trust, betrayal) are the very ones survival taught us to notice". It calls it "a real answer." It then presses only the residue: that such minds grasp necessary truths at all, "in logic and number as well as of right." Because logic is treated the same way, the text does not single out morality unfairly.
*Rejoinder.* Then W2 is an argument from a priori knowledge in general, not specifically a moral argument.
*Reply.* The text accepts this ("in logic and number as in right") and claims only a judgment of where explanation stops.
*Verdict.* **Held** at parity.

**E4. The hardest case: equal standing for the permanently non-rational.**
*Attack.* The text says my school takes as given "why that relation, and not her powers, gives her full and equal standing." But origin is plausibly essential to a person (Kripke, verify), so "born of us and of our kind" is part of *what she is*. The text's contrast between relation and nature is therefore thinner than it looks. The theist's own ground is also relational: a call to communion that is "a gift no nature could claim." Both views rest equal standing on a relation she has from the start.
*Defense.* Grant both points. The difference is whether the relation explains why it confers worth. On the theist's account the One who calls her is the source of all worth. He wills her "for itself" (GS 24) and measures her against one end, God, beside which differences of power drop out ("the genius and the newborn are for the same thing"). Her origin and kind matter because they are the nature He made in His image and called. On my account, "of human born" confers standing because it does. To a Singer or a McMahan it looks morally arbitrary, and I have nothing deeper to say back.
*Rejoinder.* The theist also stops at a primitive: that what God wills for its own sake has worth that binds *us*.
*Reply.* True, but that is the one stopping point the text admits for everything, and here it does explanatory work my relation does not. The text also anticipates the Thomist objection that greater love makes greater goodness (ST I q.20 a.3). It answers that the good grounding dignity is given to everyone alike.
*Verdict.* **Held.** My rejoinder does not reach parity on this point, and I concede it. One small wording repair would make the conclusion match W1(2) (Repair R5).

**E5. The debt to the dead.**
*Attack.* Wallace and Darwall agree with the text that the standing to forgive belongs to the victim alone. But owing does not entail receivability. Some victims, through dementia, a coma or death, permanently lose the power to forgive. The claim stays valid and the wrongdoer stays liable. What remains is irreparable loss, which an honest moral life has to carry (compare agent-regret). The phrase "in the full sense" assumes what it needs.
*Defense.* The text claims no more than this. It calls W1(1) "a clue, not a proof", and says the clue "weighs as much as the reader finds that a debt truly owed to her is one she must be able, one day, to receive." It even concedes that "That guilt asks proves nothing." It admits that the one who refuses to the end leaves the debt unreceived "on this road as on the second."
*Verdict.* **Held**, as a clue, at parity. (The parenthesis about the error theorist overstates that school; see Minor M6.)

**E6. Evil judged by the text's own standard of justifiability to her.**
*Attack.* The text's best ethic is mine: "each person must be answered one by one, and no total answers her." Apply it to God's permission of a child's agony. The text answers with an "order of the whole" (ST I q.19 a.9), a side-effect structure ("Side effects may be weighed"), and a good "offered to her as well." My school holds that intention does not settle permissibility (Scanlon, *Moral Dimensions*, verify). It also holds that a good paid afterward is not a justification given beforehand. A surgeon who harms you without need and then pays you generously has still wronged you. So the "Second" point does not lessen the weight.
*Defense.* The text does not lean on the order of the whole. It says that "an order of the whole cannot answer it by itself, and only what is offered to her can, which is a hope, not a proof." The Catholic claim is not payment beside the evil. It is that God permits no evil "unless He brought a good from that very evil" (CCC 324), and that the evil "never becomes good" (CCC 312). This is the distinction between *defeating* an evil within a person's life and merely balancing it off (Chisholm; Marilyn McCord Adams, verify, non-magisterial). Whether the side-effect distinction matters is disputed among non-theists too (Foot, Quinn, Kamm), so that is at parity. The text also admits that "Whether a world of real causes needs this much pain ... the evidence is not weak," and it judges, rather than proves, that evil does not overturn the case.
*Rejoinder.* Then the text should say "defeat," not "offered." As written, "only what is offered to her can" answer reads as if a later payment could justify.
*Verdict.* **Held with repair** (R3). The weighing of evil itself is a standoff the text openly names.

**E7. 1 Samuel 15: is her claim really hers?**
*Attack.* W1(2) says "what protects her is her own worth." Yet it holds, with St. Thomas, that the Lord of life could command human hands to kill children, and that the killer would act "by God's right." A claim that a third party can authorize others to override is not a second-personal claim in her own right. It is closer to the protection of something owned. Kant and I hold her claim against every will. The text weakens its central datum (A4, "never, if innocent, to be deliberately killed") on the very ground the conclusion leans on.
*Defense.* The Thomist answer is that God's command changes the matter of the act. Death decreed by the Lord of life is not undue, so the act is not murder (ST I-II q.100 a.8 ad 3, paraphrase, verify). No human gains a right of his own (CCC 2258). The text holds "no one may now claim such a command" (CCC 67). It names the cost against Kant, and therefore against me ("This has a cost: Kant protects her here more firmly than this book does"). It places the wars "on the other side of the scale." And it answers that the One who holds that right gave her being, wills her for her own sake, and does not lose her in death.
*Rejoinder.* That is coherent given theism and openly priced. But a reader meets A4 before W1(2), and A4's "never" is stronger than what the book itself holds. A4 should say so.
*Verdict.* **Held with repair** (R4). The cost itself is an honest standoff the text has already paid.

**E8. A request made by the person herself.**
*Attack.* The value of choice means that a principle forbidding a competent, suffering person any help to die could reasonably be rejected. Grounding the ban in a trust held from God moves the wrong toward God and away from her. Grounding it in "the just love of herself" overrides her considered judgment about her own good, which is the paradigm of what cannot be justified to her.
*Defense.* The text claims none of this as a shared premise. A4, A6 and W1(2) all label it "this book's claims, not shared ones," and the conclusion does not depend on it. On the book's own premises, her life is a good held in trust (CCC 2280: stewards, not owners). Aiming at its destruction turns against that good, and the ban protects the vulnerable as well (Samaritanus Bonus).
*Verdict.* **Held** as labeled. This is a real disagreement in the first-order ethics, not a defect in the argument.

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**As written: NONE.**

**Minor items**
- M1. The contractualist formula leaves out Scanlon's restriction of the theory to one part of morality, "what we owe to each other." It also narrows justifiability to "the one it is done to," when the test is justifiability to each person affected (R1).
- M2. Rawls is missing from the contractualist row. His basis-of-equality section openly sets aside those who permanently lack moral personality (TJ §77, verify). Including him strengthens the text's point honestly. His political constructivism also declines the word "true," so "holds as truths that simply hold" fits Scanlon and Darwall but not the Rawlsian wing (R1, R2).
- M3. The relation-versus-nature contrast in the contractualist and care rows is thinner than it looks, since origin and kind are plausibly part of what she is. The real question, which the text does press, is why that kind carries such worth (R2).
- M4. Evil, Second: the wording reads as compensation, and in my school a payment is not a justification. The defeat reading, from that very evil (CCC 324), is the stronger and more accurate Catholic claim (R3).
- M5. A4's "never" runs ahead of the book's own view on 1 Samuel 15 without a cross-reference (R4).
- M6. W1(1)'s parenthesis says "the error theorist shares this book's premise that a debt owed in the full sense needs someone to receive it." This is not my school, but I know of no error theorist whose argument runs through receivability. Mackie's ground is the queerness of objective prescriptivity. Soften it (R6, verify).
- M7. The conclusion's "it follows what she is" should match W1(2), where the call that grounds equal worth is "a gift no nature could claim" (R5).

**Misstatements of my school**
None is serious. Only M1 and M2 above, and the Darwall attributions on forgiveness (the victim's standing to forgive is hers alone) are accurate in substance (verify page references).

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Where explanation may stop: reasons fundamentalism versus one Source (W2, E2, E3).
- The debt to the dead: irreparable loss versus receivability (W1(1), E5).
- Evil judged by justifiability to her. From my standpoint this is the heaviest weight against theism. The text weighs it heavy and judges against it (E6).
- 1 Samuel 15 and whether God's right can authorize human killing of the innocent. The text pays this cost openly to Kant, and so to me (E7).
- Whether a request can make help to die permissible. This is a first-order disagreement, correctly labeled as the book's own claim (E8).

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1. C row, contractualist formula.**
Old: `**What we owe each other** (contractualism: a wrong is what principles no one could reasonably reject would forbid, and so what could not be justified to the one it is done to)`
New: `**What we owe each other** (contractualism, above all T. M. Scanlon, with Stephen Darwall and R. Jay Wallace near him: within the part of morality that concerns what we owe one another, a wrong is what principles no one could reasonably reject would forbid, and so what could not be justified to each person it touches, above all the one it is done to; John Rawls's constructivism is its cousin, though it declines to call its principles true)`

**R2. C row, the relation and Rawls.**
Old: `Why being born one of us puts her among those to whom we must answer, its best defenders explain by a relation: she is born of us and of our kind, and others may speak for her as trustees; why that relation, and not her powers, gives her full and equal standing, it takes as given.`
New: `Why being born one of us puts her among those to whom we must answer, its best defenders explain by a relation: she is born of us and of our kind, and others may speak for her as trustees (Rawls, who rested equality on a capacity for moral personality that nearly everyone has, set aside those who never have it as a hard case). Her origin and kind belong to what she is, as fully as yours belong to you; but why that origin and kind, and not her powers, give her full and equal standing, it takes as given.`

**R3. Evil, Second.**
Old: `an order of the whole cannot answer it by itself, and only what is offered to her can, which is a hope, not a proof.`
New: `an order of the whole cannot answer it by itself, and neither could a good paid to her afterward, for a payment is not a justification; only a good brought from that very evil within her own life, so that what was done to her is answered and not merely outweighed, though it never becomes good (CCC 312, 324), could answer it, and that is a hope, not a proof.`

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
1. **Origin is part of what she is.** The secular "relation" answer to infant standing (being born of us) is plausibly an answer from her nature too. The book's strongest point against my school is therefore not "relation versus nature." It is that only on theism does the relation explain why it confers worth: the One who calls her is the source of worth and measures all against one end. Saying this sharpens W1(2) and the conclusion.
2. **Defeat, not compensation.** In the evil section, the claim that God brings good "from that very evil" (CCC 324) is exactly the distinction between defeating an evil within a life and balancing it off (Chisholm; M. M. Adams, verify, non-magisterial). It answers the contractualist's strongest point, that a payment is not a justification, more accurately than "offered to her as well."
3. **The individualist restriction as an ally.** Scanlon's refusal to add lesser complaints across persons is the strongest secular support for holding the line against the many. The book could credit it in W1(2), next to Foot, and so narrow the real difference to the second half of A6 (not hers to waive). Contractualists differ on the gravest large-number killing cases (verify).
4. **Relational normativity as an ally on A2.** Wallace's *The Moral Nexus* (verify) is the current philosophical home of A2's claim that a wrong is done *to her*, and of the secret-and-letter contrast. Citing it would show the book's premise is shared, not sectarian.
5. **The protest is second-personal.** The closing invitation of the evil section is stronger in my school's terms. To demand that God justify Himself *to her* is to grant that she has standing to be answered. The book can say that this standing is what it has argued she has.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent."
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."
- "Equal worth is not equal duty."
- "Heaven and Earth give life to things; God wanted *you*."
- "The rebel can take her side; he cannot keep her."
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it."
- The secret told to the far stranger, set against the letter blown into the street (A2). It is one of the best cases in the literature for the difference between wronging and worsening.

## 8. Score

**8 / 10.** The text states my school accurately, grants what it must grant, presses the one point where my school really strains (equal standing for the permanently non-rational) without overclaiming, and openly pays the costs I would charge it, Kant's firmer line and evil. **Honest ceiling for any theistic case against my school: 8.5**, because a reasons realist who takes mutual recognition and the "of human born" relation as basic stays coherent, and from my standpoint the weight of evil judged by justifiability to her cannot be brought below a standoff.
