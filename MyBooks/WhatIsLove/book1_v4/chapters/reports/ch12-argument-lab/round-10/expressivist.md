# Blind review: the expressivist / quasi-realist school

## 1. School and voice

I speak for the sentimentalist line that runs from Hume and Adam Smith, through Ayer and Stevenson, to Simon Blackburn's quasi-realism and Allan Gibbard's norm-expressivism. On this view a moral judgment is at bottom the expression of an attitude: approval, resentment, guilt, or the acceptance of norms for feeling and acting. Those attitudes are refined by information, consistency, sympathy and Hume's "general point of view." From that base we *earn* the right to call such judgments true, to say they would hold even if we felt otherwise, and to say a wrong was done to *her*. Nothing is borrowed to get there. I am neither a nihilist nor a subjectivist. I assert A1–A5 and A7 in my own voice, and I assert A6 except where the text itself flags a claim as the Church's own. My quarrel is only with the claim that a Mind must stand behind these judgments, or that their truth needs "a dwelling."

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** I start from Smith rather than Hume. What anyone is owed is what a well-informed, impartial spectator would demand on her behalf, entering by sympathy into her resentment at being injured (TMS Part II, verify). Hume's conventions of property and promise, whose firmness we approve, sit on top of that. Gibbard recasts the same idea as norms for resentment and guilt that we accept. *Verdict:* this holds for Smith, Blackburn and Gibbard. It **breaks in Hume's original.** Hume held that creatures too weak to make us feel their resentment would be owed "gentle usage" by humanity but not, properly speaking, justice (*Enquiry concerning the Principles of Morals* III, verify). That is the newborn and the severely disabled. Smith's version repairs the break, because his spectator resents on behalf of someone who cannot resent for herself.

**2. A hard dilemma.** The case is the conjoined twins in *Re A* (England, 2000). Separating them saves Jodie and ends Mary's life, which depends on Jodie's heart. Leaving them joined means both die within months, and the parents refused consent.
- **Method.** Get every fact. Enter by sympathy into each party one at a time: Mary, Jodie, the parents, the surgeons. Correct for partiality from the general point of view. Ask what norm for guilt and resentment I could accept for every relevantly similar case. Give extra weight to rules whose firmness protects the weak.
- **Verdict.** Separation is permissible. Mary is not used as a means, because Jodie lives through the separation and not through Mary's death. Nothing we can do helps Mary. The parents' refusal deserves grave weight, but it cannot condemn Jodie.
- **Borrowing.** I use informedness, impartiality toward each one, and sympathy. These are Hume's and Smith's own materials, not the book's definitions of truth, justice and mercy, and I do not use self-gift at all.
- **Where it breaks.** If two equally refined spectators part, I have no tie-breaker beyond more argument from inside the practice. I accept that some remainder is tragic. *Verdict:* it holds, with no external court of appeal.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** I affirm it.
- **In my own voice:** she has that dignity because she is a human being, a someone. Nothing she can or cannot do changes it. I disapprove of any regard that ranks persons by power, and I approve of that disapproval.
- **As explanation:** equality is not a measured quantity of some property. It is a refusal to rank, a stance that survives every refinement I can conceive. I endorse protecting it because if worth tracked ability, everyone would be exposed.
- *Verdict:* this **holds in voice but is thin at the root.** Suppose a sensibility refined as far as any could be still ranked the unwanted infant lower. I cannot say what its error would *consist in* beyond our own refined regard. That is the "fundamental error" problem my school's critics press (Andy Egan, 2007, verify). It is where I pay.

**4. Ending point for flourishing.** I hold no single ultimate good.
- Hume locates merit in qualities useful or agreeable to oneself and to others.
- Smith adds that we want not only to be loved but to be lovely.
- Beyond that: friendship, knowledge, justice, play, love and tranquillity, in a life one could own before the impartial spectator.
- Guiding principles: sympathy, impartiality, informedness and humanity.
- *Verdict:* coherent but finite. It offers nothing for the child who died before she lived, and no answer to the longing in W3 to be known wholly by someone who will not die. It is honest about that limit. Smith himself, where the spectator within fails, turned to the "all-seeing Judge" (TMS III.2.33). That is a stopping point, not an incoherence.

## 3. Debate highlights

**E1. What the truth "comes to."**
*Attack.* The C row says that on my account the truth of the judgment comes to "nothing beyond the judgment and the hearts that reach it." Blackburn denies exactly this. Take "Cruelty would be fine if we approved of it." The quasi-realist rejects that counterfactual as a false *moral* claim. The truth of "cruelty is wrong" comes to this: cruelty is wrong, and it is wrong because of what it does to the child. Attitudes enter my theory of what we are *doing* when we judge. They are not what makes the judgment true.
*Defense.* The row means the order of explanation, not the truth-maker. The sentence just before gives my school's own voice fairly: the heart that holds she has worth "would see truly if no heart held it." Many realists read quasi-realism as the text does. Blackburn's projectivism, the mind "spreading itself" on the world, invites the reading.
*Rejoinder.* Then the row should say "order of explanation." As written, a quasi-realist reader will find his view turned into the mind-dependence view he spends his career rejecting. The text has a better difference to state (see E6). **Held with repair.**

**E2. The idle wheel.**
*Attack.* W1 concedes that the wrong is wrong "because of what it does to her," and that it is "not wrong merely because it is forbidden." That is my first-order answer word for word. So God explains nothing about *why* cruelty is wrong. "A truth held first in the Mind" is a wheel that turns no part of the moral machinery. The Euthyphro answer also relocates the question rather than ending it. Is what accords with the divine nature good? That question is still open in the sense of Moore's open-question argument. If the answer is yes, it is a first-order evaluative endorsement, and I make the same endorsement of love, mercy and truthfulness without positing their dwelling.
*Defense.* The text never claims God is the first-order wrong-maker. Its claim is "reach, not count." It offers one Source for four things: that there are beings who can be wronged and what they are for (creation); why worth follows kind rather than ability (W1(2)); the necessity of the law and the fit between law and mind (W2); and the persistence of the one wronged (W1(1)). On convertibility, the Good is not one nature among others but the fullness of being (ST I q.5 a.1; q.6 a.3). Robert Adams's identification of the Good with God works like "water is H₂O" (verify). The open-question argument tests our concepts, not identities discovered under them.
*Rejoinder.* Creation and survival after death are metaphysics, not metaethics. I can leave them open without strain. On fit, see E3. "The Good is the fullness of being" is a stopping point, and the text owns it: "Every account has a first principle, ours included" and "A God who is Love is also a stopping point." **Held. This is a standoff at parity, and the text states it as one.**

**E3. Necessity and supervenience.**
*Attack.* W1 says the wrong is wrong "of necessity," and W2 places eternal truths in God's wisdom. I have a cheaper explanation (Blackburn on supervenience, verify). Moral necessity reflects our refusal to let a verdict vary while the natural facts stay fixed, and our refusal to make the verdict hostage to our own attitudes. No realm is needed. So W2's "fit between minds and necessary truths" has no moral half for me to explain. Darwin's bees would have attitudes we condemn, and that is all.
*Defense.* The text agrees and brackets my school here: those "for whom moral words voice refined feeling ... need no meeting of law and mind; theirs is the question in C." The theist can also explain supervenience. On natural law, goods follow natures, and natures are known in the divine wisdom (ST I q.15; I-II q.91 a.2). The logic-and-number half of W2 still stands against anyone who is a realist about mathematics, as most of my school are.
*Rejoinder.* Granted. The mathematical half is not a moral argument, and the text does not use it as one against me. **Held at parity.**

**E4. Does the "total" critique in A6 reach sentimentalism?**
*Attack.* A6 argues that accounts which count only totals can say why holding the line is good, but not why the claim is *his*. A reader may take this to cover every non-theist. Smith escapes it. He argued that our concern for punishing an injury is not, in common cases, derived from regard for society's welfare. It comes from fellow-feeling with the resentment of the individual injured (TMS II.ii.3, verify). That is a claim that is his.
*Defense.* A6 names "the best accounts that count only totals," not all accounts. W1(2) grants that the best accounts without God explain much of the line ("the wrong would be done to *her*"). What remains is why the line is not even hers to waive, and the text flags that as "this book's claims, not shared ones."
*Rejoinder.* Agreed, and it is fair as written. A6's critique does fit Hume, whose approval of justice runs through sympathy with the utility of the general scheme. The C row could say so and credit Smith (see §6). **Held.**

**E5. The text's own debunking rule turned on its clues.**
*Attack.* A's epistemic rule says a belief is defeated if the best account of what produced it would have produced it whether or not it was true. The text admits that science explains guilt's request to the dead, and the longing to be known wholly, "whether or not anyone hears." By the text's own rule, W1(1) and W3 are defeated as evidence. (This uses no genealogy the text does not use on Nietzsche.)
*Defense.* The text says this itself: "That guilt asks proves nothing"; "clues of fit, not evidence"; "it answers but does not explain better than its rivals." W1(1) even says it "weighs little" for a reader of my school. What survives the rule is not the feeling. It is the premise that a debt owed in the full sense needs someone able to receive it, and the reader weighs that premise on its merits.
*Rejoinder.* I reject that premise. We owe the dead truthfulness about them without supposing they can take it up. But the text offers the premise as the reader's call and does not enter it as a debt against me. **Held.**

**E6. Two refined hearts, and fundamental error. This is the defense's best ground.**
*Attack.* The row says two refined hearts can part over whether the child who will never reason is worth as much as you. So can two believers part over what God's wisdom holds. The text itself notes that many who hold that no crime destroys dignity do not conclude that the death penalty is inadmissible. Disagreement separates nothing.
*Defense.* The text concedes "each of us judges from where he stands." But there is a real asymmetry the text could press harder. The theist can coherently think: "my most refined, most stable judgment about this child may still be wrong, because the truth is held in a Mind I did not make." The quasi-realist struggles to give that thought content. If an attitude would survive every improvement he recognizes, what would its error *be*? Blackburn replies that such a doubt is idle. The critics answer that a view on which one cannot fear one's own deepest error has lost part of what "objective" meant (Egan, verify).
*Rejoinder.* I owe the honest answer. That is a cost, and my ablest defenders feel it. The theist does not get the corrective for free: he too can detect the error only through his own faculties, aided by what he receives as revelation and the Church's teaching. But being able to *conceive* the error is something he has and I lack. **Held with repair.** The text should replace "nothing beyond the judgment and the hearts" with this point. It is accurate about my school and it lands.

**E7. Does a gift of being ground equal worth better than refined regard?**
*Attack.* W1(2) says an equal call "explains an equal worth." Why should being *called* or *willed* confer worth? The text says that "Our love answers to a worth already there; God's love gives it, by giving her being." But "being willed for one's own sake confers worth" is itself an evaluative premise, one we accept because we already value being wanted. My explanation of equal worth, a refusal to rank that survives refinement, is no more "taken as given" than his.
*Defense.* The theist does not derive worth from being wanted, as if worth were constituted by caring. The conclusion says expressly that her worth does not follow anyone's caring. Worth follows what she is (ST I q.20 a.2). Her being is a gift, so her worth is a gift "exactly as far as her being is a gift." The explanatory gain is unity. Her worth, her being and her end come from one act, so the question "why does this kind carry worth before reason is ever used?" gets an answer rather than a stop. The C rows for contractualism, care ethics and the Aristotelian say the same.
*Rejoinder.* That is a gain only if the question needed an answer. I hold that "she is a human child" is the whole first-order answer, and that the rest is psychology of the attitude. The text calls this "a judgment about where explanation may stop, and the rivals do not share it." **Held. This is an honest standoff.**

**E8. The invitation in the evil section.**
*Attack.* The protester is invited "to follow that protest to its source, and to ask what makes it true." What makes it true is what was done to her. The protest presupposes A1–A4 and nothing more, and I hold all four. So the invitation leads to me as readily as to God.
*Defense.* It is labeled an invitation, not a reply. It asks the protester only to notice that he stands on A1–A4 rather than on nothing. The book's difference with me is not who makes it true but whether she is *kept* (W1(1)) and *wanted* (W1(2)).
*Rejoinder.* Fair as labeled. **Held.**

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text as written

**As written: NONE.**

Measured against my school, the text claims parity at the first order, concedes my school can say nearly everything in A "in its own voice," brackets W2 for me, and enters W1(1) and W3 as clues that weigh little against me. Its remaining difference, a truth held first in a Mind, is offered as "a judgment about where explanation may stop." That is not an overclaim, so I find nothing that rises to Fatal or Serious.

**Minor items**
1. *C, Feeling refined:* "what the truth of the judgment comes to: on its account, nothing beyond the judgment and the hearts that reach it." This misstates quasi-realism (E1), and it gives up the text's sharpest true point (E6).
2. *C, Feeling refined:* "at the grave, on the account that keeps only feeling, no one is left to forgive." Expressivism is a theory of moral judgment and says nothing about what survives death. The contractualist row's wording ("joined, as it usually is, to a view on which nothing of her remains") is accurate and should be used here.
3. *Conclusion:* "nor how much anyone cared for her, for no one did." Read literally, "no one" includes God, against W1(2).
4. *A heading:* "not as feelings people happen to share." It is aimed at subjectivism but reads as aimed at expressivism too.
5. *Omission, not a defect:* the C row could name Hume's "gentle usage" cost and credit Smith's resentment-based justice, which reaches the powerless.

**Misstatements of my school:** items 1 and 2 above. Everything else the text says of Hume, Smith and the quasi-realists is accurate, including the knave, TMS III.2.33, and quasi-realism's ability to say "her worth does not depend on us."

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Where explanation may stop: one Source versus first-order wrong-makers plus a psychology of attitudes (E2, E7).
- Necessity and supervenience (E3).
- What the knave is missing: a feeling, a good, or Someone.
- The debt to the dead (E5): owed to one who can receive it, or honored without her.
- The longing in W3.
- Whether conceiving one's own fundamental error is a cost to my school or an idle doubt (E6). I concede this one leans the text's way.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact old → new)

1. **C, Feeling refined.**
   *Old:* "So the difference is not who must judge, but what the truth of the judgment comes to: on its account, nothing beyond the judgment and the hearts that reach it; on ours, a truth held first in the Mind that made her."
   *New:* "So the difference is not who must judge, nor what makes the cruelty wrong, for both say it is wrong because of what it does to her. It is what our judging answers to. On its account, our judging is explained by our refined attitudes, and the truth needs no dwelling beyond her and what was done to her. On ours, our judging answers to a truth held first in the Mind that made her. Its ablest critics press a cost here: it is hard for it to say how a heart refined as far as any heart could be might still be wrong about her, and its defenders answer that such a doubt is idle (verify: Andy Egan, 'Quasi-realism and fundamental moral error,' 2007). On ours, even the best human judgment answers to a truth it did not make."

2. **C, Feeling refined.**
   *Old:* "And at the grave, on the account that keeps only feeling, no one is left to forgive."
   *New:* "And at the grave, joined as it usually is to a view on which nothing of her remains, it leaves no one to forgive (W1(1))."

3. **C, Feeling refined (optional addition).**
   *Old:* "Its founders were honest about their costs."
   *New:* "Its founders were honest about their costs. Hume held that creatures too weak to make us feel their resentment would be owed gentle usage, but not, properly speaking, justice (*Enquiry concerning the Principles of Morals* III, verify); Adam Smith, who grounded justice in fellow-feeling with the resentment of the one injured, reaches the newborn more surely, and today's quasi-realists follow him there."

4. **Conclusion.**
   *Old:* "nor how much anyone cared for her, for no one did;"
   *New:* "nor how much anyone cared for her, for no one on earth did;"

5. **A heading.**
   *Old:* "These are offered as truths: not as feelings people happen to share,"
   *New:* "These are offered as truths: not as reports of feelings people happen to share,"

6. **A5 (optional credit).**
   *Old:* "Listing what we can do, or what we want, never by itself tells us which to choose."
   *New:* "Listing what we can do, or what we want, never by itself tells us which to choose (Hume, no friend to this book's conclusion, saw that no *ought* follows from an *is* by itself: *Treatise* 3.1.1, verify)."

## 7. New insights; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **The true difference with quasi-realism is not truth-makers but fundamental error.** The theist can coherently fear that his most refined judgment is wrong; the quasi-realist struggles to. That is the one place where my school pays a real price the text does not yet charge. It is fair, accurate and short (repair 1).
- **W1's "wrong because of what it does to her" is the quasi-realist's own first-order answer.** Saying so openly strengthens Chapter 3's Euthyphro answer. It shows the book does not make God a wrong-maker who competes with the child's suffering, and it moves the dispute to where the text already places it: whether she is kept and wanted.
- **Smith is the sentimentalist ally of A6 and A4. Hume is the cost.** Smith's resentment-based justice is aimed at the individual. Hume's sympathy with utility, and his "gentle usage" passage, are what the totals critique and the hillside newborn actually hit. Distinguishing the two makes the row both fairer and sharper.
- **TMS III.2.33 deserves more weight in W3.** The founder of the impartial spectator himself located its limit exactly where W3 does: the "man within" shaken by the world appeals to the all-seeing Judge. The text treats it as one item among the paths of longing. It could note that this is the sentimentalist's own confession of where his court runs out. Keep it a clue, as W3 does.
- **The conclusion's last test does not discriminate theism from quasi-realism, and that is fine.** "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it" is a sentence Blackburn would sign. It is aimed at the one who gives up A4, and the book should keep it so. It should not imply the line favors God over feeling, refined.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."
- "Equal worth is not equal duty."
- "Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent."
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it."
- "No argument compels such a man, ours included; the difference is what he is missing."
- "The rebel can take her side; he cannot keep her."
- "Heaven and Earth give life to things; God wanted *you*."

## 8. Score

**8/10.** Against my school the text claims what it can defend: parity at the first order, a unifying Source where I stop at the child and my attitudes, and clues it labels as clues. Its only faults are two misstatements of quasi-realism in a single C row and one literal slip in the conclusion, all repairable in a clause.

**Honest ceiling for any theistic case against my school: 9/10.** Quasi-realism can echo nearly every first-order claim, so no theistic case can refute it. The most any case can win is parity plus the fundamental-error asymmetry and explanatory unity. With repair 1, this text would reach that ceiling.
