# Argument Lab, Round 4: Secular Neo-Aristotelian Naturalism / Virtue Ethics vs. v4

## 1. School and voice

This is the school of Philippa Foot (*Natural Goodness*; "The Problem of Abortion and the Doctrine of the Double Effect," 1967; "Euthanasia," 1977; "Utilitarianism and the Virtues," 1985), Rosalind Hursthouse, Michael Thompson, John McDowell and Martha Nussbaum. Goodness is measured against the form of life of one's kind, and justice has two poles: a wrong is done *to* someone. In Round 3 this school's one Serious objection was that W1(2) said the rival could not explain "may not be killed for others," when Foot explained it with A6's own case, and that the Church's real ground (life held in trust, CCC 2258, 2280) was missing. Round 4 checks whether v4 took that repair, and presses the new text: the Lord of life, the credit to Foot, the reworked flourishing row, A5's list, and the parasite.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

*(The Round 1–3 answers stand. Briefly:)*

- **Q1. Justice.** Justice gives each what is due, and it is bipolar: the wrong is done to *her*, and she has the complaint (Thompson; NE V; ST II-II q.58). **Holds.** It borrows only the *equality* of all parties, which Aristotle did not have.
- **Q2. A hard case.** Take a ventilator from a stable disabled patient to give it to a younger one with a better outlook? No. That is doing harm, not failing to help, and the newcomer has no claim on *his* ventilator (Foot). **Coherent.** It reaches the Church's verdict by the Church's structure.
- **Q3. Equal dignity.** Membership in the rational kind, from conception to death, plus the shape of justice. **Holds for equality and for "not for others."** It breaks, by the Church's lights, on euthanasia: Foot allowed ending a life that is no longer a good to the person, at his request.
- **Q4. Ultimate good.** *Eudaimonia*: a complete life of virtuous activity among friends, bounded by death. The virtues take their shape from need and danger (Nussbaum). **Coherent.** It is the strongest secular reason to deny that W3's longing belongs to human nature.

### 3b. Exchanges

**E1. The credit to Foot: right in spirit, slightly off in fact.**
*Opponent:* W1(2) lists four reasons: the wrong is done to her; each person must be answered one by one; the five have no claim on her life; doing harm is not failing to help. It then says "That is Philippa Foot's own answer to the magistrate and the mob." Only the last is Foot's 1967 answer. She explained the magistrate case by the greater strictness of negative duties over positive ones. "Done to her" is Thompson's bipolarity. "Answered one by one" is Scanlon's and Nagel's language about the separateness of persons. The next sentence is a second slip. It says that, asked why justice has that shape "without exception and whatever the numbers," the rivals answer that this is simply what justice is. Not all of this school holds the line at every number. Hursthouse gives a whole chapter to tragic and irresolvable dilemmas, and Nussbaum to what she calls the tragic question. Your own A6 says honestly that fewer hold the line when the numbers grow vast. Don't make us all Anscombeans in W1(2) after granting in A6 that we aren't.
*Defense:* Both conceded. The list is a fair composite of the best secular accounts, and the attribution should sit only on its last item. The second sentence should speak of "those of them who hold the line whatever the numbers," which is also the only group for whom the question arises.
*Result:* **Held with repair (Minor).** These are two misstatements of the school, and neither breaks the step. The step is "they stop at 'what justice is'," and it stays true of the absolutists among them (R4.1).

**E2. Jurisdiction and the paradox of deontology.**
*Opponent:* You now ground the line in stewardship: "No one may take what is not his to take, even to keep others from taking more." Press it. The Owner of all six lives wants each kept. A steward who kills one to keep five from being killed seems to serve the Owner's will better than one who keeps his hands clean. Stewardship alone gives you an agent-neutral goal (keep the Owner's goods), not a constraint on the agent.
*Defense:* Two things together close it, and v4 has both. First, the trust is a trust over one's *own acts*. A steward is given authority to keep and use, not to destroy, and no number of others' thefts gives him a title he was never given. Second, the Owner does not want a total of lives. He wants each one for her own sake (GS 24; "not for the species, not for the total, not for us"). An Owner who wills each for herself cannot be served by spending one of them as a means. Worth in the victim alone gives a goal. Lack of title in the agent alone gives a rule with no reason behind it. Together they give a constraint with a reason.
*Opponent's rejoinder:* So it is the "for her own sake" doing the work after all, which is the value we said yields only a goal.
*Defense:* No. It is the conjunction. A rule against taking what is not yours, laid down by One who loves each for herself, is exactly the shape of an agent-relative constraint. Your account reaches the same shape and stops at "what justice is."
*Result:* **Held.** This is parity on "for others," with a stated reason behind the shape. It is what Round 3 asked for.

**E3. "That is why the line has no exceptions," when your own Doctor gives it one.**
*Opponent:* You make jurisdiction the reason the line has no exceptions. Aquinas, from whom you take it, draws the opposite conclusion from the same premise. Because God is Lord of life and death, he says, one who kills an innocent man at God's command does not sin, any more than God does (ST II-II q.64 a.6 ad 1; I-II q.94 a.5 ad 2, on Abraham). Death can be brought by God's command on anyone, guilty or innocent, without injustice. So on your ground the line has exactly one exception, the Owner's say-so. On Foot's ground, which is what justice is, it has none. Your book says "not even God makes cruelty right." Your ground for the absolute against killing the innocent is weaker than ours, because it rests on who has authority and not on what the act is.
*Defense:* The line in v4 is the line that binds every creature: "No one may take what is not his to take." The Church states it exactly so: no one can, in any circumstance, claim for himself the right directly to destroy an innocent human being (CCC 2258). It is intrinsic to the act, in its object (VS 80), and it is necessary given what a creature is. For a creature to dispose of an innocent life is to claim a lordship he does not have. That is a fact about the act, not a decree, so the creature's absolute is as necessary as yours. The Giver who takes back the life He gave, in the death that comes to all, does not take what is not His. On v4's own evil section, He never spends anyone as a mere means against her own good (SCG III.112). How to read the command to Abraham, which was stopped (Genesis 22:12), is argued within the tradition. The book need not settle it, and it does not put it in a premise.
*Opponent's rejoinder:* Then for creatures your absolute and ours have the same strength. Your "reach" over us on the line comes down to one leg: the life that is not even its owner's to end.
*Defense:* Yes. That is what the God row should claim, and it is what v4 claims.
*Result:* **Held.** No repair is needed for soundness. An optional clause (R4.2) gets ahead of the objection without bringing up Abraham.

**E4. The circle: A4 now carries the very verdict that W1(2) explains and the God row counts as reach.**
*Opponent:* Here is what the fixes broke. In Round 2 this school got the euthanasia verdict taken out of A4, and v3's "not lost when life grows hard" kept it out. To answer the censor (legitimate defense), v4's A4 now says every human being is "never, if innocent, to be deliberately killed." That is doctrinally right. But it puts back into a premise of "what we take to be true" a thesis that the nearest rival's founder denied: Foot allowed ending, at his request, a life that is no longer a good to the one who lives it. Then W1(2) explains it ("not hers to throw away … held in trust"), and the God row counts it as reach over us ("not even her own to cross"). Against this school, the one place v4 claims to explain more than we do is a datum we deny, and A states it without notice. A6 handles its own contested part honestly: "that is this book's claim … not a shared one." A4 does not. The A heading names Singer as a denier of parts of A4, but not Foot. And your conclusion, "it cannot compel the one who gives up A4," now seems to sweep Foot in with those who deny equal worth. She held equal worth. Last, "throw away" misdescribes her case. She was not throwing a life away; she was ending one, for the person's own sake.
*Defense:* The method allows it. A offers truths the reader is invited to hold, names the deniers, and lets C show the cost, and the flourishing row does show it ("as though a person owned his life … CCC 2280"). For the reader who holds A4 whole, the reach is real. But the objection is right about the inconsistency. A4 should flag its contested part as A6 does. The God row should say that this item is reach for those who hold it. "Throw away" should become the Catechism's own word: life is not ours to dispose of (CCC 2280). The censor's clause stays. It is only labeled.
*Opponent's rejoinder:* With those labels, I have no complaint. A reason given for a verdict I reject is not a fault. A reason presented as explaining a datum we share would be one.
*Result:* **Held with repair (Minor).** This is not Serious, because the text's own structure already discloses the cost: deniers are allowed in A, and the C row names Foot's position. As written, though, a careful reader of this school would see the circle before the book names it. This is the most important Minor point in this report (R4.3, R4.4).

**E5. Bipolar justice: is the victim first, or is God "before all else"?**
*Opponent:* In one paragraph the W1 frame says each such wrong is "also, and before all else, an offense against Him (CCC 1440)." Four sentences later it says guilt is "owed to her first (A2)." Which is first? If God is before all else, the victim has become a second creditor in her own case, which is the worry this school pressed in Round 3. The closing adds that the wrong is wrong "because of what it does to her, and to Him whose she is." Your evil section says, with St Bernard, that God cannot suffer. What does a wrong *do* to Him?
*Defense:* The two "firsts" are in different orders. In the order of what we see, and of the debt between two people, the wrong is to her, and v4 says plainly that we see it first in what it does to the child. In the order of depth, every wrong against a creature is an offense against her Creator. That offense is not a competing claim. It is the root of her claim, because His love is what made her precious (CCC 1850). An offense is not a harm. God is not made worse off. He is the One whose order of love is refused. Both points are in v4, but the wording invites both of the misreadings the opponent named.
*Result:* **Held with repair (Minor)** (R4.5).

**E6. The flourishing row misstates the school's third answer.**
*Opponent:* "Its best defenders answer that a nature binds simply because it is ours." That makes our view possessive, and so it invites the obvious reply: why should I care that it is mine? None of us says that. Foot says that goodness of the will is a case of natural goodness, so that practical rationality is part of what it is to be a good human. McDowell says that asking why the reasons we see are really reasons asks for a standpoint outside the space of reasons, and there is none. Thompson says natural-historical judgments are neither statistical nor accidental. The answer is not "because it is ours." It is "the question asks for a place to stand that does not exist." I admit that the shorthand "binds simply because it is ours" came from this school's own Round 3 list of standoffs. That was a fault in the shorthand, and the book should not inherit it.
*Defense:* Accepted. The row should state the answer as the school states it, and still call it a stopping point. The standoff at parity is unchanged.
*Result:* **Held with repair (Minor)** (R4.6).

**E7. The evil section: "not as a means" is false of the fawn on Aquinas's own lion.**
*Opponent:* v4 now says that God does not intend the harms of a world of real causes "either as an end or as a means," though He wills the order they come with (ST I q.19 a.9; q.49 a.2). For the fawn, that is not Aquinas. He says that a universal provider allows defects in particular things so as not to hinder the good of the whole: the lion would not live if there were no slaying of animals (ST I q.22 a.2 ad 2). In willing the natural order, God wills some things to be corrupted (q.19 a.9). And q.49 a.2, which you cite, says that God *is* the author of the evil of penalty. The fawn's death is not a side effect next to the wolf's life. It is the wolf's food. And you have now said, rightly, that the parasite's nature is "a wisdom" too. A wise nature whose good is through the child's loss looks like a design with the loss as its means.
*Defense:* For persons, v4 is right, and it says why: within the order God governs each person for her own sake (SCG III.112), and He never spends anyone as a mere means against her own good. The parasite's good is not willed through *this child*. Her infection is permitted in an order of real causes, and it is not ordered to the parasite's life as its price. For the other animals the objection is correct. In Aquinas's own picture the order of animal life is partly built from such losses, and they are willed for the sake of the whole. That is weight. v4 already owns it ("so is theirs … on this side of the scale"; "for the fawn that hope is thinner"). The "means" clause should be limited to persons, and the lion should be named as part of the weight.
*Opponent's rejoinder:* SCG III.112 then makes the child heavier for you: the one whose life is governed for her own sake is the one the parasite kills.
*Defense:* Yes, and v4 puts exactly that on the scale. The theist carries more on the parasite, and the naturalist holds the tortured child less firmly (A1, W2). Each account carries what it carries.
*Result:* **Held with repair (Minor).** This is a precision fault in the exegesis. The step it serves, that God's permission is no trade *in persons*, stands (R4.7).

**E8. Smaller points: the second road, W3, A5's list, the A2 example.**
*Opponent:* (a) The second road in W1(1) is credited to "the great ancestral traditions." It is also the secular tragic view: the dead can be wronged as they were (NE I.10–11; Feinberg; Pitcher), and the debt is fixed forever. Name it, or the reader will think the only rival to "she is still someone" is ancestor rites. (b) W3 still does not answer the deeper form of Nussbaum's point: the human virtues are shaped by need and danger. (c) A5's list: is it fair to Aquinas, and can this school grant it?
*Defense:* (a) Conceded. One clause fixes it, and it makes the clue fairer, not weaker. (b) Repair 6.8 from Round 3 is still good: the risen Christ kept His wounds. (c) The list matches q.94 a.2: preservation of life; what nature teaches all animals, the union of male and female and the raising of offspring; and, proper to reason, knowing the truth about God and living in society. This school grants each as a good of a rational animal. "The truth about God" is neutral, since an atheist holds that there is a truth about God to know, namely that there is none. "By itself" in the heading answers Foot. The new A2 example matches our own distinction between harming and wronging. On Pitcher's view the blown letter frustrates her interest without wronging her, which is exactly v4's contrast.
*Result:* (a) **Minor repair** (R4.8). (b) **Minor, carried over** (R4.9). (c) **Held.**

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**Against v4 as written:**

**As written: NONE.**

Round 3's one Serious objection (W1(2) overclaimed against Foot and lacked the jurisdiction ground) is answered. In E2 and E3 the new ground holds against the two strongest attacks this school can bring on it: the paradox of deontology, and Aquinas's own exception for the Lord of life. In each case it reaches parity or better. What the fixes broke (E4) is a fault of labeling, not of logic. The A heading already allows contested truths and names deniers, and the flourishing row already states Foot's view and its cost. So the circle is disclosed in the text, just not where it should be. None of the remaining points breaks a step.

**Minor (wording and precision):**
1. **A4 and the God row form a small circle against this school** (E4). The censor's clause, which must stay, puts back the euthanasia verdict without the flag A6 uses. W1(2) then explains it, and the God row counts it as reach. "Throw away" misdescribes Foot. *This is the most important Minor point.*
2. **W1(2) misstates the school twice** (E1): the four-item list is all attributed to Foot, and every rival is said to hold the line "whatever the numbers" (against A6's own honesty, Hursthouse and Nussbaum).
3. **Flourishing row:** "binds simply because it is ours" misstates the school's answer (E6). The phrase came from this school's own Round 3 shorthand.
4. **W1 frame and closing:** "owed to her first" against "before all else" (CCC 1440), and "what it does … to Him" against impassibility (E5).
5. **Evil *Second*:** "not … as a means" overreaches Aquinas for non-rational creatures, and the q.49 a.2 citation points the other way (E7).
6. **W1(1):** the second road is credited only to the ancestral traditions, not to its secular tragic form (E8).
7. **W3:** the virtues of finitude; the risen wounds, carried over from Round 3 (E8).

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**
- Whether a nature's standards need a source, or whether asking for one wants a standpoint outside the life of reason (McDowell).
- Whether necessary truths, the core of the moral law among them, need a ground in a Mind (ST I q.15; q.16 a.7) or none.
- Whether the arrival of minds that can see what holds for any mind needs explaining beyond evolution (W2 "reach").
- The tragic view of the dead (a debt fixed forever that no one receives) against "she is still someone" (W1(1)).
- Whether the exceptionless shape of justice is simply "what justice is," or is explained by a life held in trust from One who wills each for her own sake (E2, E3).
- Whether a life that is no longer a good to the one who lives it may be ended at his request: Foot against CCC 2280. This is the single real moral disagreement between this school and the Church.
- Whether W3's longing belongs to human nature, or is a Christian shaping of the wish for more, and whether glory keeps the virtues of finitude in what is formal to them (ST I-II q.67 a.1).
- The theist's heavier load on the wolf, the lion and the parasite, against the naturalist's weaker hold on the tortured child (E7).

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**R4.1 W1(2), the Foot sentences (E1).** Replace from "the wrong would be done to *her*;" through "this is simply what justice is." with:
> the wrong would be done to *her*; each person must be answered one by one, and no total answers her; the five who need her organs have no claim on her life; and doing harm is not the same as failing to help, which is Philippa Foot's own answer to the magistrate and the mob. Those of them who hold the line whatever the numbers, asked why justice has that shape, answer that this is simply what justice is.

**R4.2 W1(2), the Lord of life (E3, E4).** Replace "and not hers to throw away: it is held in trust from the One who alone is Lord of life (CCC 2258, 2280). That is why the line has no exceptions. No one may take what is not his to take, even to keep others from taking more." with:
> and not hers to dispose of, even when it has grown hard: it is held in trust from the One who alone is Lord of life (CCC 2258, 2280). That is why the line has no exceptions. No one may take what is not his to take, even to keep others from taking more. *(Optional:)* The Giver takes back no life that is not His, in the death that comes to all, and spends no one as a mere means; no creature may claim that right for himself (CCC 2258).

**R4.3 A4 (E4).** Keep the censor's clause. After "(*Dignitas Infinita* 1, 15; CCC 2261, 2267)." insert:
> That this holds even when a dying man asks to die is the Church's claim, argued in W1(2), and not a shared one: some of the best thinkers without God, who hold the rest of A4, deny it (C).

**R4.4 C, the God row (E4).** Replace "a reason why the line that protects the innocent has no exceptions and is not even her own to cross," with:
> a reason why the line that protects the innocent has no exceptions, and, for those who hold it, why it is not even her own to cross,

**R4.5 W1 frame and closing (E5).**
- Frame: "and this book agrees: it is owed to her first (A2)." → "and this book agrees: it is owed to her, truly and in her own right (A2)."
- Closing: "The wrong is wrong because of what it does to her, and to Him whose she is." → "The wrong is wrong because of what it does to her, and it offends Him whose she is."

**R4.6 C, the flourishing row (E6).** Replace "its best defenders answer that a nature binds simply because it is ours, and that is a stopping point." with:
> its best defenders answer that the question asks for a place to stand outside the life of reason, and there is none: for a rational animal, seeing what is good is part of being what it is. That is a stopping point.

**R4.7 Evil, paragraph 2, *Second* (E7).** Replace "and the harms that follow in a world of real causes God does not intend, either as an end or as a means, though He wills the order they come with (ST I q.19 a.9; q.49 a.2)." with:
> and the harms that follow in a world of real causes God does not will for their own sake, but only as part of an order He wills (ST I q.19 a.9; q.49 a.2). Within that order no person is willed as a means against her own good, for He governs each person for her own sake (SCG III.112). Among the other animals, Aquinas grants, the order is partly built of such losses: the lion lives by the slaying of animals (ST I q.22 a.2 ad 2). That belongs to the weight already named.

**R4.8 W1(1), the second road (E8).** After "care for those she loved." insert:
> Careful thinkers without God take this road too: the dead can be wronged as they were, and the wrong stays fixed forever.

**R4.9 W3 (carried over from Round 3, 6.8).** After "made whole (CCC 1025, 999)." insert:
> The risen Christ kept His wounds (John 20:27): what a finite love lived through is not erased but kept, and healed.

## 7. New insights and lines worth keeping

**New insights**
- **Jurisdiction plus "willed for itself" answers the paradox of deontology, and neither does alone.** Lack of title gives a rule; worth in the victim gives a goal; a rule from One who loves each for herself gives a constraint with a reason (E2). The book should keep the two side by side, as v4 does.
- **The Lord-of-life ground has a classical exception (ST II-II q.64 a.6 ad 1), and the book is stronger for knowing it.** The creature's absolute is intrinsic to the act: to dispose of an innocent life is to claim a lordship one lacks. So it is as necessary as Foot's. The book's real gain over Foot is the one leg Foot gave up, the life not even its owner's to dispose of (E3).
- **A censor's fix can reopen an opponent's fix.** "Never, if innocent, to be deliberately killed" is right doctrine and a contested premise. The cure is A6's own honesty, labeling, not deleting (E4). This applies beyond A4: wherever the Church's full teaching must enter A, flag it as the book's claim.
- **Two "firsts" need two names.** First in what we see: the child. Before all else in depth: God, whose love is the root of her claim and not a rival to it (E5).
- **For persons "not as a means"; for the fawn, honest weight.** SCG III.112 is the hinge. It protects the child's place in God's order and, for that very reason, makes her loss weigh more (E7).

**Lines worth keeping**
- "Not hers to dispose of, even when it has grown hard."
- "No one may take what is not his to take, even to keep others from taking more."
- "We agree she is owed. We part at the grave, and over what gives her the claim at all."
- "Not every child is wanted by those who made her; every child was wanted by the One who did."
- "Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent."
- "Measured against one end that is God Himself, the genius and the newborn are for the same thing."
- "The risen Christ kept His wounds."

## 8. Score

**v4 as written: 9/10. With the repairs above: 9.5/10.** v4 took every substantive repair this school asked for. The new ground, life held in trust from One who wills each for her own sake, holds against the strongest attacks this school can bring on it. What remains are a labeling fault the censor's fix created in A4 and a few places where the text misstates Foot's school, none of which breaks a step.
