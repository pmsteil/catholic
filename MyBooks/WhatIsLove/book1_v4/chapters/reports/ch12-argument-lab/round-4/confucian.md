# Argument Lab, Round 4: Confucian opponent vs. v4

## 1. School and voice

The opponent speaks for the Confucian tradition at full strength: the *Analects*, Mencius (the nature is good) against Xunzi (the nature must be shaped; Heaven is nature's constant course), the *Mean*, the *Great Learning*, the *Classic of Filial Piety*, the Neo-Confucian school of principle (Zhang Zai, the Cheng brothers, Zhu Xi), and Mou Zongsan's "immanent transcendence." He grants nearly everything the book says about love and denies only that its Source is Someone. This round he tests the text v4 took from his Round 3 report, and the new ground in W1(2). *Notes:* *Analects* numbering follows Slingerland. Everything is paraphrased, and Chinese terms are given only so the passages can be checked.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges (short; unchanged in substance from Round 3)

1. **Justice.** It starts in repayment (*bao*): we owe first what we received, from parents, from the sages, and from Heaven and Earth. Reciprocity measures it (15.24). *Holds without borrowing, and it is close to Chapter 4.* But repayment needs a receiver (Exchanges 2–3).
2. **A hard case.** Shun carries his murderer father to the sea (Mencius 7A35): he does not bend the law, and he does not abandon his father. *Coherent and honest about the cost.* It strains A4 at one point only: the victim's claim yields.
3. **Equal dignity.** Each person carries the whole of principle, as the one moon is whole in ten thousand rivers. *Holds on equality.* It breaks at "for her own sake": drain a river and the moon has lost nothing.
4. **Ultimate good.** Sagehood: to form one body with all things. For the world, the Great Unity, where widows, orphans and the disabled are all cared for. *Coherent and noble.* It stops short of communion, and of the dead.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1: did v4 take the Round 3 repairs?**
*Audit against my Round 3 §5 and §6:*
1. W1(1)'s either/or (my one Serious objection): **fixed.** There are now three roads. The second is honored ("noble, and the Church walks much of it"). The clue rests on what the second road cannot do: no one receives, no one releases, and the kinless dead get nothing. Mozi's "rites without guests" is in, and so is "it asks to be forgiven by her." The weight is now limited ("as much as the reader finds the third road truer than the second"). The debunker's line ("science ... cannot show that no one does") replaces "people everywhere act as though she is." That is better than what I asked for. Two parts of my repair were dropped: the Xunzi clause, and the closing "as though it were shaped for someone who does." Dropping the second was right. Dropping the first left a fault (Exchange 2).
2. The sacred-order row: **taken nearly word for word.** Zhu Xi is named in the heading. "Gives life to each thing and intends none" is in, and so are the moon in the rivers, "most systematic," Mou's refusal of "impersonal," "others love me back," Zhu Xi's answer to evil beside CCC 310, "set side by side," Xunzi as the limit case sent to the flourishing row, and Chrysippus's foot ("spends the part for the whole"). Two new faults are in my own wording, and I own them (Exchanges 6, 9a).
3. The evil section's Confucian hope: **taken**, and set inside a better sentence that names the Christian hope as "more particular."
4. A2, "Shame, left to itself": **taken.**
5. The W1 frame: **taken**, in a stronger form ("We agree she is owed. We part at the grave").
6. W1(2), "even the child no one wanted": **taken**, better ("Not every child is wanted by those who made her; every child was wanted by the One who did").
7. W2, "one Source of both": **taken.**
*Result: Held.* Every Serious and Minor point from Round 3 is closed. What follows is new.

**Exchange 2: "the road of the great ancestral traditions." That misdescribes the ancestral traditions, and it puts Confucius on a road he never claimed.**
*Opponent.* "Your second road, as v4 now states it, is a debt owed to her *as she was*, with no one there to hear. You call that 'the road of the great ancestral traditions.' It is not. For most of the people who have kept those rites, from the Zhou temple through the *Book of Rites* to Zhu Xi's *Family Rituals* and the village altar, the ancestors *come* to the offering and *partake* of it (*lai ge*, *xiang*). They are reported to, they are asked for blessing, and faults are confessed before their tablets. Most of the ancestral world walks your *third* road. The second road is how some of our most reflective teachers *read* the rites. Xunzi is the clearest: sacrifice is the feeling of remembrance and longing, the height of loyalty and love; among gentlemen it is the human way, and among the common people it is serving ghosts (ch. 19). Your Round 3 text named him. v4 cut him and kept Confucius, which is the wrong cut. Confucius respected the spirits and kept them at a distance (6.22). He would not talk about them (7.21). He praised Yu for being lavish toward them (8.21). And he said that whoever offends against Heaven has no one left to pray to (3.13). He was reticent, not a denier. 'Without claiming that they hear' is literally true. In a sentence about a road on which no one hears, though, it enrolls him there. And because Xunzi is gone, Mozi now has the last word. Xunzi answered Mozi: the rite is shaped for the *host*, whose grief it completes."
*Defense.* "Conceded on all three counts. The phrase came from this school's own Round 3 repair, but the text has to be accurate whatever its source. Notice which way the error runs, though. It gives the rival more than he is owed. If most ancestral practice takes the dead to receive, then the second road is the reading of a learned minority, not the witness of the ancestral world. Even so, the book may not cash that in as evidence: the debunker's sentence already rules out arguing from what people do at graves. So the repair is for accuracy, not for advantage. Name Xunzi as the teacher of the second road. Describe Confucius as reticent. And let Xunzi answer Mozi, because both are right about something: the rite completes the giver, and it is shaped for a receiver."
*Result: Held with repair (Minor).* It is not Serious: the inference (no one receives on the second road) does not depend on who walks that road, and the clue limits its own weight. Repair R1.

**Exchange 3: on Zhu Xi's account, *something* receives.**
*Opponent.* "'On that road, what the living pay, no one receives.' Our best metaphysics denies that. Zhu Xi held that a person's *qi* does not scatter at once when she dies, and that the descendant's *qi* is continuous with the ancestor's. When the descendant sacrifices with complete sincerity, the ancestor's *qi* is gathered and moved, and there is a real response (*gange*; *Classified Conversations*, book 3). Fan Zuyu, whom Zhu Xi quotes on 3.12, put it this way: where there is sincerity, there are the spirits; where there is none, there are none. So the payment *is* received, through the line, in a way no ghost story needs. Your second road has a receiver after all."
*Defense.* "It has a receiver, and that is the point. Look at what receives. It is not she but the *qi* she shares with her descendants, and Zhu Xi himself said that it disperses over the generations. That is why his *Family Rituals* keep only four generations of tablets in the offering hall. It is gathered only as far as her line carries her and only as long as her line remembers. It is moved, not met. Above all, it cannot release anything. Something can respond. Only someone can forgive. v4 already says it in the right place: 'Guilt does not only grieve a fact. It asks to be forgiven by her, and only someone can forgive.' Zhu Xi's account explains the response the mourner feels. It does not give him her pardon."
*Opponent rejoinder.* "The sincere son does not ask pardon at the tablet. He asks to complete his filial duty by the rest of his life. The *Classic of Filial Piety* says it ends in establishing yourself and making your parents' name known."
*Defense.* "Some sons ask nothing more. The guilt v4 describes is the guilt of the one who betrayed her, and that guilt asks for something no well-lived life can supply: her word. That difference is the standoff, and v4 names it honestly."
*Result: Held.* No repair. Zhu Xi's account is a fine datum for the book (§7).

**Exchange 4: the kinless dead are not left with no one. We built altars for them.**
*Opponent.* "'Many of the dead have no one left to remember them at all.' The first Ming emperor ordered altars for the unattended dead (*li* altars) at every level, from the capital down to the village, so that the dead with no descendants would be fed three times a year. Popular religion added the festival of the hungry ghosts. Then v4 answers its own point with 'at every Mass the Church prays for all the dead.' That has the same structure: a whole people's rite for those no one of their own remembers. Remembrance is not the asymmetry."
*Defense.* "Granted. Both peoples remember the forgotten dead, and both do it without names. What is left of the point is what was always its core. On the second road, the altar for the forgotten dead is received by no one. On the third, the prayer reaches someone. The sentence should say exactly that, so that it does not claim an advantage the Confucian state matched."
*Result: Held with repair (Minor).* Repair R2.

**Exchange 5: W1(2)'s new ground, life held in trust. We hold life in trust too, without God.**
*Opponent.* "W1(2) now rests the exceptionless line on this: 'her life is not ours to spend ... and not hers to throw away: it is held in trust from the One who alone is Lord of life.' The first chapter of the *Classic of Filial Piety* says that body, hair and skin are received from our parents, and that not daring to harm them is the beginning of filial piety. Life held in trust, not one's own to throw away, and no God needed. And if you say a trust needs a trustor beyond the parents, Zhang Zai's *Western Inscription* names one: Heaven is my father and Earth my mother. Xunzi calls Heaven and Earth the root of life (ch. 19)."
*Defense.* "The *Classic of Filial Piety* is a real witness that 'not hers to throw away' is not a Christian invention, and the book should use it. But look where a trust held from parents leads. First, it gives the parents a jurisdiction they cannot bear. Imperial law punished a parent who killed a child far more lightly than a child who struck a parent, and Confucian moralists had to preach for centuries against the drowning of unwanted daughters. A trust held *from* the parents is weakest exactly where the parents are the danger. Second, it has exceptions. 'The man of *ren* will give up his life to complete *ren*' (15.9). Later the state honored widows who killed themselves to stay chaste, until the Yongzheng emperor tried to stop the practice. On this ground the line is hers to cross for rightness, and others are glad when she crosses it. Third, take the trust back to Heaven and Earth, and it is held from a source that intends no one. Zhang Zai's 'Heaven is my father' is a figure, as Zhu Xi's 'like a command' was in Round 3. The book should not argue from the figure to a Father, and neither can the Confucian argue from the figure to a trustor. A trust needs someone who entrusted it. So v4's claim is accurate: 'That is why the line has no exceptions.' The Confucian trust explains why she should not throw her life away. It does not explain why no one, parent or state or she herself, may ever cross the line."
*Opponent rejoinder.* "Mencius makes the line absolute without any trust: not one innocent killed, even to gain the empire (2A2)."
*Defense.* "Yes, and in Round 3 we saw where his absolute sits: in the doer's rightness, not in her. v4 does not deny that the best accounts without God hold much of the line. It says they stop at 'this is what justice is.' Mencius stops at 'the sage would not.'"
*Second press.* "'An equal call explains an equal worth.' Mencius says anyone can become a Yao or a Shun (6B2). That is an equal call, and it needs no caller. Your sentence now claims a difference it does not state."
*Defense.* "Round 3 settled the substance: the call to sagehood waits on effort, and the child who will never speak cannot answer it, while the call to communion is given by grace. v4 states that only in the Dhamma row (CCC 1250, 1261). W1(2) should state it where the claim is made."
*Result: Held, with one Minor repair* (R8: the call that does not wait on what she can do).

**Exchange 6: Mou Zongsan's heart *knows*. "No one at the ground of things knows me as myself" denies his central doctrine.**
*Opponent.* "The row now says, of Mou, 'no one at the ground of things knows me as myself.' Mou's life work (*Intellectual Intuition and Chinese Philosophy*; *Phenomenon and Thing-in-Itself*) argues against Kant that the infinite heart has *intellectual intuition*: it knows things as they are in themselves, and in knowing them it realizes them in their worth. Kant gave that power to God alone. Mou gives it to the moral heart. And that heart loves: *ren* is resonance with every other heart. So the ground of things, for Mou, both knows and loves. Your closing row then says that against the sacred order God is 'the one that knows and loves.' Against Mou that is no contrast at all."
*Defense.* "On the wording: conceded. 'No one ... knows me' is true only if 'no one' means no person over against me, and the sentence does not say so. But the real divide is now plain, and Mou himself names it: he calls the Christian God the infinite set over against us, transcendence placed outside, and he rejects that. Then the heart that knows me, on his account, is my own heart at its depth, the same in you and in me. Its knowing of me is my self-knowing made perfect. That is union, not communion. And the other two clauses hold untouched. My heart can correct what I will do; it cannot release what I did to her. And when she dies, nothing in his account keeps *her*. So the row should credit the intellectual intuition and put the divide where it is: *besides me*. The God row should say the same."
*Opponent rejoinder.* "To us, your 'Someone besides me' is the infinite made into an object."
*Defense.* "And to us, your infinite heart is a self that has no one to be loved by. That is the standoff, and both sides can state it fairly."
*Result: Held with repair (Minor).* Repairs R4 and R6. The union vs. communion standoff should stay named.

**Exchange 7: "a principle that does not steer can promise no one anything." Confucius promised something.**
*Opponent.* "Bo Yi, the very man Sima Qian grieved over, sought *ren* and got *ren*. What had he to regret (7.15)? Is *ren* far away? I want it, and here it is (7.30). Our Heaven promises the one thing that matters to whoever seeks it, and it keeps that promise every time. 'Can promise no one anything' is false."
*Defense.* "True, and worth crediting. It is the Stoic answer as well: virtue is its own reward, and fortune is *ming*. But look who the promise reaches. It reaches the one who seeks. The child who died before she could seek anything gets nothing from it, and neither does the infant whom no instinct favored. The sentence should say what the principle can promise and to whom. That is a sharper line than the one v4 has."
*Result: Held with repair (Minor).* Repair R5.

**Exchange 8: "A yes said to everything is said to no one." Your own Scripture says God says yes to everything.**
*Opponent.* "Our Heaven's heart is to give life to all. Its yes goes to every thing, and your evil section says such a yes is 'said to no one.' But your own Wisdom of Solomon says that God loves all things that exist and loathes nothing He has made (Wis 11:24). By your sentence, God's love is said to no one too."
*Defense.* "The line is aimed at a god beyond good and evil 'who makes and breaks out of sheer fullness': a yes to whatever *happens*, the breaking along with the making. God's yes is to every thing He made, and it is said to each one, knowing each (ST I q.14 a.11). It does not include the breaking: He wills the order, never the loss for its own sake (ST I q.19 a.9). Read in its place, the line is right. Lifted out of it, it reads against Wis 11:24, and against the Neo-Confucian too, whose Heaven also does not will the breaking. A few words fix it."
*Result: Held with repair (Minor).* Repair R7.

**Exchange 9: smaller presses.**
(a) *The sacred-order row's citations.* "'Confucius, in danger, in grief, and when no one understood him, spoke of a Heaven that knows him (7.23, 9.5, 11.9, 14.35).' I gave you those citations, but only 14.35 says Heaven *knows* him. In 7.23 Heaven gave birth to the virtue in him. In 9.5 Heaven has not let this culture perish. In 11.9 Heaven has bereft him." *Defense:* "Say what each passage says." *Minor (R3).*
(b) *Mozi's target.* "Gongmengzi, whom Mozi answered (*Mozi* 48), did not doubt the spirits. He said there are none, and still taught that the gentleman must learn the sacrifices. 'Doubting' softens what Mozi was attacking." *Minor (in R1).*
(c) *"Walked it honestly."* "That implies that those who believe the dead hear walk their road dishonestly." *Minor (in R1).*
(d) *The moon in ten thousand rivers.* "Zhu Xi took the image from the Chan *Song of Enlightenment* (Yongjia Xuanjue) and said so (*Classified Conversations* book 94). v4 does not call it his own, so the text is clean. If the chapter ever says 'Zhu Xi's own image,' it should add that he borrowed it." *Note only.*
(e) *The Great Unity.* "The *Book of Rites* ('Liyun') sets the Great Unity in the past, in the age of the Great Way. Confucius says only that he aspires to it, and Kang Youwei later made it a future hope. 'Hopes for a world' is fair as aspiration." *No fault.*
(f) *A6's consent clause* (which other schools this round press as a contested premise). "Most Confucians grant it on the ground of the *Classic of Filial Piety* (Exchange 5). Fewer grant it at once once 15.9 is in view. For this school it is not a defect." *No fault from this school.*
(g) *Hiddenness (optional).* "The Neo-Confucians practiced quiet-sitting and the investigation of things for centuries and found a principle, not a Person. They belong beside the Buddhist contemplatives." *Optional (R9).*

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**As written: NONE.**

No Fatal or Serious objection from this school stands against v4 as written. The one Serious objection from Round 3 (W1(1)'s either/or) is closed: three roads are named, the ancestral road is honored, and the clue rests on what that road cannot do. I tested the new text for faults of the same kind. The nearest candidate is "the road of the great ancestral traditions" (Exchange 2). It is not Serious. The error concedes more to the rival than he is owed, the inference ("on that road ... no one receives") does not depend on who walks the road, and the clue's weight is self-limited. It is still a misstatement of this school, so it should be fixed.

**Minor (accuracy and wording; none changes the argument):**
1. W1(1): the second road is called "the road of the great ancestral traditions," but most ancestral practice takes the dead to receive. Confucius is placed on that road; he was reticent, and Xunzi is its teacher. With Xunzi cut, Mozi's jab stands unanswered. Gongmengzi *denied* the spirits; he did not doubt them. "Walked it honestly" implies that believers are dishonest. (R1)
2. W1(1): "no one left to remember them at all" overlooks the Ming altars for the unattended dead. Once v4 cites the Mass, the remembrance point is matched; only the reception point is left. (R2)
3. The sacred-order row: "spoke of a Heaven that knows him" glosses four passages, only one of which says "knows." (R3)
4. The sacred-order row: "no one at the ground of things knows me as myself" denies Mou's intellectual intuition, unless it says "besides me." (R4)
5. The sacred-order row: "a principle that does not steer can promise no one anything" overlooks 7.15 and 7.30. (R5)
6. The God row: "the one that knows and loves" does not separate the book from Mou. (R6)
7. Evil, *First*: "A yes said to everything is said to no one," lifted out of its place, reads against Wis 11:24. (R7)
8. W1(2): "an equal call explains an equal worth" needs, in its own place, the clause that this call does not wait on ability (Mencius 6B2). (R8)

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**
- *Debts to the dead.* Repayment by the living, completing the giver (Xunzi), or a *qi*-response through the line (Zhu Xi), against a debt that waits for its creditor. Theism is ahead only where guilt asks for *her* pardon. The weight is as much as the reader finds that to be the guilt he knows.
- *Equality without capacity.* The moon in the rivers matches the theist ground on equality. Theism is ahead on "for her own sake" and "for good."
- *Life held in trust.* Trust from parents (the *Classic of Filial Piety*) against trust from the Lord of life. The first is real, but it has exceptions and gives the parents jurisdiction. Mencius's absolute (2A2) rests in the doer's rightness, against an absolute that rests in her.
- *Union vs. communion.* Mou's infinite heart that knows and loves, which is my own heart and yours, against Someone besides us who knows and loves us. Each side calls the other's summit a loss: the infinite made into an object, or a self with no one to be loved by.
- *Autonomy.* Mou's charge that received worth is lesser worth, answered by Chapter 4 and VS 41. Neither side can force the other.
- *The problem of evil.* A principle that intends no one carries a lighter problem and promises only the seeker. A God who knows each one carries the heavier problem and promises this child.

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

1. **Mencius 6A17: what a lord makes noble, a lord can make base.** Everyone has something truly noble in himself, Mencius says, and what another person's favor confers, that person's disfavor can take away. This is a pagan witness to A4's "prior to anyone's recognition": worth that recognition gives, recognition can withdraw. (Ch. 12, A4; Ch. 2.)
2. **Analects 3.13 with Zhu Xi's gloss.** "Whoever offends against Heaven has no one left to pray to." Zhu Xi read Heaven here as principle: to go against principle is to offend Heaven, and no flattery of lesser spirits can pray it away. In the rival's own commentary, guilt against an order is guilt no one can lift. That is W1's frame. Use it as a datum, not a genealogy. (Ch. 12, W1; Ch. 8 on mercy.)
3. **Zhu Xi on ancestral *qi*.** The ancestor's *qi*, continuous in the descendant, is gathered and moved by sincere sacrifice. That is the strongest non-theist account of the dead "receiving," and it shows exactly what the book claims: something can respond; only someone can forgive. (Ch. 12, W1(1).)
4. **The *Classic of Filial Piety*: body, hair and skin are received from our parents.** A non-Christian witness that one's life is held in trust and "not hers to throw away." Its limit is the book's point: a trust held from parents gives jurisdiction to those who may be the danger, and it yields to rightness (15.9). Only a trust held from the Lord of life has no exceptions. (Ch. 12, W1(2); Ch. 7 on justice.)
5. **Ode 260, quoted in Mencius 6A6.** "Heaven gave birth to the people; to every thing its norm; the people hold to it and love virtue." The classical Chinese form of W2: the law in things and the love of it in us, from one source. Confucius is reported as saying the poet knew the Way. (Ch. 12, W2.)
6. **The Ming altars for the forgotten dead.** A whole state fed the kinless dead three times a year. That honors the Church's prayer at every Mass for all the dead, and it shows that the real divide is reception, not remembrance. (Ch. 12, W1(1); Ch. 10 on communion.)
7. **Mou's intellectual intuition.** The strongest modern Confucian gives the human heart the knowing that Kant reserved to God. The book's true contrast is not "knows" but "besides me": union against communion. (Ch. 12, C; Ch. 10.)
8. **Verify before anything enters the book:** *Mencius* 2A2, 6A6, 6A16–17, 6B2, 7A35; *Analects* 3.12 (with Zhu Xi's citation of Fan Zuyu), 3.13 (with Zhu Xi's gloss), 6.22, 7.15, 7.21, 7.23, 7.30, 8.21, 9.5, 11.9, 14.35, 15.9; *Xunzi* ch. 19; *Mozi* 48; *Classic of Filial Piety* ch. 1; Zhang Zai, *Western Inscription*; *Zhuzi yulei* books 1, 3, 94; Zhu Xi, *Family Rituals* (four generations); the Hongwu-era *li* altar ordinances; the Yongzheng edict on widow suicide (1728); Qing law on parents who killed children; Mou Zongsan, *Intellectual Intuition and Chinese Philosophy* (1971), *Phenomenon and Thing-in-Itself* (1975), *The Characteristics of Chinese Philosophy* (1963); Wis 11:24; ST I q.14 a.11, q.19 a.9. Paraphrase only.

**Lines worth keeping (short, plain, warm):**
- "The rite completes the one who gives it, and it is shaped for one who receives."
- "Something can respond. Only someone can forgive."
- "A trust needs someone who entrusted it."
- "What one man's favor makes noble, his disfavor can make base. Her worth was never his to give."
- "Offend against Heaven, the Master said, and there is no one left to pray to."
- "Goodness is near to whoever wants it. To the child who died before she could want anything, a principle can promise nothing."
- "Not 'knows and loves,' but Someone besides me who knows and loves me."

## 8. Score

**v4 as written: 9/10. With the repairs above: 9.5/10.** v4 took every repair this school asked for in Round 3, and its new ground (life held in trust from One who wills each for her own sake) holds against the strongest Confucian parallel, the *Classic of Filial Piety*. What remains are accuracy faults, several of them in this school's own Round 3 wording: the ancestral traditions placed on the wrong road, Confucius in Xunzi's place, Mou's knowing heart, the promise *ren* makes to the seeker. None breaks a step, and the true standoffs (repayment against reception, union against communion) are now fairly drawn.
