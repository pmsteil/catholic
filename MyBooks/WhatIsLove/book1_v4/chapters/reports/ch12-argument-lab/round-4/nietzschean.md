# Argument Lab, Round 4: Nietzschean opponent vs. v4

## 1. School and voice

Nietzsche of the mature works (*Beyond Good and Evil*, *Genealogy*, *Gay Science*, *Twilight*, *Antichrist*, *Ecce Homo*), argued as Leiter, Reginster and Bernard Williams argue him. In Round 4 he checks what v4 did with his Round 3 repairs. Then he attacks the new text with the moves Nietzsche scholars would make first: the undercutter clause v4 now admits, the priest's shift of the debt to God (*GM* II §§21–22), Christian truthfulness overcoming itself (*GM* III §27), the ascetic ideal, the eternal return, and "Dionysus versus the Crucified." All Nietzsche material is paraphrased. The fairness rule is applied both ways.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges (short)

1. **Justice:** exchange between parties of roughly equal power (*GM* II §8). At its height it overcomes itself in mercy, which only the powerful can afford (*GM* II §10). *Coherent, borrows nothing. It breaks A4: no one who could never be a party to exchange is owed anything.*
2. **Dilemma:** one ventilator, same prognosis, a young composer of genius and a man with profound disability. Give it to the composer, and feel no remorse (*BGE* §126). *Decides the case without borrowing. It breaks A4 openly, and nearly every reader rejects it when it is said about a named patient.*
3. **Equal dignity:** denied. The weak and ill-constituted should perish (*Antichrist* §2). *A consistent denial. v4's W1(2) now meets it head-on ("the genius and the newborn are for the same thing").*
4. **Flourishing:** become who you are; self-overcoming; *amor fati*. *Breaks as an answer to what is good for* humans*: it is good for a few, and the many serve as their base (*BGE* §258).*

### 3b. Exchanges

**Exchange 1. A2's new case: the sovereign individual.**
*Nietzsche:* Your betrayed secret proves less than you think. The noble man keeps his word because he alone has earned the right to make promises (*GM* II §2). When he breaks one, he has made himself low. That is self-contempt, and it needs no her. Your contrast with the letter in the street proves only that *an agent* acted. It does not prove that the act was aimed at the dead woman. Both readings explain the contrast.
*Patrick's side:* Granted: the contrast shows agency, not on its own direction. v4 does not claim more. Right after the case it gives both readings, "Shame, left to itself, says I am less; guilt says I wronged her." Your reading is the first one, and the A heading already lists you among those who deny A2. What the case adds is the test. If self-contempt were all of it, the right repair would be to restore my own rank, and nothing would be owed to her. Ask the man who told the secret what he would want to say, and to whom. Almost no one answers "to myself."
*Rejoinder:* That is your reader's trained conscience.
*Reply:* Then that is the undercutter question, and it belongs to Exchange 3.
**Held (standoff at A2).**

**Exchange 2. A4 and A6 still close only the utilitarian door.**
*Nietzsche:* I raised this last round, and v4 half-took it. A4 now says "never, if innocent, to be deliberately killed," with no qualifier. Good: that closes my door for killing. But the mere-means clause still reads "never to be used as a mere means *for better totals*." My trade is not for totals. Mankind as a mass given up so that one stronger kind of man might prosper would be progress (*GM* II §12). The many are reduced to instruments for the few (*BGE* §258). A reader can sign A4's operative clause and *BGE* §258 together. And your heading lists my denials as A1–A4 and A7, but I plainly deny A6's second half as well.
*Patrick's side:* Both points are fair, and both are wording. The substance is already in v4. W1(2) says the person is willed "not for the species, not for the total, not for us." A6 says "persons may not be spent." The heading names you as denying A4, so the case does not pretend you can sign it. Two small edits close the gap (section 6).
**Held with repair (Minor).**

**Exchange 3. The undercutter clause turned on guilt.**
*Nietzsche:* Your new heading admits undercutters: trust a seeming until something shows "that we would believe it whether or not it were true." Thank you. Here is one. Bad conscience is the instinct of cruelty turned inward once man was shut inside society (*GM* II §16). The priest then cultivated it for two thousand years. Your W1(1) clue, that guilt "asks to be forgiven by her," is what that training produces whether or not she exists. By your own rule, the clue is undercut. And your heading still counts hands: "Most people find, on reflection, that they cannot give these up." Then: "This is not a vote."
*Patrick's side:* On the count, agreed again. The sentence adds nothing to the principle and still sits badly beside "This is not a vote." It should go. On the undercutter, take your genealogy at its word and see what it predicts. Cruelty turned inward predicts self-torment. The priest's work, on your own account, predicts guilt before *God*, a debt that swells until it can never be paid (*GM* II §§20–22). Neither predicts the datum W1(1) uses: guilt aimed at the *human* victim, asking *her* pardon. That shape is older than the priest, and it is found where your genealogy says bad conscience had not yet taken hold. The Greeks, whose gods took the guilt on themselves, still had Agamemnon pay Achilles in full. So the genealogy predicts the wrong shape here, just as ressentiment predicts an inverted order and not a floor that holds even for one's enemies. Fairness rule: v4 already concedes the matching point about speaking to the dead ("so the speaking alone settles nothing"). It concedes what the evidence warrants and no more.
*Rejoinder:* A trained conscience can aim anywhere the trainer likes.
*Reply:* Then the genealogy no longer predicts anything, and an undercutter that predicts nothing undercuts nothing. W1(1) weighs the clue only "as much as the reader finds the third road truer than the second." That is the honest weight.
**Held at parity, with a new strength.** The count sentence remains Minor.

**Exchange 4. "Before all else" and "first": the priest's shift of the debt.**
*Nietzsche:* This is my best point against v4, and v4 made it for me. In one paragraph of W1 you write that every wrong against the child is "also, and before all else, an offense against Him (CCC 1440)." Four sentences later you write that guilt "is owed to her first (A2)." Which is first? This is the exact move I described. The priest takes the debt from the human creditor and gives it to God, until the debt to God swallows everything and the victim fades away (*GM* II §§21–22). If God comes first, you have done what the priest does. If she comes first, you have contradicted the Catechism you quote. (The censor flags the same clash on doctrinal grounds.)
*Patrick's side:* The wording clashes, and it must be fixed. The substance does not, and the substance is the strongest answer this book gives to *GM* II §22. Look at what v4's God does with the debt. He does not take it from her. His forgiveness is whole, yet "God does not take her place in giving the pardon that is hers to give." Amends are still owed to her (CCC 1459). He keeps "her claim, and her." Your priest's God absorbs the victim's claim. The Church's God refuses to. "Before all else" is the order of gravity: what makes the wrong an offense against Him is what it does to her, whom He loves. "First" was meant as the order of seeing, and v4 says so itself: "We see it first in what it does to the child." The two do not compete. The text must say that in one place, not in two sentences that seem to collide.
*Rejoinder:* Then say it.
*Reply:* Section 6 does.
**Held with repair (Minor for this school; the censor rates it an Error).** The repair turns an apparent concession into an answer to *GM* II §22.

**Exchange 5. The truthfulness dilemma: Christian truthfulness overcoming itself.**
*Nietzsche:* My Round 3 wording, now your text, gives horn 2 as "his unmasking can rank us but cannot correct us … it cannot show anyone that he ought to give it up." That misses my central method. I do not correct Christians with my taste. I correct them with *their own* truthfulness. Christian truthfulness, schooled for two thousand years, at last draws its conclusion against Christianity itself (*GM* III §27; *GS* §357). The ought is yours, and I turn it on you. Any Nietzsche reader will see that the row overlooks *GM* III §27.
*Patrick's side:* Fair, and I concede my own wording was wrong. The dilemma survives, but horn 2's cost has to be stated properly. An immanent critique can correct us. The authority it uses, though, is ours, not his. If the ought binds us only because we believe it binds, it binds no one. If it binds us because it binds every reasoning mind, that is horn 1. So the unmasking can correct us only on borrowed authority. And it can do so only by doing what the A heading requires: showing A false, or showing that we would believe it even if it were false. Ancestry alone does neither (the row's own next sentence). Whether honest inquiry ends in your conclusion or ours is not decided by the dilemma. It is the whole case. That is parity, and it is the right place for the question.
*Rejoinder:* Borrowed authority is still authority.
*Reply:* Yes, and then the case is won or lost on the evidence, which is what A7 asks. The row should say so plainly instead of claiming that he cannot correct us.
**Held with repair (Minor; misstates his method).**

**Exchange 6. "Cannot finally lead away from life": the ascetic ideal.**
*Nietzsche:* "Finally" gives you away. The will to truth "cannot finally lead away from life," because truth and life have one Source. But the life you mean is beyond this one. You move life to a true world and call that the answer. That is what I mean by nihilism (*Twilight*, "How the 'True World' Finally Became a Fable"). And your answer to my hardest question is your martyrs, who chose truth over life. That is the ascetic ideal itself (*GM* III). You *do* value truth above life. The row says "honesty need not be fatal." For your martyrs it was.
*Patrick's side:* Two answers. First, the Catholic life is not a world behind this one. It is this life, raised: "the one we love will go on being herself, body and all" (W3), "this child, herself, body and all" (evil section, Third). The Church fought the despisers of the body, the Manichees among them. The resurrection of the flesh is the opposite of escaping the body. Second, the martyr does not set truth against life. He refuses to buy survival with a lie. You also deny that mere survival is the highest thing: life wants to spend its strength, and self-preservation is only one result of that (*BGE* §13). So both sides hold that some things are worth more than survival. What we deny is that truth can be the *enemy* of life. "Need not be fatal" is the wrong phrase, because the martyr shows that honesty can cost a life. The claim the row needs is that honesty can never finally be life's enemy.
*Rejoinder:* "Finally" is still a promise you cannot show me.
*Reply:* It is the hypothesis being weighed. The row says only that on your account nothing promises it, which is why you turned to art to bear the truth (*GS* §107). Each account carries its own hope.
**Held (standoff at parity), with a one-phrase repair** in the C row.

**Exchange 7. Dionysus: a yes to each one, forever.**
*Nietzsche:* "A yes said to everything is said to no one." That is false of *amor fati*. My yes is the most particular yes there is. The eternal return wills *this* life, this moment, this child, again and again forever, every detail. Your God wants to change her and heal her. I want her exactly as she is. And Ecce Homo ends with the choice your evil section stages without naming it: Dionysus against the Crucified (*EH*, "Why I Am a Destiny" §9).
*Patrick's side:* The return is particular, granted. But look at what it returns: the same child with the same cancer, forever. A yes that must also say yes to what was done to her cannot take her side. Love says yes to *her* and no to what destroys her. That is the difference between "it is good that you exist" and "it is good that everything happened." The evil section's Third already states the Christian alternative: "this child, herself, body and all, will be raised and made whole." Your return gives her back the same. The resurrection gives her back whole. On "Dionysus against the Crucified": v4's First names the Dionysian rival honestly, and admits the privation argument does not rule him out ("answered only by the whole case"). Its Third gives the Crucified who "will not leave it in the ground." The text could name the contest you named. It does not need to in order to be sound.
*Rejoinder:* On the wolf and the fawn, Dionysus still fits better.
*Reply:* v4 says so itself. Nature "still weighs," and "for the fawn that hope is thinner." That weight is owned, not a defect.
**Held, with an optional added sentence.**

**Exchange 8. W3: "desire is not to be killed but healed."**
*Nietzsche:* Christianity gave Eros poison to drink. He did not die, but he degenerated into vice (*BGE* §168).
*Patrick's side:* v4's W3 now answers exactly this: "desire itself is not to be killed but healed" (*Deus Caritas Est* 5). Benedict XVI raises your charge by name in that very encyclical (DCE 3) before he answers it. The book could say so.
**Held.**

**Exchange 9. The conclusion: "wrong" is not yet "wronged."**
*Nietzsche:* Your last question asks whether "it was wrong to leave her there because she was weak," then infers: "If it was wrong, her worth never followed her strength, and it is no less than yours." A rank-theorist can answer *yes* and still deny the inference. The exceptional man owes the mediocre more gentleness than he gives himself, and that is his *duty* (*Antichrist* §57). The father on the hillside acted basely. He shamed his own rank. It does not follow that she was worth what the father was worth. (My own harshest line goes the other way, *Antichrist* §2. But the school at full strength takes the gentler line, and against that line your inference fails.) "Wrong" covers two things: what is low in the doer, and what is owed to her. Only the second gives you your conclusion.
*Patrick's side:* Granted. This is the A2 distinction, shame against guilt, and the conclusion should use it. Ask whether *she* was wronged, not only whether the deed was wrong. If she was wronged when they left her there because she was weak, she had a claim that her weakness did not lessen, and a claim that weakness cannot lessen, strength cannot raise. Then it is no less than yours. That reaches the rank-theorist too. His gentleness is a duty to his own nobility. It is not her claim, and on his account it can be withdrawn. Asked whether she was *wronged*, he must either say yes and grant the claim, or say no and say it about her by name. That is the price C says he must pay.
**Held with repair (Minor).** The inference as written is invalid against a floor-with-rank view. The question itself stands, and v4's own A2 supplies the fix.

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**As written: NONE.**

No Fatal or Serious objection from this school stands against v4 as written. Every Round 3 point was taken or covered. Each new Nietzschean move (Exchanges 1–9) is answered at parity or better by text already in v4. In two cases (Ex. 3 and Ex. 4) v4's own text gives the strongest available reply to the *Genealogy*: the bad-conscience genealogy predicts the wrong shape of guilt, and the Church's God refuses to take the victim's place. What remains is wording, though three of the Minor points are close to the line.

**Minor (as written), in order of importance:**
1. **Conclusion** (Ex. 9). "Wrong to leave her there" lets a rank-theorist agree without granting equal worth, so the stated inference ("If it was wrong … no less than yours") is invalid against him. Use "wronged" (A2's guilt, not shame) and "a claim that weakness cannot lessen, strength cannot raise."
2. **W1 frame** (Ex. 4). "Before all else … against Him" and "owed to her first" collide in one paragraph. To this school it reads as the priest's shift of the debt (*GM* II §22). The substance answers that shift, but the wording does not show it. (The censor rates this an Error on doctrinal grounds.) Also, "every account agrees" should be "every account that keeps those words agrees."
3. **C, Power and rank** (Ex. 5, 6). Horn 2 misses *GM* III §27, his immanent correction of Christianity by its own truthfulness. "Honesty need not be fatal" is answered by the martyrs. "The Church agrees with that history" is ambiguous. I wrote this row in Round 3, and this corrects my own wording.
4. **A heading.** The count sentence ("Most people find…") should go. Add A6's second half to Nietzsche's denials.
5. **A4.** The mere-means clause still says "for better totals." Add "not for the many and not for the few."

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**
- **Rank vs. equal worth.** He is surer of rank. The case cannot compel him and says so. It shows him the price: saying "she was not wronged" about her, by name.
- **The sovereign individual vs. directed guilt** (A2). Self-contempt and "I wronged her" both explain the letter contrast. The test is whom the guilty man wants to address.
- **Undercutters on guilt.** The bad-conscience genealogy against W1(1)'s clue. Our edge: it predicts guilt before God and self-torment, not pardon sought from the human victim.
- **Immanent critique.** Whether honest inquiry, held to A7, ends in "God is dead" or in the Lawgiver. The dilemma does not decide this. The whole case does.
- **"Finally."** Whether truth and life cannot finally conflict (one Source) or might (art to bear the truth). Each side carries a hope the other calls unshown.
- **Dionysus vs. the Crucified** on the evidence of nature alone. Dionysus fits the wolf and the fawn at least as well. v4 owns this as weight.
- **Free death** (W1(2), "not hers to throw away") against Zarathustra's free death. This is the book's claim, argued as such.

## 7. New insights for the book

1. **The genealogy of guilt predicts the wrong shape.** Bad conscience as cruelty turned inward predicts self-torment. The priest's debt to God predicts a debt that swallows the victim. Neither predicts what W1(1) relies on: guilt that seeks the *human* victim's pardon. This is the same kind of reply as "ressentiment predicts inversion," and together they are the cleanest answer to the *Genealogy*.
2. **The Church's God refuses the priest's shift.** *GM* II §§21–22 says Christianity moved the debt from the creditor to God. v4's W1(1) says God "does not take her place," amends are still owed to her (CCC 1459), and He keeps "her claim, and her." Once the "first / before all else" wording is fixed, this is a direct answer to Nietzsche's deepest charge about guilt.
3. **Borrowed authority.** Nietzsche's real method is immanent: Christian truthfulness overcomes Christianity (*GM* III §27). The honest cost of his second horn is not that he cannot correct us. It is that he can correct us only with an ought he says binds no one.
4. **Wrong vs. wronged at the climax.** The A2 distinction between shame and guilt is also the hinge of the conclusion. A rank-theorist will call the hillside base. Only "she was wronged" carries her claim.
5. **The return and the resurrection.** The eternal return is as particular as the resurrection, and it gives her back the same. The resurrection gives her back whole. This is the exact point where "Dionysus versus the Crucified" (*Ecce Homo*'s last line) is decided, and the evil section already stages that contest.
6. **Benedict answered Nietzsche by name.** *Deus Caritas Est* 3 raises the charge that Christianity poisoned eros (*BGE* §168), and DCE 5 answers it: desire healed, not killed. v4's W3 already uses DCE 5. A footnote naming DCE 3 would show the book has met him on his strongest ground about love.
7. **The martyr and the ascetic ideal.** Both sides hold that something is worth more than survival (*BGE* §13). The difference is whether truth can ever be life's enemy. "May cost a life, never finally its enemy" states the Catholic claim without overclaiming.
