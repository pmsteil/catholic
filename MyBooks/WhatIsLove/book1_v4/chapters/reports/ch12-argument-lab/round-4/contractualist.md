# Round 4: Contractualism and the Second-Personal Standpoint vs. v4

## 1. School and voice

The voice is a composite of T. M. Scanlon (a wrong is what could not be justified to the one it is done to; reasons need no metaphysical home), Stephen Darwall (obligation is the standing of free and equal persons to make claims on one another; second-personal reasons cannot be built out of impersonal ones), John Rawls, and R. Jay Wallace (guilt and blame as the reactive attitudes by which we hold one another to account). This round also draws on Feinberg and Pitcher on wrongs to the dead, H. L. A. Hart's will theory of rights, and Locke's "workmanship" argument as Darwall reads it. All positions are paraphrased; there are no direct quotations.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

1. **Justice.** What anyone is owed is what follows from principles no one affected could reasonably reject. *Holds; it presupposes the equal standing it then applies.*
2. **A hard case.** The TRC amnesty (*AZAPO*, 1996; verify): a state may waive punishment, but it cannot forgive what was done to Biko. *Holds, and it supports A3. It has no account of mercy as a gift that heals.*
3. **Equal dignity.** Being born of human parents is reason to count her among those to whom we answer, through a trustee. *The scope holds. The ground is a reason that "simply holds."*
4. **Ultimate good.** Mutual recognition in a social union of social unions. *Coherent, and it ends at death.*

**Exchange 1. The new C row overstates what my school "cannot explain."**
*Contractualist:* The row now states my view fairly. I accept almost all of it. One clause is still wrong, and it is the clause I wrote last round. It says contractualism "cannot explain ... why answering to each one is what right and wrong are." Scanlon does explain this. Wrongness has the force it has because to act on principles others could not reasonably reject is to stand against them, and standing with each person in mutual recognition is a value we have reason to care about. You may think that value is a stopping point. It is. But that is not the same as "cannot explain." The first clause is different. Why being born of human parents brings her within the circle is something Scanlon himself calls a substantive moral question, and he answers it only with a reason that simply holds. "Cannot explain" fits there.
*Defense:* Granted. The honest form is that the school explains the second by a value it must take as given. That still puts the stopping point where the row says it is (the realist's question, W2), and that is all the comparison needs.
*Verdict: Held with repair (Minor).* Wording in section 6.

**Exchange 2. "Owed to her first," "before all else an offense against Him," and "the child is His": Pufendorf's problem comes back.**
*Contractualist:* The censor, the Aristotelian and the debunker have already noted the clash of words in the frame: "before all else an offense against Him (CCC 1440)" and, four sentences later, "it is owed to her first (A2)." My school's point is not about the words but about direction. The reason the frame gives for the offense against God is possession: "because the child is His." The closing repeats it: "to Him whose she is." That is Locke's workmanship argument: we are God's property, so murder wrongs the Owner. Darwall's objection to every account of this kind is that it turns the victim into a second creditor in her own case. Last round you answered that her claim is "truly her own," given and not borrowed. A ground that rests on ownership takes that answer back.
*Defense:* There are two orders here, and the text already holds both of them. In the order of knowing, the wrong is first seen in her ("We see it first in what it does to the child"). In the order of being, God is first. But the reason God is offended is not that His property was damaged. W1(1) gives the true reason: the offense is against "the One whose love made her precious (CCC 1440, 1850)." God is offended *because* she is wronged. He is offended by what goes against the good He wills for her. That is not a rival claim standing beside hers, and it does not compete with hers. The frame should use the reason W1(1) uses, love and not possession, and it should drop "first" from the sentence about her.
*Contractualist:* With love in place of possession, I withdraw the charge that her claim is second-hand. A claim given to her in love, which God guards as hers, is hers.
*Verdict: Held with repair (Minor).* This school's repair agrees with the censor's E1 and adds a change from possession to love in the frame and the closing (section 6).

**Exchange 3. The three roads: two places where road two is drawn too thin.**
*Contractualist:* The three roads are honest, and road two is mine. Two things are still off.
(a) "On that road, what the living pay, no one receives." But your own A2 rests on the Feinberg–Pitcher thesis: after her death, telling the secret wrongs *her*, the woman who lived, though nothing reaches her experience. By the same reasoning, keeping the secret, honoring her wishes and caring for those she loved benefit *her*. In that thin sense she does receive. You cannot use the thesis in A2 and deny it in W1(1).
(b) "Guilt does not only grieve a fact." On road two guilt does not grieve a fact. It faces a person who was, and it is lucid about what it did to her (Gaita's remorse). Then too, the text calls road two the road of "the great ancestral traditions." It is also the road of most philosophers who hold no religion, Feinberg, Pitcher, Williams and Gaita among them. Leaving them out makes it look as though only ancestor rites keep the debt.
*Defense:* (a) Granted in the thin sense. The point that carries weight was never receipt as benefit. It is that she cannot *take up* what is paid or *answer* it, and above all that no one releases. The text says that in the next clause ("what can never be repaid, no one ever releases"), and it ends on release: "It asks to be forgiven by her." So narrow the first clause to what road two really lacks. (b) Granted on both counts. Name the philosophers, and let guilt "mourn her," not "grieve a fact."
*Contractualist:* On the Mozi line: it fits because the text has already called road two "noble." It is a fair blow from a rival school against a rival practice, not a sneer at mourning.
*Verdict: Held with repair (Minor).* The clue's weight is unchanged, and the stated standoff is now fair to this school.

**Exchange 4. The Catholic answer: does Christ's fire make her pardon optional?**
*Contractualist:* Last round you kept her veto: "She will be able to forgive; she will not be made to," and if she does not, "his debt to her stands." v4 adds *Spe Salvi* 47: Christ's look burns away what is false "so that the guilty too can be healed." If the guilty can be healed by Christ, is her pardon still needed? If it is not, then "the pardon that is hers to give" decides nothing at the end. If it is, the wrongdoer's healing waits on her.
*Defense:* The text refuses both of those readings, and it says so: "How the last reconciliation ... is completed, the Church does not spell out." What it holds is consistent. God forgives the sin (CCC 1441), through the Church (CCC 1442). He "does not take her place in giving the pardon that is hers." The purifying fire heals the wrongdoer's own falseness, and the wrongdoer "does not sit at the eternal banquet beside his victim as though nothing had happened" (*Spe Salvi* 44). The healing of his heart and her pardon of the bond are different gifts. That is also Kant's rule of pardon (6:337), which the Kant row already quotes against him.
*Contractualist:* So you hold two things and decline to say how they meet.
*Defense:* Yes, and the text names that refusal openly instead of hiding it. Your own view holds that the meeting can never happen.
*Verdict: Held, at parity.* An honest standoff, named in the text.

**Exchange 5. "Life held in trust": second-hand claims and the competent adult.**
*Contractualist:* The new ground for the absolute is this: "her life is not ours to spend, not even for many, and not hers to throw away: it is held in trust from the One who alone is Lord of life ... No one may take what is not his to take." Whose, then, is it to take? God's. So the line that protects her is a property line. On Hart's will theory a right is a claim its holder may waive. A claim she may not waive is not hers; it is a duty owed to the One who holds the title. And in the case that matters to my school, a competent adult who is suffering and asks for help to die, His title overrides her own considered will. A principle that let a third party's claim override her will about her own life is one she could reasonably reject.
*Defense:* Three replies. (i) Claims that cannot be waived are common ground. On the interest theory (MacCormick) a child's rights and inalienable rights are rights all the same. Your own school rejects a principle that would let anyone sell himself into slavery, and A3 relies on that, rightly. So "she cannot waive it" does not show "it is not hers." (ii) The text grounds the trust in her before it names the Lord of life: she is "willed for itself ... not for the species, not for the total, not for us" (GS 24). The One who holds her life in trust holds it *for her*. In the surgeon and magistrate cases His claim and hers point the same way, and the trust is what makes her claim unbreakable even by many. (iii) The flourishing row gives the deeper point: a person does not own her life, she is the one who lives it (CCC 2280). A life is not a possession beside her that someone else's title can trump. To be held in trust is to be kept as the one she is.
*Contractualist:* Then for the competent adult, what decides is not whose title wins but what her good is.
*Defense:* Yes. The book holds that ending her life is not her good, and it argues that claim; it does not assume it (A6's label, W1(2), the flourishing row). That is the euthanasia dispute itself: a real disagreement, not a fault in the argument. The text could make (ii) explicit in one clause, so that no reader takes the trust for a landlord's title.
*Verdict: Held, at parity, with a Minor repair.* This is the most important point in this report: the Locke reading is easy to fall into and easy to close off.

**Exchange 6. A4 again carries the ruling on assisted dying.**
*Contractualist:* In Round 2 this school got the assisted-dying clause out of A4, and v3 kept it out ("not lost when life grows hard"). To answer the censor on legitimate defense, v4 now says every human being is "never, if innocent, to be deliberately killed (CCC 2261)." That is right as doctrine. But A is "what we take to be true," which "most people find, on reflection, that they cannot give up." Rawls and Scanlon signed the 1997 Philosophers' Brief for a right to assisted suicide (verify the signatories), and so did others. A4's list of deniers names only Singer. So a premise offered as common ground now includes a conclusion this school's founders deny, and the C row on God then counts explaining that conclusion as reach.
*Defense:* Granted on the label, not on the doctrine. The censor is right that the clause is needed: without "if innocent," the line collides with CCC 2263–2265. A6 already shows the honest method: "The Church holds the line at every number ... that is this book's claim, argued in W1(2), not a shared one." A4 needs the same marker for "even at his own request."
*Verdict: Held with repair (Minor).* The Aristotelian found the same fault; my school adds that it undoes this school's own Round 2 fix. The repair keeps the censor's wording.
*Side note on A6 ("not even his own consent could make it right"):* my school mostly supports this verdict. A principle permitting lethal experiments with consent could reasonably be rejected by those who would be pressed to consent: the poor, prisoners, the desperate. So "almost everyone sees these at once" is closer to true than the realist and debunker allow. But my school reaches the verdict through others' reasonable rejection, not because her consent counts for nothing. Not an objection.

**Exchange 7. The C row on God: "what the best rivals leave out" is too broad against this school.**
*Contractualist:* The God row says the rivals leave out "a reason why the line that protects the innocent has no exceptions and is not even her own to cross." But W1(2) itself credits the reason my school gives: "each person must be answered one by one, and no total answers her." That is a reason why no number crosses the line. It is the one secular structure designed to keep numbers from ever adding up against one person. Whether its defenders hold it at catastrophe scale is debated among them. What we leave out is not *a* reason; it is a *ground* for that reason, and a reason why she cannot waive it in her own case. And as the debunker notes, "has no exceptions" is not shared (A6 says so), so it can count as reach only for a reader who already grants it.
*Defense:* Granted. The honest claim is narrower and still real: one ground that explains the anti-aggregation the rivals share, why it holds for those who can enter no relation of justification, and (for a reader who grants the absolute) why it has no exceptions.
*Verdict: Held with repair (Minor).*

**Exchange 8. The evil section: "a mere means against her own good" in place of "no one is the price."**
*Contractualist:* v3's "No one is the price of someone else's good, not even in God's hands" was this school's individualist restriction applied to God. v4 replaces it with "God never spends anyone as a mere means against her own good," with two qualifiers. Does God, then, use some people as means, as long as it is not against their good?
*Defense:* Yes, in the way your own ex ante test allows. A principle is justifiable to each person if, as things stand beforehand, it serves her interest. The good offered to each sufferer is her own (CCC 1261 and SCG III.112 cover the infant who cannot join her suffering to Christ's). The suffering that bears fruit for others is Christ's, given freely (John 10:18), and the suffering of those who join theirs to His. "What Christ gave was given, not taken." The censor required that change, because the old line read as a denial of the Cross.
*Contractualist:* Ex ante, I accept that. Whether "offered" is kept is the promise I cannot inspect.
*Verdict: Held, at parity.* The individualist restriction survives the doctrinal fix.

## 7. New insights for the book

- **Love, not ownership, is the Catholic reason God is offended.** The Locke reading ("we are His property, so murder damages the Owner") is the oldest secular objection to grounding rights in God, and it is easy to step into by saying "the child is His." The Church's own reason is better and closes the objection: sin goes against God's love for us (CCC 1850), so He is offended *because* she is wronged, and His claim is her claim guarded, not a rival claim. Aquinas says it plainly: we offend God only by acting against the good He wills for us (SCG III.122, as the realist also notes; verify).
- **A claim she cannot waive is still hers.** Children's rights and the ban on selling oneself into slavery show that being unable to give a claim away does not make it someone else's. This answers the will-theory form of Darwall's worry, and it is common ground with every rival that accepts A3.
- **"Held in trust for her."** The trust is not a landlord's lease. It is the way the One who willed her for her own sake keeps her life from being spent, even by many. In the surgeon and magistrate cases His claim and hers are one claim. The single case where they seem to differ (the suffering adult who asks to die) turns on what her good is, and the book argues that openly.
- **Road two belongs to the philosophers too.** The strongest version of "the debt outlives her" is not only ancestral. It is the view of Feinberg, Pitcher, Williams and Gaita. Naming them makes the clue fairer and therefore stronger.
- **The posthumous secret works for both sides.** A2's example shows the dead can be wronged. By the same logic they can be honored. What road two lacks is not that she is reached, but that she can answer and release. That is where the book's weight should rest, and v4 mostly puts it there.
- (To verify; paraphrase only) Jeremy Waldron (*God, Locke, and Equality*, 2002) argued that Locke's basic equality cannot easily be detached from its theological ground. A liberal legal philosopher reached A4's question from the other side.

**Lines worth keeping:**
- From v4: "We agree she is owed. We part at the grave, and over what gives her the claim at all."
- From v4: "Had the same stranger found it in a letter blown into the street ... no one would have wronged her."

- From v4: "No one may take what is not his to take, even to keep others from taking more."

- New: "A claim she cannot give away is still hers."
- New: "On that road the dead are owed, and never answer."
