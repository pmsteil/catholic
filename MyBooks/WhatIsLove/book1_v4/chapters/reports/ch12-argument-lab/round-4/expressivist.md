# Round 4: Humean Sentimentalism / Quasi-Realist Expressivism vs. v4

## 1. School and voice

The voices are David Hume (*Treatise* Book 3; *Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals*, EPM; *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion*; the essay "Of Suicide"), Adam Smith (*Theory of Moral Sentiments*, TMS), Simon Blackburn's quasi-realism, Allan Gibbard's norm-expressivism, and the sensibility theorists David Wiggins and John McDowell. Moral judgment voices refined attitudes and plans, and the quasi-realist then earns the realist's words at first order: "true," "wrong even if all approved," "my guilt is owed to her." This round checks whether v4 took the Round 3 repairs, and then looks for what the new text, much of it the censor's fixes, got wrong about this school.

## 2. The four standing challenges (short)

**1. Justice.** For Hume, justice is a set of conventions approved through sympathy with the public good. For Smith, it is fitting resentment of injury. The modern school widens it to everyone by commitment. *Coherent; it breaks at the edge, as Hume admits.* The weak are owed gentleness "by the laws of humanity" but not justice (EPM §3 pt 1). v4's C row now states this exactly.

**2. A hard case.** The secret promise to a dying friend (v4's own A2). Hume's theory of promising cannot find the wrong, because no one's trust depends on the promise. The modern quasi-realist finds it at first order: "I wronged her, whoever knows." *Holds in result; the founding theory breaks on the reason, and the modern school repairs it by commitment.*

**3. Equal dignity.** "Every human being has equal worth" voices a stance of equal regard, held as independent of our attitudes. *Coherent; it declines to ground.* Against Singer it can say "wrong" only from where it stands. That is parity on proof.

**4. The ultimate good.** Plural happiness, and a review of one's own life that one can approve. Hume's calm death is the school's witness. *Coherent, and smaller.* It shows the longing of W3 can be borne. It does not show the longing is answered.

### Exchange 1. The three roads: where does this school stand?

*Expressivist:* Road 2 is described as "the road of the great ancestral traditions," with Confucius as its example. My school walks it too. So do Feinberg and Pitcher, and Gaita's secular account of remorse. We hold that the dead can be wronged, and that the debt stays owed forever to the woman she was and can never be received. Road 1 still carries "remorse, a wound that will never be healed by the one it was done to," and my C row still ends on remorse. A reader will put the feeling school on road 1. But road 1 denies A3, and your own C row says I can say "nearly everything in A." Worse, road 1 is offered as one of "three ways to read" "still we owe her," yet "the debt died with her" does not read that sentence. It denies it.

*Patrick's side:* Both points are fair, and the realist found the same two. The book does not mean to place you on road 1. Road 1 should say what it costs, which is A3. Road 2 should name its secular holders, so the reader sees that the nearest secular rival walks the noble road and not the thin one.

*Expressivist:* Then the clue stands as v4 now weighs it. I grant that on my road what cannot be repaid is never released. I call that tragedy.

*Verdict: HELD WITH REPAIR (Minor).* What v4 now says no longer harms the argument, because the weighting sentence makes it a standoff. But it still misplaces this school by what it leaves out. There are two clauses in §6.

### Exchange 2. Mozi's guests.

*Expressivist:* You quote Mozi: keeping the rites while doubting the spirits is like learning the ceremonies for guests when no guests come. That image assumes the whole point of the rites is delivery to a receiver. That is the question at issue. Xunzi, and Hume after him, answer that the rites are acts of faithfulness: they keep the living true to the dead, and they need no receiver to be fitting. You honor road 2 and mock it in the same paragraph.

*Patrick's side:* The image is sharp, and it belongs to the debate inside the ancestral traditions. But it is not where our argument lives. The next sentence is: "Guilt does not only grieve a fact. It asks to be forgiven by her." Faithfulness answers grief. It does not answer a request for pardon. Keep Mozi, give his opponents their reply, and let the next sentence do the work.

*Verdict: HELD WITH REPAIR (Minor).* One sentence in §6 keeps the image and states the reply. The Confucian has the final say on Mozi and Xunzi.

### Exchange 3. The C row at full strength: three precision points, two of them my own errors.

*Expressivist:* (a) The row never states my school's central reply at the level of explanation. That reply is that no further "why" is wanted. Cruelty is wrong because of what it does to the child, and that first-order answer is the whole explanation. The C heading promises each rival "as its best defenders state it."
(b) "It reaches only as far as refined hearts agree" misstates the color-model heirs. Wiggins makes worth a matter of which sentiment is *appropriate*, and appropriateness is settled by argument, not by head count. McDowell says that when sensibilities part, one of them is defective, and we can show which from within. Neither counts votes.
(c) "He admitted he had nothing to say to the 'sensible knave'" is wrong about Hume. In EPM's Conclusion, Hume gives the knave an answer: he is the greatest dupe, trading peace of mind, integrity and a satisfying review of his own conduct for small gains. What Hume admits is narrower. If the knave's heart does not rebel, no answer will *appear satisfactory to him*.

*Patrick's side:* (b) and (c) are your Round 3 wording, and v4 took it. They should be corrected now. On (a): grant the reply and state it. The book's answer does not change. "No further why" is exactly where two refined hearts can part, and then the reply has nowhere further to go. On (b), the corrected claim bites the same way. If the heirs say which heart is right only from within a human sensibility, then equal worth still "wins only by being ours." That is the circle McDowell owns: Neurath's boat, rebuilt at sea.

*Expressivist:* Stated that way, I accept the row as fair. We stand at parity on proof.

*Verdict: HELD WITH REPAIR (Minor ×3).* The row is the right row. Each clause is small, and each makes the rival stronger, which is what the fairness rule asks for. Wording in §6.

### Exchange 4. "Remorse can end the feeling of guilt" contradicts the row it closes.

*Expressivist:* The censor changed "Remorse can end the guilt" to "the feeling of guilt." That was right for doctrine, but it now says something false about my school. The row opens by granting that I can say "my guilt is owed to her." At first order, guilt for me is not a feeling. "I am guilty" is true whatever I feel. And remorse does not end the feeling of guilt. Smith calls remorse the most dreadful sentiment that can enter the human breast. It is the feeling of guilt at its sharpest. As written, the last line turns my account into a theory of guilt-feelings, and the row's opening says it is not one.

*Patrick's side:* Granted. The line's real point is the one W1(1) makes. While she lives, both accounts have forgiveness. At the grave, this account keeps the debt but has no one to give the pardon. The line should say that, and it should still not say that remorse ends guilt.

*Verdict: HELD WITH REPAIR (Minor).* The fix created the fault. The repair in §6 keeps the censor's point: it nowhere says remorse ends guilt.

### Exchange 5. "On one point every account agrees: the wrong is necessarily wrong."

*Expressivist:* Not Hume. *Treatise* 3.1.1 argues that morality is not a relation of ideas and cannot be demonstrated. For Hume, moral distinctions rest on the constitution of human nature, which is uniform and not necessary. The sensibility heirs tie wrongness to a human sensibility. Your own C row says worth is "worth to a human heart," and your own W2 treats "wrong for any mind in any world" as a reach that is contested. Blackburn can say "necessarily wrong" at first order. The founder and the color heirs cannot. "Every account agrees" is false as written, and it is inconsistent with C and W2.

*Patrick's side:* The sentence is a concession, made so that the release point does not seem to decide anything. It gives the rivals more than some of them claim. But a concession that misstates a rival is still a misstatement. The work the sentence does needs less: that no command and no consent could make the wrong right. Every account on the panel except Nietzsche's can say that much. The book's own claim that the wrong is necessary is already in the closing ("goodness is His nature").

*Verdict: HELD WITH REPAIR (Minor).* I passed this sentence in Round 3, and I should not have. The inconsistency with C and W2 is the reason to fix it now.

### Exchange 6. Hume's "Of Suicide": the censor's fixes put a contested Church teaching into shared premises, and the God row counts it as a gap in rivals.

*Expressivist:* Three places.
(i) A4, offered to every reader, now lists "the dying" and says they are "never, if innocent, to be deliberately killed." In v3 the killing clause was bound to "for better totals." In v4 it stands alone, so it excludes chosen death.
(ii) A6 puts "not even his own consent could make it right (CCC 2295)" under "Almost everyone sees these at once."
(iii) The God row lists, among "what the best rivals leave out," "a reason why the line ... has no exceptions and is not even her own to cross."
My founder did not *leave this out*. He argued against it. In "Of Suicide," Hume takes on the claim that a life is held from God and may not be disposed of by its bearer. If disposing of life belonged to God alone, he argues, then saving a life would be as much a trespass as ending one. Turning a few ounces of blood from their channel is no more a usurpation than turning a river. Mill, many secular readers, and majorities in several countries agree with the conclusion, if not with every step. A premise "almost everyone sees at once" cannot include it. A datum that only your hypothesis supplies cannot count as evidence for that hypothesis.

*Patrick's side (on W1(2)):* First, the trust ground itself survives Hume's argument. His reductio runs together two things: caring for what was entrusted, and disposing of it. A steward who heals what was given into his care keeps the trust. One who destroys it breaks the trust. Aquinas makes this the third of his reasons (ST II-II q.64 a.5), and Locke's version is in the *Second Treatise* §6. Healing preserves the gift. Killing ends it. So W1(2), offered as the Church's explanation, holds at parity against "Of Suicide."

*Expressivist:* I accept that W1(2) is a fair explanation of a datum you hold. My charge is not against W1(2). It is that (i) and (ii) present that datum as shared, and (iii) counts it as something rivals fail to explain.

*Patrick's side:* That is right, and the debunker and the realist found the same. The censor's corrections were doctrinally necessary. Their placement was the error. v4 already uses the honest pattern for numbers: "The Church holds the line at every number; that is this book's claim, argued in W1(2), not a shared one." Consent and the dying belong in the same sentence. The God row should claim what it can, which is a reason the line is there at all, and, for those who hold with the Church that it has no exceptions, a reason why it has none.

*Verdict: FAILED as written at these three clauses (Serious, narrow: shared with the debunker and the realist, and the same finding). HELD at parity once repaired.* This school adds the founder's own text. That makes "leave out" a misstatement of this school, not only a fault of logic. W1(2) itself holds. Wording in §6.

### Exchange 7. The conclusion's last step, and Hume's weak creatures.

*Expressivist:* "Ask which you are surer of: that it was wrong to leave her there because she was weak ... If it was wrong, her worth never followed her strength, and it is no less than yours." I am surer it was wrong. But your own C row already shows how to grant that and still deny "no less than yours." Hume holds that we are bound by the laws of humanity to treat the weak gently, while they have no rights of justice against us. On that view, leaving her on the hillside is wrong, because it is inhuman. Yet her claims are not equal to yours. So the step from "wrong to leave her" to "no less than yours" skips a position the book itself records two pages earlier. Singer also holds a version of it.

*Patrick's side:* The conclusion is an appeal, not a proof, and it is addressed only to the one who gives up A4. Still, an appeal should not take a step its own C row shows is open. The honest form keeps what follows, that her claim never followed her strength. Then it turns the rest into a question: what else could make her worth less than yours, and could you say it to her? That fits the second-personal shape of the whole case (A2: guilt faces a person).

*Expressivist:* As a question, I have no complaint. Hume's middle position then becomes one answer the reader may test, and I think most readers will not be able to say it to her.

*Verdict: HELD WITH REPAIR (Minor).* The realist logged the same step (his M8). This school supplies the counterexample from the book's own text.

### Exchange 8. "Not because certainty is out of reach" and the dropped concession.

*Expressivist:* v3 said, "An honest reader may weigh it differently, and should still weigh it." I praised that line in Round 3. v4 dropped it and added that this book offers the case for weighing "not because certainty is out of reach." But with this school every witness ends in a standoff that the text itself names: "it weighs, it does not settle"; "a clue"; "as much as the reader finds." Philo would ask how witnesses that each only weigh add up to certainty.

*Patrick's side:* CCC 31's full sentence says that converging arguments "allow us to attain certainty." That is the Church's teaching, and the censor asked for it to be stated. Newman's account of how converging probabilities yield real certainty is the Catholic answer to Philo's question (*Grammar of Assent*, the illative sense). So the claim is not that the reader must be certain. It is that certainty can be reached. That fits with an honest reader weighing differently, and CCC 847 and the hiddenness section say so. The dropped sentence should come back. It says what CCC 31 leaves room for, and it is the book's own tone.

*Verdict: HELD WITH REPAIR (Minor).* Restore one sentence. Newman can go in the notes.

### Exchange 9. "A stopping point, but not a brute one": Hume's *Dialogues* Part IX.

*Expressivist:* In Part IX, Cleanthes answers Demea's argument for a necessary being. Whatever we conceive as existing, we can conceive as not existing, so no being's non-existence implies a contradiction. And if some necessary being must be granted, why not the material world? The God row now claims that God, unlike the rivals' first principles, needs "nothing outside Himself to explain Him."

*Patrick's side:* Hume's premise that what is conceivable is possible is widely doubted now. There are necessary truths we can "conceive" false, and the realist on this panel holds necessary truths. The world is composite and changing, and those are the marks of what is caused (ST I q.3). That is parity. But the row should say this is Aquinas's claim, and that every rival says something like it of its own first principle.

*Verdict: HELD AT PARITY; wording Minor* (with the realist's M6).

### Exchange 10. The Dhamma row: "justice built into a world looks like the work of a mind."

*Expressivist:* This is a design inference, and Philo answers design inferences (*Dialogues* VII): order also arises by generation and growth, and we have seen too little of the world to say which principle made it.

*Patrick's side:* The row says "looks like." It does not say "proves." A process that sorts fortunes by the moral quality of hearts is not order in general. It is order that reads intentions. Generation and growth do not read intentions.

*Verdict: HOLDS* (parity; "looks like" is the right modesty). The Buddhist logged NONE here.

### Exchange 11. W2's new "reach" argument.

*Expressivist:* On my view, "cruelty would be wrong for any mind in any world" is not something conscience *tracks*. It expresses our refusal to let wrongness vary with anyone's attitudes or makeup. So no puzzle of reach arises for me.

*Patrick's side:* That is exactly why the scope note sends this school to C. W2 does not count against you.

*Verdict: HOLDS.* The scope note is now right in both directions.

### Exchange 12 (our side presses). Anscombe began from Hume.

*Patrick's side:* W1 opens with Anscombe. Her essay draws on Hume. She credits him with showing that "owes" and "ought" do not follow by logic from facts about what was done (the grocer and the potatoes). She also credits him with showing that once the law conception lost its lawgiver, "ought" kept only a kind of mesmeric force. Hume dropped the Lawgiver. Anscombe proposed dropping the words. The book keeps both.

*Expressivist:* That is fair to both of them. Hume would add that he kept the words by grounding them in sentiment, and that is our C standoff.

*Verdict: An insight, not a defect* (see §7). It also suggests crediting Hume for A5, which is his own is/ought.

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**As written: ONE Serious objection stands (narrow, and shared with the debunker and the realist; it is one finding, not a new one). No Fatal.**

**Serious**
1. **Contested Church teaching on chosen death is placed in shared premises and then counted as a rival's gap** (Exchange 6). A4 now says "the dying ... never, if innocent, to be deliberately killed." A6 puts "not even his own consent could make it right" under "Almost everyone sees these at once." The God row counts "not even her own to cross" among "what the best rivals leave out." This school's founder did not leave it out. He argued against it in "Of Suicide," and many secular readers agree with his conclusion. So these clauses (a) break the brief's rule against putting Christian-only demands into premises a non-Christian must grant, (b) misstate this school as an omission, and (c) let a datum the hypothesis supplies count as evidence for it. The censor's corrections were doctrinally right. Their placement is the fault. W1(2) itself holds at parity against Hume. This is answerable by exact wording, using the pattern v4 already uses for "every number" (§6, R1–R3). It does not touch the conclusion, which the God row's other items carry.

**Minor (wording and scope; none changes a conclusion)**
2. W1(1): road 1 denies A3 rather than reading it. Road 2 should name its secular holders, so that this school has a home (Exchange 1).
3. W1(1): Mozi's guests need the reply of road 2's holders (Exchange 2).
4. C, Feeling, refined: add the "no further why" reply. Correct "as far as refined hearts agree" (Wiggins, McDowell). Correct "nothing to say" to the knave. The last two were my own Round 3 wording (Exchange 3).
5. C, Feeling, refined: "Remorse can end the feeling of guilt" contradicts the row's opening and Smith (Exchange 4).
6. W1 frame: "every account agrees: the wrong is necessarily wrong" misstates Hume and the color heirs, and conflicts with C and W2 (Exchange 5).
7. Conclusion: the step to "no less than yours" (Exchange 7); restore the honest-reader sentence (Exchange 8).
8. God row: mark "not a brute one" as Aquinas's claim and give the parity clause (Exchange 9, with the realist).

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- **Is there a further "why"?** The quasi-realist says the first-order answer (what cruelty does to the child) is the whole explanation. The book says necessity, unity and a worth that may never be spent ask for a ground. Once Exchange 3(a) is added, the row states both sides.
- **The debt to the dead.** On road 2 the debt is owed forever and never received, which this school calls tragedy. Road 3 is a hope reason cannot prove. v4 weighs it exactly: "as much as the reader finds the third road truer than the second."
- **"Of Suicide" against life held in trust.** This is a first-order disagreement about whether a life is the bearer's to end. The trust account answers Hume's reductio (care against disposal). Neither side compels the other.
- **The coherent dissenter** (the knave, Singer). No argument compels him, ours included. The difference is in what he is missing.
- **Certainty and proportion.** Philo's proportioning against CCC 31 and Newman's convergence. Certainty can be reached, but it is not compelled.
- **Necessary being** (*Dialogues* IX) against God as "the One whose very being is to be." Each side has a first principle.
- **The longing.** Hume's serene death against W3. A longing that can be borne is not thereby answered.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**R1 (Serious, A4). Replace** "never to be used as a mere means for better totals, never, if innocent, to be deliberately killed, and not lost when life grows hard, nor even by the worst of crimes (*Dignitas Infinita* 1, 15; CCC 2261, 2267)." **with:**
> never to be used as a mere means for better totals, never, if innocent, to be killed for anyone else's good, and not lost when life grows hard, nor even by the worst of crimes (*Dignitas Infinita* 1, 15; CCC 2267). The Church holds more: that the innocent may never be deliberately killed at all, not even at his own request (CCC 2261, 2277). That is this book's claim, argued in W1(2), not a shared one.

**R2 (Serious, A6). Replace** "no one may be experimented on to death, however many would be saved, and not even his own consent could make it right (CCC 2295). Almost everyone sees these at once." **with:**

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **The censor's fixes and the shared premises pull in opposite directions, and v4 already has the tool that reconciles them.** "That is this book's claim, argued in W1(2), not a shared one" lets the book state the Church's teaching exactly without asking a non-Christian to grant it. Use it everywhere a correction adds a Church-only absolute: consent, chosen death, every number.
- **Hume's "Of Suicide" is the classic opponent of "life held in trust," and the trust account answers it.** Hume's reductio (if life is God's to dispose of, saving it trespasses too) runs together care and disposal. A steward keeps the trust by healing and breaks it by destroying (ST II-II q.64 a.5; Locke, *Second Treatise* §6). That answer belongs in the notes to W1(2).
- **Anscombe's essay begins from Hume.** He showed that "owes" does not follow by logic from what was done, and that "ought" cut off from its lawgiver keeps only a kind of mesmeric force. Hume kept the words and grounded them in feeling. Anscombe proposed dropping them. The book keeps the words and their Lawgiver. One sentence in W1 could say so, and it would credit the rival fairly.
- **A5 is Hume's own is/ought.** Crediting him there is fair and costs nothing. The Church agrees with him on the gap and parts from him over where the "ought" lives (A5's last sentence).
- **Hume's weak creatures are the best test of the conclusion.** He shows how a person can grant "it was wrong to leave her" and still deny "no less than yours." Turning the last step into a second-personal question ("could you say it to her?") meets him on the ground of A2: guilt faces a person.
- **Remorse in the best secular accounts is not a thin feeling.** Gaita treats remorse as the clear recognition of the reality of the one wronged. Wherever v4 uses "remorse" for the rival's road, it should not use the word as a lesser thing.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "It weighs as much as the reader finds the third road truer than the second to the guilt he actually knows." (v4; exact)
- "No argument compels such a man, ours included; the difference is what he is missing." (v4)
- "Guilt does not only grieve a fact. It asks to be forgiven by her." (v4)
- "Every child was wanted by the One who did, before anyone saw her." (v4)
- "Remorse can be lived through; only forgiveness can heal the bond."
- "Ask what else could make her worth less than yours, and whether you could say it to her."
- "Faithfulness answers grief. It does not answer a request for pardon."
