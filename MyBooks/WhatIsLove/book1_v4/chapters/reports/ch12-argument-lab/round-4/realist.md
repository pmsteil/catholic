# Argument Lab, Round 4: Non-Naturalist Secular Moral Realism vs. v4

## 1. School and voice

Godless normative realism: Wielenberg (*Robust Ethics*), Enoch (*Taking Morality Seriously*), Parfit (*On What Matters*), Shafer-Landau, Huemer. Joined by the theory of directed duties (Darwall, Wallace, Thompson), the third-factor reply to Street, Fine's view that necessity lives in essences, and, this round, Kamm and Nagel on why constraints have the shape they do. Moral truths are objective, necessary, grounded in non-moral features, known by reflection, and need no God. This school checked each of its nine Round 3 repairs against v4, then looked for what the new text, and especially the doctrinal fixes, broke.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

1. **Justice.** What each person is owed, much of it owed *to* her (directed duties), fixed by necessary truths about persons. *Holds as a working principle; it posits its ground.*
2. **A hard case.** Re A (the conjoined twins): Mary may not be killed as a means, and Jodie is not bound to keep sustaining her. *Coherent, and it borrows justice and mercy in all but name* (Rom 2:14–15).
3. **Equal dignity.** Kind-based essentialism: every human being has the rational nature essentially, and worth follows the kind, not how far its powers are used now (Liao). *Holds, but it posits the measure.* v4's "one end that is God Himself" gives a reason for it. Our side leads, narrowly.
4. **Ultimate good.** An objective list (knowledge, friendship, achievement, beauty, autonomy, pleasure) lived with integrity; death ends it. *Coherent for a life. It breaks on unity, and on the child who died before any of it reached her.*

### New exchanges

**Exchange 1: the doctrinal repairs put the Church's own claims back into the shared premises. (Serious)**
*Realist:* A is "what we take to be true," and "most people find, on reflection, that they cannot give these up." The censor's repairs are correct doctrine, but look where they landed. A4 now says every human being, "the dying" included, is "never, if innocent, to be deliberately killed." That rules out voluntary euthanasia at the request of a dying, competent patient. Most of my school allows it (Parfit, Huemer, Shafer-Landau, and on the record a large share of secular moral philosophers), and so do majorities in many countries. A6 says that no one may be experimented on to death, "and not even his own consent could make it right," and then: "Almost everyone sees these at once." I do not see that at once, and neither does a Huemer, for whom a person's life is his own. In Round 2 this school found euthanasia built into A4, and v3 took it out. v4 has put it back. Two sentences later, A6 itself shows the honest standard: "The Church holds the line at every number (VS 80); that is this book's claim, argued in W1(2), not a shared one." The consent and euthanasia clauses get no such flag. Then the God row counts them as a win. Among "what the best rivals leave out" it lists "a reason why the line that protects the innocent ... is not even her own to cross." That is not a datum I fail to explain. It is a claim of yours that I deny.
*Defense:* Partly answered. Some of it is shared more widely than you say. Secular research ethics forbids experiments expected to kill even when the subject consents (the Nuremberg Code, point 5), and Mill would not let anyone sell himself into slavery (A3 relies on that, rightly). But the defense cannot hold "almost everyone" for the euthanasia clause. The Nuremberg Code itself makes an exception when the physicians are their own subjects. And the book's own flourishing row criticizes Foot because she allows a life to be ended, which shows the book knows this is not common ground. The fix is to move the clauses, not to drop them. W1(2) already argues them ("not hers to throw away: it is held in trust from the One who alone is Lord of life," CCC 2258, 2280). They belong under A6's honest sentence, labeled as the book's claims, and the God row should say "as the Church holds."
*Realist rejoinder:* Then I have no objection. And note what follows: once it is labeled, the trust account is the theist's best card here. Kamm's inviolability explains why *we* may not cross the line, but not why *she* may not waive it. Jurisdiction explains both.
*Result:* **Serious as written. Held with repair (S1).** It is a premise offered as shared that is not shared, contrary to the brief ("don't put Christian-only demands into premises a non-Christian must grant") and to the standard A6 sets itself. It is also counted as an explanatory advantage in C. Not Fatal: the argument for the claim is already in W1(2), and the repair keeps every word of the censor's doctrine.

**Exchange 2: the W1 frame's leftover grammar, and "what it does to Him." (Minor)**
*Realist:* (a) "An order of cause and fruit, or a necessary truth, can say the deed was wrong and its fruit will come." I still do not say any fruit will come. Split the sentence. (b) "We see it first in what it does to the child," then "every such wrong is also, and before all else, an offense against Him," then in the closing, "wrong because of what it does to her, and to Him whose she is," and in C, the law "holds first in a Mind." That is three "firsts" and one "before all else." And "what it does to Him" sounds as though cruelty harms God. Your own tradition says nothing harms Him (the censor's impassibility point). Which comes first, and what does the deed "do" to God?
*Defense:* There are two orders. In the order of knowing, the child comes first. In the order of being, God comes first. Aquinas has the sentence that joins them: God is offended by us only because we act against our good (SCG III.122). The offense against God is not a second injury beside the injury to her. It is the same act against the good He wills for her. That keeps CCC 1440 and the realist's "wrong because of what it does to her" together. It also removes "does to Him."
*Result:* **Held with repair (M1, M2). Minor.**

**Exchange 3: W1(1)'s three roads. The contest is fair now; two small slips remain. (Minor)**
*Realist:* The three roads are an honest dilemma, and the clue is weighted exactly as it should be: "as much as the reader finds the third road truer than the second." Three points. (a) The first road, "the debt died with her," is not a *reading* of A3 ("still we owe her"). It denies A3. The text says "three ways to read that." Say instead that some deny it. (b) The second road is credited only to "the great ancestral traditions" and Confucius. It is also the secular tragic view, which is mine (Gaita, Williams; Feinberg and Pitcher on wrongs to the dead as they were). The C row places me there. W1(1) should say so, or a reader will think the godless have only the first road. (c) "Guilt asks for what only that hope could give: to be forgiven by her." What is needed is that *she* is still someone. A survival without God (McTaggart's) would give it too. So the clue points first to her survival, and only through that to God.
*Defense:* (a) and (b) granted; both are a phrase. On (c): granted in logic, and the weight barely changes. Survival without a keeper has almost no defenders. The text already joins the steps ("What He keeps is her claim, and her"). Name the step and keep it.
*Realist rejoinder:* Guilt's asking for her pardon is fitting. That does not make it satisfiable. Fitting attitudes can have no object that could answer them.
*Defense:* Agreed, and v4 says so: "the speaking alone settles nothing." The clue is fit, not proof. That is the standoff at parity.
*Result:* **Held, at parity. Minor wording (M3).**

**Exchange 4: W1(2) says the best rivals stop at "this is simply what justice is." Some of them give a reason. (Minor)**
*Realist:* (a) "That is Philippa Foot's own answer": the list includes "each person must be answered one by one," which comes from Scanlon, Nagel and Taurek, not Foot. Foot's answer was doing versus allowing, and negative duties against positive ones. (b) "Asked why justice has that shape, without exception and whatever the numbers, they answer ...": most of the best rivals do not hold it "whatever the numbers." A6 itself says "fewer hold the line when the numbers grow vast." (c) Those who do hold it give a reason, not a shrug. Kamm says the constraint expresses the status of persons as inviolable, and that we matter more because we have it. Nagel says the constraint lies in what it is to aim at another's harm.
*Defense:* Granted. The point survives with each named: inviolability is a status that the realist posits, and aiming at harm is wrong because of a worth already assumed. Each is a stopping point one step further back. And neither explains what W1(2) is really after: why the line is not hers to waive (Exchange 1).
*Result:* **Held with repair (M4). Minor.**

**Exchange 5: "Life held in trust." Does jurisdiction bring voluntarism back?**
*Realist:* If her life is held in trust from the Lord of life, the line binds us because the life is not ours. But the owner may dispose of what He owns. Aquinas says God may take any life without injustice (II-II q.64 a.6 ad 1), and that Abraham's command did not make the killing of Isaac murder (I-II q.100 a.8 ad 3). So the line "has no exceptions" only because of whose will stands behind it. That is the second horn of Euthyphro coming back.
*Defense:* No. What the precept forbids, the unjust taking of innocent life, is never dispensed. God's taking a life is not that act, because He takes back what He gives, and He takes it without loss to her: in His hands death is not her end, and "God never spends anyone as a mere means against her own good" (Evil section). His lordship is not mere power. It is the lordship of the Giver who keeps her, and He is the Good (Chapter 3). That is why the trust explains the line without making it arbitrary.
*Realist rejoinder:* Then the absolute rests on God's goodness and His keeping her. That is coherent, but it is the book's whole case, not a separate support.
*Defense:* Yes. W1(2) says so: "here the moral question and the question of creation meet."
*Result:* **Held, at parity.** This is the standoff the synthesis named (Foot's "what justice is" against life held in trust).

**Exchange 6: reach and the mathematics parallel.**
*Realist:* (a) v4 states my answer to reach itself: "the same reasoning that serves us everywhere does the rest." Then it says "neither explains the reach." Those two cannot both stand unless "explains" means "explains why a priori reasoning works at all." (b) Clarke-Doane: for necessary truths, the worry "you would have believed it even if it were false" has nothing to grip, because they could not have been false. (c) If the point holds equally of mathematics, it is the argument from reason, not a moral argument, and it must not be counted twice.
*Defense:* (a) Yes, it means exactly that, and the very next sentence says so: "The same question hangs over the mind that reads mathematics." (b) That is contested, and v4 says "the rivals contest it; it weighs, it does not settle." (c) Fair. In this chapter W2 is one witness among three, and it is offered as fit, so nothing is counted twice. When the case is condensed, the chapter should not present the reach point as special to morality.
*Result:* **Held, at parity.** No change needed; one caution for the condensed chapter.

**Exchange 7: two phrases in C. (Minor)**
*Realist:* (a) The realist row asks "whether the reach of conscience ... is only good fortune." That was my own Round 3 wording, and it was wrong. W2 now credits my answer as one in which evolution and the truth "meet without luck." Say "whether it can be explained without a Mind." (b) The God row opens: "also a stopping point, but not a brute one: He ... needs nothing outside Himself to explain Him." Wielenberg says exactly that of basic ethical facts, and Fine says necessity is explained by essence. The next sentence ("not fewer first principles but one source for three things") states the honest asymmetry correctly, so a careful reader is not misled. Still, the opening clause reads like a jab at the parity this lab settled.
*Defense:* (a) granted. (b) The doctrinal point stands: God is not a brute fact (Exodus 3:14; ST I q.3 a.4). Add six words that grant the realist says the same of his truths.
*Result:* **Held with repair (M5, M6). Minor**, not Serious. The realist contrast that follows is stated correctly.

**Exchange 8: "It holds first in a Mind." Does God think the truths, or make them?**
*Realist:* Is cruelty wrong because God thinks it so, or does He think it because it is so?
*Defense:* Neither. God knows His own essence, which is the Good, as something creatures can share. The truth about what a creature is owed is that knowledge (ST I q.15 a.2; q.16 a.7). It is not a decree that could have gone otherwise, and v4 says so: "He did not choose whether cruelty is evil, for goodness is His nature."
*Realist rejoinder:* And if, per impossibile, there were no God?
*Defense:* That is a counterpossible, and our sense of what would hold if there were no God is no guide to what depends on Him (Round 2).
*Result:* **Held, at parity.** Holding of itself or holding in a Mind is the standoff.

**Exchange 9: "not intended ... as a means," and the wolf. (Minor)**
*Realist:* The evil section says "the harms that follow in a world of real causes God does not intend, either as an end or as a means." A paragraph earlier it says "the wolf lives by the fawn," and Aquinas says the lion would not live without the slaying of animals (ST I q.22 a.2 ad 2). The fawn's death is how the wolf lives. That looks like a means within the order God wills. Pain is necessarily bad on my view, so this is where your theodicy is thinnest.
*Defense:* Aquinas's own term is that God causes these corruptions "as it were by accident," because He wills the good of the order (ST I q.49 a.2). He does not aim at the death. The realist is right that the line between means and side effect is hard to draw for the fawn, and v4 already carries the fawn as weight ("thinner"). The text should say that the line is sharp for persons and hard to draw for the fawn.
*Result:* **Held at parity by concession. Minor wording (M7).**

**Exchange 10: the conclusion's last step. (Minor)**
*Realist:* "If it was wrong, her worth never followed her strength, and it is no less than yours." The first half follows. The second does not. McMahan holds that leaving the infant to die was gravely wrong and that her worth is still less than an adult's. Equality needs one more step.
*Defense:* That step is the kind-based one, which you yourself accept: if worth does not follow what she can do, it follows what she is, and she is that as fully as you are. That takes one sentence.
*Result:* **Held with repair (M8). Minor.**

**Exchange 11: W2's unity clause, and "the law binds because He is good."**
*Realist:* (a) "one can always refuse to do evil (VS 52, 102)": VS 52 itself gives the reason, and it is the *form* of prohibitions, which needs no single Source. The sentence about the goods fitting together, which was the real evidence, was dropped. (b) "The law binds because He is good" sits beside "the wrong is wrong because of what it does to her." On my view wrongness already gives decisive reasons. A Lawgiver adds nothing to the binding.
*Defense:* (a) Granted. Restore the sentence on the fit of the goods; that is the book's own theme (Chapters 5–9). (b) This is no longer a slip. It is Anscombe's question, which W1 states openly: whether "obligation" keeps its sense without a law-giver. It is a standoff.
*Result:* **(a) Minor, repair M9. (b) Held, at parity.**

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**Fatal against v4 as written:** none.

**Serious against v4 as written:** one.

1. **Contested Catholic absolutes are offered as shared premises (A4, A6), and C counts them as a win.** A4's "never, if innocent, to be deliberately killed," with "the dying" in its list, and A6's "not even his own consent could make it right," under "Almost everyone sees these at once," are denied by most of this school (Parfit, Huemer, Shafer-Landau on voluntary euthanasia and consensual self-sacrifice) and by many readers. The book itself knows this (its criticism of Foot in the flourishing row). A6 flags its parallel claim about numbers as "this book's claim ... not a shared one," but not these. The God row then lists "not even her own to cross" among what "the best rivals leave out." The cause is placement, not doctrine: the censor's Round 3 fixes are correct, but they were inserted into A. *Serious*, because it breaks the brief's rule on premises a non-Christian must grant and reverses a fault this school found in Round 2 and v3 had fixed. Repair S1 keeps every word of the doctrine and moves it under A6's honest sentence; W1(2) already argues it.

**Minor:** M1 (W1 frame: "a necessary truth ... its fruit will come"); M2 ("first" against "before all else"; "what it does ... to Him"; SCG III.122); M3 (W1(1): road 1 denies A3; road 2's secular holders; "only that hope"); M4 (W1(2): "Foot's own answer"; "whatever the numbers"; Kamm and Nagel give reasons); M5 (C realist: "only good fortune"); M6 (God row: "not a brute one" without the parity clause); M7 (evil: "not ... as a means" for the fawn); M8 (conclusion: "no less than yours" does not follow without one step); M9 (W2 unity clause is a point about form; restore the fit of the goods); and still open from Round 3, the A7 scope sentence in W1.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**
- *The dead:* the tragic view (a real debt, forever unanswered) against "she is still someone." Guilt's seeking fits the second better; the first is coherent and honestly held. v4 now states this exactly.
- *Where necessity lives:* in essences, or first in a Mind. Both stopping points need nothing outside themselves. Our side's edge is breadth (law, beings and minds from one Source), not fewer axioms.
- *Reach:* whether reliable general reasoning plus the third factor explains how conscience reaches necessary truths, or whether that is the unsolved access problem from mathematics (Field, Benacerraf; against them Clarke-Doane).
- *Why the line has that shape:* "what justice is," or persons' inviolability (Kamm, Nagel), against life held in trust from the Lord of life. The trust account alone explains why the line cannot be waived. That counts only for a reader who grants it cannot be.
- *Whether wrongness binds of itself* or needs a Lawgiver: Anscombe's question, stated openly in W1.
- *Equal worth:* kind-based essentialism posits the measure; the theist gives a reason (one end, God Himself). Our side leads, narrowly.
- *Evil sharpened by realism:* the fawn's pain, judged by an independent standard. It is owned as weight; parity holds only by concession, which is the right posture.

**After S1 (and the Minor repairs): NONE below parity.**

A6: replace

with

God row: replace

## 7. New insights; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
1. **A rule for future doctrinal fixes:** when the Church's teaching is stricter than common ground ("not even with consent," "not even at his own request," "at every number"), say it in full, and put it after A6's sentence "this is the book's claim, not a shared one." Doctrine then stays whole and A stays shared. This round's one Serious item came from the right doctrine in the wrong place.
2. **The trust account's real advantage is that the line cannot be waived.** Status accounts (Kamm's inviolability, Nagel on aiming at harm) explain why *we* may not cross the line. Only jurisdiction ("not hers to throw away") explains why *she* may not release it. That is a sharper and fairer claim than "the rivals cannot say why there are no exceptions," and it is the place where W1(2) clearly gains over the best secular deontology, for a reader who grants the datum.
3. **SCG III.122 settles the order of priority:** God is offended only because we act against the good. The child comes first in the order of knowing, God in the order of being, and it is one act, not two injuries. It also keeps the realist's "wrong because of what it does to her" inside the Catholic account.
4. **In W1(1) the real contest is between the second road and the third.** The first road denies A3, so it is not one of the readings. And the clue points first to her survival, then to God as the One who keeps her. Saying both steps makes the witness stronger, not weaker.
5. **The reach argument is general.** It applies to mathematics as much as to morals. In the condensed chapter, present it as fit shared with mathematics, not as a point only morality makes.
6. **Chapter text (still open from Rounds 2 and 3):** chapter_12_current.md still has "something only a person can do" (line 77) and "Only a Someone can" (line 105). Directed-duty realism grants that claims are laid by persons, namely the victim. Rework both along the lines of v4's W1 frame when the chapter is redlined.
