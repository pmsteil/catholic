# Round 4 — Kantian Constructivism vs. v4

## 1. School and voice

The opponent speaks for Kant (*Groundwork*, the second and third *Critiques*, *Metaphysics of Morals*, *Religion*, *Conflict of the Faculties*, the Collins lectures) and his heirs Korsgaard, O'Neill, Hill, Wood, Reath, and Kleingeld and Willaschek. His core claims: the moral law is the law of a free will as such; humanity is an end in itself, with dignity and not price; God is a postulate of practical reason, never the ground of obligation. In Round 3 he logged NONE against v3, with seven Minor repairs. This round he checks those repairs and then presses the new text: the rewritten Kant row, W1(2)'s life held in trust and its dignity rooted in image and call, W1(1)'s rebuilt Catholic answer, and the W1 closing.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

1. **Justice.** Each person has one innate right, freedom from another's forcing choice, so far as that fits everyone's freedom under universal law (*Doctrine of Right* 6:237). The duties of virtue are added to it. *Holds, borrows nothing.* It reaches the enemy and the dead (§35).
2. **Hard case.** The convicted murderer. Kant says he must be executed (6:333), and clemency may be granted only for crimes against the sovereign himself (6:337). *Coherent and unborrowed, but this is justice with mercy torn away.* Modern Kantians who reject the death penalty bring mercy back in under the name of respect for the convict (cf. CCC 2267).
3. **Equal dignity.** Humanity, as capable of morality, has worth above all price (*Groundwork* 4:434–435), and even the vicious man keeps it (6:463). *Holds for the enemy. For the newborn and the profoundly impaired it holds only by reading "humanity" as rational nature present where no reasoning is happening, which is a claim about what a being is for.* Kant himself faltered once at the illegitimate infant (6:336; see §7).
4. **Final end.** The highest good: virtue crowned with proportionate happiness in an ethical commonwealth. *Kant borrows openly, by postulating God and immortality. His secular heirs drop the borrowing, and their realm of ends is one the dead never enter* (*Critique of Judgment* §87, the wide grave).

### Exchange 1: Does Kant's law of pardon now cut against W1(1)?

- *Kantian:* The Kant row now says my rule of pardon (a ruler may forgive only what was done against himself, never what one subject did to another) "is the very shape of W1(1)." In v3 that was true, because God forgave "that part," the offense against Himself. The censor removed "that part." v4's God forgives sins and His forgiveness "is whole" (CCC 1441). So the dilemma is this. Either God wholly pardons what one creature did to another, and by my rule that is the greatest wrong to the victim (6:337, *impunitas criminis*). Or He pardons only the offense against Himself, and His forgiveness is partial, which is the censor's Error. Also, my rule is about *clemency*, the remission of punishment, not about forgiveness in A3's sense. Forgiveness between persons I treat elsewhere, as the duty of placability (*Doctrine of Virtue* 6:460–461). The row conflates the two.
- *Patrick's side:* The dilemma equivocates on "whole." What God forgives wholly is the *sin*. The text says that is "before all else, an offense against the One whose love made her precious" (CCC 1440). That is not a third party's interest standing beside hers. It is the depth of the same act. "Whole" means nothing of the sin's guilt before God is left unforgiven. It does not mean He remits what she is owed. The text says so in its next breath: absolution "does not remedy all the disorders sin has caused" (CCC 1459); we must still repair; "God does not take her place in giving the pardon that is hers to give"; and the wrongdoer does not sit beside her "as though nothing had happened" (*Spe Salvi* 44). So there is no impunity for the crime against her. Kant's own rule even fits the Catholic structure more tightly than it fit v3. Kant allows the sovereign to pardon crimes against his own majesty. On the Church's view every wrong against a person *is* an offense against the Sovereign, so Kant's rule lets God remit the whole offense against Himself and forbids Him to pardon in her name. That is exactly what v4 says.
- *Kantian rejoinder:* Then the row should say clemency and not "forgive," and it should say how the shape holds. "The very shape" invites my dilemma.
- **Verdict: Held. Minor wording (§6, repair 1).**

### Exchange 2: "Owed to her first" vs "before all else an offense against Him"

- *Kantian:* Two sentences apart, the W1 frame says that every such wrong is "before all else, an offense against Him (CCC 1440)," and that guilt "is owed to her first (A2)." Which is first? A hostile reader will call this incoherent at the hinge of the main witness.
- *Patrick's side:* The frame already marks two orders: "We see it *first* in what it does to the child" is the order of knowing, and CCC 1440 gives the order of gravity. "Owed to her first" means the first that we see.
- *Kantian:* Granted. It is recoverable, but only by work the reader should not have to do.
- **Verdict: Held. Minor (§6, repair 3).**

### Exchange 3: "The child is His," "Him whose she is," and "what it does to Him"

- *Kantian:* In Round 3 you blocked the ownership reading with "whose love." v4 keeps that in W1(1) but adds two bare possessives: "because the child is His" (frame) and "what it does to her, and to Him whose she is" (closing). §28 says a person cannot be anyone's product or property, not the parents' and by the same argument not God's. There is a second problem. Wrongness now rests on "what it does to … Him." But v4 itself says God cannot suffer (*Spe Salvi* 39) and needs nothing (Ex 3:14). What does a wrong *do* to Him? If our deeds can do something to God, then religious acts can do something *for* Him, and religion slides into courting favor, which is what I call pseudo-service (*Religion* 6:167ff). I also deny that ethics contains any duties *to* God as such (*Doctrine of Virtue* 6:443–444).
- *Patrick's side:* On ownership: the belonging meant here is the belonging of love ("I have called you by name, you are mine," Isaiah 43:1), the way a child is her mother's. It is not the way a field is a farmer's. W1(1) says so, and CCC 2280 says we are stewards, not owners. On "what it does to Him": Aquinas has the exact answer. God is offended by us only when we act against our own good (*Summa contra Gentiles* III.122, paraphrase; verify). The offense to God is not a harm done to Him. It is the wrong done to the one He loves, seen from His side, and that is why it is "before all else." This blocks pseudo-service directly: nothing we do benefits or injures God, and what He asks is our good. Kant's point that there are no duties to God within ethics is the old standoff over knowledge of God (CCC 36 and *Dei Filius* against the first *Critique*). It is not a defect.
- **Verdict: Held. Minor wording in two places (§6, repairs 2 and 4).** Aquinas's line is a gain: it answers Kant's sharpest charge against religious ethics in one clause, and it fits the book's Euthyphro answer.

### Exchange 4: "Held in trust from the Lord of life": heteronomy, and the Abraham problem

- *Kantian:* W1(2) now grounds the exceptionless line in jurisdiction: her life "is not ours to spend … and not hers to throw away: it is held in trust from the One who alone is Lord of life … That is why the line has no exceptions. No one may take what is not his to take." A ground of the form *not yours, because it is His* forbids everyone who lacks the right and leaves the holder of the right free. Your own tradition draws that inference. Aquinas holds that God, as Lord of life and death, may take any life, guilty or innocent, without injustice, and that one who kills at His command does not murder (ST I-II q.94 a.5 ad 2; q.100 a.8 ad 3; the case of Abraham). So the trust ground does not explain a line with *no* exceptions. It explains a line with one exception, the divine will. Your own closing says a will that invents values "can command, but it cannot bind." My ground, dignity that admits no equivalent, has no such exception, and I drew the conclusion myself: Abraham should have answered the voice that he was certain he must not kill his son, and was not certain the voice was God (*Conflict of the Faculties* 7:63, paraphrase).
- *Patrick's side:* Look at what the line forbids. A6 and W1(2) forbid *spending* persons: killing the innocent as a means to others' good. God never does that. v4 says God "never spends anyone as a mere means against her own good," and He does not lose the one whose earthly life ends: He keeps her and raises her (CCC 998). The ground, then, is not a property title that its holder may use as he likes. It is the reason the line binds every creature without remainder. Only He gives life, so only He can take it without spending her, because only He can give it back. That is not voluntarism, because His lordship is His being the Good (W1 closing), and He cannot will her harm as an end. As for any voice claiming a command to kill the innocent, the Church answers as Kant did: "no one can under any circumstance claim for himself the right directly to destroy an innocent human being" (CCC 2258), and acts that are intrinsically evil admit no exception (VS 80). In Genesis 22 the hand is stopped.
- *Kantian rejoinder:* Then the exceptionlessness *for creatures* rests on two things: our lack of title, and our inability to restore what we take. My ground rests it on her alone. Neither ground is shown to be better. And "That is why the line has no exceptions," with no qualifier, invites my objection.
- *Reply:* Agreed on both points. The comparison here is at parity (self-standing dignity vs dignity as gift), and the sentence should say "for any of us."
- **Verdict: Held (parity). Minor wording (§6, repair 5).**

### Exchange 5: The God row misstates Kant

- *Kantian:* The God row lists, among "what the best rivals leave out," a "reason why the line that protects the innocent has no exceptions and is not even her own to cross." That second clause is my signature doctrine. The first worked example of the categorical imperative is suicide (*Groundwork* 4:421–422, 4:429). To destroy humanity in one's own person is to root out morality's existence from the world, so far as it lies in one (*Doctrine of Virtue* 6:422–423). We are not our own property (Collins 27:386, paraphrase; verify). And my absolutism holds the line at every number. W1(2) answers Foot ("simply what justice is") and the sentientist ("others can always suffer more"), but it is silent on mine: she is an end in herself, whose worth has no equivalent. The row claims to state each rival "as its best defenders state it."
- *Patrick's side:* Conceded as to Kant. In Round 3 the parallel item ("a worth that may never be spent") could be read distributively, and it was let stand. The new clause names Kant's own doctrine, so it must credit him. The substance is untouched. Kant's ground is also a stopping point: it says her worth has no price, not why there is a *she* whose worth has none. Kant is an ally on "not hers to throw away," and the book gains by saying so. Kant's own lecture teaching that suicide is not wrong because God forbids it, but forbidden because it is wrong (Collins, paraphrase; verify 27:375), is the book's Euthyphro answer.
- **Verdict: Held with repair. Minor: it misstates the school in a parenthetical list, and no argument against Kant rests on it (§6, repairs 6 and 7).**

### Exchange 6: The image of God, and the tu quoque on "must stretch"

- *Kantian:* W1(2) now roots dignity in "a person made in God's image." Aquinas puts the image in the intellectual nature, as a natural aptitude for knowing and loving God that all humans share (ST I q.93 a.2, a.4). That is rational nature present where no reasoning happens, which is the very reading of "humanity" my school uses for the newborn. If Kant's dignity "must stretch," so does yours.
- *Patrick's side:* Not in the same way. For Aquinas the rational soul is the *form* of the newborn's body from its beginning (CCC 365). Her rational nature is not inferred from what she can do; it is what she is. Kant's idealism cannot say that. It can ascribe a rational nature to an organism only as a practical idea (§28). That is the stretch the row names, and Kantians from Wood to Kain have worked hard to cover it. v4 also adds two things the image alone does not give: the call that comes with her being, and her being willed for her own sake (GS 19, 24). The tu quoque fails, though it is a reason to keep "capacity to reason" in the Kant row precise (§6, note to repair 6).
- **Verdict: Held.**

### Exchange 7: The Kant row should name the postulate

- *Kantian:* "The philosopher of autonomy found that he had to think of Someone" is true. But a row that states me "as my best defenders state it" must add my reply: I held that thought as rational faith, not knowledge, and I deny that a necessary idea is evidence for its object. Without that, a reader takes me for a half-convert.
- *Patrick's side:* Fair. Naming the standoff in the row also shows the reader exactly where the book parts from Kant, which is the narrowest gap in C.
- **Verdict: Held with repair. Minor (§6, repair 6).**

### Exchange 8: The ransom against A3

- *Kantian:* A3 says no one can give the pardon in her place, and I hold that guilt is the most personal debt, which no one else can pay (*Religion* 6:72). Yet the evil section cites Christ's life given "as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45). Doesn't the book let a third party pay what it says only the debtor owes and only the victim can release?
- *Patrick's side:* v4 does not use the ransom for pardon, and it does not say guilt is transferred. It uses it to show that suffering freely given bears fruit for others ("given, not taken"), in the evil section and nowhere in W1. The pardon stays hers (W1(1)). Satisfaction in Catholic teaching is offered by the Head for the members of one body (ST III q.48 a.2). It is not a debt handed to a stranger, and it does not release the sinner from the amends he owes (CCC 1459). The question belongs to Chapter 9, as flagged in Round 3.
- **Verdict: Held.**

### Exchange 9: Certainty in the Conclusion

- *Kantian:* The Conclusion now says that arguments of this kind can bring a person to certainty (CCC 31, 36; *Dei Filius*). My moral proof yields faith, not knowledge.
- *Patrick's side:* The book reports what the Church teaches and then offers the case "for weighing." It does not claim to compel. CCC 33 counts conscience among these ways. On whether reason may attain certainty here, Kant and the Church disagree, and the disagreement stays at parity.
- **Verdict: Held (parity).**

### Exchange 10: Smaller checks

- **A2, the betrayed secret.** Kant agrees that the dead can be wronged (§35), and the letter case cleanly separates a frustrated wish from a wrong. *Held.*
- **A4, holiness vs worth.** This is Kant's distinction between the worth of a character and the dignity of humanity, and it matches DI 7. *Held.*
- **A6, "the Church holds the line at every number … not a shared one."** Accurate. Kant also holds it at every number, so the claim is shared by one rival; crediting him is optional. *Held.*
- **W2, reach.** My answer to "reach" is apriority. A law of rational will as such reaches every possible rational will analytically. W2 targets the naturalized answers, and the Kant row correctly says what returns against me: how free wills arise in nature, which I called incomprehensible. *Held (parity-plus).*
- **W2, "one can always refuse to do evil" (VS 52, 102).** That is my "ought implies can" and my claim that duties do not truly collide (6:224). *Held.*
- **Evil, "never the loss for its own sake … governs each person for her own sake" (ST I q.19 a.9; SCG III.112).** This answers the worry from the Formula of Humanity: the harm is not intended "either as an end or as a means." *Held.*
- **Evil, "why not begin with heaven."** The book says love must first be freely given before it is confirmed. I would say holiness belongs to no creature and lies only in endless progress (CPrR 5:122). Both views are coherent and they sit at parity. *Held.*

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**As written: NONE.**

No Fatal or Serious objection from this school stands against v4 as written. v4 took all seven of the Kantian's Round 3 repairs, several of them verbatim. The new text survives the Kantian's strongest new attacks at least to parity:
- the law of pardon turned against "whole" forgiveness;
- heteronomy and the Abraham problem against life held in trust;
- the tu quoque against the image of God;
- pseudo-service against "an offense against Him";
- the ransom against A3.

**Minor (wording; the arguments stand without them):**
1. **Kant row, rule of pardon.** Kant's 6:337 concerns clemency, the remission of punishment, not forgiveness. "The very shape of W1(1)" should say how the shape holds under v4's whole forgiveness (Exchange 1).
2. **Kant row, postulate.** Name Kant's own reply, that he held the thought as rational faith and not as evidence, so the row states him as his defenders would (Exchange 7).
3. **W1 frame, "owed to her first."** This sits beside CCC 1440's "before all else … against Him." Remove "first" or mark which order is meant (Exchange 2).
4. **W1 frame, "because the child is His," and W1 closing, "Him whose she is."** These are bare possessives that reopen the ownership reading Round 3 closed. And "what it does … to Him" invites the impassibility and pseudo-service charge. SCG III.122 answers both (Exchange 3).
5. **W1(2), "That is why the line has no exceptions."** Without "for any of us," it invites the Abraham objection (Exchange 4).
6. **W1(2) and the God row, misstatement of Kant.** "Not even her own to cross" is listed among what the best rivals leave out, but it is Kant's own doctrine. W1(2) is silent on Kant's answer to why the line has that shape (Exchange 5).

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**
- **Postulate vs ground.** Kant treats a Judge who knows the heart as a necessary idea that yields rational faith. The book treats it as a clue in a converging case.
- **Where necessity lives.** For Kant, validity needs no "where"; the book says the law holds first in a Mind.
- **Self-standing dignity vs dignity as gift.** Kant grounds it in an end in itself, worth without equivalent. The book grounds it in image, call and life held in trust. Kant's ground has no Abraham problem. The book's ground explains why there is a *she* at all.
- **Form vs end.** Kant reads any grounding in the Good as heteronomy; Aquinas reads the first precept as categorical.
- **The noumenal creditor vs the kept person.** Kant's own postulate of immortality leans toward the book.
- **Duties to God.** Kant puts them outside ethics (6:443–444); the book holds that every wrong is before all else an offense against Him (CCC 1440). This is the same critical limit again.
- **Holiness.** For Kant it is endless progress; for the book, love freely given and then confirmed.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**1–2. C, Kant row: replace** "But where guilt and goodness are inward, the philosopher of autonomy found that he had to think of Someone; and his own rule of pardon, that a ruler may forgive only what was done against himself and never what one subject did to another, is the very shape of W1(1)." **with:**
> But where guilt and goodness are inward, the philosopher of autonomy found that he had to think of Someone. He held that thought as rational faith, not knowledge, and would not count it as evidence; this book counts it as a clue (CCC 35), and there we part. His own rule of clemency, that a ruler may remit the punishment only of what was done against himself and never of what one subject did to another (*Doctrine of Right* 6:337), has the shape of W1(1): every wrong is before all else an offense against God, and that He forgives wholly; the pardon that is hers, He does not give in her place.

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **Aquinas answers Kant's charge of pseudo-service in one clause.** We offend God only by acting against our own good (SCG III.122). The offense to God is not a harm to Him; it is the wrong done to the one He loves, seen from His side. This joins CCC 1440 ("before all else") to the book's Euthyphro answer ("not wrong merely because it is forbidden") and to impassibility, and it shuts out any reading in which morality does God a service.
- **Kant's rule of clemency fits v4 better than it fit v3.** Kant lets the sovereign pardon crimes against his own majesty. On the Church's view every wrong is also against the Sovereign. So Kant's rule *permits* God to forgive the whole offense against Himself and *forbids* Him to pardon in her name, which is exactly v4's structure. Say "clemency" and "remit," not "forgive."
- **Kant is an ally on W1(2)'s "not hers to throw away."** The first example of the categorical imperative is the duty not to take one's own life (*Groundwork* 4:421–422). In the Collins lectures Kant says suicide is not wrong because God forbids it; God forbids it because it is wrong (paraphrase; verify 27:375). That is the book's own anti-voluntarist line, and it answers the Abraham worry from the believer's side.
- **Kant's infanticide passage (*Doctrine of Right* 6:336) is an honest witness for A4 and the Conclusion, if used symmetrically.** Kant, the philosopher of dignity, said the commonwealth could look past the killing of an infant born outside marriage, as though she had slipped in like contraband (paraphrase; verify). His own principle condemns his application, just as Christian principle condemned the slavery Christians were slow to see (A4). It shows how easily the infant's worth is lost even to the clearest mind. Under the fairness rule it may be used only beside "Christians too were slow," never as a genealogy against Kant's ground.
- **The image needs the soul as form.** Against the tu quoque, the book's advantage over Kant on the newborn is not "image" alone. It is that the rational soul is the form of her body from the beginning (CCC 365), so her nature is not inferred from what she can do. W1(2) may say so in a clause if the chapter version needs it.
- **To verify before use (all paraphrased here):** *Groundwork* 4:421–422, 4:429, 4:434–435, 4:459; *Doctrine of Right* 6:237, 6:330, 6:333, 6:336, 6:337, §28, §35; *Doctrine of Virtue* 6:422–423, 6:443–444, 6:460–461, 6:463; *Religion* 6:72, 6:98–99, 6:167ff; *Conflict of the Faculties* 7:63; Collins 27:375, 27:386; CPrR 5:122; SCG III.112, III.122; ST I q.93 a.2, a.4; I-II q.94 a.5 ad 2; q.100 a.8 ad 3; III q.48 a.2; CCC 365, 2258.

- "Only He can take a life without spending her, because only He can give it back."
- "Her worth has no price; the question is why there is a *she* whose worth has none."
- "He forgives wholly what was done against Him; the pardon that is hers, He does not give in her place."

## 8. Score

**9/10 as written (9.5 with the Minor repairs in §6).** v4 took every Kantian repair from Round 3, and its new grounds (image and call, life held in trust, the Church's full teaching on forgiveness) hold against Kant's strongest new attacks at least to parity. What remains is wording: one misstatement of Kant in the God row, two bare possessives, and a law of pardon that needs "clemency" and one clause on how it fits.
