# Argument Lab, Round 4: Utilitarianism / Consequentialism vs. v4

## 1. School and voice

The opponent speaks for realist, impartialist consequentialism at full strength: Sidgwick's axioms of impartiality and benevolence, Hare's two levels, Parfit's Triple Theory and non-metaphysical realism, de Lazari-Radek and Singer's "point of view of the universe," and Railton's sophisticated consequentialism. Neighbours are called in where they help: Hooker's rule consequentialism, threshold deontology, and Scheffler's "paradox of deontology." The constraints-vs-totals standoff is settled at parity. This round tests whether v4's new sentences about this school are exact, and whether the Round 3 repairs (several in my own wording) broke anything.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges (short)

1. **Justice:** each counts for one (Sidgwick's impartiality); justice is the rules that protect the most vital interests. **Coherent, borrows nothing; breaks** where "owed" comes second to "best."
2. **Hard case:** the hijacked airliner over a stadium: shoot it down, since the passengers die either way. **Holds as a decision.** Catholic moralists divide on it, so it is a parity case. The school pays at the secret transplant.
3. **Equal dignity:** equal *consideration*, yes. Equal *worth* whatever one's capacities, no (Singer's replaceability). **Breaks at the ground, honestly.** W1(2) now answers it directly ("not for the species, not for the total, not for us").
4. **Ultimate good:** well-being seen impartially. **Coherent; thin at the summit.** Impartial reason can see that each one counts; only a person can want each one (W3; Railton's counterfactual condition).

The new attacks follow.

**Exchange 1: "No account that counts only totals can say why the line is there at all" (A6).**
*Utilitarian:* That is false of my school's two most developed theories. Hare's *Moral Thinking* exists to say why the line is there. At the intuitive level we should hold firm, near-inviolable rules against killing the innocent, because creatures like us cannot be trusted to calculate in the moment, and people who hold the rule do far better than people who calculate. Hooker's rule consequentialism (*Ideal Code, Real World*) says the same at the level of codes. The best code forbids killing the innocent and adds a rule to prevent disaster. Your own datum, "those who would cross it for a city will not cross it for five," is not evidence against us. It is what both theories *predict*: the rule holds in ordinary cases, and critical thinking or the disaster clause takes over at catastrophe. Your C introduction promises to state each rival "as its best defenders state it." This sentence states us as our worst defenders would.
*Defense:* The sentence means something narrower. What totals cannot explain is why the line is *true* in the very case A6 built: "even if no one would ever know." Hare's critical level says the secret surgeon would act *rightly*. It explains only why we should believe otherwise. Hooker says the act is wrong, but only because a code forbidding it would do best if most people accepted it. Then the wrong depends on what would happen if others held a rule, not on what is done to *her*. That is A2's point again: wronging is not worsening, even worsening at the level of codes. And neither theory holds the line at every number.
*Rejoinder:* All true, and none of it is what the sentence says. "Cannot say why the line is there at all" is a universal negative, and Hare and Hooker are counterexamples. Your real claim, that totals can say why the rule is *useful* but not why crossing it would wrong *her*, is fair. Then the matter is the Round 2 standoff, "true or only useful," and the book holds it at parity.
*Result:* **Failed as written; held with repair. Serious.** The clause misstates this school, in the item the book uses to turn the catastrophe concession into an argument, and against the C introduction's own fairness promise. The repair is one sentence (§6), and it makes the case sharper: it moves the dispute onto A2, where the book is strongest.

**Exchange 2: "Not even his own consent could make it right," under "Almost everyone sees these at once" (A6).**
*Utilitarian:* The censor's Round 3 fix (CCC 2295) is right as Catholic doctrine. But v4 placed it *before* "Almost everyone sees these at once," so a contested Church claim again sits inside the shared core. This is the exact fault the lab fixed for the numbers. It is not shared. The Nuremberg Code itself, the document you hold up as reflection moving toward an absolute, says no experiment should be run where death or disabling injury is expected, "except, perhaps," where the physicians themselves are the subjects. The post-war secular consensus forbade lethal experiments on the unwilling and left room for the willing. Most reflective people, and every liberal from Mill's harm principle onward, would call a competent volunteer who gives his life to test a vaccine for a million children a hero. A6 itself opens with "real good for another always asks something of the giver … sometimes far more." Your firefighter gives his life for one child and you praise him. My volunteer gives his life for a million, by the same consent, and you call it wrong and say almost everyone sees that at once.
*Defense:* The Church draws a real line here. A man may lay down his life (John 15:13): the firefighter accepts a death he foresees as the price of a rescue, and he does not aim at it. The subject of a lethal experiment is handed over to be destroyed as the means of the result. His own consent cannot make that right, because his life is not his to throw away (W1(2); CCC 2280–2282). Pius XII taught the same limit on what a patient's consent can authorize (1952).
*Rejoinder:* That is a good argument, and it belongs in W1(2), where the book argues the line held at every number. It is not a datum. As written, A6 tells the reader that almost everyone already sees what only the Church and a few others hold. It also leaves the tension with self-gift unanswered in the very item that praises self-gift.
*Result:* **Failed as written; held with repair. Serious.** Move the consent clause after the honesty sentence, mark it as the Church's claim, and state the distinction: a man may lay down his life, but he may not hand it over to be destroyed as a means (§6). Doctrine is not at issue; the fault is in what the text claims everyone sees.

**Exchange 3: The trust ground and Scheffler's paradox of deontology (W1(2)).**
*Utilitarian:* Every deontology faces the paradox. If each person's inviolability matters so much, why not *minimize* violations? Kill one to stop five others from being killed. God willed each of the five for themselves too. So "willed for itself" seems to give you a goal, and a goal is something to be promoted.
*Defense:* W1(2) does not ground the line on a value to be promoted. It grounds it on jurisdiction: "her life is not ours to spend … it is held in trust from the One who alone is Lord of life (CCC 2258, 2280) … No one may take what is not his to take, even to keep others from taking more." A trustee who may not spend what is not his does not gain that right because others are spending theirs wrongly. The constraint is on what *I* may do, because I lack the authority. It is not a quantity of inviolability that I am charged to maximize. That is why it is agent-relative, and why it holds even against the minimizing argument.
*Rejoinder:* Then everything rests on the claim that a Lord of life exists, and that He has withheld this authority from us.
*Defense:* Yes, and that is W's structure: an explanation offered, not a premise taken from the reader. What the secular deontologist must take as brute (Foot, Kamm on inviolability), the trust explains.
*Result:* **Held, strongly.** This is the best answer in the whole case to this school's oldest structural objection. The sentence "No one may take what is not his to take, even to keep others from taking more" *is* the answer to Scheffler's paradox, and the book should know that it is.

**Exchange 4: Trust gives way to necessity, in your own Aquinas.**
*Utilitarian:* Jurisdiction gives way to necessity. Aquinas holds that in urgent need it is not theft to take another's goods, because in need all things become common (ST II-II q.66 a.7). Your Catechism agrees (CCC 2408). If trust over property bends to urgent necessity, why does trust over life not bend to a city's?
*Defense:* Because of what each is held in trust *for*. Material goods are held for the needs of the whole human family (the universal destination of goods, CCC 2402–2403). Urgent need shows what they were always for, so in that moment they become "his to take." A person is not held for anyone's need. She is the one for whom things exist, willed "for itself" (GS 24): "not for the species, not for the total, not for us," as W1(2) already says. Necessity can redistribute things. It cannot redistribute persons.
*Result:* **Held as written.** The answer is already in the paragraph. One optional sentence would close the rejoinder before a reader thinks of it (§6, optional).

**Exchange 5: The A heading's new debunking clause, turned on A6 (de Lazari-Radek and Singer).**
*Utilitarian:* v4's A heading adopts our epistemic test: trust what survives reflection "until something … shows that we would believe it whether or not it were true." De Lazari-Radek and Singer apply exactly that test (Singer 2005; *The Point of View of the Universe* ch. 7, building on Greene). The aversion to killing one for five is just what evolution would build into a social primate, so it is debunked. Universal benevolence is not what selection would favour, so it survives. By your own heading, A6 falls and our axiom stands.
*Defense:* Three replies, all within v4's text. (1) A6's cases are chosen to remove the evolutionary story. The city hangs the man *secretly*, by committee, with no personal force. The surgeon acts "even if no one would ever know," with no reputation at stake. (2) Benevolence is not immune either: kin altruism and reciprocity explain plenty of concern for others (Kahane 2011), and the debunking, pressed hard, reaches A7 too (W2). (3) Most tellingly, apply the test to the *Church's* line. No selection pressure rewards a group that lets a city die rather than kill one stranger. What evolution would build is the threshold pattern: hold for five, cross for a city. So the test debunks the shared threshold and spares the line held at every number, much as it spares universal benevolence.
*Rejoinder:* Then the only survivor of the debunking is a view almost no one holds.
*Defense:* As A6 now honestly says. Few is not none, and it is not false.
*Result:* **Held at parity**, and the book gains from it: the debunker's own tool favours the book's claim over the shared threshold. This is the "Moorean trust vs. the undercutter" standoff, and it is not a defect.

**Exchange 6: A2's new contrast case vs. the success theory.**
*Utilitarian:* On a success theory of well-being (Parfit, Hare), the betrayal frustrates a second desire that the blown letter does not: her desire that *her friend* keep faith. So the betrayal does make her life go worse than the letter does, and the contrast does not separate wronging from worsening.
*Defense:* Her desire "that you keep faith with me" is a desire not to be wronged. If its frustration is the harm, the wrong comes before the harm and is not made of it. And suppose she cared only that the secret stay hidden, not who kept it. The betrayal still wrongs her, and the two cases now frustrate her equally.
*Result:* **Held; Minor.** One word, "her wish *that it stay hidden*," makes the contrast exact against the success theory.

**Exchange 7: Whole over part in Aquinas vs. the Stoic sentence and "never spends anyone."**
*Utilitarian:* You charge the Stoic Providence with caring "for the whole" and spending "the part for it." Aquinas says a universal provider allows some defect in particular things lest the good of the whole be hindered (ST I q.22 a.2 ad 2; q.48 a.2 ad 3). Chrysippus used the same defense you now use, that evils come "by concomitance" (*kata parakolouthēsin*, in Aulus Gellius 7.1). The fairness rule forbids you to fault the Stoics for what your own Doctor says of God.
*Defense:* The difference is persons. Aquinas holds that rational creatures are governed for their own sake, and other things for the species (SCG III.112, which v4 cites for exactly this). The Stoic sage accepts his lot as a part assigned for the whole, as Epictetus's foot accepts the mud for the sake of the body. For non-rational creatures the book concedes the whole-over-part weighing openly ("for the fawn that hope is thinner").
*Result:* **Held.** The Stoic sentence is fair as it stands, because it contrasts the two on persons. The concession about the fawn keeps the fairness rule.

**Exchange 8: The moral-hazard objection (Hasker's form).**
*Utilitarian:* If God permits no evil without bringing a good from it (CCC 324), then preventing an evil forfeits that good. Your theodicy, taken seriously, weakens the duty to pull the child from the fire.
*Defense:* v4 answers this in the text: "The hands that pull a child from the fire are part of what He made real, so His permission never excuses ours." CCC 324 promises *a* good from that evil, not a greater total than the world would have had without it. Our duty is set by what we are for, not by what Providence can bring out of our failure.
*Result:* **Held as written.**

**Exchange 9: The Conclusion's appeal to "the one who gives up A4."**
*Utilitarian:* The Conclusion is addressed to Singer, and it asks whether it was wrong to leave the infant "because she was weak," then concludes that "her worth never followed her strength, and it is no less than yours." Singer's criterion was never strength. It is self-awareness and a sense of one's own future, which no newborn has, the strong ones included. He can grant that leaving her for weakness was wrong (and leaving her to suffer is wrong on any view) and still deny that her worth equals yours. So the inference misses the dissenter it is aimed at.
*Defense:* The Conclusion asks for a seeing and says itself that it "cannot compel." Still, the question should name what the dissenter actually thinks she lacks.
*Result:* **Held with repair. Minor.** Add "or her mind" (§6). Then the seeing is aimed at Singer's real criterion, and the question bites.

**Exchange 10 (brief): The second road and the success theory (W1(1)).**
*Utilitarian:* "What the living pay, no one receives." On Pitcher's and Feinberg's view, the antemortem person *is* benefited when her wishes are honoured. She receives it, as she was.
*Defense:* She is benefited as she was, but no one is there now to receive it, and no one is there to release what cannot be repaid. The forgiveness point carries the weight, and the success theory does not touch it.
*Result:* **Held; Minor** wording ("no one is there to receive").

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**As written: TWO SERIOUS. No Fatal.** Both are in A6, and each closes with one sentence.

**S1. Serious: A6 presents "not even his own consent could make it right" as something "almost everyone sees at once."** It does not belong to the shared core. The Nuremberg Code itself left an exception ("except, perhaps") for physicians who serve as their own subjects. Liberal reflection generally treats a competent volunteer who gives his life for many as a hero. A6's own opening ("sometimes far more") and the book's praise of self-gift unto death make the clause read as inconsistent, because the distinction that resolves the tension (one may lay down one's life, but may not hand it over to be destroyed as a means) is not in the text. This is the same fault the lab fixed for the numbers in Round 3. It was brought back by placing the censor's correct doctrinal fix (CCC 2295) on the wrong side of "Almost everyone sees these."

**S2. Serious: "No account that counts only totals can say why the line is there at all" (A6) misstates this school.** Hare's two-level theory and Hooker's rule consequentialism both say why the line is there, and both predict the threshold pattern A6 cites. What they cannot say is why crossing it, where no one would ever know, would be a wrong done to *her* and not merely a breach of a useful rule, and neither holds the line at every number. The book has that answer (A2, W1(2)), but the sentence does not say it. It breaks the C introduction's promise to state each rival as its best defenders state it. The wording was this school's own Round 3 repair. It overreached, and I correct it here.

**Minor**
1. A6: "The absolute can cost lives" → "can cost lives, even many"; cite *VS* 52 beside 80.
2. A4: add "(A6)" after "deliberately killed," so the reach of "never" is sent to its honest statement.
3. A heading: Singer denies "parts of A2, A4, A6 and A7," not only A4 and A6, as v4's own C row implies.
4. A2: "her wish *that it stay hidden* would be just as frustrated" (exact against success theorists).
5. W1(1): "what the living pay, no one *is there to* receive" (fair to Pitcher and Feinberg).
6. C, utilitarianism: restore de Lazari-Radek and Singer; credit two-level and rule theories (to match the S2 repair).
7. C, godless realism: one clause for Parfit's view that necessity needs no dwelling.
8. Conclusion: "her strength *or her mind*" (aim the seeing at Singer's real criterion).

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Whether constraints are true or only useful (Hare's two levels; Hooker): the book now rests this on A2 and on jurisdiction, and the dispute is evenly matched.
- The survival lottery: safety as a status vs. safety as odds.
- The hijacked airliner and the vital conflict: Catholic moralists themselves divide.
- Evolutionary debunking of constraints (Singer; Greene): evenly matched, and the test spares the line held at every number more readily than the shared threshold (Exchange 5).
- Where the expanding circle leads (animals; CCC 2417–2418).
- Whether necessity needs a dwelling (Parfit vs. ST I q.15).
- The Old Testament "dark passages" and Aquinas's Lord of life (Exodus 12; ST I-II q.94 a.5 ad 2): the trust ground explains why God alone may take a life, and the chapter need not settle the texts.
- Hell as weight; the fawn and the parasite: named, not explained away.
- The coherent dissenter who gives up A4: no argument compels him, and the book says so.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**A6: replace from "And some things must never be done:" to the end with:**
> And some things must never be done: not to save ourselves, and not to buy a better outcome for many. A surgeon may not kill one healthy patient to give his organs to five, even if no one would ever know; a city may not secretly hang an innocent man to calm a riot; no prisoner may be experimented on to death, however many would be saved. Almost everyone sees these at once. Fewer hold the line when the numbers grow vast, and many who would never hang the innocent man would bomb a city to end a war. Fewer still think that a man's own consent could never make such an experiment right, since he may lay down his life for others, as a firefighter does. The Church holds the line at every number, and even against his own consent (*Veritatis Splendor* 52, 80; CCC 2295): a man may lay down his life, but he may not hand it over to be destroyed as a means, and no one may destroy it so. That is this book's claim, argued in W1(2), not a shared one. Yet even those who would cross the line for a city will not cross it for five. The best accounts that count only totals can say why it is good that we hold the line so firmly, and why a rule against crossing it does more good than a rule that allows it; they cannot say why crossing it would be a wrong done to *her*, and not only the breaking of a useful rule, and none of them holds it at every number. Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent. The absolute can cost lives, even many. Without it, no innocent is safe from being spent. One may never do evil so that good may come of it (CCC 1789).

## 7. New insights for the book

- **The trust ground answers the paradox of deontology.** "No one may take what is not his to take, even to keep others from taking more" explains why the line is agent-relative and does not invite the minimizing argument. The constraint comes from lacking authority, not from a value to be maximized. Secular deontology has to take that as brute (Kamm). W1(2) explains it.
- **Things are held for everyone's need; persons are not.** The Church's own teaching on necessity (CCC 2408) explains why trust over goods bends and trust over life does not: necessity can redistribute things, never persons. GS 24's "willed for itself" is what makes the difference.
- **The debunker's test spares the Church's line.** No selection pressure rewards a group that lets a city die rather than kill one stranger. Evolution would build the threshold pattern. So the test de Lazari-Radek and Singer use to save universal benevolence also spares the line held at every number, and it debunks the shared threshold.
- **Self-gift vs. being destroyed as a means.** The firefighter and the lethal-experiment volunteer mark the distinction that makes A6, Chapter 9 and CCC 2295 one teaching: a man may lay down his life, but he may not hand it over to be destroyed.
- **"Given, not taken" was better than my "no one may be given away."** The Father gave the Son (John 3:16; Romans 8:32). v4's line keeps the Cross whole; mine would not have.
- **Move the quarrel with rule theories onto A2.** Rule theories explain why the line is useful. Only A2 and W1(2) explain why crossing it wrongs *her*. That is where the book is strongest, so that is where the quarrel should be held.
