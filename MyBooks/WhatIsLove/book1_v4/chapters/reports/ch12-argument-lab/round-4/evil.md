## 1. School and voice

The staunch "a good God would never allow this" atheist: Mackie (the logical problem, and "why not begin with heaven?"), Rowe (the fawn; the policy question), Draper (indifference: pain falls where survival puts it), Schellenberg (non-resistant non-belief), Maitzen (theodicy against ordinary morality), Law (the evil god), Adams (hell as a horror never defeated), and Ivan Karamazov's refusal. In Round 4 he checks whether v4 took his repairs. Then he turns v4's own new sentences against each other: the infant in the Dhamma row against "heaven first," the lion in Aquinas against "not as a means," and karma's built-in justice against our world.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

1. **Justice.** Brute badness of agony (Rowe), invention (Mackie), or ordinary intuition (Maitzen). *Holds as a position; Rowe's moral force borrows A1.*
2. **Hard case.** The newborn in pain that cannot be relieved: many in the school would end her life. *Coherent; it shares a premise the book denies (A4, A6), so the God of A4 is not faulted for refusing to "solve" sufferers by ending them.*
3. **Equal dignity.** Invented, brute, or interests that come in degrees. *Breaks or stops at a brute fact; the argument from evil weighs most on our premise (A4).*
4. **Flourishing.** Love, knowledge and relief of suffering, inside mortality. *Coherent and bounded; on his view history's victims are never answered, which v4 rightly declines to count as evidence (A7).*

### Exchange 1. Mackie: "heaven first" is answered by a necessity that v4's own infant refutes

*Attack:* In the weight paragraph v4 says free will "answers why God did not simply begin with heaven, where the saints are free and cannot sin: love must first be freely given before it can be confirmed forever." In the hell sentence it says: "a love that could never be refused would not be a gift (Chapter 4)." Now read your own Dhamma row: "the Catholic says the highest good is a gift, which God can give to the infant and to the one who will never think (CCC 1250, 1261)." And CCC 1250 speaks of "the sheer gratuitousness of the grace of salvation" in infant Baptism. So on your own account God brings some human persons to the highest good, confirmed forever, who never first gave their love freely and never could have refused it: the baptized child who dies before she can choose, and the one who will never think. Then "must" is false. God *can* begin a person very near heaven. My question returns at full strength: why not do for all what you say He does for her?

*Reply:* The blessed child's love in glory is free. The freedom of the saints is freedom made perfect, not freedom removed. So she is not loved by force.

*Rejoinder:* Granted, and that is my point. If love can be free and confirmed without first having been refusable, then refusability is not what makes love free or a gift. Your two sentences say it is. Your own next clause ("the saints are free and cannot sin") already separates the two, and your infant settles it. As written, your answer to the logical problem is a necessity claim that your own text denies.

*Second reply:* Then the answer must be a good, not a necessity. God can give beatitude as pure gift, and does. He also willed creatures who give their love back of their own accord, in a life where it could be refused, before it is confirmed. That is a further good: the dignity of giving, not only of receiving. It is the same dignity CCC 306 names for being causes. Aquinas holds that even the angels reached beatitude by their own act, though God could have made them blessed from the start (ST I q.62 a.5, to verify).

*Final rejoinder (Mackie):* Then you have turned the logical problem into the evidential one: is that good worth this world's cost?

*Final reply:* Yes, and that is where it belongs. The evil section already says that question is weighed, not settled.

**Failed as written; Held with repair.** As written, both sentences rest on "a love that could never be refused would not be a gift." v4's own Dhamma row and CCC 1250 contradict that, so the answer to Mackie does not stand. With the repair (Section 6, R1) the answer becomes a greater-good claim. That claim is answerable at parity, and the question of proportion is an owned standoff. **Serious** (Section 5).

### Exchange 2. Adams: hell, "real and eternal," and a reason that reaches only the possibility

*Attack:* v4 now says plainly that "hell is real and eternal (CCC 1035)." That is honest. It also says, "The Church sees in this the seriousness of freedom: a love that could never be refused would not be a gift." Grant the premise (Exchange 1 shows you should not). It still shows only that refusal must be *possible*. What you weigh is that refusal is *eternal*. Talbott and Hart ask why an offer that can be refused today cannot be accepted after ten thousand years. Your reason does not reach that question. And v4 adds a sharper edge in W1(2): dignity "cannot be lost, not even by her own refusal (DI 15)." Then a being of infinite worth is kept forever in final loss. That is my horror at its height.

*Reply:* The Church does not rest the finality on the freedom argument. She teaches that the refusal becomes final when it is held to the end: to die in mortal sin without repenting means remaining separated from Him for ever by our own free choice (CCC 1033). That requires full knowledge and deliberate consent (CCC 1857). And the angels' sin cannot be forgiven because their choice was irrevocable, not because of any defect in God's mercy (CCC 393, paraphrased). Why death fixes the will is something the Church teaches, not something this case shows. v4 already puts hell on the weight side, and CCC 1735 and 1860 are now in the sentence. On DI 15: that she keeps her dignity even in refusal is why her loss is a loss and not a deletion. It makes the weight heavier. It is not a contradiction.

*Rejoinder:* So the finality rests on revelation, and the weight stays.

*Final reply:* Yes. The text should say that, and stop offering a reason that reaches only half of the claim.

**Held with repair (Minor).** It is bound up with Exchange 1's repair. Replace the freedom clause with the Church's actual ground, and say that the finality is taught, not shown (R2). Adams against the Church on final self-exclusion remains an honest standoff.

### Exchange 3. Draper and the parasite: "not as a means" meets Aquinas's lion

*Attack:* v4's new sentence is the most honest in the section: "the parasite lives by the child; if our nature is a wisdom and not an accident (W2), so is theirs." I credit it. But set it beside the sentence that follows: "the harms that follow in a world of real causes God does not intend, either as an end or as a means." Your own Aquinas says otherwise for nature. Providence permits defects in particular things so the good of the whole is not hindered, and a lion would not live without the slaying of other animals (ST I q.22 a.2 ad 2). The fawn's death is the very means of the wolf's life, and that life is part of the order God wills. And the parasite brings this to persons. A creature whose nature you call a wisdom reaches its good *by means of* the child's loss. Your Stoic row criticizes a Providence that "cares for the whole and spends the part for it." Thomist providence does exactly that for animals.

*Reply:* Two answers, by scope. (a) For animals, granted. Ordering one creature's life to another's and to the whole is what Aquinas teaches, and the Church lets us use animals within limits (CCC 2417). That is not the trade A6 forbids, because A6 concerns persons. So v4's "not as a means" should not be stated of natural harms in general. The phrase was this school's own Round 3 wording, and it overclaimed. The Stoic row stays fair, because its point is that the Stoic whole spends *persons* and does not keep the dead. (b) For persons, the claim holds, and v4 already gives its ground: within the order, God "governs each person for her own sake" (SCG III.112). The parasite takes from the child; God does not give her to it. He wills each nature and a world in which real causes meet and can harm (CCC 306, 310). Her loss is neither His end nor His means; what He wills for her is her own good.

*Rejoinder (Draper):* "God made the creature whose good is to feed on her, but did not give her to it." That is a distinction no one would accept from a human designer.

*Final reply:* A human designer cannot raise the child, heal her, and answer for her (Round 3, Exchange 4: the parent test is passed by defeat, not by permission). Whether that is enough is the weight, and v4 now puts the parasite on the scale in so many words.

**Held with repair (Minor).** Scope "not as a means" to persons, say plainly that for animals the order does give one creature's loss to another's life (Aquinas's lion), and say that the parasite takes and God does not give (R3). The parasite, like Darwin's wasp, is Draper's best datum and remains an owned standoff.

### Exchange 4. Draper turns the Dhamma row: built-in justice, and the rival v4 forgot

*Attack:* (a) Against the Buddhist, v4 argues: "a world whose bodies and fortunes follow the moral quality of each heart ... is a world with justice built into it, and justice built into a world looks like the work of a mind." Accept it. Evidence works both ways: if a world that tracks justice is evidence for a just Mind, a world that does not track it is evidence against one. Your evil section says ours does not: pain "falls roughly where survival would put it, not where justice would." So the Dhamma row hands me my premise and does not say so. (b) v4 says "one rival bears this weight more easily than we do," and names the Buddhist. The rival who bears it most easily is mine. An indifferent world with no one behind it predicts pain exactly where survival puts it. That is the whole of Draper's argument, and v4 leaves it out where it counts the rivals' advantages.

*Reply:* (a) Granted, and it costs nothing that v4 has not already paid. The Church does not say that the present world distributes by desert. Christ refused to read disaster as desert (Luke 13:4; John 9:3), and justice is completed at the judgment, not in the share-out of pain. So the Catholic expects the present pattern less than karma does, and v4 already says "the pattern of pain in nature still weighs." The two sentences should name each other. (b) Granted. v4's first reason says indifference "is answered only by the whole case." It should also be counted where v4 lists the rivals who bear this weight more easily. That is a fair misstatement by omission of this school.

**Held with repair (Minor).** Name the indifferent world beside the Buddhist, and tie the Dhamma row's inference to the owned weight (R4). Draper's comparative likelihood is the standoff it has been since Round 2.

### Exchange 5. Ivan and Caiaphas: the Cross paragraph

*Attack:* v4 replaced "No one is the price" with "God never spends anyone as a mere means against her own good," and then the Cross: a ransom for many (Mark 10:45). Read A6: "a city may not secretly hang an innocent man to calm a riot." That is Caiaphas, word for word in substance: better that one man die for the people (John 11:50). Your God let it happen and called it redemption. And "a *mere* means" concedes that the child is a means, only not a mere one. My Ivan does not want her tears to buy anyone's harmony, even if she is paid back.

*Reply:* (a) Caiaphas is the best text v4 could have. A6 says the city may not do it, and Scripture agrees: those who reasoned so were guilty (Acts 2:23). The Cross does not justify Caiaphas. It is the Church's own example of CCC 312: the greatest moral evil ever done, the rejection and murder of God's only Son, from which God brought the greatest good, and the evil did not become good. So the Cross belongs to *what He brings*, not to *why He permits*, and v4 places it there. (b) What separates Christ from the child is the giving: He laid down His life of His own accord (John 10:18), and the Father raised Him. "What Christ gave was given, not taken, and it was not lost." (c) On "mere": the sentence's force is "against her own good." God never acts against her good, and her suffering is not the price of the harmony. The reason He permits is a world of real causes. What He brings is an answer, not a purchase (CCC 312: the evil never becomes good).

*Rejoinder (Ivan):* I still return the ticket.

*Final reply:* The book honors that. It is love's protest, and it attacks no premise.

**Held.** The Cross paragraph keeps the two answers apart and answers the censor without reopening Maitzen's dilemma. Insight (Section 7): A6's riot case is Caiaphas's reasoning, and the Cross is where God answers it.

### Exchange 6. Schellenberg: "comes late" understates the datum

*Attack:* v3 said the conscious knowing comes "late, or only at the end." For the censor, v4 cut "or only at the end." Now the cost reads "for many honest seekers the conscious knowing comes late." The next sentence is "This life is where the choice is made (CCC 1021)." Read together, this says the knowing comes late but within this life. Your own paragraph opens with the counterexample: Buddhist contemplatives who "looked inward for a lifetime" and found no Person. My argument rests on lifelong non-resistant non-belief. v4 states the cost as smaller than it is, in the very paragraph that claims to own it.

*Reply:* Granted as wording. v4 owns the full datum in the weight paragraph ("Honest seekers look for God and do not find Him"), so the version does not hide it. But W3 should state it at full size. The Catholic answer then becomes clearer, not weaker. The choice made in this life can be made without the name: by following the light one has, by grace (LG 16), and the Spirit offers every person a share in the Paschal Mystery in ways known to God (GS 22).

*Rejoinder:* An offer "in ways known to God" is not the conscious relationship a loving parent gives.

*Final reply:* No. That is why v4 says "not a full answer to why Love would choose this way," and calls the cost a summons to mission.

**Held with repair (Minor).** Restore the full cost without suggesting a chance after death, and join it to CCC 1021 with "yet" (R5). This was a misstatement of this school by understatement. Schellenberg's standoff is unchanged.

### Exchange 7. Rowe's friendly atheism: the honest reader dropped from the conclusion

*Attack:* In Round 3 I logged the conclusion as Held because it said, "An honest reader may weigh it differently, and should still weigh it." v4 drops that sentence and adds: "This book offers the case for weighing, not because certainty is out of reach, but because each reader must reach it with his own reason, and many of us find the way hard (CCC 37)." CCC 37 speaks of minds that persuade themselves that what they would not like to be true is false. So the reader who weighs evil more heavily than you now reads as someone who has not yet done what is in reach. Yet the decisive step, whether evil overturns the case, is by your own words "this book's judgment."

*Reply:* (a) The certainty sentence is the Church's teaching, and the censor rightly asked for it: arguments of this kind *can* bring a person to certainty (CCC 31), and God *can* be known with certainty by reason (CCC 36; *Dei Filius*). "Can" is not "will, for every honest weigher." (b) A certain judgment can come from a weighing. Newman's account of certitude is exactly this: many probabilities converging in one mind, none of them compelling every mind. (c) Nothing in Catholic teaching forbids the dropped sentence. The Church does not presume that the honest seeker is at fault (CCC 847; LG 16), and v4 says so in W3. The sentence should come back.

*Rejoinder:* With it restored, I accept the certainty claim as the Church's claim, which I deny. That is a standoff, not a fault.

**Held with repair (Minor).** Restore the honest-reader sentence with CCC 847, and add Newman's one clause so that "certainty" and "this book's judgment" do not seem to pull apart (R6).

### Exchange 8. "He did not keep her from what was done to her"

*Attack:* (a) I asked for this sentence, and now I test it. The Psalms say "The Lord will keep you from all evil" (Psalm 121:7). You now concede He did not, and then say "What He keeps is her claim, and her." Is the second keeping a consolation that hides the first failure? (b) v4 adds that Christ's look "burns away what is false so that the guilty too can be healed" (*Spe Salvi* 47). Ivan: I do not want the torturer healed at the price of her embrace.

*Reply:* (a) The sentence does what Round 3 asked. It concedes the failure in plain words, and it sends the accusation to the scale, where the book weighs it ("that weight is on the other side of the scale, and this book weighs it there"). It does not offer the later keeping as an answer to the earlier one. It offers it only as justice: what was done to her is not the last word. That is honest. (b) v4 does not make her embrace the price of his healing. The pardon "is hers to give," and God does not give it in her place. Grace "does not make wrong into right," and the wrongdoer does not sit beside her "as though nothing had happened" (*Spe Salvi* 44). His healing passes through judgment, not through her duty.

**Held.** This is the best sentence in W1(1) from this school's side.

### Exchange 9. Law and Wielenberg: the Lord of life and the innocent

*Attack:* v4 now grounds the absolute in trust: "her life is not ours to spend ... it is held in trust from the One who alone is Lord of life (CCC 2258, 2280). That is why the line has no exceptions." Then the line protects her from us, not from Him. The Owner may take what the trustee may not. He does so in every natural death, and in the Scriptures He commands it (1 Samuel 15:3). So the ground of your absolute is jurisdiction, and jurisdiction is the very thing whose goodness is in question.

*Reply:* (a) It is not bare jurisdiction. v4 says the wrong is wrong "because of what it does to her, and to Him whose she is," and that He is the Good, not a commander who picks rules. Jurisdiction explains why the line has *no exceptions for us*, which Foot's account could not. Goodness explains why it holds at all. (b) The asymmetry is not power alone. He who gives life can give it back: the soul does not perish, and He will raise her (W1(1); CCC 366, 998). We can do neither, so what we take we destroy, and what He calls home He keeps. (c) The conquest texts are a real problem, but they lie outside v4. The Church reads them within the whole of Scripture, whose earlier books contain "matters imperfect and provisional" (*Dei Verbum* 15, to verify).

*Rejoinder:* Your God may still take any innocent life whenever He wills.

*Final reply:* He may take back what He gave, and He loses no one in taking it. That is the whole difference, and it is the same difference that answers the parent test.

**Held at parity.** An optional clause makes the asymmetry explicit (R7). The conquest texts are a standoff for the chapter, not a defect in v4.

### Exchange 10. Rowe's policy question and the first reason, unchanged

*Attack:* Could God stop *this one* beating unseen without unmaking a world of real causes? And the god "beyond good and evil who makes and breaks out of sheer fullness": your privation argument does not touch him.

*Reply:* The policy question is the honest standoff from Round 3: no line short of constant hidden rescue, against Rowe's "some arbitrariness would be better than this much horror." v4 claims no proof here: "not no evidence." On the Dionysian god, v4 grants that privation does not rule him out, and answers him only with the whole case: "A yes said to everything is said to no one." That is the right scope.

**Held.**

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**As written: ONE Serious objection stands. Nothing is Fatal.**

**Serious (1):**
1. **The answer to Mackie's "why not begin with heaven?", and the reason given for hell, rest on a necessity that v4's own text denies** (Exchange 1). The weight paragraph says "love must first be freely given before it can be confirmed forever" and "a love that could never be refused would not be a gift." The Dhamma row says the highest good "is a gift, which God can give to the infant and to the one who will never think (CCC 1250, 1261)." CCC 1250 calls that "the sheer gratuitousness of the grace of salvation." And the clause between them says the saints "are free and cannot sin." So on v4's own terms God can and does confirm persons in love who never first gave it in a life where it could be refused. As written, the answer to the logical problem's sharpest form is false by the book's own lights, so our side cannot answer it without changing the text. It is answerable at parity by exact wording: the answer becomes a good, not a necessity (R1, R2). Then it falls to the owned standoff on proportion. It is not Fatal: the evil section's verdict does not rest on this sentence, and the weight is already called heavy.

**Minor (wording and scope; none changes a conclusion):**
2. **"Not as a means" overclaims for nature** against Aquinas's lion (ST I q.22 a.2 ad 2), and the newly named parasite brings the question to persons. Scope it to persons, and say that the parasite takes and God does not give (Exchange 3; R3). The overclaim began in this school's own Round 3 wording.
3. **"One rival bears this weight more easily" omits the indifferent world**, which is Draper's whole argument. This is a misstatement of this school by omission. The Dhamma row's "justice built in looks like a mind" should be tied to the owned weight (Exchange 4; R4).
4. **Hell: the freedom clause reaches only the possibility of refusal, not its eternity.** Give the Church's actual ground (CCC 393, 1033, 1857), and say that the finality is taught, not shown (Exchange 2; R2).
5. **Hiddenness: "comes late" understates lifelong non-resistant non-belief.** This is a misstatement of this school by understatement, contradicted by the paragraph's own contemplatives (Exchange 6; R5).
6. **Conclusion: the honest-reader sentence was dropped.** With the new certainty sentence and CCC 37, the honest dissenter now reads as someone who has fallen short of what is in reach (Exchange 7; R6).
7. **Optional:** make explicit that the Lord of life can give life back, which is why the trust ground is not bare power (Exchange 9; R7).

**Where v4 misstates this school:** items 3 and 5 (by omission and understatement), and item 6 (the honest dissenter's standing). None is a doctrinal matter.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**
- **Draper's pattern, now with the parasite.** Pain falls where survival puts it, and a creature whose nature v4 calls a wisdom lives by the child. CCC 306 and 310 make this compatible with God, not expected. Owned weight.
- **The policy question (Rowe).** Can God stop *this one* evil without unmaking a world of real causes?
- **Proportion,** now also for the repaired "heaven first": is the good of love freely given back, and of real causes, worth deep-time agony?
- **The fawn.** Creation's hope is real but does not name her, and v4 says so.
- **Schellenberg.** "Through persons," grounded in CCC 306, as strong as the free-will answer and no stronger; GS 22's offer "in ways known to God" is not a conscious relationship.
- **Hell (Adams).** Final self-exclusion, now with undiminished dignity in final loss (DI 15). Its finality rests on revelation, not on this case.
- **The Lord of life and the conquest texts** (outside v4).
- **Certainty (CCC 31, 36; *Dei Filius*) against Rowe's friendly atheism.** It is the Church's claim, which this school denies.
- **Ivan's refusal.** It attacks no premise. It is love's protest, and the book honors it.

## 7. New insights and lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **Refusability is not what makes love free; the saints and the baptized infant show it.** The honest answer to "why not heaven first?" is a good (love given back of one's own accord), not a necessity. It is weaker, and it is true. It also stops v4 from contradicting its own best line against the Buddhist (the highest good is a gift).
- **Double effect in nature is scoped by persons.** Aquinas's providence does give one animal's loss to another's life, and the Church lets us do the same within limits (CCC 2417). What it never does is give a person away. That scope, not "not as a means" in general, is the defensible claim, and it is what makes the parasite bearable to state.
- **A6's riot case is Caiaphas's reasoning (John 11:50).** The Cross is where God answers it. The men who reasoned so were guilty, the evil did not become good, and God brought the greatest good from that very evil (CCC 312). This is the strongest single illustration of "what He brings" kept apart from "why He permits."
- **Arguments about built-in justice cut both ways.** If karma's justice looks like a mind, our world's lack of it weighs against a just Mind. The Catholic can bear that only because he places justice at the judgment, not in the share-out of pain (Luke 13:4; John 9:3). Saying so is part of the honesty of the case.
- **The Lord of life can give it back.** That asymmetry, not power alone, is why the trust ground does not fall to voluntarism, and it is the same asymmetry that passes the parent test.
- **Stating a cost at full size makes the Catholic answer clearer.** "It does not come in this life at all, yet the choice can be made before the name is known" says more for LG 16 and GS 22 than "comes late."

**Lines worth keeping**
- "He did not keep her from what was done to her; that weight is on the other side of the scale, and this book weighs it there."
- "The parasite lives by the child; if our nature is a wisdom and not an accident, so is theirs." (v4; the most honest sentence in the section)
- "What Christ gave was given, not taken, and it was not lost."
- "The Buddhist never said it is good that we exist. We say it, and so we must answer for the child."
- "A yes said to everything is said to no one."
- "The parasite takes from the child; God does not give her to it." (R3)
- "That is a good, not a necessity." (R1)
- "He who gives life, and can give it back, may call it home; we, who can do neither, may not take it." (R7)

## 8. Score

**v4 as written: 8/10.** v4 is more honest than v3 at every place this school pressed: the parasite, the Buddhist's advantage, hell stated as Church teaching, the two answers kept apart, and the fawn. But its new "heaven first" and hell sentences rest on a necessity that its own Dhamma row refutes, so one Serious objection stands. **With R1–R6: 9/10.** That is near this school's ceiling against any theistic case, which is set by weight that can be owned but never removed.
