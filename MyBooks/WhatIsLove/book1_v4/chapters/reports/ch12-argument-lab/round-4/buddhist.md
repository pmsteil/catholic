# Argument Lab, Round 4: Buddhist opponent vs v4

## 1. School and voice

The same composite voice as Rounds 1–3: the Theravada Pali Canon and its commentaries (Buddhaghosa), and Indian Madhyamaka (Nagarjuna, Candrakirti, Santideva), with wider Mahayana sources brought in where a Madhyamaka thinker would bring them. It holds anatta (no lasting self), tanha (craving rooted in ignorance) as what keeps the round turning, karma as intention ripening by its own order with no one appointing it, the four boundless states as what a purified heart is like, and nirvana (for the Madhyamaka, emptiness realized) as the highest good. Argued in the spirit of *Nostra Aetate* 2, which I checked on vatican.va: the Church "rejects nothing that is true and holy" in it.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges (short)

1. **Justice.** The reflexive principle: no one is dearer to anyone than himself, and each holds himself just as dear, so harm no one (SN 3.8; Dhp 129–130). The wronged one must be asked for pardon (Vinaya, Cullavagga I). **Holds**; nothing is borrowed.
2. **Dilemma** (a prenatal diagnosis, and abortion is urged). No: the human being begins with the first moment of mind in the womb (Vinaya, Parajika 3). The will to end the life comes with aversion, and what is owed is care (Mahavagga 8.26). **Holds**, without borrowing.
3. **Equal dignity.** Every being suffers and holds its life dear; the mind is luminous by nature (AN 1.51); Mahayana adds buddha-nature. **Holds for equal compassion.** For equal dignity of persons it **bends**: in the Pali texts the highest good is open according to capacity (AN 8.29).
4. **Ultimate good.** Nirvana, or emptiness realized, in which wisdom and compassion cannot be separated; reached by the Eightfold Path and the boundless states. **Holds; coherent.** It places something beyond love, or at least does not make love the ground of things.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1. W1(1)'s three roads: rebirth walks the third road without a Keeper, and "only that hope" overclaims (new text).**
*Buddhist:* v4 now says there are "three ways to read" the debt to the dead. The third is "she is still someone, and the debt can one day be received by her." My tradition has walked that road for twenty-five centuries without a Creator. The dead are born again. The departed receive what their kin dedicate to them and rejoice in it (Khp 7, the Tirokudda Sutta). In Theravada lands the living ask the dead person's pardon at the funeral. Mozi's jibe about ceremonies with no guests does not touch us, because we believe the guests come. So when v4 says guilt "asks for what only that hope could give: to be forgiven by her," the claim is false against the rival you yourselves call the most searching. And because the clue "weighs as much as the reader finds the third road truer than the second," a reader who takes the third road has not moved one step toward God.
*Patrick's side:* Your practice grants W1(1)'s starting fact: guilt still asks for her pardon after she is gone, in your tradition too. We part over who answers. On your own account the one born again is "neither the same nor another" (*Milindapanha*). She will not know what was done or by whom. Your teachers call the wish to be forgiven by *her, as herself* the self-view at work. And your path ends the debt by the doer's own purification, not by her pardon: Angulimala was freed without one word of pardon from those he killed (MN 86). The guilt W1(1) describes asks for her, the very one wronged. Only a hope in which persons are kept as themselves promises that.
*Buddhist rejoinder:* We have also recalled past lives: "there I was so named" (MN 4). In principle, the stream that was her could one day remember and forgive in her own name.
*Patrick's side:* Then it would forgive as a name over a stream, and your highest good is the end of the one who would forgive. We say she is kept as herself, forever. That is the old standoff over whether persons are real at the ground of things, and it is fair.
*Verdict:* **Held at parity, with repair (Minor).** The answer is good, but none of it is in the text. v4's list of three roads presents itself as complete and never mentions rebirth. "Only that hope could give" is new in v4 (v3 said "guilt is shaped for it"), and against rebirth it holds only through the persons standoff. One sentence naming rebirth, plus "promises" in place of "could give," closes the gap. (Repair W1(1).) I considered rating this Serious and rejected that: the text does not misstate Buddhism here, "by her" already marks the point where the two part, and the clue limits its own weight.

**Exchange 2. The frame and the Dhamma row disagree about whether the Buddhist owes guilt to persons.**
*Buddhist:* The frame now says: "the nearest rivals answer that guilt is owed to the one we wronged, and this book agrees." Good. But the sentence just before it pairs "an order of cause and fruit" with "a necessary truth" as things that are not "anyone the guilt could be owed to." The Dhamma row then sums us up on A2 by ripening alone: "A ripening debt is owed to no one, and it cannot forgive." A reader comes away thinking we have only the ripening. We do not. The Buddha had the Sangha send Sudhamma to ask Citta's pardon (Vinaya, Cullavagga I), and he accepted Ajatasattu's confession of his wrong (DN 2).
*Patrick's side:* Granted. The row's sentence about ripening is true of karma, but it leaves out the half of A2 the Buddhist keeps. The honest row says: we agree she is owed while she lives; we part when she is gone, and over who she then is (Exchange 1).
*Verdict:* **Held with repair (Minor).** Name the Buddhist in the frame, and have the row say what he keeps. (Repairs W1-frame and C-Dhamma.)

**Exchange 3. "It explains why no one can purify another (A3)."**
*Buddhist:* We are glad to be credited, but read your own A3. It says no one can consent the gravest wrongs into being right, and no one else can pardon in the victim's place. It never says that no one can purify another. That is our teaching (Dhp 165), and your Church denies it: Christ's blood cleanses us from all sin (1 John 1:7), and your priest absolves. The row has your book endorsing our teaching against your own.
*Patrick's side:* Correct. This was the Buddhist's own Round 3 wording, offered to replace "no one can cancel a deed's fruit," and v4 put it in the wrong place. It belongs as a description of Buddhist teaching, set against the Catholic one, and it makes a better parting than the one it replaces: purity reached by one's own walking, or purity given by Another. That fits the insight-versus-gift contrast exactly.
*Verdict:* **Held with repair (Minor), and a gain.** (Repair C-Dhamma.)

**Exchange 4. "The Buddhist never said it is good that we exist" (new text, said twice).**
*Buddhist:* Said that baldly, it is not accurate. The Buddha said that a human birth is hard to win (Dhp 182). He gave the simile of the blind turtle surfacing once in a century through the yoke on the ocean (SN 56.47–48). The whole tradition speaks of the precious human birth. We rejoice in another's good (mudita), and we say the mind is luminous by nature (AN 1.51). And dukkha does not mean simply "bad"; it means unsatisfactory, unable to rest. "Never said it is good that we exist" makes us sound like gloomy pessimists.
*Patrick's side:* Granted on the wording. The contrast survives restating, and is sharper for it. You prize a human birth as the *chance to be free of birth*. Pieper's word, "it is good that you exist," is said to her being, not to her chance. The first sermon still counts birth among the forms of suffering (SN 56.11), and that is why a world full of suffering is what you expect. So the concession stays, and only the description of your view changes.
*Verdict:* **Held with repair (Minor).** It is my own Round 3 phrase, and I correct it here. (Repairs Evil-weight and C-Dhamma.)

**Exchange 5. "Its highest good is reached by insight ... the Catholic says the highest good is a gift."**
*Buddhist:* For the Theravada and the Madhyamaka, yes. But the row is titled "The Buddha's Dhamma," and your own Council describes Buddhists who reach supreme illumination "by their own efforts or through higher help" (*Nostra Aetate* 2; verified on vatican.va). The Pure Land, the largest school in Japan, teaches birth through Amida's vow, by other-power, and holds that the evil man is received even more surely than the good one (Shinran, *Tannisho* 3). Second, your own highest good is also a *seeing*: the blessed see God by a light of glory that God gives (ST I q.12 a.5). So "insight versus gift" is not a clean opposition.
*Patrick's side:* Both points granted, and the true contrast comes out more exactly. Where Buddhists look for higher help, it comes from a buddha who was once bound as we are and who gave no one being. That is the row's own last point, and it now does double duty. And the Catholic highest good is indeed a seeing, but a seeing *given*, not one reached. That is why God can give it to the baptized infant (CCC 1250) and why the Church entrusts to His mercy the infant who dies unbaptized (CCC 1261). The Pure Land still asks for a hearing and an entrusting the newborn cannot give.
*Buddhist rejoinder:* Amida's vow does not wait on the hearer's strength.
*Patrick's side:* Then that is the nearest point between us in this round. Even so, the One who gives is not the One who gave her being.
*Verdict:* **Held with repair (Minor).** (Repair C-Dhamma.)

**Exchange 6. "Love is the flower of the path" and "who places wisdom beyond love."**
*Buddhist (Madhyamaka voice):* For the Pali texts, fair: boundless love leads to Brahma's company (DN 13), and the Buddha asked Sariputta why he stopped there (MN 97). For my own Madhyamaka, no. Candrakirti opens his work by calling compassion the seed of buddhahood, the water that makes it grow, and its ripened fruit (*Madhyamakavatara* 1.1–2). Kamalasila calls compassion the root (*Bhavanakrama*). In buddhahood, wisdom and compassion cannot be separated. "Flower" makes compassion something that appears late, and "beyond" makes it lower.
*Patrick's side:* Granted. And the line's real contrast was never flower against root. It was *path* against *world*. "For the Buddhist, compassion is the seed and the fruit of the path; for the Catholic, love is also the root of the world." Put that way it is more exact, and kinder to the Buddhist. In C, "places wisdom beyond love" should become "for whom love is not the ground of things."
*Verdict:* **Held with repair (Minor).** I am correcting a line I proposed myself. (Repairs C-Dhamma and C-God.)

**Exchange 7. W3: equanimity "lets each one go," and the Catholic "adds" that desire is healed.**
*Buddhist:* Two things. First, the commentaries say the near enemy of equanimity is worldly indifference. True equanimity holds every being alike because it sees that each owns his deeds (Visuddhimagga IX). "Lets each one go" reads like the near enemy. Second, "the Catholic adds that desire itself is not to be killed but healed" implies that we kill all desire. We do not. We end craving, but the path runs on wholesome desire: Ananda tells the brahmin Unnabha that one uses desire to abandon desire (SN 51.15).
*Patrick's side:* Granted on both. A real difference remains, though. In the Unnabha sutta that desire subsides once its goal is reached, and sensual desire is a hindrance to be abandoned, not healed. The Catholic says eros itself is purified and raised, not uprooted (*Deus Caritas Est* 5). Say exactly that.
*Verdict:* **Held with repair (Minor).** (Repair W3.)

**Exchange 8. The fit argument meets the book's own evil section.**
*Buddhist:* The row says a world whose fortunes follow the moral quality of each heart "looks like the work of a mind." The sentence just before it says karma explains only "some" of how suffering falls. And your evil section says pain in nature falls where survival puts it, not where justice would. So by your own account the world you believe a Mind made shows no such fit, while the world karma describes (across lives, unseen) does. A Mind evidently does not build justice into fortunes, and karma does it without one.
*Patrick's side:* The inference runs from fit to mind, not from mind to fit in this life. The Catholic expects justice completed at the Judgment (CCC 1038–1040), not dealt out through bodies and fortunes; Christ refused to read the blind man's blindness or the tower of Siloam as desert (John 9:3; Luke 13:4). The row's point is conditional: *if* karma is as the Buddha taught, its fit asks for an explanation, as Kant argued about happiness in proportion to virtue (*Critique of Practical Reason* 5:124–125), and as the classical Indian theists argued against the Buddhists. "Some" against "each heart" is a real tension in the wording, though, and "so far as" fixes it.
*Buddhist (Madhyamaka) rejoinder:* The fit arises in dependence on conditions and is empty. Asking for a coordinator is asking for a ground.
*Verdict:* **Held at parity**, with a wording repair (Minor). (Repair C-Dhamma.)

**Exchange 9. W1(2): one leftover sentence.**
*Buddhist:* "Has done nothing to earn it" is fixed, and the sentence about sentience is good. But the next sentence still says an order of deeds "cannot say why anyone matters before she has done anything." In a round with no first point that can be found (SN 15.1), there is no "before." And karma was never our ground for regard.
*Patrick's side:* Granted. Change the sentence to say that karma was never meant to ground regard.
*Verdict:* **Held with repair (Minor).** (Repair W1(2).)

**Exchange 10. Buddha-nature as a ground of equal worth: the Confucian gets credit and the Buddhist does not.**
*Buddhist (Mahayana voice):* The Confucian row credits "worth follows nature, not capacity" through the moon whole in ten thousand rivers. East Asian Buddhism says the same of buddha-nature: every being has it whole. (Candrakirti treated that teaching as provisional, so this is not my Madhyamaka's main ground, but it is the ground of most Buddhists alive.)
*Patrick's side:* Fair. Give it the same credit and the same answer: a nature held whole by all is not a call to this one.
*Verdict:* **Held with repair (Minor, optional).** (Repair C-Dhamma.)

**Exchange 11. The weighing itself: if the Dhamma explains A1, A3, A6 and A7 and bears evil more easily, why is it not the better explanation?**
*Buddhist:* v4 now concedes our advantage on evil, and credits us on most of A. Then the conclusion's "best explanation" needs to show what it beats us on.
*Patrick's side:* It does, and v4 names each point: guilt that asks to be forgiven by her, when she is gone (W1(1)); a worth that does not follow capacity and is a call to this one (W1(2)); love as the last word, not the flower of the path (W3; C); and whether it is good that we exist. The book judges the case, "in this book's judgment," and does not claim to compel.
*Verdict:* **Held at parity.** No defect. This is the honest form of the deepest standoff.

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**As written: NONE.**

No Fatal or Serious objection stands against v4 as written. The one Serious objection from Round 3 (the desert-and-cost clause, and the missing concession on evil) is closed, and closed with the stronger repair. I tested the new text for faults of the same kind. The nearest candidate is W1(1)'s three roads with "only that hope could give" (Exchange 1). It is not Serious, for three reasons: the text does not misstate Buddhism there; "to be forgiven by *her*" already marks exactly where rebirth and resurrection part; and the clue limits its own weight. It is still a real gap, and should be closed.

**Minor**
1. **W1(1):** rebirth (the dead born again, receiving merit, asked for pardon at Theravada funerals) is a third road without a Keeper and is not named; "only that hope could give" should read "only that hope promises" (Exchange 1).
2. **W1 frame / C-Dhamma:** the frame should name the Buddhist among those who hold guilt owed to the one wronged, and the row should say so instead of describing A2 by ripening alone (Exchange 2).
3. **C-Dhamma:** "no one can purify another (A3)" is not A3's content and is endorsed against Catholic teaching (1 John 1:7). Recast it as a parting (Exchange 3).
4. **C-Dhamma and Evil-weight:** "never said it is good that we exist" is too absolute; the tradition prizes a precious human birth (Dhp 182; SN 56.47–48), as the chance to be free of birth (Exchange 4).
5. **C-Dhamma:** "insight versus gift" overlooks the Pure Land and NA 2's own "through higher help," and the Catholic highest good is itself a seeing, though given (ST I q.12 a.5) (Exchange 5).
6. **C-Dhamma / C-God:** "love is the flower of the path" and "places wisdom beyond love" fit the Pali texts, not Madhyamaka (*Madhyamakavatara* 1.1–2) (Exchange 6).
7. **W3:** "an even mind that lets each one go" reads like equanimity's near enemy; "the Catholic adds that desire ... healed" implies Buddhists kill all desire (SN 51.15) (Exchange 7).
8. **C-Dhamma:** "Karma explains some ..." sits uneasily beside "a world whose ... fortunes follow ... each heart"; use "so far as" (Exchange 8).
9. **W1(2):** "before she has done anything" still begs the question against a beginningless round (Exchange 9).
10. **C-Dhamma (optional):** buddha-nature deserves the moon-and-rivers credit and the same answer (Exchange 10).
11. **C-Dhamma:** restore the citations dropped since v3 (Sn 1.8; SN 36.21; AN 8.29).

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Whether persons are real at the ground of things, drawn on the dead victim: rebirth keeps a stream, and guilt asks for her (Exchange 1).
- Whether it is good that we exist, or only that a human birth is a precious chance to be free of birth (Exchange 4). This is the root of every other parting.
- Purity by one's own walking or given by Another; the highest good as an insight reached or a seeing given (Exchanges 3, 5). The Pure Land's other-power is the nearest point between the two.
- Whether the fit of hearts and fortunes needs a mind, or arises from conditions and is empty (Exchange 8). Better than parity against traditional karma; parity against Madhyamaka.
- Whether love is the ground of things or the seed and fruit of a path (Exchange 6; DN 13, MN 97; 1 Cor 13:8).
- Whether a cause that lacks nothing can act for a purpose (BCA 9.119–126; ST I q.44 a.4).
- Each tradition's reading of the other's summit, which v4 now rightly says is not evidence.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**Repair W1(1).** After "...it cannot show that no one does." insert:
> The Buddhist walks a form of the third road without a Keeper: the dead are born again, they can receive what the living dedicate to them, and in some lands the living ask the dead person's pardon at the funeral. But on his own account the one born again is neither the same as she was nor another, will not know what was done, and is not, at the ground of things, a self at all; and his path ends the debt by the doer's own purification, not by her pardon.

And replace "It can see that guilt asks for what only that hope could give: to be forgiven by her." with:
> It can see that guilt asks for what only that hope promises: to be forgiven by her, herself.

**Repair W1-frame.** Replace "But the nearest rivals answer that guilt is owed to the one we wronged, and this book agrees" with:
> But the nearest rivals, the Buddhist among them, answer that guilt is owed to the one we wronged, and this book agrees

(Optional, same paragraph: "On one point every account agrees: the wrong is necessarily wrong." → "On one point these accounts agree: the wrong is wrong of necessity, not up to anyone.")

**Repair W1(2).** Replace "An order of deeds and their fruits can only return to each what each has done; it cannot say why anyone matters before she has done anything." with:
> An order of deeds and their fruits returns to each what each has done; it was never meant to say why anyone matters, and the Buddhist, who grounds regard in suffering, does not ask it to.

## 7. New insights; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **The Buddhist grants W1(1)'s starting fact.** Asking the dead person's pardon at Theravada funerals (to verify), and dedicating merit to the departed (Khp 7), show that guilt still asks for her pardon after death even in a tradition without a Creator. The parting is not over whether she is still owed; it is over whether the one who answers is *her*.
- **Purity given, not only pardon given.** "No one can purify another" (Dhp 165) set against "the blood of Jesus cleanses us" (1 John 1:7) is a cleaner parting than anything about desert, and it goes with the parting over the highest good: insight reached against a seeing given.
- **The Council has already described the Pure Land.** NA 2's "by their own efforts or through higher help" lets the book be fair to the Buddhists who look for other-power, and its answer still holds: the helper was once bound and gave no one being.
- **The precious human birth sharpens the contrast with Pieper.** The Buddhist prizes her birth as a chance; the Catholic prizes it because she exists.
- **For the Madhyamaka, compassion is the seed, water and fruit of the path** (Candrakirti). The true contrast is path against world, not flower against root.
