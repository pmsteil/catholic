# Blind review: the pragmatist / anti-representationalist and relativist school

## 1. School and voice

I speak for the pragmatist family: Richard Rorty's anti-representationalism (no vocabulary is nature's own; "true" endorses and cautions, it does not name a correspondence; solidarity comes before objectivity; moral progress is the widening of "we" by sentimental education); Gilbert Harman's relativism (moral "oughts" hold relative to implicit agreements; moral facts do no explanatory work beyond our psychology) and David Wong's pluralistic relativism (several adequate moralities, bounded by human nature and by what morality is for); and behind them Dewey (inquiry as the intelligent resolution of problematic situations; growth as the moral end) and James (the pragmatic test; meliorism; the moral life as meeting as many demands as possible). I attack the starting points as well as the steps. I keep every moral commitment the liberal West has won, and I deny that those commitments need, or can use, a ground beyond the reasons we give one another.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** No first principle. Justice is a "larger loyalty" (Rorty's phrase for the idea; verify): what anyone is owed is what our widening practice of justification, carried on in reflective equilibrium (Rawls read historically, as Rorty reads him), would give him; for Harman, what implicit agreements among those who share them would give; for Dewey, what intelligent inquiry into conflicting claims settles in this situation. **Coherent but borrowed in content.** The content (each one must be answered; the weak count) is inherited from the Christian and Enlightenment past, and Rorty admits this openly ("we liberals" are heirs). What breaks: I cannot say what the excluded were owed *before* inclusion except by our present lights, applied backward.

**2. A hard case and my method.** Obstructed labour where mother and child will both die unless the child is killed, or the conjoined twins case (Re A, English Court of Appeal, 2000), where separation would kill Mary and save Jodie, and without it both would die. Deweyan method: state the problematic situation honestly; rehearse the courses in imagination; weigh every claim present (the parents', each child's, the doctors'), and the habits that law and medicine embody (necessity, double effect); choose the course that resolves the conflict with the least irreparable loss, and keep the verdict open to revision. I would separate, and permit the craniotomy, as the court did. This book forbids the craniotomy (W1(2), "the line's hardest cost"); Catholic moralists divided over the twins (verify). **Holds as method; borrows content.** I use truthfulness, fairness to each, and compassion, though not as covenant; my "least irreparable loss" is a weighing the book rejects on principle. My method has an underdetermination problem: "best resolution" by what measure? Dewey answers "growth," and the newborn who will not grow strains that.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** I deny it has a ground and affirm it as a commitment. Rorty: human-rights culture needs no foundation; we extended "we" to the newborn, the disabled and the enemy by stories, and we refuse to make their standing conditional. It is unconditional *within* our practice; nothing outside guarantees it. Wong, honestly: not every adequate morality must include it; a communitarian morality could be adequate. Harman: we may say the Romans' exposure of infants was wrong by our standards, not that the Romans had reason not to do it. **Breaks at exactly the point the brief presses.** I can say the hillside infant was wronged; I can only say "before anyone said so" in the sense that *we* now say so of then. That is a real cost, and I pay it openly.

**4. The ultimate good.** No "ultimately." Dewey held that growth itself is the only moral end (*Reconstruction in Philosophy*, ch. 7; verify); James, that the good is the satisfaction of demands, and that we should invent ways to satisfy as many as we can (*The Moral Philosopher and the Moral Life*; verify); Rorty's liberal utopia combines private self-creation with public non-cruelty. Guiding principles: avoid cruelty, keep inquiry open, widen the circle, prefer the revisable. **Coherent as a refusal; breaks at the edges of life.** Growth and satisfied demand do nothing for the child already dead, and nothing for one who will never grow. The text is right that my hope is for those who come after; to the dead I offer memory, not repair.

## 3. Debate highlights

**E1. The A-frame begs the question.**
*Attack.* A is "offered as truths: not as reports of feelings... nor as the best words one community has come to so far." The contrast builds representationalism into the premises: it presupposes that "true" means more than "what our best words endorse." Grant the contrast and the grounding question follows; refuse it and it does not.
*Defense.* The frame does not ask Rorty to grant the contrast. It lists him among those who "assert most of them" and states his denial exactly: that their truth needs any ground beyond our reasons. Its trust principle is fallibilist and conservative (keep what survives honest reflection until defeated), which Harman himself defends as a norm of belief revision. The question of ground is held over for W and C.
*Rejoinder.* Then A does no work against me, and the whole contest moves to W and C. Fair enough. **Held.**

**E2. Harman's explanatory test is in the text's own defeat clause.**
*Attack.* A is trusted "until something... shows that its truth plays no part in the best account of why we hold it." That is Harman's test, and Harman says it is already met: our upbringing and sympathy fully explain why we judge the child-torturer wrong, and the wrongness adds nothing. A's warrant "as truths" is therefore defeated on the text's own terms.
*Defense.* (i) The test proves too much. A7's epistemic "ought" explains no observation either, and Harman does not give up epistemic norms. That is the companions-in-guilt reply, which the Nietzsche row runs as a dilemma. (ii) Realists such as Sturgeon argue that moral facts do explain (his depravity explains what Hitler did). (iii) A6: to call the alarm false, one must already know that the difference it tracks does not matter. (iv) The Nietzsche row: if God makes Himself known, the truth *does* figure in the best account, and which account is best is the very question.
*Rejoinder.* As a pragmatist I welcome the companion: epistemic norms are practice-internal too. So the companions argument gets the text to parity with me, not past me to a Mind. **Held (parity).** The text should name the source of its test, which would make its fairness visible (Section 7).

**E3. W2's "fit" is a pseudo-problem of the Mirror of Nature.**
*Attack.* The puzzle that "minds built for survival grasp necessary truths" assumes minds that mirror a fixed order. Drop the mirror and there is nothing to explain: we keep the vocabularies (logic, arithmetic, the language of rights) that let us cope, and "necessary" marks how we refuse to revise them.
*Defense.* The text already concedes this: pragmatists "need no meeting of law and mind; theirs is the question in C." And it calls its own preference for one Source "a judgment about where explanation may stop, and the rivals do not share it." The pragmatist's dismissal is just such a judgment. Beyond that, the applicability of mathematics far beyond what our ancestors ever needed is a fact any account must accommodate.
*Rejoinder.* Selection of the mathematics that works, plus its generality, accommodates it. **Held (an acknowledged standoff, stated in the text at parity).**

**E4. A7 binds every asserter, with no Mind.**
*Attack.* The Nietzsche dilemma (truthfulness binds every mind by what a mind is, which is W2's question, or only those who hold it) misses a third option. Norms of inquiry bind everyone who plays the game of giving and asking for reasons, and anyone who asserts anything plays it. Peirce showed that the method of tenacity fails for social creatures. No Source is needed.
*Defense.* That answers the first clause of A7 (believing against the evidence) at best. It does not answer the second: "we may not stop looking because we fear what we will find." One can stop playing a game. A constitutive norm binds only while one is inside the practice, and the agent who walks out (Enoch's "shmagency" worry) is not condemned by it. If instead the pragmatist says no reasoner *can* walk out, he has stated a necessary truth about what a reasoning mind is, and W2 returns.
*Rejoinder.* I bite part of it. With James, I hold that inquiry is obligatory only where the question is live and its neglect harms others; a man who will not reopen a grief has not wronged anyone. That is a real cost, since the text's A7 is more plausible to most readers than my exception. **Held**; the exchange leans toward the text.

**E5. James's test cuts against the text's own A7.**
*Attack.* The C row answers "what difference does it make?" with James: looking forward, theism promises that the good is kept. But by A7, a promise may not be believed because it comforts. So against me the case reduces to W1(1) and W3, which the text itself calls clues. And W1(1)'s list of readers for whom it "weighs little" leaves me out, though it weighs little for me: "owed" says where our practice of answering to one another stands, and needs no receiver.
*Defense.* The text claims no more. Its conclusion is a judgment, two of its witnesses are labelled clues, and the row says "That is what W1(1) and W3 weigh." James's test is used to show that the dispute is not idle, which is all Rorty's question challenged. A theist might go further and use James's *Will to Believe* (a live, forced, momentous option that evidence cannot settle may be settled by the passional nature). Catholic teaching does not take that road: faith's assent is not a blind impulse but rests on motives of credibility (CCC 156; *Dei Filius* ch. 3), and the text rightly does not use it.
*Rejoinder.* Agreed, and that agreement is the repair. The row should say outright that James's test gives meaning, not evidence, and W1(1) should name my reader. **Held with repair** (Section 6, R2 and R4).

**E6. The conclusion's dichotomy.**
*Attack.* I am as sure as anyone that the hillside infant was wronged, and wronged no less than the strong one. I affirm the antecedent and deny the consequent, that her worth "follows what she is and what she is made for." There is a third option: worth "follows" nothing. "She was wronged" is warranted by our norm that each human counts equally, and that norm is exactly the one that refuses to let worth follow strength or anyone's caring.
*Defense.* (i) The text says "this book answers," not "it follows." It offers an answer, not a deduction. (ii) The pragmatist's norm does make worth follow what she is: she counts *because she is human*. Asked why being human carries that weight, he answers that we have come to regard it so. But collective regard is "how much any of us cared," and her own world did not care. So he must say that she was wronged by a standard that came later, applied backward. That is what the conclusion's "before anyone said so" presses.
*Rejoinder.* Yes. On my view "before anyone said so" never meant more than that we now say so of then, and the demand for more is the demand for a view from nowhere. The reader must weigh which he is surer of: that she was wronged then, in a sense her world could have been answerable to, or my account of what "wronged" can mean. **Held.** This is the deepest honest standoff, and most readers will feel my bullet.

**E7. The evil section fails James's test the other way.**
*Attack.* A God who permits any evil only if He draws good from it (CCC 324) fits any distribution of suffering, so theism makes no difference to what we should expect *here*. James also warned that an Absolute that guarantees the outcome grants "moral holidays" (*Pragmatism* VIII; verify). Meliorism, which promises nothing and makes effort count, is the more moral faith.
*Defense.* The text does not treat theism as predicting nothing. It says the pattern of pain in nature is "not weak" evidence against it, and that hell and 1 Samuel 15 are costs. It claims no forward difference in this world except one: "His permission never excuses ours" (CCC 2269), and the hands that pull the child from the fire are part of what He made real. That answers the moral holiday directly. The forward difference it does claim (the dead are kept) is exactly what meliorism cannot offer the dead.
*Rejoinder.* Fair. **Held.**

**E8. Grounding does no work for practice.**
*Attack.* The claim that "her claim holds first in the Mind that made her" did not stop Christian slaveholding; the text concedes this. Grounds do not move people; stories do.
*Defense.* The text never claims grounds produce practice. It says outright that the belief's history "does not decide," and that it may not use that history to vindicate A4. Its claim is about truth. And ungrounded solidarity has also shrunk as often as it widened.
*Rejoinder.* Parity, which the text already grants. **Held.** One sentence in W2 does make an empirical claim about what moves people, and that is overstated (Section 5, Minor 3).

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**As written: NONE.**

No Fatal or Serious objection from my school stands. The text never claims to compel the pragmatist. Where its case meets me (the grounding question, W2's fit, the James test, the hillside infant), it states the standoff itself and makes a judgment, not a demonstration. My strongest point (E6) is answered at parity by the force of "before anyone said so."

**Minor items**
1. *A heading:* "nor as the best words one community has come to so far" invites the charge (E1) that representationalism is built into the premises. The rest of the paragraph defuses this, but the phrase is the first thing a pragmatist reads.
2. *W1(1) parenthetical:* the readers for whom the debt "weighs little" omit the pragmatist, who holds that "owed" is said from within our practice of answering to one another and needs no receiver (R2).
3. *W2:* "what moves hearers against their own interest... is reason holding itself to consistency" is an empirical overclaim. Sympathy widened by stories (*Uncle Tom's Cabin*, the testimony of the enslaved) moved hearers against their interest too, and Rorty's case is that it moved them more. Fixable in a way that strengthens the point, since a story moves rightly only when it is true (R3).
4. *C, pragmatist row:* the James sentence can be read as saying that the pragmatist's own criterion favours theism. It shows only that the question is not idle; the row should say that the difference is in meaning, not evidence, to stay consistent with A7 (R4).

**Misstatements of my school**
- *C, pragmatist row:* "deny that anything makes that true." Rorty does not deny truthmakers in a metaphysical register; he declines the idiom as doing no work. The row's own earlier sentence ("declines to ask what makes A true") has it right, and the two sentences sit slightly at odds (R1).
- No other misstatement. Grouping Harman and Wong as "nearby," not identifying Rorty as a relativist, is correct (Rorty rejected that label). The reading of *Pragmatism* III is accurate. The Dewey sentence in the evil section is fair.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Whether truths need a ground beyond our reasons (A heading, C row).
- Whether the fit of mind and necessary truth needs explaining (W2).
- Whether "follow the evidence" binds one who walks out of inquiry (A7, E4).
- Whether "she was wronged before anyone said so" means more than "we now say so of then" (Conclusion, E6). This is the hinge between the text and my school, and the text states it in the right place.

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1. C, "Our best words, widening"**
Old: "it will say, from within, that the slave was wronged whether or not anyone said so, and deny that anything makes that true, our words included;"
New: "it will say, from within, that the slave was wronged whether or not anyone said so, and decline to say that anything makes that true, our words included, holding that the question does no work;"

**R2. W1(1), closing parenthetical**
Old: "(It weighs little for a reader who holds that \"owed\" voices refined feeling or names what a people's laws assign, and does not reach the error theorist,"
New: "(It weighs little for a reader who holds that \"owed\" voices refined feeling, names what a people's laws assign, or says where our practice of answering to one another now stands, and does not reach the error theorist,"

**R3. W2**
Old: "but what moves hearers against their own interest, toward those who can never repay, is reason holding itself to consistency with goods it already sees: the law of truth again."
New: "and what moves hearers against their own interest, toward those who can never repay, is a story that lets them see the wronged truly, and reason holding itself to consistency with goods it already sees: in both, the law of truth again, for a story moves rightly only when what it shows was truly done."

**R4. C, "Our best words, widening"**
Old: "looking forward, one promises that what is good is kept and the other does not (*Pragmatism* III). That is what W1(1) and W3 weigh."
New: "looking forward, one promises that what is good is kept and the other does not (*Pragmatism* III). That shows the question is not idle; it is not evidence, for a promise may not be believed because it comforts (A7). That is what W1(1) and W3 weigh."

**R5 (optional). A heading**
Old: "nor as the best words one community has come to so far."
New: "nor as true only by the lights of one community so far."
(This keeps the exclusion of relativism without seeming to rule out the pragmatist's way of holding a truth before C has heard him.)

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **Rorty agrees with Anscombe's history.** Rorty held that the hunger for moral absolutes is a secular survival of the wish to submit to a non-human authority (paraphrase; verify, e.g. "Pragmatism as Romantic Polytheism"). He and Anscombe agree on where the law-sense of "ought" came from and part over what to do about it: she would set the words aside, he would keep them re-described. One clause in the W1 frame would show the reader that the book's diagnosis is shared by its sharpest secular critic.
- **The Peircean pragmatist is a stronger rival than Rorty on A1.** Peirce, and today Cheryl Misak, hold that a moral truth is what would survive inquiry carried as far as it could fruitfully go. That gives "wrong even if every culture approved" real content without God. The book's difference from it is the same one it draws with "Feeling, refined": whether inquiry carried as far as it could go might still be wrong about her. One sentence in the pragmatist row would close this gap.
- **Dewey's *A Common Faith*** is the pragmatist's own answer to W3: "God" as the active union of the ideal and the actual, and the religious as the unification of the self toward ideal ends (paraphrase; verify). W3's "that yes is said by me, not to me" already answers it, so naming it costs one line and closes a door the pragmatist will try.
- **Name Harman next to the defeat clause.** The A heading's "plays no part in the best account of why we hold it" is Harman's explanatory test. Saying so shows the book adopting its opponent's standard of fairness, which is a strength.
- **A concrete divergence for the chapter.** The conjoined-twins case (Re A, 2000) is a real, public instance of the line W1(2) calls its "hardest cost." The pragmatist, the utilitarian and the court go one way; the book's line goes the other. Naming a real case would make the cost feel paid rather than merely stated.
- **James's *Will to Believe*, declined.** One sentence noting that the Church does not take James's route (faith rests on motives of credibility, CCC 156) would forestall a reader who thinks W1(1) and W3 are licensed by "the heart may decide."

**Lines worth keeping**
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."
- "that yes is said by me, not to me"
- "Heaven and Earth give life to things; God wanted *you*."
- "The rebel can take her side; he cannot keep her."
- "Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent."
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it."
- "A longing does not prove its answer, but it shows what answer would fit" (W3: the right register for a clue).

## 8. Score

**8/10.** The text states my school accurately, uses no genealogy against me that it denies itself, concedes every standoff where it actually stands, and presses the one point (the hillside infant wronged "before anyone said so") on which my position costs most. Four small repairs remain.

**Ceiling: 8.5/10.** My school rejects the demand for a ground rather than failing to meet it, so no theistic case can do better against it than parity on grounding plus a sharper sense of what the pragmatist must give up. This text is close to that ceiling.
