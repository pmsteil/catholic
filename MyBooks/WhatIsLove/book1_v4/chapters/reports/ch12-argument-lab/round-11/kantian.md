# Blind review: the Kantian school

## 1. School and voice

I speak for Kant and his best modern readers: Christine Korsgaard (constitutivism, *Fellow Creatures*), Allen Wood (*Kantian Ethics*, *Kant's Moral Religion*), Barbara Herman, Onora O'Neill (reason's self-vindicating authority, the possible-consent reading of the Formula of Humanity), and Thomas Hill (dignity and self-regarding duty). We are non-theist in the critical sense. The moral law is the law of a free rational will. It has no author, God included, and it binds by its own necessity. Theoretical reason cannot know anything supersensible. Kant himself held God and immortality as postulates of pure practical reason, which is rational faith and not knowledge. Most of his secular heirs drop those postulates altogether. Kant agrees with most of what this text asks the reader to take as true, so our quarrel is narrow and exact. It concerns who authors the law, what kind of inference reaches God, God's right over innocent life, and the human being who never will reason.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** Start with the Universal Principle of Right: an action is right if it can coexist with everyone's freedom under a universal law (MM 6:230). Add the single innate right, freedom as independence from another's constraining choice, which every human being has by virtue of his humanity (6:237). Behind both stands the Formula of Humanity: treat humanity, in your own person and in every other, always also as an end and never merely as a means (G 4:429). What anyone is owed is what respect for that end requires: rightful freedom by right, and beneficence and respect by virtue. **Holds.** It borrows nothing from the book. Its first principle, that rational nature exists as an end in itself, is taken as given, as every account takes something. The regress argument for it (Korsgaard, Wood) is contested.

**2. A hard dilemma.** Case: a captured bomber knows where a device is that will kill hundreds. May he be tortured? No. Punishment must keep free of any maltreatment that makes the humanity in the sufferer abominable (MM 6:333). Even a vicious man keeps the respect owed to him as a human being (6:463). Torture uses his agency against itself as a mere instrument, and no number changes what the maxim is. The principles at work are the Formula of Humanity, the priority of perfect duties over imperfect ones, and the line between right and virtue. **Holds without borrowing.** But Kant breaks on his own most famous case, the murderer at the door (*On a Supposed Right to Lie*, 8:425–430), where he forbids even a lie to the murderer. His best readers repair it: Korsgaard's two-level reading in "The Right to Lie" lets the Formula of Humanity govern nonideal conditions. The repair is coherent, but it is a repair.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** Dignity belongs to humanity: the capacity to set ends and to be the subject of the moral law. It is beyond price and has no equivalent (G 4:434–436). It is equal because it is not a magnitude. It is unconditional because it does not depend on use: the criminal keeps it (6:463), and the newborn is a person by the very act that brought her into the world (6:280–281). **This is where we strain.** For the human being who never will reason, or never again will, we have two answers. The first is the species predisposition to personality (Religion 6:26–28; Patrick Kain, "Kant's Defense of Human Moral Status," verify). Since Kant puts it there, it may come apart from the person's empirical powers, as the noumenal subject. That borrows either an Aristotelian kind-essence or a metaphysics of the noumenal self, and secular Kantians want neither. The second is Korsgaard's widening to every creature with a good of its own. That keeps the newborn but gives up uniquely human equal dignity and leaves the permanently unconscious unsecured. **Holds for the newborn and the enemy; borrows or breaks for those who never reason.**

**4. Ending point for flourishing.** The supreme good is virtue, the worthiness to be happy. The complete good is happiness in proportion to virtue (CPrR 5:110–111). Its social form is the ethical commonwealth, or kingdom of ends (Religion Bk. III). Kant held openly that we can think the highest good possible only by postulating God and immortality (CPrR 5:122–126). **Kant's own ending point therefore leans on rational theism, and he says so.** His secular heirs either reinterpret it as a this-worldly social ideal (Andrews Reath; Rawls, verify) or set it aside. Their ending point, the moral life itself with its hope unsecured, is coherent but thin. **Holds for Kant by rational faith; for the secular heirs it holds only by giving up the guarantee.**

## 3. Debate highlights

**E1. Heteronomy.** *Attack:* Calling God "the author of the law" (W1 closing; VS 36, 40) makes obligation heteronomous. A law authored by any will is either contingent positive law, or it is necessary, and then the "author" only promulgates it. Kant says this in so many words (MM 6:227; G 4:441–444; Collins lectures, 27:283, verify). *Reply:* The text is not voluntarist. It holds that the deed is wrong because of what it does to her, "not wrong merely because it is forbidden." It holds that the law is God's own wisdom, and that our share in it is "reason's own light, not a foreign rule" (VS 41). Kant comes very close: religion, for him, is recognizing all duties as divine commands, not as decrees of a foreign will but as essential laws of every free will in itself (CPrR 5:129, paraphrase). *Rejoinder:* Granted. Then "author" adds no binding force. The dispute is about where the necessary law has its home, not about why it binds. **Held.** The text never makes the law's binding force depend on a command.

**E2. Critical limits.** *Attack:* The conclusion says "the best explanation … is a Lawgiver." That is a theoretical inference from moral data to a supersensible cause, which the Transcendental Dialectic forbids (A590/B618 ff.). Physico-theology at most yields an architect proportioned to the evidence. The moral argument yields a postulate held for practical purposes, not knowledge (CJ §87; CPrR 5:125–126). *Reply:* The text claims less than proof. It calls two of its witnesses clues and says evil weighs heavily against it. It reports Kant's position exactly ("near this book in what it says, far in what it claims"). Its Catholic frame (*Dei Filius* ch. 2) rejects Kant's restriction, and that restriction is itself a contested epistemology that most current Kantian ethics does not rely on. *Rejoinder:* So we disagree about what reason may know, and on that we stand at parity. The text says as much: "a judgment about where explanation may stop, and the rivals do not share it." **Held (honest standoff).**

**E3. "Why there are beings at all whose own law it is."** *Attack:* This question changes the subject. Moral authority needs no answer to why rational beings exist. Theism's answer also stops at a necessary being, and Kant showed that the cosmological argument rests on the ontological one, which fails (A603–614/B631–642). *Reply:* The text does not make authority depend on the answer. It compares how far each account explains. It admits the difference is "not fewer first principles," and it presents God's aseity as Church teaching, not as something shown by reason. *Rejoinder:* Then the text's reach over Kant is narrower than its closing section suggests. Kant already finds one source, rational nature, for the law, the mind that reads it, and the beings it is about. Against him, only two things remain: why rational beings exist, and the human being who never reasons. **Held with repair (R7).**

**E4. The human being who never reasons.** *Attack (my strongest defense, turned against the text's challenge):* The predisposition to personality belongs to the kind and cannot be rooted out (Religion 6:26–28). Dignity attaches to the kind of subject the moral law is about, not to the exercise of reason. *Reply:* The text asks exactly why "that kind carries such worth before reason is ever used." The predisposition answer puts dignity in what she is, in a noumenal or kind-level nature, which is just where the book puts it. It then gives that kind a source and an end. Korsgaard's alternative pays for the newborn by giving up the uniqueness of human dignity. *Rejoinder:* We can say that humanity is the only kind whose members, as such, are subjects of the law. But we cannot say why a capacity that some members will never have confers that worth on them without help from an essence we did not earn. **Held (by the text). The Kantian borrows here.**

**E5. The Lord of life and 1 Samuel 15.** *Attack:* The text says "Not even God makes cruelty right," and grounds the wrong in what it does to her. Yet it holds that God may command human hands to kill infants, and that the executor acts "by His right." That seems to make the act's wrongness depend on who commands it. Kant has three weapons. Not even God may use a rational being merely as a means (CPrR 5:131). A being endowed with freedom cannot be its maker's product or property, so giving being grounds duties to her, not dominion over her (MM 6:280–281). And no hearer can be surer that a voice is God's than that he must not kill this child (*Conflict* 7:63n). *Reply:* With Aquinas, the text holds that the act's matter changes. Death comes to all by divine decree (CCC 1008). The One who gives being can give it back, and keeps her (W1(1)), so her death is not the end of her good. Lordship is not ownership for use: she is willed for her own sake (GS 24). Kant's parent analogy even helps the book. If a maker owes the one he made her contentment with her condition (6:281), only One who raises the dead could pay that debt to a child killed. And the text concedes, in the section on evil, that a reader who weighs by reason alone may be surer those infants were wronged. *Rejoinder:* The text keeps its consistency only because its absolute line for us rests on title, the trust in which her life is held, as well as on her worth. "What protects her is her own worth" should say so. Kant's epistemic argument also has no expiry date, while the text's barrier ("now," CCC 67) is ecclesial, and it does not say that it parts from Kant over Abraham's warrant as well. **Held with repair (R4, R5). The cost remains on the scale, as the text already admits.**

**E6. The hardest case.** *Attack:* Mother and unborn child will both die unless the child is killed. On the possible-consent reading of the Formula of Humanity (O'Neill, "Between Consenting Adults," verify; Korsgaard), the child could not reasonably refuse a procedure that saves her mother and takes nothing she could keep. So she is not used merely as a means, and the text's stated reason ("to treat one willed for her own sake as existing for them") does not reach this case. *Reply:* The text grounds it at once in something else: "That right is never ours" (CCC 2258). It calls this "the line's hardest cost" and labels A6's absolutes "this book's claims, not shared ones." *Rejoinder:* Fair, but then this is a case where some Kantians draw the line less absolutely than the book, and the text's general picture of Kant as the stricter party should note it. **Held with repair (R5).**

**E7. The debt to the dead.** *Attack:* Moral guilt is "the most personal" of debts (Religion 6:72, paraphrase). It is settled before the law by a change of heart and by restitution. The wish to be pardoned by the dead is a natural desire for relief, not a demand of reason, so W1(1) shows nothing. *Reply:* The text already grants this: "That guilt asks proves nothing." It keeps W1(1) as a clue whose weight depends on whether a debt truly owed must one day be received. Kant also hoped for the soul's survival (5:122–124). *Rejoinder, and a concession:* Kant's own account of posthumous wrong regards the dead person, in that relation, apart from everything physical (6:295, verify). That puts him nearer the third road ("she is still someone") than the text's three-way division suggests, not on the second. **Held.** The text could use this point.

**E8. Good before law.** *Attack:* A5 offers the Thomist first principle ("good is to be done") and a list of natural goods as things "we find," but Kant reverses that order. The good must be fixed by the law, not the law by the good (CPrR 5:62–64). Only a good will is good without limitation (G 4:393). *Reply:* A5 leaves the ordering open ("is what W must weigh"), and it claims only that reflection finds these goods good. *Rejoinder:* Mostly granted, but a reader who knows Kant will see one school's ordering presented as what everyone finds. **Held with repair (R6).**

**E9. "Reason can condemn us; it is persons who forgive."** *Attack:* This sets reason against persons as if Kantian reason were an impersonal court. For Kant it is persons, through their own reason, who both judge and forgive, and placability is a duty of persons (6:460–461). *Reply:* The line is aimed at the dead victim, not at Kant's theory of agency. *Rejoinder:* Then say that. As written, it misdescribes the school. **Held with repair (R1).**

## 4. Ledger

- **A heading (epistemic rule, deniers):** Holds. Kant would accept the trust rule for practical reason. He is rightly not listed among the deniers, since he affirms A1–A3 and A6–A7 and most of A4.
- **A1:** Holds. This is Kant's own claim: necessary, binding on everyone, independent of approval.
- **A2:** Holds. Kant agrees that the secret told for a laugh wrongs her as a person even though no one experiences the betrayal (cf. 6:295). Guilt toward a person is distinct from worsening the world.
- **A3:** Holds. No one can contract himself into slavery (6:283, 6:330), and humanity in one's own person cannot be waived.
- **A4:** Holds with repair (R8). The death-penalty note on Kant is accurate (6:331–335). For parity the history needs Kant's own lapse at 6:336.
- **A5:** Holds with repair (R6). It quietly assumes the good comes before the law.
- **A6:** Holds. Kant holds the line at every number, and also against the person's own request (6:422–423). The point about debunking the alarm is sound. Some Kantians would part in the maternal case (E6).
- **A7:** Holds. It matches Kant's duty of truthfulness to oneself and his ban on the inner lie (6:429–431).
- **W1 frame:** Holds. It is fair to Anscombe (fairer than Anscombe was to Kant). "Not wrong merely because it is forbidden" blocks the heteronomy charge.
- **W1(1):** Holds as a clue, as the text says. A Kantian refinement is available (E7).
- **W1(2):** Holds with repair (R4, R5). The ground of the line for creatures is twofold (worth and trust), and the text should say so plainly. The epistemic side of Kant's Abraham argument is not answered beyond "now." The 1 Samuel cost is honestly kept on the scale.
- **W1 closing ("not a commander who picks rules"):** Holds. "A will that invents values can command, but it cannot bind" is Kant's point too, and CPrR 5:129 is a natural ally here (R3).
- **W2:** Holds. Its parenthesis rightly grants that Kantians "need no meeting of law and mind." Darwin's bees are no threat to Kant (rational bees would be bound by the categorical imperative against their instinct), and the text does not claim they are.
- **W3, first paragraph:** Holds as a clue. Kant has a version of "it is good that you exist": the moral being is the final end of creation (CJ §84), held by rational faith.
- **W3, second paragraph (hiddenness):** Holds. It could be strengthened by Kant's own argument that a wise concealment protects the purity of moral motive (CPrR 5:146–148). See §7.
- **C intro:** Holds. The fairness rule is stated and kept.
- **C, Nothing really binds:** Holds. The Kantian agrees that the cost to A7 is real: on error theory, theoretical normativity falls with moral normativity.
- **C, Feeling, refined:** Holds. The Kantian presses the same question: could the most refined heart be wrong? For us, yes, because the law is not a feeling.
- **C, Our best words, widening:** Holds.
- **C, Reason's own law:** Holds with repair (R1, R2, R3, R4). The Kant citations check out: G 4:431, 4:436; MM 6:227, 6:280, 6:295–296, 6:460–461; CJ §§84, 86–87; CPrR 5:122–126, 5:131; *Conflict* 7:63. There are three soft spots. First, "Reason can condemn us; it is persons who forgive" sets reason against persons. Second, the readers are not uniformly kind-based. Third, the immortality postulate rests on endless progress toward holiness, not on the victim's pardon, so the text should say this is what Kant's principles imply, not what Kant argued.
- **C, What we owe each other:** Holds. The limit on Darwall (it "reaches only those who can be addressed") is fair.
- **C, Care between persons:** Holds.
- **C, Well-being, counted impartially:** Holds. Kant would sign every cost listed.
- **C, Flourishing of our kind:** Holds.
- **C, Necessary truths that belong to no one:** Holds.
- **C, An impersonal sacred order:** Holds (not my school; no Kantian objection).
- **C, The Way:** Holds. The comparison "like Kant, draws the line more absolutely" is accurate.
- **C, God, or Nature:** Holds.
- **C, The Buddha's Dhamma:** Holds.
- **C, History and class:** Holds.
- **C, Power and rank:** Holds. The truthfulness dilemma is one Kant would sharpen: truthfulness binds every reasoning mind because reason is self-legislating. The text routes that horn to W2 and C, which is fair.
- **C, Freedom that makes its values:** Holds. "Whence that form binds" is exactly where the Kantian would press Sartre too.
- **C, A God who is Love:** Holds with repair (R7). Against the realist the "one source" claim is fair. Against Kant it should be narrowed.
- **Evil section, paragraph 1 (the weight, hell, 1 Samuel, free will, the fawn):** Holds. It has the candor Kant praised in Job against the theodicists ("On the Miscarriage of All Philosophical Trials in Theodicy," 8:265–267, verify). The Mary premise (grace brings a yes without overriding) blocks Kant's autonomy-based relief on hell, and the text rightly lets the strain stand.
- **Evil section, First (evil as privation):** Holds. It properly limits itself to ruling out a dark god.
- **Evil section, Second (permission is not a trade):** Holds. The narrower claim, that she is never spent as a mere means and the good is offered to her as well, is in substance Kant's requirement at 5:131 applied to God. The text thus meets the Kantian test on its own terms.
- **Evil section, Third (God suffers with; the hopes of other accounts):** Holds. Adorno and *Spe Salvi* 42–43 are well used.
- **Evil section, closing invitation to the protester:** Holds.
- **Conclusion:** Holds. Its question ("which are you surer of?") has the form of Kant's own principle that moral certainty outweighs any theoretical argument. The step from "her worth follows what she is" to "and what she is made for" is offered as the book's answer, not forced.

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**As written: NONE.**

My strongest attacks (E2, E3, E5) end either in honest standoffs at parity, which the text already names (the Critical limits on theoretical theology; where explanation may stop), or in costs the text already keeps on the scale (1 Samuel 15 and the line's hardest case). None shows an incoherence the text cannot answer with its own stated resources: the trust in which life is held (CCC 2258) and a law that is God's wisdom, not His decree.

**Minor items**
1. W1(2): "what protects her is her own worth" understates the second ground, the trust, which carries the hardest case (E5, E6). Repair R5.
2. C, Reason's own law, and W1(2): Kant's Abraham argument is epistemic and timeless. The text answers it only for "now" (CCC 67), and does not say that with Aquinas (ST I-II q.100 a.8 ad 3) it also parts from Kant over Abraham's warrant. Repair R4.
3. A5 presents the Thomist ordering of good before law as something everyone finds. Repair R6.
4. The closing section's "one source" reach is overstated against Kant, who unifies law, mind and the beings the law is about in rational nature. Repair R7.
5. A4's history omits Kant's own lapse (6:336), which parity with Seneca and the slow Christians invites. Repair R8.

**Misstatements of my school**
- "Reason can condemn us; it is persons who forgive." For Kant, reason is the person's own, and persons both condemn and forgive (R1).
- "His best readers, who ground her dignity in the rational nature she has by kind": some do (Kain; Wood, verify), but Korsgaard widens dignity to every creature with a good of its own. The challenge lands harder when both answers are named (R2).
- "That the one we wronged can ever forgive, he could hope only by rational faith in the soul's enduring" is accurate as an implication but could be read as Kant's own argument. Kant grounded the postulate of immortality on endless progress toward holiness (R1).
- "Kant made forgiving a duty": strictly, a forgiving disposition (placability), which he distinguishes from tolerating wrongs (6:461). Small.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Whether reason may infer the supersensible (Kant's Critique against *Dei Filius*).
- Whether God can be the "author" of a necessary law without being idle or voluntarist. The text answers this with participated theonomy, and Kant's CPrR 5:129 comes close to it.
- Whether God may hold the right over innocent life that Kant denies even to Him (5:131).
- Whether grace can bring a free will to its yes without overriding it (the Mary premise against Kantian autonomy, Religion 6:44).
- Why rational beings exist at all, which every account takes as given or traces to God.

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1 (C, Reason's own law: forgiveness and the dead).**
Old: "Reason can condemn us; it is persons who forgive. Kant made forgiving a duty and held that the dead can be wronged (*Metaphysics of Morals* 6:460–461, 6:295–296); that the one we wronged can ever forgive, he could hope only by rational faith in the soul's enduring (*Critique of Practical Reason* 5:122–124), which his secular heirs set aside."
New: "On Kant's account it is persons, by their own reason, who both condemn and forgive. He made a forgiving spirit a duty, and held that the dead can be wronged, regarding them in that relation as persons apart from anything physical (*Metaphysics of Morals* 6:460–461, 6:295–296). But that the one we wronged can ever forgive, his principles leave to rational faith in the soul's enduring, a faith he grounded on another need, endless progress toward holiness (*Critique of Practical Reason* 5:122–124), and which his secular heirs set aside."

**R2 (C, Reason's own law: the best readers).**
Old: "his best readers, who ground her dignity in the rational nature she has by kind, must say why that kind carries such worth before reason is ever used, a question this book faces too, and answers with a call that does not wait on her powers (W1(2))."
New: "his best readers divide over the one who never will reason. Some ground her dignity in the rational nature she has by kind, and must say why that kind carries such worth before reason is ever used. Others, with Christine Korsgaard, make an end in itself of every creature that has a good of its own, which keeps the newborn but no longer says why every human being, and only she, has the worth of every other. This book faces the first question too, and answers with a call that does not wait on her powers (W1(2))."

**R3 (C, Reason's own law: add after "near this book in what it says, far in what it claims.").**
Insert: "He even defined religion as recognizing all our duties as divine commands, not as the decrees of a foreign will but as the essential laws of every free will in itself (*Critique of Practical Reason* 5:129), which is close to what this book means by a law that is His own wisdom and reason's own light (W1; *Veritatis Splendor* 41)."

**R4 (C, Reason's own law: Abraham).**
Old: "This book agrees with him about every voice that now claims such a command (CCC 67), and parts from him about God:"
New: "His argument there is about knowing as well as about God: no hearer, he held, could be surer that a voice is God's than that he must not kill this child. This book agrees with him about every voice that now claims such a command (CCC 67), and parts from him about God, and about Abraham, of whom St. Thomas held that he did not consent to murder, since the Lord of life and death had commanded it (ST I-II q.100 a.8 ad 3):"

**R5 (W1(2): the two grounds of the line, and the hardest case).**
Old: "That is why, on this account, the line has no exceptions for any of us: what protects her is her own worth, which no total can make it right to aim against,"
New: "That is why, on this account, the line has no exceptions for any of us: what protects her against every creature is her own worth, which no total can make it right to aim against, and the trust in which her life is held, which no creature can claim the right to end,"
And old: "That is the line's hardest cost."
New: "That is the line's hardest cost, and here the line rests on the trust more than on her being used for others, for she would lose nothing she could keep; some who hold her worth as firmly, some Kantians among them, would let her mother's physicians act, since she could not reasonably refuse what saves her mother and costs her nothing."

**R6 (A5).**
After "(ST I-II q.94 a.2)." insert: "Kant, who held that only a good will is good without limitation, would let the law fix what is good rather than the good fix the law (*Critique of Practical Reason* 5:62–64); he grants these goods, but as good only under the law."

**R7 (C, A God who is Love).**
After "…rather than through a common source (W2)." insert: "Kant, too, finds one source for all three, rational nature itself; against him the difference is narrower: why there are rational beings at all, and why the human being who never will reason shares their worth (C, Reason's own law)."

**R8 (A4, history).**
Old: "and Christians too were slow to see what slavery denied."
New: "and Christians too were slow to see what slavery denied; and Kant, who counted every newborn a person, still wrote that the state might ignore the killing of a child born outside marriage, as of one who had entered the commonwealth like contraband (*Metaphysics of Morals* 6:336)."

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**New insights**
- **CPrR 5:129 is the text's best Kantian ally.** Kant's definition of religion, duties recognized as divine commands but not as a foreign will's decrees, is close to *Veritatis Splendor* 41's "participated theonomy." It shows that the book's Lawgiver is not the heteronomous lawgiver Kant rejected (R3).
- **Kant on hiddenness (CPrR 5:146–148).** If God and eternity stood always before our eyes, Kant argues, we would obey from fear and hope, our conduct would become mechanism, and moral worth would vanish. A wise concealment preserves a freely given good will. This fits Chapter 4 ("a gift forced is no gift") and the W3 hiddenness paragraph. It is part of an answer, not a full one, and it is worth adding there.
- **Kant's parent passage (MM 6:280–281) cuts both ways.** It denies the maker dominion over a free being he brought into the world. But it also says that one who brings a person into the world without her consent owes it to her to make her content with her condition. Only One who can raise the dead could pay that to the child already spent. This gives W1(2) and the section on evil a Kantian form for "He keeps her claim, and her."
- **Kant's posthumous-wrong passage (6:295)** treats the dead person in the moral relation apart from everything physical. That sits nearer the third road of W1(1) than the second, and it could be used there.
- **Kant's honesty about theodicy** ("On the Miscarriage…," the verdict for Job against his friends) supports the section on evil's refusal to explain more than it can.
- **On hell:** Kantian respect for a will's final self-legislation supports the claim that permitting a refusal treats the refuser as a person. But the book's own Mary premise limits how far that help reaches. The book is right not to lean on it.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "A will that invents values can command, but it cannot bind; even a promise binds only under a law no promiser invented."
- "Not even God makes cruelty right; He cannot, because He is the Good."
- "Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent."
- "Our love answers to a worth already there; God's love gives it, by giving her being."
- "near this book in what it says, far in what it claims." (A fair one-line verdict on Kant's moral theology.)
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it." (A Kantian could sign this.)
- The whole of "Second" in the section on evil: the claim that she is never spent as a mere means and the good is offered to her as well is the right test, and it is ours.

## 8. Score

**8 / 10.** The text states Kant accurately and borrows his best lines honestly. It already concedes the costs a Kantian would press hardest, and it presses the one real Kantian weak point fairly: the human being who never will reason. What is left is standoffs at parity over epistemology and over God's right to life, plus a handful of Minor misstatements.

**Honest ceiling for any theistic case against my school: 8.** Kant himself already grants the moral theism at the level of rational faith, so a theistic case can win him on what it says. No theistic case can move a Kantian from postulate to knowledge without defeating the Critique of speculative theology, and that can be contested only at parity, never compelled.
