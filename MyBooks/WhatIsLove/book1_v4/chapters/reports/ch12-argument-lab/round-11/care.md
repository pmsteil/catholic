# Blind review: the ethics of care

## 1. School and voice

I write for the ethics of care: Nel Noddings, Virginia Held, Joan Tronto, Eva Feder Kittay, Carol Gilligan and Annette Baier. Our starting claim is that moral life begins in relations of dependency, not in contracts between equals. Every one of us was once wholly in someone's hands, and many of us will be again. Being cared for is where moral knowledge starts, and the work of care, mostly unpaid and mostly done by women, is the hidden ground of every theory of justice. I am a non-theist. I take the text at its strongest, and I test it where my school is strongest too: the newborn no one holds, the severely cognitively disabled (Kittay's daughter Sesha is the case behind our best work), and the people who carry them.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** We start from need and dependency. Each person is owed the care she needs to survive and do well, and each carer is owed the conditions that let her give it without being used up. That second principle is Kittay's *doulia* (verify). Held makes care the more basic value and treats justice as the fair framework within care. Tronto treats justice as the fair sharing of caring responsibilities. **Holds, and borrows at one point.** The step from "she needs care" to "she is owed care" is taken as basic. We do not derive it.

**2. A hard dilemma by our principles.** Take the "Ashley X" case (2004). A girl with profound cognitive disability was given growth attenuation, a hysterectomy and breast-bud removal so that her parents could keep lifting her and caring for her at home. Tronto's four elements are attentiveness (to Ashley's good, not the carers' convenience), responsibility, competence and responsiveness (to her own signs of comfort and pain). Add *doulia*: the parents' exhaustion is a real claim, but it is a claim on all of us for hoists, respite and home support, not a licence to reshape her body to fit the care on offer. So we would refuse the procedures and meet the parents' need. Kittay was among the critics (verify). **Holds as a method.** It does lean on truthful attention, fairness and responsiveness, which are close cousins of the book's truth, justice and mercy, though we see them as parts of one practice. We break from the book's "sacrifice" where it would ask a carer to give herself without limit. Gilligan describes mature care as including care for the self. The book's verdict here is the same as ours (CCC 2297).

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** Kittay's answer is that each of us is "some mother's child." Every human being is born helpless into others' hands, so every human being has an equal standing as someone whose good must be someone's concern. That standing does not depend on reason, skill or what she can give back. It holds whole for the newborn and the severely disabled. For those two cases it is the strongest secular answer in the literature. **Holds for the newborn and the disabled. Strained for the enemy.** Noddings allows that caring may lapse toward the one who chooses evil (verify). **Brute at the bottom.** Why being given into care confers worth, we take as given.

**4. Ending point for flourishing.** We do not name one highest good. What we offer is a life held in good relations of care, received and given across a lifetime. With Fisher and Tronto (verify), care is the work of maintaining, continuing and repairing our world so that we can live in it as well as possible. Joy, which Noddings ties to relation, is its sign. **Holds within a life. Breaks at the grave.** For the one who dies unloved, and for the dead in general, our account has memory and repair for the living, not completion for her.

## 3. Debate highlights

**E1. "She ought to have been wanted."**
- *Attack.* The C row says care can say of the unwanted infant only that she ought to have been wanted, "not that anyone did want her." But A4 places her worth "prior to anyone's recognition." If being actually wanted is what grounds worth, the book has made worth relational, which is our thesis. If it is not, "someone did want her" adds nothing to her dignity.
- *Defense.* God's wanting is not recognition. It is creative love that gives her being. "Our love answers to a worth already there; God's love gives it" (W1(2); ST I q.20 a.2). The book is not saying worth needs an admirer. It is saying the worth no deed confers is a gift, exactly as far as her being is one. The contrast is about ground, not about who cares more.
- *Rejoinder.* Granted. Then the real dispute is creation and W2's question about where explanation may stop, not care, and the row says no more than that.
- **Held.** (One insight follows from this: see section 7.)

**E2. Care's own test applied to God.**
- *Attack.* By our criteria a "wanting" that leaves the child on the hillside is caring-about with no care-giving. Noddings holds that care is completed only when the cared-for receives it (verify), and this infant receives nothing. A human parent who wanted a child and let her die of exposure when he could have saved her would be our paradigm of failed care. So "every child was wanted by the One who made her" names a care that fails at every phase after the first.
- *Defense.* The text says exactly this: its answer has "the shape of care's first act... not of its next, for He did not take her up from the hillside." It puts the matter on the scale against itself. On evil, its claim is narrow: only a good brought "within her own life", so that what was done to her is "answered and not merely outweighed," could meet the objection, and that is "a hope, not a proof." It adds that God's care-giving usually comes through us ("the hands that pull a child from the fire are part of what He made real"; W3: "a mother's love before a creed").
- *Rejoinder.* Delegation fails at the very infant the book chose: the one no one holds. Care put off until after death is not care she receives in the life she had.
- **Held.** The text makes no claim beyond what it can support, and it books the cost. This is a real weight on the evil side, a standoff and not a defect.

**E3. The case where mother and child both die.**
- *Attack.* W1(2) forbids killing the unborn child even when otherwise both will die. Gilligan's "web" reasoning asks who is left and who will care for the woman's other children. It sees a rule that produces two deaths where one could be prevented. The mother is spent for a principle, which is just what the book says may never happen to persons.
- *Defense.* She is not spent. Her death is foreseen, not aimed at. Aiming at the child's death would treat someone willed for her own sake as existing for another. Treatment aimed at the mother's own illness is allowed. The text calls this "the line's hardest cost" and presents it as the book's claim on its own premises, not a shared one. The Catholic teaching is stated accurately.
- *Rejoinder.* We doubt that the difference between intending and foreseeing tracks anything the mother, or the children she leaves, could care about. But the book does not ask us to accept this as a premise.
- **Held (standoff at parity).**

**E4. "Where instinct fails" and Noddings's ethical caring.**
- *Attack.* A4 says that where instinct fails "we learn whether what we meant was worth or only attraction." A careless reader will hear this as aimed at care, as if care were attraction. But Noddings separates natural caring from ethical caring. When no one is drawn to the child, the memory of having been cared for and the ideal of oneself as a carer still bind whoever finds her (verify).
- *Defense.* The sentence is not aimed at care ethics, and the C row says care "reaches even the infant no one holds." The defense can also press further: Noddings grounds the obligation in the carer's ideal of herself, so the infant's claim becomes a by-product of my self-image rather than hers by right. Noddings also limits obligation by the prospects of a completed relation.
- *Rejoinder.* That is a fair hit on Noddings, but not on Kittay or Held, who ground the claim in the infant's need and in the universality of dependency. Against them the C row is accurate.
- **Held with repair.** The row would be fairer to Noddings, and sharper against her, if it named ethical caring (R2).

**E5. The severely disabled and the language of lack.**
- *Attack.* The Aristotelian row, which the book says it largely shares, reads: "what falls short is the life she can live, not what she is," and her end is one "she will never reach." Kittay's whole work on Sesha argues that such a life is not a failed rational life. It has its own goods: joy, music, love given and received. On the book's view her dignity is rescued, but her earthly life is still filed as a lack.
- *Defense.* For the book, her end is "God Himself," against which "the genius and the newborn are for the same thing." Her calling does not wait on her powers (DI 24, 53, "whatever her power to understand or choose"). The Church teaches that persons with disabilities love and are loved, and give to others. What is lacking is a power, never her person, her worth or her calling.
- *Rejoinder.* Then the text should say that, and not borrow "falls short" for the life itself.
- **Held with repair** (R4). This is minor: the error is in phrasing, not in the argument.

**E6. Who pays for the absolutes? (doulia)**
- *Attack.* The book draws absolute negative lines: no aiming at death, no withdrawing food and water while they nourish, no euthanasia even on request. The cost of those lines falls on the carers, mostly women, who keep the severely disabled and the dying alive. A rule that forbids every exit while leaving support as a looser positive duty passes the cost of the absolute down to the people doing the care.
- *Defense.* The text does see this: "those who carry that care must not be left to carry it alone (CCC 2208–2209)." Doulia is credited in C. A6 says real good "always asks something of the giver," and CCC 2269 calls society's indifference to remediable deaths a grave offence.
- *Rejoinder.* It is present, but as a clause. The book's own logic is stronger than its wording: the same worth that forbids aiming at her death also binds the community to the people who hold her.
- **Held with repair** (R3).

**E7. Hell: the carer who could, and does not.**
- *Attack.* The book's deepest ground is that God wills each person for her own sake and never spends anyone against her own good. Yet on Catholic teaching some may be lost forever, and the book says God can bring a free will to its yes without forcing it, as with Mary. A carer who could bring the beloved home without coercion and does not is no carer in our sense. Noddings's *Women and Evil* presses this against the traditional theodicy (verify).
- *Defense.* The text concedes this is "the heaviest thing there," that reason cannot show how permitting a final refusal serves the one who refuses, that the Church declares no one lost, and that it prays for all. Care also respects a competent person's refusal: care cannot be forced on someone and still be received.
- *Rejoinder.* Care respects refusal, but it never stops offering. The teaching that death fixes the will ends the offer, and the text admits that this is teaching, "not something this case shows."
- **Held** as an open concession. It is a heavy standoff, honestly entered on the scale.

**E8. The infants of 1 Samuel 15.**
- *Attack.* The conclusion's test ("which are you surer of...") is our own test of what care knows. Applied to Amalek's infants, it tells the reader to be surer they were wronged than of St. Thomas's argument that they were not. The book's method then cuts against one of its own claims.
- *Defense.* The text says this outright: for such a reader "it is a cost against the case, not only a mystery." It limits the claim to God's right as giver and keeper of life, and denies any human a right of his own.
- **Held** as a declared cost. This is not a defect in honesty, but it is a real weight against the case for my school's readers.

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**As written: NONE.**

My school's strongest pressures are real. The God who "wanted" the infant did not take her up (E2). Hell is the carer who could and does not (E7). Amalek's infants (E8). Two deaths in the obstetric case (E3). The text either concedes each one openly as a cost on the evil side, or labels it a book-only claim. None of them shows the text misstates, overclaims or contradicts itself. They are weights, and the text has already put them on the scale.

**Minor items**
1. The C care row compresses the argument from universal dependency into a sentence that reads like a non sequitur ("since that dependence is universal") (R1).
2. "What falls short is the life she can live" frames the severely disabled life as a lack. The book's own view does not require that (R4).
3. The carers' clause in W1(2) is thinner than the book's logic supports, given how far it draws its absolute lines (R3).
4. "The one relation no one can refuse her" is ambiguous, given the book's teaching that she may refuse God (R5).

**Misstatements of my school.** None substantive. There are omissions:
- Noddings's ethical caring is missing. Without it, care can be heard as resting on attraction (A4).
- Gilligan and Held are not given distinct voices.
- Kittay's case of her own daughter, the strongest secular testimony for A4 at the severely disabled, goes unused.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Whether "she ought to have been wanted" needs grounding in an actual wanting that gives her being. This is W2's question about where explanation stops.
- Whether care given after death counts as care given to her (E2).
- Whether the difference between intending and foreseeing carries the obstetric case (E3).
- Whether permitting a final refusal can be caring (E7).
- Whether the embryo falls under A4 (many care ethicists deny it). The case's shared weight rests on the newborn.

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1 (C: Care between persons).**
Old: "Its best defenders argue that no one's standing can rest on powers some never have, since that dependence is universal;"
New: "Its best defenders argue that no one's standing can rest on powers some never have and all of us lack for a time, since each of us began, and many will end, wholly in others' hands;"

**R2 (C: Care between persons, optional; after "has failed a care it owed her together.").**
Insert: "And when no one is drawn to her, Noddings answers that the memory of having been cared for, and the ideal of oneself as one who cares, still bind whoever finds her (verify); that grounds the duty in the carer's ideal, where Kittay and Held ground it in her need."

**R3 (W1(2)).**
Old: "and those who carry that care must not be left to carry it alone (CCC 2208–2209)."
New: "and those who carry that care must not be left to carry it alone (CCC 2208–2209): the worth that forbids aiming at her death binds the rest of us to those who hold her, and a people that keeps the line and leaves its carers alone has not kept it."

**R4 (C: The flourishing of our kind).**
Old: "and what falls short is the life she can live, not what she is."
New: "and what is lacking is a power, not what she is, nor the worth of the life she lives, which has goods of its own."

**R5 (C: A God who is Love).**
Old: "Against the ethics of care, it is the one relation no one can refuse her;"
New: "Against the ethics of care, it is the one relation no one can deny her, though she may refuse it;"

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**New insights**
1. **The book's ground of dignity is itself a care-relation.** Her worth is given by being willed for her own sake by One who gives her being. That is care ethics' central claim, that worth is first received in being wanted, taken all the way down. Saying so openly would make care-minded readers the book's nearest allies rather than one rival among many: care is right about how worth is first received, and the book claims that this is true at the root.
2. **Noddings's completion criterion supports W1(1).** If care is completed only in the cared-for's reception, then care for the dead is completed only if she is still someone. Spe Salvi 48's "reciprocal giving and receiving" beyond death (quote the encyclical, not Noddings) is the Catholic answer to that exact need.
3. **Kittay as a witness for A4.** Kittay pressed the case of the severely cognitively disabled against Singer and McMahan, at the 2008 Stony Brook conference among other places (verify), as a mother who knows it from the inside. Her work shows that A4 at the severely disabled survives the most informed secular reflection. That is evidence for A's test of trust, offered by an opponent.
4. **The newborn who will die.** Perinatal palliative care, and Samaritanus Bonus on prenatal and paediatric care (V.6, verify), are where the Church and care ethics meet most concretely. They illustrate "owed care and not only spared harm."
5. **Tronto's "privileged irresponsibility"** (verify) is a precise secular name for one way of sharing in structures of sin: living comfortably on care work one never sees (A2; CCC 1868).
6. **The unwanted newborn under "reason's own law."** Kant's Doctrine of Right lets the state's protection lapse for the illegitimate newborn killed by her mother for honour's sake (6:336, verify). It shows that being unwanted is what most endangers the newborn even inside a theory of dignity. The book's "every child was wanted by the One who made her" answers that very danger.
7. **Ashley X and CCC 2297** give a concrete case where the book and care ethics reach the same verdict on different grounds. It would be useful in Chapter 11 on prudence.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "Where instinct fails, for the infant no one is drawn to hold, we learn whether what we meant was worth or only attraction."
- "This book's answer has the shape of care's first act... not of its next, for He did not take her up from the hillside." It is the most honest sentence a theist has written to my school.
- "Not every child is wanted by those who conceived her; every child was wanted by the One who made her, before anyone saw her."
- "Equal worth is not equal duty."
- "The hands that pull a child from the fire are part of what He made real, so His permission never excuses ours."
- "Only a good brought from that very evil within her own life... so that what was done to her is answered and not merely outweighed."
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it."

## 8. Score

**8.5 / 10.** The text states care ethics accurately, meets it at the newborn and the disabled with an answer that has the shape of care itself, and openly admits the one point where care's own test cuts deepest (He did not take her up). What is left for my school is weight on the scale of evil, not a flaw in the argument.

**Honest ceiling for any theistic case against care ethics: 9 / 10.** At the bottom the dispute is whether "she ought to have been cared for" needs a Mind and a Giver, which is a standoff over where explanation stops. And no theistic case can show, by reason alone, that the hillside infant receives in her own life the care that was owed her.
