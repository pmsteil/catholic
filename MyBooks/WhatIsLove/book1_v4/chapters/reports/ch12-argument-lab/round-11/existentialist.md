# Blind review: the atheist existentialist school

## 1. School and voice

I speak for the atheist existentialists: Sartre (existence precedes essence; there is no human nature because there is no God to conceive it; freedom is the one foundation of values, and anguish is what it feels like to know this), Camus (the absurd is the clash between our demand for meaning and the world's silence; the honest answer is lucidity and revolt without hope, and revolt shows a solidarity: "I rebel, therefore we exist," paraphrased, verify), and Beauvoir (the ethics of ambiguity: to will oneself free is to will others free; oppression is cutting off another's open future; there are no ready-made rules, only concrete situations taken on without excuses). I attack the case's starting points (that values are *found*) as well as its steps, and I try to be as fair to the text as I would want it to be to me.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** Every person is a freedom: a project thrown toward a future. My freedom needs a world opened by other freedoms, so (Beauvoir) to will myself free authentically is to will others free. What anyone is owed is not to be reduced to a thing: not to have their transcendence stopped by oppression, lies, or violence that treats them as an object. **Verdict: coherent, but it borrows.** The "is to" in "to will oneself free is to will others free" is a requirement no freedom chose, and it binds before anyone chooses. That is the text's point (C, Freedom) and it is fair. In this one respect my school is a realism about form, as Kant's is.

**2. A hard dilemma.** Sartre's own case is the student who must choose between staying with his dependent mother and joining the Free French. Sartre's answer was that no principle decides it: you are free, so choose, which means invent. Camus's case, in *The Just Assassins*, is sharper. Kaliayev will not throw the bomb because the Grand Duke's carriage holds children, and a rebel who kills must be ready to pay with his own life. The guiding principles are lucidity (no bad faith), refusing to treat a freedom as a thing, and revolt that respects the limit it was raised to defend. **Verdict: Sartre's answer does not borrow, but it does not resolve the case. Camus's does resolve it, and it borrows.** His "limit" is found rather than made, and he admits it points toward a human nature. So either I resolve the case and borrow something close to A6, or I stay pure and leave it unresolved.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** Freedom has no degrees. Every consciousness is wholly a for-itself: the newborn already is one, at a pre-reflective level; so are the severely disabled person who is conscious, the dying person until the end, and the enemy (even the oppressor is a freedom, which is why Beauvoir says he must not be made a thing even while he is resisted). **Verdict: holds for every conscious human being, and holds equally. It breaks in two places.** (a) The never-conscious and the never-again-conscious have only the worth our freedom gives them. (b) "Unconditional" in the found sense: dignity is a value we commit to, not one we discover, and Sartre and Beauvoir let it bend for liberation (Sartre's preface to Fanon). Camus held the line on the innocent more firmly.

**4. Ending point for flourishing.** There is no final good waiting for us. What is left is freedom willing itself and other freedoms, lived with lucidity, revolt, solidarity and measure (Camus's "thought at the meridian," paraphrased, verify). In Camus's image, one must imagine Sisyphus happy. **Verdict: coherent as a place to stop, and honest about what it cannot do.** It gives no one back the dead, and it treats the wish that they be given back as the absurd itself, not as a clue. This is where my school is at its strongest, and also where it is barest.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1: the starting point (the A heading).**
*Attack.* The A heading's rule is to trust what still seems true after honest reflection. That rule builds in what Sartre and Beauvoir call the *spirit of seriousness*: treating values as properties of things that make demands on us, when in fact they light up only inside a project I have chosen. Sartre's alarm-clock passage (*Being and Nothingness*, the section on anguish, paraphrased, verify) describes what reflection actually finds behind a value: nothing but my freedom. So the rule favors the believer's phenomenology from the start.
*Defense.* The rule is not "trust your feelings." It is to trust what survives reflection, including reflection on how the belief arose, until something more certain defeats it. It names Sartre among the deniers ("that any is found rather than made by freedom"). Sartre's own practice testifies against his theory: he and Beauvoir condemned the torture in Algeria whatever France approved (C, Freedom), and they did not condemn it as a project they happened to have chosen. Your phenomenology is one seeming among others, and it has to be weighed with the rest.
*Rejoinder.* Sartre would say that condemning torture is a commitment made in the name of all ("in choosing for myself I choose for all," paraphrased, verify). It is universal in form and chosen in source. The phenomenologies really do diverge.
**Held.** The text claims only that A is offered as true and that "none is beyond denial." It does not claim the seeming compels anyone. At this point the case and my school stand at parity, and the text says so.

**Exchange 2: "it even grants the hinge" (A5).**
*Attack.* The text turns Sartre's conditional around. Sartre argues: no God, therefore no nature we are for. The text argues: we have a nature we are for, therefore God. But nothing in our experience shows a nature we are *for*. Human lives differ without limit, and Aquinas's list in A5 is what one tradition chose to put first.
*Defense.* A5 claims only what Sartre himself holds: what we can do and what we want do not settle what we ought to do. A5 explicitly hands the question of whether our nature carries its own ends to W. The text uses Sartre's conditional only to show what is at stake (C, Freedom), not as a premise.
*Rejoinder.* Fair enough. The conditional is contested anyway, by Camus's suspicion of a human nature and by secular Aristotelians who hold that nature carries ends without a God. So the "hinge" cuts against my school as much as for the text.
**Held.** The text uses the conditional accurately and leans on it no more than it can bear.

**Exchange 3: "a will that invents values can command, but it cannot bind" (W1 closing).**
*Attack.* That sentence is aimed at me, and it assumes what is in dispute. Wanting a law that binds before anyone chooses is the "useless passion" Sartre diagnosed: the wish to have one's values guaranteed by being, to be in-itself-for-itself, which is to wish to be God. The Lawgiver is that wish projected outward.
*Defense.* That is a genealogy. By the brief's fairness rule, and the text's own (A heading; C, Power and rank), it can be used only if the text may say the same of you: that the doctrine of self-made values is the wish to owe no one anything, projected outward. Neither story settles anything. On the claim itself, Sartre's phenomenology supports the text. His gambler, who resolved yesterday never to gamble again, finds tonight that the resolution has no hold on his freedom (*Being and Nothingness*, anguish, paraphrased, verify). A self-invented value does not bind the self that invented it. That is exactly what the sentence says.
*Rejoinder.* I grant it. For Sartre, the gambler's freedom is what anguish shows. For the text, it is the reason a value has to answer to something beyond the will that holds it. The phenomenon is the same, and our readings of it part.
**Held.** The text's claim is one my school's own analysis confirms. The book would gain by citing it (section 7).

**Exchange 4: the binding form and A7 (C, Freedom; W2).**
*Attack.* The text says Beauvoir's requirement to will others free "is made by no freedom and binds every freedom before it chooses, as a law binds." But that is just the structure of freedom: what willing freedom *means*, not a law imposed from outside. It needs no Mind behind it, any more than Kant's form of the will does.
*Defense.* Then you have conceded that at least one moral truth is found, not made, and "values are made, not found" holds only for content. Your school's first fault is not a chosen value either. Bad faith, the lie to oneself about one's own freedom, is the vice Sartre names first. Camus charged Kierkegaard, Chestov and Husserl with "philosophical suicide" for leaping past the absurd (*The Myth of Sisyphus*, paraphrased, verify). Both charges bind the one charged whether or not he has chosen lucidity. That is A7: we are bound to the truth even when a falsehood would comfort us. Your ethic is built on it.
*Rejoinder.* Camus would answer that lucidity is a fidelity he keeps, not a law. But then he would have no standing to fault the leapers, and he does fault them. So I concede the point: at bottom my school is a realism about the form of freedom and the duty of lucidity. Whether those need a Source is the dispute the text puts to Kant, and there we stand at parity.
**Held, with a small repair.** The text's row sends this question to "W2's question." But W2's own parenthesis says Kantians "need no meeting of law and mind; theirs is the question in C." My school's form is Kantian in structure, so the cross-reference should point to the Kant row's question: why there are beings at all whose own law it is. The row should also name bad faith and philosophical suicide as A7 in my school's own voice (section 6).

**Exchange 5: equal worth and the hillside (A4; W1(2); conclusion).**
*Attack.* The conclusion's test is the newborn left on the hillside. My school passes it, and the text admits as much: "the newborn on the hillside is wronged as fully as you." Freedom has no degrees, so her worth does not follow "her strength, or her mind, or how much she can feel or want." Yet the conclusion moves straight from that test to "it follows what she is *and what she is made for*." The test shows only that worth follows something that does not come in degrees. Freedom qualifies, and so do Kant's rational nature by kind and the Aristotelian's form. "Made for" is not established by the test.
*Defense.* The conclusion speaks to "the one who gives up A4." That is not you. The step to "made for" is introduced with "this book answers," so it is marked as the book's answer, and it is argued in W1(2). The text puts the real cost to your school where it belongs: the one "who never will be, or never again will be, conscious" (C, Freedom). That is a case the hillside test was not built to decide.
*Rejoinder.* Agreed. The cost is real but narrow, and it falls where intuitions are least settled. A reader who skims could still take the hillside test to establish teleology. A cross-reference would block that misreading.
**Held, with a small repair (Minor).**

**Exchange 6: the longing and the debt to the dead are the absurd, not clues (W1(1), W3).**
*Attack.* Guilt that asks the dead to forgive, and the longing to be known wholly by someone who will not die, are exactly Camus's absurd: the human demand meeting the world's silence. To read them as clues is the first step of philosophical suicide. Tragedy, a debt that can never be received, is not incoherence. It is the truth.
*Defense.* The text does not leap. W1(1) says that guilt's asking "proves nothing, for a mind made for living bonds would ask whether or not she hears." W3 says the longing is "not evidence, for we may not believe a thing because it comforts (A7)." The evil section says "a dark god or an indifferent world would leave them just as we find them." The text claims only fit: it weighs as much as the reader finds that a debt truly owed must one day be receivable. Camus may decline that premise, and the text tells him he may.
*Rejoinder.* Then the case against me rests on W1(2) and W2, not on W1(1) or W3. The text says so itself ("clues of fit, not evidence," C, A God who is Love). I have nothing left to press here.
**Held.** The text's own A7 disarms the charge of philosophical suicide. This is one of the most honest parts of the case.

**Exchange 7: the Look (W3).**
*Attack.* W3 replies to Sartre that his analysis is not true of "the longing to be given being... by One who is no rival beside us." But Sartre's deeper point is the Look. To be seen wholly by another is to become an object under a gaze I cannot return. God is that Other taken to the limit: the absolute subject before whom I am an object forever (*Being and Nothingness*, Part III, paraphrased, verify). To long to be known wholly is to long for my own reduction to an object.
*Defense.* The Look objectifies because the one looking is a rival freedom, another for-itself beside me, competing for the same ground. The Creator is not a being alongside me. He gives my freedom its being. St. Thomas: God, by moving voluntary causes, does not take away their being voluntary; He is the cause of it in them (ST I q.83 a.1 ad 3). He is the first cause, who works in and through secondary causes (CCC 308). A gaze that makes my freedom be cannot freeze it. And the text's "One who is no rival beside us" already says this in brief.
*Rejoinder.* That is a metaphysical claim about God that phenomenology cannot check. But it does answer the argument rather than ignore it, and it answers at parity. The text would be stronger if it named the Look, since that is the strongest form of Sartre's objection.
**Held, with a small repair (Minor).**

**Exchange 8: Abraham, the instrument, and 1 Samuel 15 (W1(2); evil section).**
*Attack.* Sartre's Abraham (in *Existentialism Is a Humanism*): even if an angel speaks, I must decide that it is an angel and that I will obey. Responsibility cannot be passed up the chain. "One who carried out such a command would act as the instrument of God's right, not by any right of his own" has the structure of the executioner's bad faith: I was only the instrument.
*Defense.* Aquinas does not shift responsibility. He shifts justification. The one who obeys is still the agent, and his act is just only because the Lord of life has dominion over life. The text also closes the door now: "No one may now claim such a command" (CCC 67). For every living agent, then, the practical result is Sartre's own: refuse the voice. The text names the cost openly, against Kant, Mencius and the Way, and it leaves the wars "on the other side of the scale," adding that for the reader weighing by reason alone "it is a cost against the case."
*Rejoinder.* Camus would say that a God who could rightly command the killing of infants is the God metaphysical revolt is right to accuse. The text does not deny that the reader may weigh it that way. A standoff, honestly recorded.
**Held (as a standoff the text records itself).**

**Exchange 9: the child, Rieux and Paneloux (evil section, invitation).**
*Attack.* In *The Plague*, after the magistrate's son dies, Dr. Rieux refuses to love a scheme of things in which children are tortured (paraphrased, verify). Paneloux answers that we must will what we cannot understand. The text's invitation, "follow that protest to its source," turns my revolt into evidence for the One I am revolting against. If the standard I protest by is God's own, then God is the one protesting against God's world.
*Defense.* The text does not take Paneloux's line. It never asks anyone to will the child's suffering. "The evil never thereby becomes good" (CCC 312). God "says no to what was done to her" (Wisdom 1:13). "His permission never excuses ours" (CCC 2269), so no one is led into quietism. It grants that the protest "does not refute himself" and is "this weight at its strongest." On the Christian account, God does share the protest, and on the Cross He suffered with the one it is made for. The invitation is offered "as an invitation, not a reply."
*Rejoinder.* As an invitation it is fair: it asks what makes my protest *true*, and I owe the question an answer. My answer is the solidarity that revolt itself reveals. The text's answer is a Mind. A standoff.
**Held.** The evil section's candor ("for the fawn that hope is thinner... and this book does not pretend otherwise") is beyond what my school usually meets.

## 4. Ledger

- **A heading.** Holds. It names Sartre's denial accurately ("found rather than made by freedom"). Its epistemic rule allows my phenomenology to be weighed and does not presume against it. The two parenthetical routings (W2 for the one who says no Mind is needed; A4 and the conclusion for the one who says nothing binds) are fair.
- **A1.** Holds as offered. Sartre denies that any value is found, yet Sartre and Beauvoir condemned torture in Algeria as wrong whatever was approved, which is a real point against my school's theory, used fairly.
- **A2.** Holds. My school can say guilt faces a person, since the wronged one is a freedom I made into a thing. The secret example works against my school too: the wrong is real even though no freedom experiences anything worse.
- **A3.** Holds. Beauvoir would agree that no vote can turn oppression into justice. That the debt outlives the victim, my school leaves to the absurd.
- **A4.** Holds as offered, with its book-only claims clearly marked. My school agrees on every conscious human being and parts on the never-conscious and on "unconditional" in the found sense.
- **A5.** Holds. Its first claim is one Sartre affirms. It sends the teleological question to W rather than assuming it.
- **A6.** Holds. It honestly reports that my school's masters let the line bend (C). The firefighter and suicide distinctions are clearly marked as the book's own claims.
- **A7.** Holds, and it is the text's strongest point against my school: lucidity and the refusal of bad faith are unchosen demands at the heart of existentialist ethics.
- **W1 frame.** Holds. Anscombe is fairly reported. My school keeps "wrong," but not in the sense of "wrong of necessity," so the frame excludes it by an honest definition. The offense-against-God clause is careful (Job 35:6–8; SCG III.122).
- **W1(1).** Holds. The three roads are fair, and my school walks the first or the second (Sartre: the dead are at the mercy of the living, paraphrased, verify). It explicitly claims only a clue. Camus's absurd is answered by its own concession that "that guilt asks proves nothing."
- **W1(2).** Holds, with cost recorded. The grounding of equal worth in being willed for one's own sake is the case's real argument against my school, and it does not overclaim. The 1 Samuel 15 doctrine is stated accurately (ST I-II q.94 a.5 ad 2; q.100 a.8 ad 3; II-II q.64 a.6 ad 1) and closed off for now by CCC 67. Its cost is paid openly.

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**As written: NONE.**

I find no Fatal or Serious objection. My school's deepest challenge, that values are made and not found, is named by the text itself, weighed fairly, and left at parity. The text's best points against my school (A7 and the unchosen form of freedom) are sound, and my school's own literature confirms them.

**Minor items**
1. *Cross-reference mismatch (C, Freedom).* "Whence that form binds is W2's question" conflicts with W2's own parenthesis, which says Kantians "need no meeting of law and mind; theirs is the question in C." My school's form is Kantian in structure, so the question belongs to the Kant row's "why there are beings at all whose own law it is."
2. *The Look goes unnamed (W3).* The reply to Sartre is correct but answers his weaker form of the objection. Named and answered, it stands at parity.
3. *The conclusion's step to "made for."* "This book answers" already flags it, but the hillside test cannot tell several rivals (mine included) apart from the theist. A pointer to W1(2) would prevent a teleology reading.
4. *Missed confirmation (W1 closing).* Sartre's gambler supports the text's claim about self-invented values. It is not an error, only a missed witness.

**Misstatements of my school.** None of substance. "Only a Creator, Sartre held, could give us a nature we are *for*" is a fair reading of the paper-knife passage in *Existentialism Is a Humanism*. "Its masters let the line bend for liberation" is fair for Sartre (the Fanon preface) and for Beauvoir (*The Ethics of Ambiguity* on violence), and the text rightly sets Camus apart. One attribution to verify: "Beauvoir counts the child a freedom not yet taken up, so the newborn on the hillside is wronged as fully as you." The first half is a fair paraphrase of her account of childhood. The "so" is the text's inference on her behalf. It is generous to her, and arguably more generous than her public positions on the unborn would support, but it is not unfair to my school.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Phenomenology of value: found (A) or projected (Sartre). Neither side can compel the other.
- Where explanation stops: the form of freedom and the duty of lucidity as brute, or held first in a Mind (W2 / the Kant row).
- The longing and the debt to the dead: clue of fit (the text) or the absurd (Camus). The text claims no more than a clue.
- The God of 1 Samuel 15 and of hell: the text keeps both on the scale and grants that the rational reader may count them as costs.
- The protest on the child's behalf: does its truth rest on solidarity or on a Source? The invitation is fair and the question is open.

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**New insights**
- **Existentialism concedes A7.** The whole existentialist ethic stands on an unchosen duty of lucidity: the refusal of bad faith (Sartre), and the charge of philosophical suicide against those who leap (Camus). This is the cleanest place to show the Nietzsche dilemma working on a second rival. The point is made without any genealogy, so it is fair under the book's own rule.
- **Sartre's gambler** independently confirms "a will that invents values... cannot bind." It is the rare case where a rival's own phenomenology supplies a premise.
- **Rieux beside Ivan.** In *The Plague*, Camus gives the protest with a priest beside it: Paneloux's second sermon asks us to will what we cannot understand, and Rieux refuses. The evil section already avoids Paneloux's error ("answered and not merely outweighed," CCC 312). Naming Camus there, in paraphrase only, would show the reader that the book has heard the strongest atheist novel on the suffering child and does not answer it the way the priest in the novel did.
- **Camus as the nearest neighbor.** The text is right that Camus "stood nearer to us." In *The Just Assassins*, Kaliayev refuses to kill children for the cause. A secular voice that holds the line on the innocent where Sartre let it bend would strengthen A6's witness list, though Camus offers it as a limit felt in revolt, not as a theory.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "The rebel can take her side; he cannot keep her."
- "A will that invents values can command, but it cannot bind."
- "Whoever protests against God on the suffering child's behalf does not refute himself; his protest is this weight at its strongest."
- "So that what was done to her is answered and not merely outweighed... and that is a hope, not a proof."
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it." (This is the exact line my school must contest. It is well placed and fairly stated.)
- "For the fawn that hope is thinner than it is for the child, and this book does not pretend otherwise."

## 8. Score

**8.5 / 10.** Against the atheist existentialist the text is accurate, does not overclaim, and finds the real fault line in my school: an unchosen form of freedom and an unchosen duty of lucidity. It leaves my deepest denial, that values are made, at an honestly recorded standoff, not a refutation.

**Honest ceiling for any theistic case against my school: about 9.** The dispute bottoms out in a clash of phenomenologies (value found, or value projected) and in where explanation may stop. Argument can show that my school borrows a found form; it cannot compel me to locate that form in a Mind.
