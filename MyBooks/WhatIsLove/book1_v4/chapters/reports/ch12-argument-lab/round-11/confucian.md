# Blind review: the Confucian school

## 1. School and voice

I speak for the Ru (Confucian) tradition, both classical and Neo-Confucian: Confucius, Mencius and Xunzi; Zhang Zai, the Cheng brothers and Zhu Xi; Wang Yangming; and the modern New Confucians, above all Mou Zongsan and Tu Weiming, along with Anglophone scholars such as P. J. Ivanhoe, Stephen Angle, Irene Bloom, Bryan Van Norden, and Roger Ames and Henry Rosemont (role ethics). I argue as a non-theist. The ground is Heaven (*tian*), read either as principle (*li*), as Zhu Xi reads it, or as the moral heart-mind (*xin*), as Wang and Mou read it. The ethics rests on humaneness (*ren*), rightness (*yi*), ritual propriety (*li* 禮), wisdom (*zhi*) and sincerity (*cheng*). The good is achieved by cultivation within real relationships, ends in "one body with Heaven, Earth and the myriad things," and needs no Creator who wills. The text treats my school more seriously and more accurately than most Christian apologetics does. That makes it a harder target, and I have tried to be harder in return.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** Justice starts from the heart of shame and disdain (*xiuwu zhi xin*, *Mencius* 2A6). This is the sprout of rightness (*yi*), what is fitting toward this person in this relation. Reciprocity (*shu*: "what you do not want, do not do to others," *Analects* 15.24) and the extension (*tui*) of the heart from near to far (*Mencius* 1A7) carry it outward. What anyone is owed is set by the relation, plus a floor owed to every human: never kill one innocent (*Mencius* 2A2, 7A33). Xunzi adds ritual distinctions (*fen*) that apportion goods so that people can live together (*Xunzi* 19). **Verdict: holds.** It borrows nothing Christian. The strain shows with the stranger outside every relation, where "owed" rests on Zhang Zai's kinship-of-all (*Western Inscription*), which is a metaphysical claim and not an observation.

**2. A hard dilemma.** *Mencius* 7A35: Shun is Son of Heaven, and his father Gusou commits murder. Mencius rules that the judge Gao Yao must arrest Gusou and that Shun may not forbid it, because the law is not Shun's to bend. Shun casts off the empire "like a worn sandal," carries his father away on his back and lives by the sea. The principles at work are *yi* (the office may not be corrupted), filial *ren* (the son may not abandon his father), and *quan*, discretionary weighing (compare *Mencius* 4A17: rites forbid touching a sister-in-law's hand, but you pull her out when she is drowning). The cost falls on Shun himself, who gives up the throne. **Verdict: holds without borrowing** the book's definitions; *cheng*, *yi*, *shu* and "giving one's life to complete *ren*" (*Analects* 15.9) are our own. **It breaks at one point:** Mencius never asks what is owed to the murdered man and his family. This is exactly the gap the text's A2 and W1 press, and I concede it.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** Mencius holds that every person has in himself a nobility (*liang gui*) that others did not confer and cannot take away (*Mencius* 6A16–17, "heavenly nobility"; see Irene Bloom on Mencian dignity, verify). For Zhu Xi, every person carries the whole of principle, however clouded the endowment of *qi*. The child at the well calls out alarm in anyone (*Mencius* 2A6), and the call comes from her vulnerability, not her charm. **Verdict: holds for the newborn, the dying and the disabled. It wobbles for the enemy and the tyrant** (*Mencius* 1B8: the tyrant is "a mere fellow"; 4B19: what separates human from beast is slight, and the common run discards it). That strand reads as achieved dignity. My repair is the text's own Dignitas Infinita distinction (DI 7, 22) between ontological dignity, which cannot be lost, and moral dignity, which can. **It borrows** in one place: Mencius's "what Heaven gave us" (*Mencius* 6A15) is ambiguous between gift and nature. A naturalist Confucian has to read it as nature, and then the question "why does this kind carry such worth?" ends at Zhou Dunyi's answer that humans receive the finest *qi* and are the most spiritual of things. That answer is capacity-shaped.

**4. Ultimate good.** To become fully human (*cheng ren*) and finally a sage. To complete oneself and others through sincerity (*Zhongyong* 25), to form a triad with Heaven and Earth (*Zhongyong* 22), and to be one body with all things (Cheng Hao; Wang Yangming, *Inquiry on the Great Learning*). The guiding principles are *ren, yi, li, zhi, xin*, and the sequence of the *Great Learning* from cultivating oneself to bringing peace to the world. **Verdict: coherent, but it breaks at the margin.** The end is an achievement. The child who dies in infancy and the person who will never reason cannot reach it. We can say only that she is cared for within the whole and completes others' *ren*, which edges toward making her good serve others. This is the same standoff the text records with the Aristotelian, over gift and not over worth.

## 3. Debate highlights

**E1. Ritual and the dead (W1(1)).**
*Attack.* The text gives Xunzi the "second road" and says the rites "do not reach her." That treats ritual as therapy for mourners. For the Ru, ritual constitutes the bond. One serves the dead as one served the living (*Xunzi* 19), sacrifices as though the spirit were present (*Analects* 3.12), and attends carefully to the end so that the people's virtue thickens (*Analects* 1.9). The demand that guilt be "forgiven by her" is a juridical, person-to-person picture of guilt. Ours is forward-looking correction of the fault (*Analects* 15.30) and the repair of the web she belonged to.
*Defense.* The text already marks this as a clue, weighing "as much as the reader finds that a debt truly owed to her is one she must be able, one day, to receive," and states the condition openly. It also reports Xunzi correctly: the gentleman keeps the rites as the human way without holding that the dead hear (*Xunzi* 19: for the gentleman it is the human way, for the common people it is serving ghosts). If she does not hear, then whatever the rite does, it does not reach her as someone who can answer.
*Rejoinder.* That is fair, but "does not reach her" claims more than "she is not there to answer." It assumes that she is only the individual who died, when on our account she is partly constituted by relations the rites keep.
**Held with repair** (wording in §6).

**E2. "No eye through which that Heaven sees" (C, sacred order).**
*Attack.* *Mencius* 5A5 quotes the Zhou "Great Declaration" (Heaven sees as the people see) in a political argument about how the mandate is shown. It is not a theory of what Zhu Xi's principle can perceive. The text uses the Zhou Heaven, which it later concedes "was nearer a Someone," to show that Zhu Xi's impersonal principle cannot see the abandoned infant. That runs two Heavens together. Zhu Xi also explicitly granted that Heaven and Earth have a mind. Without one, he said, an ox might give birth to a horse. It is only a mind that does not deliberate as a person does (*Zhuzi yulei* 1, verify). And his "the heart of Heaven and Earth is to give life" (*Ren shuo*) is that mind.
*Defense.* The quotation is accurate, and so is the contrast the text actually needs. Zhu Xi's principle has "no feeling or intention, makes no plans." A non-deliberating mind whose aim is life in general does not want this one. Aquinas's God does not deliberate discursively either, so the difference is not deliberation but whether particulars are known and willed. The text also brings in Mou to refuse the word "impersonal."
*Rejoinder.* The substantive contrast survives. The 5A5 inference does not, and the row should give Zhu Xi's own two-sided answer about the mind of Heaven and Earth instead of only his denial.
**Failed as written (Minor)**; repaired in §6.

**E3. Equal worth needs no Giver (A4, W1(2)).**
*Attack.* Mencius's inner nobility and Zhu Xi's moon whole in ten thousand rivers ground worth in nature, not capacity. Your conclusion grounds worth in "what she is and what she is made for." Then "wanted for her own sake" adds nothing to the ground. It is ornament.
*Defense.* The text concedes the first half: worth follows nature, "taught outside Christianity too (the sacred order; the Dhamma)." With the Aristotelian it calls the parting "over gift and hope, not over what she is worth." Its claim for "wanted" is not that it grounds worth. It explains two further things: why the kind exists with an end that does not wait on her powers, and why she may not be spent for others.
*Rejoinder.* Then against my school A4 is at parity, and the text's advantage has to come from the line (E4) and from reach (E5).
**Held.** The text does not overclaim here, though the sacred-order row should state the parity as plainly as the Aristotelian row does (§6).

**E4. The line at every number: one body versus "willed for her own sake."**
*Attack.* Mencius holds the line against the whole realm (2A2) and says killing one innocent "is not *ren*" (7A33). In Wang Yangming's one-body humaneness, killing her cuts off a limb of one's own body. That is not aggregation, and the line needs no Someone.
*Defense.* Wang's own text undoes this. Asked why the great man who is one body with all things still loves with gradations, he says one uses the hands and feet to shield the head and eyes, feeds plants to animals and animals to parents, and that principle is simply so (*Chuanxi lu* III, Chan §276, verify). An organic whole legitimately sacrifices lesser parts for greater ones. That is exactly the text's claim: "a law or an order gives a nature to every member of a kind; it does not want any one of them." Mencius 2A2 is phrased as what the sages would not do, which is a reason from the agent's integrity and not from her claim. The text grants that kind of reason to the Theravādin ("a real reason, not a count").
*Rejoinder.* Ren in 7A33 answers to her: the alarm at the well is for the child, not for my integrity. The *Classic of Filial Piety* (ch. 1) also gives a non-theistic version of "not hers to waive": the body is received from parents and may not be harmed. That trust-structure mirrors CCC 2280.
*Counter.* A trust held from parents fails exactly where the text's hardest case sits, the child her parents did not want. Zhang Zai's extension to Heaven and Earth as parents brings back an impersonal Trustor that "does not want." Our tradition has also honored suicide for righteousness, so it does not hold the line against a man's own consent.
**Held.** The ground of the line is a real standoff, but the text's narrower claim, that an order as such does not want this one, is confirmed from inside my tradition by Wang's own passage. I gain the filial-trust parallel as an insight (§7).

**E5. One Source without Someone (W2).**
*Attack.* "One principle, many manifestations" (*li yi fen shu*) gives one source for the law, the minds that read it and the beings it governs. What Heaven decrees is the nature (*Zhongyong* 1). Fully realizing the heart, one knows the nature, and knowing the nature, one knows Heaven (*Mencius* 7A1). The investigation of things works because the principle in the mind is the principle in things. W2 does not favor God over Zhu Xi.
*Defense.* The text says so itself: Wang and Mou "agree with this witness," and "W1 and W3 give the reasons to think that Source is Someone." Its final C row restricts its claim against the sacred order to the One "that wills each of us for her own sake," and calls W1(1) and W3 "clues of fit, not evidence."
*Rejoinder.* Then, against my school, the case's distinctive weight rests on W1(2)'s "wanted," which is at a standoff (E4), plus two admitted clues, set against evil, which is admitted to be evidence. The conclusion holds only as a judgment that weighs these, not as a demonstration.
**Held.** The text calls it a judgment and invites readers to weigh it differently. That is parity, not defeat.

**E6. Mencius 7B3 and 1 Samuel 15.**
*Attack.* Mencius set a rule: rather than believe that the most humane made war until blood floated the pestles, distrust the book. Your own conclusion's test ("which are you surer of?") yields the same verdict on the infants of Amalek. On the Ru account there is no possible commander for whom killing the infant is right. On yours there is, so your ground for A4 is less absolute than ours.
*Defense.* The text pays this openly in three places. It says Mencius "draws the line more absolutely than this book." It says the reader "may be surer that those infants were wronged than of any argument that they were not... for him it is a cost against the case, not only a mystery." And it states the Thomist reason: He "who gives life its being, and can give it back, does no one wrong when He calls a life home," while we can only take it. A4 is stated as "by any of us," so the line is internally consistent, and CCC 67 forecloses any present claim to such a command.
*Rejoinder.* The Thomist move makes "what it does to her" depend on who can restore her. That is coherent on its premises and unconvincing on mine. Since the text has already put it on the scale, it is a conceded cost and not a hidden defect.
**Held** (as a booked cost; a standoff at parity).

**E7. Evil, and those who bear it more easily.**
*Attack.* The Ru meets suffering through *ming*. One does what is right and accepts what Heaven decrees, and so stands under a "correct decree" (*Mencius* 7A1–2). Asking a principle that makes no plans to account for a child's death is a category mistake. The evil section lists the Buddhist, Nietzsche, the Way and Spinoza as bearing the weight more easily, and leaves us out.
*Defense.* The sacred-order row covers this: such an order "is free of the problem of evil only if it intends no one in particular," and "the more such an order cares, the more it faces our question," with Sima Qian's lament on Bo Yi and Robber Zhi (*Shiji* 61). That is accurate and sharp.
*Rejoinder.* Agreed. The omission from the evil section's list is still an asymmetry.
**Held with repair** (§6).

**E8. The longing to be known (W3) and the relational self.**
*Attack.* Confucius's longing to be known was answered by Heaven (*Analects* 14.35). On Tu Weiming's reading of immanent transcendence (verify), that is the self's own depth realized in sincerity. Role ethics (Ames and Rosemont) says there is no "whole of us" behind the roles waiting to be received.
*Defense.* The text anticipates this precisely: "even if we are made of our relationships, the longing remains, to be received whole, across every role and every year, by someone who will not die." Its account of Mou ("knows as my own deepest heart, not as another who turns to me") is fair. And the text claims only a clue.
*Rejoinder.* None at full strength. I can deny the longing reaches past the roles, but that is a standoff about phenomenology.
**Held.**

## 4. Ledger

- **A (heading and epistemic rule):** Holds. The defeater clause for genealogies is fair and symmetric. No Confucian objection.
- **A1:** Holds. Mencius and Xunzi agree that torturing a child is wrong whatever a culture approves.
- **A2:** Holds. "Shame that looks only at itself" is carefully qualified, and Confucian *chi* is not that kind of shame. A note that Confucian shame also faces persons would be generous but is not required.
- **A3:** Holds, with a standoff. We do not frame repair as a pardon only she can give, but we agree that no vote can make the wrong right.
- **A4:** Holds. Mencius 6A17 and Zhu Xi support it from outside Christianity. The ox and the sheep (*Mencius* 1A7) illustrate its "what is before our eyes."
- **A5:** Holds. Xunzi and Mencius would both agree that capacity and desire do not settle the ought, Xunzi most emphatically.
- **A6:** Holds. The Mencius 2A2 citation and paraphrase are accurate (it is said of Bo Yi, Yi Yin and Confucius).
- **A7:** Holds. Sincerity (*cheng*) as "the way of Heaven" (*Zhongyong* 20) gives my school its own reason for A7 to bind.
- **W1 frame:** Holds. Accurate, and appropriately modest about what the absence of release decides.
- **W1(1):** Holds with repair. Xunzi is reported accurately. "It does not reach her" needs the narrower wording (§6).
- **W1(2):** Holds. Its central claim, that an order does not want any one of them, is confirmed by Wang's one-body triage passage. The citation of Mencius 7B3 is accurate.
- **W1 closing ("This Lawgiver is not a commander"):** Holds. No Confucian objection to the anti-voluntarist frame. It is close to our refusal of an arbitrary Heaven.
- **W2:** Holds. Wang and Mou are fairly placed. The concession that W2 does not separate theism from the one-Source views is honest.
- **W3 (longing):** Holds as a clue. It is precise against the relational self.
- **W3 (hiddenness):** Holds. "The Neo-Confucians... found a principle rather than a Person" is fair, and the refusal to call them culpable is right.

- **C What we owe each other:** Holds. No objection.
- **C Care between persons:** Holds. It is the row nearest to ours, and fair.
- **C Well-being, counted impartially:** Holds. Mencius's rejection of Mozi's impartial care parallels it.
- **C Flourishing of our kind:** Holds. Its gift-and-hope standoff is the right model for my school too.

It is largely accurate: the moon in the rivers, "the heart of Heaven and Earth is to give life," the *Western Inscription*, the *Analects* sayings, Mou, *qi* resisting *li*, Sima Qian. The 5A5 inference fails, Zhu Xi's mind of Heaven is half-stated, and parity on worth is not said aloud (§6).

- **Evil ¶1 (natural evil, hiddenness, hell, 1 Samuel 15, rivals who bear it more easily, free will, setting):** Holds with repair. The rivals' list omits the Confucian *ming* (E7). The 1 Samuel 15 cost is booked honestly.
- **Evil, First (privation):** Holds. It is close to Zhu Xi on evil as the obscuring of principle by *qi*.
- **Evil, Second (no trade):** Holds. Its candor about the parasite and the fawn is exemplary.

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**As written: NONE.**

No Fatal or Serious objection from my school stands. Every serious Confucian pressure point is either answered at parity (equal worth from nature, the ground of the line, one Source), or openly booked as a cost (1 Samuel 15 against Mencius 7B3), or explicitly restricted to "clue" (the debt to the dead, the longing to be known). The overall verdict is offered as a weighing, and my school weighs it differently. That is a standoff, not a defect.

**Minor:**
1. The *Mencius* 5A5 inference ("no eye through which that Heaven sees") applies the Zhou Heaven's political saying to Zhu Xi's principle. The two Heavens are run together (E2).
2. Zhu Xi is quoted only on the no-intention side. His own claim that Heaven and Earth do have a mind, a non-deliberating governing one whose aim is to give life, is omitted (E2).
3. The sacred-order row does not state the parity on worth that the Aristotelian row states ("over gift and hope, not over what she is worth"), although it grants that worth follows nature (E3).
4. The evil section's list of rivals who bear the weight more easily omits the Confucian, who meets it through *ming* (E7).
5. W1(1)'s "it does not reach her" is broader than the point needs (E1).
6. "In danger and in grief" cites *Analects* 7.23 and 14.35. The grief saying is 11.9 ("Heaven has bereft me," on Yan Hui's death); 14.35 is about not being known.

**Misstatements of my school:** Items 1 and 2 above, both minor. Everything else I checked is accurate: Mencius 2A2 and 7B3, the Xunzi rites, the moon in ten thousand rivers, "the heart of Heaven and Earth is to give life" and Zhu Xi calling it humaneness, the *Western Inscription*, Zhu Xi's rule that Heaven is principle, Mou's living moral heart and intellectual intuition, *qi* not steered by *li*, and the Sima Qian and *Daodejing* 79 pairing.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**
- Whether being *wanted* adds anything to a worth both sides ground in nature.
- Whether the line against killing the innocent is better grounded in her being willed for her own sake or in *ren*'s response to her. This is complicated for us by Wang's triage passage and for the book by 1 Samuel 15.
- Whether the debt to the dead must be receivable by her.
- Whether the longing to be known reaches past every role.
- Whether ultimate good is an achievement (ours) or a gift (yours) for the child who will never reason.
- The final weighing against evil.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact old → new)

**R1 (C, An impersonal sacred order; Minor items 1–2).**
Old: "But its principle, Zhu Xi said, has no feeling or intention and makes no plans; and Heaven, its sages add, sees as the people see (*Mencius* 5A5). So it can say that each one holds the whole nature, and is seen through every eye that sees her rightly, not that *this* one is wanted for her own sake by One who sees her when no one does; for the infant no one holds, there is no eye through which that Heaven sees."
New: "But its principle, Zhu Xi said, has no feeling or intention and makes no plans; Heaven and Earth have a mind, he granted, for else an ox might bear a horse, but it is the steady giving of life to all things, not a mind that thinks of anyone. So it can say that each one holds the whole nature, as whole in the infant no one holds as in the sage, and that whoever sees her rightly answers to it; not that *this* one is wanted for her own sake by One who sees her when no one does."

**R2 (C, An impersonal sacred order; Minor item 3).**
Old: "so that worth follows nature, not capacity (A4)."
New: "so that worth follows nature, not capacity (A4). Its highest good, to become fully human and form one body with all things, is a cultivation each must achieve, which the child who dies or will never reason cannot reach, though she is cared for within it; so here, as with the Aristotelian, we part over gift and hope, not over what she is worth."

**R3 (Evil ¶1; Minor item 4).**
Old: "A Way that is not humane gives life and lets each thing go, and owes no one an answer; nor does Spinoza's God, which intends no one."
New: "A Way that is not humane gives life and lets each thing go, and owes no one an answer; nor does Spinoza's God, which intends no one; and the Confucian, who does what is right and accepts what Heaven decrees (*Mencius* 7A1–2), asks no account of a principle that makes no plans."

**R4 (W1(1); Minor item 5).**
Old: "That the rites give grief its due form and finish well what death broke off is true of the living; it does not reach her."
New: "The rites give grief its due form, serve the dead as one served the living, and finish well what death broke off; but for the gentleman who keeps them without holding that the dead hear, they keep the bond from the side of the living, and she is not there to answer."

**R5 (C, An impersonal sacred order; Minor item 6).**
Old: "spoke of Heaven as the one who gave him his virtue and who alone knew him (*Analects* 7.23, 14.35)"
New: "spoke of Heaven as the one who gave him his virtue, who had bereft him, and who alone knew him (*Analects* 7.23, 11.9, 14.35)"

**R6 (optional, W1(2); strengthens fairness).**
Old: "not from a worth she was given. That she can suffer"
New: "not from a worth she was given. The Confucian gives another: the body is received from one's parents and is not one's own to harm (*Classic of Filial Piety* 1), a trust held from the line that gave it, though he honors the one who gives his life to complete humaneness (*Analects* 15.9). That she can suffer"

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights:**
1. **Wang Yangming's triage passage** (*Chuanxi lu* III, Chan §276, verify) is the best evidence from inside a non-theist tradition for W1(2)'s claim that an order does not want any one of its members. Even "one body with all things" licenses using the hands to shield the head. The book could cite it in W1(2) or in the sacred-order row.
2. **Mou Zongsan's critique of Zhu Xi**, that Zhu Xi's principle is "only being and not activity" (*Xinti yu xingti*, verify), is a Confucian confirming the text's own diagnosis of the impersonal principle. Citing it would show the point is not a Christian imposition.
3. **The *Classic of Filial Piety*'s trust-structure** (body received, not owned) is a non-theist analogue to CCC 2280. It sharpens the text's distinctive claim, because a trust from parents fails for the child her parents did not want. That is where the book's Jeremiah 1:5 line does its real work: "Not every child is wanted by those who conceived her; every child was wanted by the One who made her."
4. **Mencius 6A17** (a nobility in each that no one conferred and no one can take away) is a strong witness to Dignitas Infinita 1 ("recognizable by reason alone") from outside the West.
5. **Mencius 1A7, the ox spared because it was seen and the sheep substituted because it was not**, is the perfect illustration of A4's "what is before our eyes" and of W2's "reason holding itself to consistency." Mencius's whole remedy is extension (*tui*).
6. **Mencius 7A35 (Shun and Gusou)** shows a great non-theist ethics that never asks what is owed to the victim. It quietly supports A2's and W1's insistence on *her*.

**Lines worth keeping:**
- "Heaven and Earth give life to things; God wanted *you*." This is the cleanest one-line statement of the real difference.
- "Such an order is free of the problem of evil only if it intends no one in particular."
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."
- "The rebel can take her side; he cannot keep her."
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it." A Mencian would recognize the sprout here and accept the sentence.

## 8. Score

**8/10.** The text states my school accurately and at its strongest (Zhu Xi, Zhang Zai, Mou, Xunzi, Mencius 2A2 and 7B3, Sima Qian), concedes parity where parity is due, books its costs against Mencius openly, and confines its advantage to the one point that is genuinely contested: whether the ground is Someone who wants each one. The few lapses are minor and repairable.

**Honest ceiling for any theistic case against my school: 9/10.** Reason can show that an impersonal principle or a moral heart does not *want* this one, and that "willed for her own sake" explains more than nature alone. It cannot show that the wanting is real rather than a fitting hope. At the last step the choice between Heaven-as-principle and the Lord who is Love stays a weighing. With R1–R5 applied, this text reaches that ceiling.
