# Blind review: the Daoist school

## 1. School and voice

I speak for the Way of the *Daodejing* and the *Zhuangzi*, read through A. C. Graham (spontaneity answering to awareness, the "that's it" which goes by circumstance), Chad Hansen (daos as guiding discourses, perspectival distinctions of *shi/fei*), P. J. Ivanhoe (Zhuangzi as a therapeutic skeptic who still trusts skill and the Way), Livia Kohn (the religious tradition, not just the two books) and Brook Ziporyn (the "wild card" and the hinge of the Course). I do not deny that there is a Source. I deny that it can be named, that it lays down law, that it *wants*, and that keeping a ledger of debts is the deepest truth about wrongdoing. I grant much of the moral data. I dispute the frame that turns it into claims, debts and a Lawgiver. All paraphrases of these scholars below are marked "verify."

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** I don't start from desert or a ledger. I start from *ziran*, each thing being "so of itself," and from the de (potency) of each thing following its own grain. What anyone is "owed" is to be let be: not mutilated, not coerced, not lengthened like the duck's legs or cut short like the crane's (*Zhuangzi* 8, verify), and not taxed into starvation by rulers who contrive (*Daodejing* 75). **Holds, but thin.** It is coherent and borrows nothing. It breaks when one thing's grain is to devour another's, because "let it be" doesn't settle who gets let be. In politics this collapses into "rulers, restrain yourselves," which isn't a theory of what is owed.

**2. A hard dilemma.** Take the text's own surgeon case: kill one to give organs to five. The Daoist refuses. This is not an absolute rule. Engineering a death to improve the totals is contrivance (*wei*) in its purest form, cutting in the master carpenter's place (*Daodejing* 74), and calculating profit is the first thing the sage drops (*Daodejing* 19). Now take the text's hardest case, mother and child both dying unless the child is killed. Here the Daoist does what cannot be avoided (*bu de yi*, *Daodejing* 31): he saves the one who can be saved and mourns as at a funeral. **Holds on its own terms, and diverges from the book.** It borrows compassion, but compassion is the Daoist's own first treasure (*Daodejing* 67), so that is not borrowing from the book. The cost: there is no line that holds "at every number." The sage takes up weapons when he must.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** Seen from the Way, no thing is noble or base (*Zhuangzi* 17). The maimed, the hunchbacked and the lipless carry virtue whole (*Zhuangzi* 5). The useless tree lives out its years because no one can use it (*Zhuangzi* 4). So I ground equality, and immunity from being *used*, more firmly than most secular rivals do, and without reference to capacity. **Borrows nothing, breaks at one point.** The equality is that of the straw dogs (*Daodejing* 5). It is impartial and not unconditional *regard*. It covers the frog as much as the infant, so there is no special human dignity, and I can't say that the exposed infant has a *claim*. I can only say that the people who exposed her abandoned the Way and the sage's compassion. I deny "dignity" as a rank. I affirm equal standing before the Way.

**4. Ultimate flourishing.** Return to the root (*Daodejing* 16). Wandering free and easy. Nurturing life and finishing the years Heaven gave (*Zhuangzi* 1, 3, 4). Simplicity, few desires, softness, not contending. Equanimity as one is changed by transformation, including death (*Zhuangzi* 6). The guiding principles are the three treasures: compassion, frugality, not daring to be first (*Daodejing* 67). **Holds.** It is coherent and borrows nothing. It gives up personal survival, and it gives it up gladly. That is a standoff with the book, not a breakdown of my position.

## 3. Debate highlights

**E1. The A-heading's epistemology.**
*Attack.* "What still seems true after honest reflection we may trust." Zhuangzi asks who will judge between us if we disagree. Someone who agrees with you can't, someone who agrees with me can't, and someone who agrees with neither can't (*Zhuangzi* 2). Every "this" is a "that" from somewhere else. A1's "for everyone" is spoken from one hinge.
*Defense.* The principle is defeasible and offered, not imposed. The heading itself says "none is beyond denial" and ends in a choice, not a proof. Zhuangzi trusts seemings too: Cook Ding's knack, the "true person," the judgment that contrivance harms life. A perspectivism that undermined every seeming would undermine those as well. On Ivanhoe's reading Zhuangzi isn't a global skeptic (verify).
*Rejoinder.* Granted. My point is only that the heading's "for everyone" is a reflective seeming of a particular kind. The text already allows that it can be refused.
**Held.**

**E2. Ren and yi as symptoms (Daodejing 18, 38).**
*Attack.* When the great Way is abandoned, benevolence and righteousness appear. When the six relations are out of harmony, filial piety appears. The vocabulary of A, made of wrong, guilt, debt and dignity, is what a people produces after it has lost the Way. These are symptoms, not data.
*Defense.* That is a genealogy, and the text's own rule (A, applied to Nietzsche) says a genealogy defeats a belief only if the belief's truth plays no part in the best account of why it is held. *Daodejing* 18 doesn't show that. It doesn't even say filial love is false, only that naming it arises in disorder. By the fairness rule the Daoist can't use this against A unless he lets the book use a genealogy of Daoist quietism against him.
*Rejoinder.* Fair. Taken honestly, my claim is weaker: the naming comes late, and the reality named doesn't. That concedes A1 and A2 as realities and contests only how they should be framed.
**Held.**

**E3. A7 and knowing when to stop.**
*Attack.* "My life has a limit; knowledge has none" (*Zhuangzi* 3). In pursuing the Way, something is dropped every day (*Daodejing* 48). The sage knows when to stop (*Daodejing* 32, 44). A7's duty to keep looking is the restless mind the Way cures.
*Defense.* A7 forbids only two things: believing against the evidence for comfort, and stopping *because we fear* what we will find. Zhuangzi does neither. He is among the most unflinching writers on uncertainty and death. The knowing-when-to-stop he recommends is wisdom about the limits of knowledge, not flight from it.
*Rejoinder.* Then A7 holds against me as written. But the C dilemma against Nietzsche ("either truthfulness binds every mind... or only those who hold it") has a third option for me. Candor is what an unobstructed mind does (the *zhenren*), and it doesn't "bind." The text's W2 already answers this in its Aristotelian form ("a rational animal simply reads its own form"), and that answer carries over.
**Held.**

**E4. The debt to the dead (W1(1)).**
*Attack.* The text puts Zhuangzi on the first road: the debt dies, and forgetting dissolves remorse. My best text isn't Zhuangzi but *Daodejing* 79: when a great grievance is reconciled, some grievance always remains, so how can that be called good? The Daoist sees the unpayable residue as clearly as A3 does. He concludes that the ledger is the disease, not that a Keeper is needed. The sage holds the creditor's half of the tally and does not press.
*Defense.* That makes *Daodejing* 79 a witness *for* A3. The residue is real, and no human settlement removes it. The Daoist then chooses not to press, which is a therapy for the wrongdoer and the creditor, not an answer to *her*. The text already rates W1(1) as only a clue and says it "weighs little" for readers who doubt that "owed" is meant in the full sense.
*Rejoinder.* Agreed. The text's modesty protects it. But it misstates me slightly when it says "in the end no one asks pardon and no one gives it." The Way doesn't forbid asking pardon. It refuses to let pardon be what settles the matter (repair R2).
**Held with repair.**

**E5. "God wanted you" against "the highest giving does not want" (W1(2), C The Way).**
*Attack.* The cinnamon tree is cut because it can be eaten, and the lacquer tree because it is useful (*Zhuangzi* 4, verify). To be *wanted* is to be at risk. The Way gives life and doesn't possess, rears and doesn't rule (*Daodejing* 10, 51). That secures "for her own sake" better than a Will that "remains its Lord." By the text's own admission, that Lord could command the killing of infants (1 Samuel 15) through human hands, and the Way never does.
*Defense.* The text concedes the second point openly in W1(2) and in C, The Way: the Way "draws the line more absolutely than this book." On the first point, God's wanting isn't for gain, because He lacks nothing (GS 24; CCC 2280). His being Lord is that of the Giver of being, and it is exactly what bars *human* disposal of her. "Rears and does not rule" can say that she was given life. It can't say to her that it is good she, this one, exists.
*Rejoinder.* The Way does say something close to that: the Great Clod that gives me life and death is to be thought well of for both (*Zhuangzi* 6, verify). But it is said to the whole and not to *her*. That is the honest dividing line, and the text names it ("whether the Giver knows her"). The text's real weakness here is an omission. It doesn't credit the Daoist's strongest ground for A4, *Zhuangzi* 5 and 17, while it credits the Neo-Confucians and the Buddhists with "worth follows nature, not capacity."
**Held with repair (R1, R4).**

**E6. W2: one Source, but not a law.**
*Attack.* The Way that can be spoken isn't the constant Way (*Daodejing* 1). The text puts "eternal truths about what is good and what we owe" in a divine wisdom. On Hansen's reading, every such truth is a dao, a guiding discourse that cuts distinctions from a standpoint (verify). Darwin's bees, who hold killing their brothers a sacred duty, are the strongest evidence that naming the law is local.
*Defense.* W2's conclusion is "one Source," and the Daoist *grants* it. The text says so ("Laozi grants one Source," *Daodejing* 1, 25). In *Daodejing* 51 the Way gives birth and de rears, so the Daoist too has one origin for things and for the pattern of their flourishing. That is exactly W2's structural claim. W2 moves the question of personhood to W1 and W3. Darwin's bees are the text's argument against accident, not for local law.
*Rejoinder.* Correct. W2 doesn't touch me, and the text doesn't pretend it does. My disagreement is over "law" and "Someone," and those are not argued here.
**Held.**

**E7. W3: the Mother who nourishes without knowing.**
*Attack.* "Only a person can love us." But the Way is the Mother of the world, and "I alone value being fed by the Mother" (*Daodejing* 20, 52). The text's own line in W3's second paragraph is "a child is loved long before he can say who loves him." That describes the Mother-Way: nourishment received without knowing who gives it, and no need to know.
*Defense.* In the image the *child* doesn't know. The mother does. W3's claim concerns the Giver: love is willing another's good, and that needs someone who knows the beloved. The Mother in *Daodejing* 20 and 52 nourishes all alike and knows no one. The Daoist image keeps the asymmetry and removes the knower, and the text says plainly that this is where the two part.
*Rejoinder.* That is fair, and it is where we really disagree: is knowing a perfection in the Source or a falling-off from it? Neither side can show the other wrong.
**Held. Standoff at parity.**

**E8. Evil: the straw dogs predict the world.**
*Attack.* "Heaven and Earth are not humane; they treat the ten thousand things as straw dogs" (*Daodejing* 5). The wolf and the fawn, the parasite in the child, pain that falls where survival would put it: the Way predicts all of it and owes no explanation. The text's weight of evil comes from positing a humane Heaven in the first place. Hell and 1 Samuel 15 add to it.
*Defense.* The text concedes all of this ("A Way that is not humane gives life and lets each thing go, and owes no one an answer"). It enters the weight on its own side of the scale and calls hell "the heaviest thing there." Its counter is that the Daoist pays elsewhere: he can't take her side against the past. The Daoist has a soft spot of his own too: "the Way of Heaven has no favorites and is always with the good" (*Daodejing* 79). The moment the Way is said to side with the good, Sima Qian's question lands on Daoism just as Ivan's lands on the Church, and the text uses this fairly (C, impersonal sacred order).
*Rejoinder.* Conceded. *Daodejing* 79 is the one line in my canon that makes a theodicy-like promise, and I'd read it as describing the sage, not Heaven. But the text's dichotomy ("it can only will it again or understand that it had to be") doesn't quite fit me. Zhuangzi's resting in what cannot be helped (*Zhuangzi* 4) is counsel about one's *own* lot. Zhuangzi never tells anyone to rest in another's torture as fated, and Laozi mourns even the enemies slain (*Daodejing* 31). The Daoist can grieve with her. What he can't do is promise she is kept. Saying so is more accurate and costs the text nothing (repair R5).
**Held with repair.**

**E9. The hillside infant (Conclusion).**
*Attack.* I am surer than of any argument that the weak newborn left on the hillside was abandoned as fully as the strong one. Exposing her is valuing the useful, the very thing the useless tree rebukes. Indeed the newborn is the Daoist image of virtue held whole (*Daodejing* 55). So I secure the equality the conclusion asks for, from "what she is" (a self-so thing of the Way), with no "what she is made for." The conclusion's last step adds the teleology without needing it.
*Defense.* The conclusion is worded "this book answers," so it is offered as the book's account, not deduced from the shared premise. W1(2) and C, A God who is Love claim only reach: an explanation of why there is such a kind, and an end offered to each. The Daoist grants equality but doesn't say she was *wronged*, in the sense of a claim that is hers. On the Daoist's terms the deed was a deviation, not a debt.
*Rejoinder.* True. My "abandoned" is not the text's "wronged." Here I trade A2's full sense for a framing and pay for it, though at parity, since the text doesn't claim to compel me.
**Held. Standoff at parity.**

## 4. Ledger

- **A heading.** Holds. The epistemic principle is defeasible and fair. It names its deniers and ends in a choice, not a proof. It omits the Daoist reframing from its list of deniers. That is not a defect, because the Daoist doesn't flatly deny A.
- **A1.** Holds. The Daoist grants that torturing for fun is contrary to the Way whatever a culture approves. He disputes only the word "wrong" as a fixed *shi/fei*.
- **A2.** Holds. The secret case shows the deed is directed at her, and the Daoist's own *xin* (trust, *Daodejing* 17, 49) is directed too. The Daoist contests "debt" and grants direction.
- **A3.** Holds. It is strengthened by *Daodejing* 79's admission that settled grievances leave a residue.
- **A4.** Holds. The Daoist reaches equal standing that doesn't follow capacity by his own route (*Zhuangzi* 5, 17). He doesn't grant "owed care" as a claim. The text's parenthetical disclaimers are honest.
- **A5.** Holds. The Daoist agrees that capacity and desire don't settle what to do. His answer is the grain of *ziran*, not ends that are given. The text leaves exactly that open for W.
- **A6.** Holds. The surgeon and the riot are refused by the Daoist as contrivance. The text correctly says the line bends for most people at vast numbers, and the Daoist is one of them (*Daodejing* 31).
- **A7.** Holds. It forbids stopping *from fear*, not knowing when to stop. Zhuangzi passes the test (E3).
- **W1 frame.** Holds. It is careful: it doesn't claim no-one-can-release decides between the accounts, and it agrees that rivals ask pardon of her. The phrase "against the law that is His own wisdom" carries the case's thesis. It is not shared, but it is flagged as the book's.
- **W1(1).** Holds with repair. It is a clue, rated modestly. Placing Zhuangzi on the first road is fair. It should note *Daodejing* 79 as a non-theist witness to the residue (insight).
- **W1(2).** Holds. "A law or an order gives a nature to every member of a kind; it does not want any one of them" is accurate about the Way. The 1 Samuel cost is paid openly, and the text names the Way as drawing the line more absolutely.
- **W1 closing ("This Lawgiver is not a commander").** Holds. It is anti-voluntarist and consistent with the Euthyphro answer. The Daoist objects only to "law," on grounds the text doesn't need to win here.
- **W2.** Holds. The Daoist grants one Source, and the text says so. Its stopping-point claim is flagged as a judgment the rivals don't share.
- **W3 (first paragraph).** Holds with a minor repair. The Zhuangzi forge passage is accurate. "Gaily" slightly mistakes equanimity for levity. The Christian reply ("we shall all be changed") answers the forge fairly, and the remaining dispute (identity as grasping) is at parity.
- **W3 (second paragraph, hiddenness).** Holds. It admits it gives "part of an answer." The Daoist is untouched, since he expects no knowing.
- **C, Nothing really binds.** Holds. Fairly stated, and the cost is named.
- **C, Feeling, refined.** Holds. "Each of us judges from where he stands" parallels *Zhuangzi* 2 and is handled fairly.
- **C, Our best words, widening.** Holds. The use of James is apt.
- **C, Reason's own law.** Holds. Its concession that Kant draws the line more absolutely is honest.
- **C, What we owe each other.** Holds.
- **C, Care between persons.** Holds. "Not of its next" is an honest admission.
- **C, Well-being, counted impartially.** Holds. The Daoist agrees about utilitarian calculation, which is profit-thinking (*Daodejing* 19).
- **C, The flourishing of our kind.** Holds. The standoff over gift and hope is honestly drawn.
- **C, Necessary truths that belong to no one.** Holds.
- **C, An impersonal sacred order.** Holds. Using *Daodejing* 79 through Sima Qian (*Shiji* 61) is accurate and fair to both schools.
- **C, The Way.** Holds with repair. It is mostly accurate and generous. Four problems: (a) several claims lack citations (*Daodejing* 5, 18, 38, 51, 55, 74); (b) it omits the Daoist ground for A4 in *Zhuangzi* 4, 5 and 17; (c) "no one asks pardon and no one gives it" overstates the position; (d) "never sends anyone to kill" should say why: the Way commands no one.
- **C, God, or Nature.** Holds.
- **C, The Buddha's Dhamma.** Holds.
- **C, History and class.** Holds.
- **C, Power and rank.** Holds with repair. Add the Way to the list of non-Christian teachings that worth follows nature, not strength.
- **C, Freedom that makes its values.** Holds.
- **C, A God who is Love.** Holds. "Reach, not count" is the right claim. "Against... the nameless Way..., it is the one that wills each of us for her own sake" is accurate.
- **Evil, paragraph 1 ("On the other side of the scale").** Holds. It is exemplary in conceding that the Way bears the weight more easily.
- **Evil, First.** Holds with repair. The privation argument is fairly limited. The closing dichotomy doesn't quite fit Zhuangzi's resting in *ming*, which concerns one's own lot (E8).
- **Evil, Second.** Holds. Its claim is deliberately narrow, and it admits the pattern of pain in nature still weighs.
- **Evil, Third.** Holds. It doesn't claim to prove anything. The note that the hopes of other accounts are "for those who come after" is fair to the Daoist, who doesn't offer even that.
- **Conclusion.** Holds. The hillside question really is the strongest ground. "This book answers" correctly frames the move to "what she is made for" as the book's answer, not an entailment. A Daoist can grant equal abandonment without granting teleology, and that is a standoff.

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**"As written: NONE."**

I found no Fatal or Serious objection. In every Daoist charge that bears on the case (E5, E8), the text has already conceded the point and put it on its own side of the scale. The Daoist frame-level objections (E1, E2, E6) either fail the fairness rule or fall at parity.

**Minor items**
1. **C, The Way: missing resources for A4.** It omits *Zhuangzi* 5 (the maimed who carry virtue whole), *Zhuangzi* 4 (the useless tree) and *Zhuangzi* 17 (no noble or base, seen from the Way). These are the Daoist's best ground that worth doesn't follow capacity or use. The Power and rank row's list ("the sacred order; the Dhamma") repeats the omission.
2. **C, The Way: thin citations.** "Heaven and Earth are not humane" (*Daodejing* 5), "gives life... does not possess" (*Daodejing* 51), "benevolence and righteousness appear when the Way is lost" (*Daodejing* 18, 38), the newborn (*Daodejing* 55) and the Executioner (*Daodejing* 74) are uncited. Only 27, 49, 67, 79, 63, 10, 51 and 31 are given.
3. **W3: "gaily."** Master Lai is gasping on his deathbed. "With an easy heart" is truer.

**Misstatements of my school**
1. **C, The Way: "so in the end no one asks pardon and no one gives it."** This overstates the position. The Way doesn't forbid pardon. It refuses to make pardon what settles a wrong, and *Daodejing* 79 itself admits that a residue remains.
2. **C, The Way: "since it never sends anyone to kill in the Executioner's place."** True, but the reason is that the Way commands no one at all. Without that reason the sentence reads like a moral rule the Way keeps. Also, *Daodejing* 74 is about rulers usurping the power of death, which is only loosely A6's question of trading the innocent for the many.
3. **Evil, First: "it can only will it again or understand that it had to be."** Zhuangzi's resting in what cannot be helped concerns one's own lot, not resignation to what is done to another. The Daoist sage grieves (*Daodejing* 31). He can't promise.
4. **(Scope, not error.)** The C row is limited to "Laozi and Zhuangzi." The religious Daoist tradition Kohn studies does have confession and pardon (see section 7). That is legitimate given the row's scope, but the book shouldn't generalize the row to "Daoism."

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Is *wanting* a perfection in the Source or a falling-off from it? (E5, E7)
- Is the person's lasting identity the fulfillment of love or the grasping that clings? Is it the sword the metal demands to be? (W3)
- Is guilt that asks *her* pardon a truth about the wrong, or the ledger the sage puts down? (E4)
- Does equal worth need "what she is made for," or only "what she is"? (E9)
- 1 Samuel 15: the text pays this cost openly against the Way. It remains a weight, not an unanswered objection.

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1. C, The Way: A4 resources and citations.**
Old: "It sees much of A: the cruelty of rulers, the newborn as the image of whole virtue, a sage who abandons no one, is good to the good and to the not-good alike, and holds compassion as his first treasure (A1, A4; *Daodejing* 27, 49, 67), and that to kill in the Executioner's place is to cut in the master carpenter's place (A6)."
New: "It sees much of A: the cruelty of rulers, the newborn as the image of whole virtue, a sage who abandons no one, is good to the good and to the not-good alike, and holds compassion as his first treasure (A1, A4; *Daodejing* 27, 49, 55, 67); that seen from the Way no thing is noble or base, and that the maimed and the misshapen can carry virtue whole, so that worth does not follow strength or use (*Zhuangzi* 4, 5, 17); and that to kill in the Executioner's place is to cut in the master carpenter's place (A6; *Daodejing* 74)."

**R2. C, The Way: pardon.**
Old: "and Zhuangzi would forget both praise and blame; so in the end no one asks pardon and no one gives it (A2, A3), and it lets the dead be transformed (W3)."
New: "and Zhuangzi would forget both praise and blame (*Zhuangzi* 6). It does not forbid asking pardon, but it will not make pardon the thing that settles a wrong: when a great grievance is reconciled, it says, some grievance surely remains (*Daodejing* 79), and its answer is to stop pressing the tally, not to have it released (A2, A3); and it lets the dead be transformed (W3)."

**R3. C, The Way: opening citations.**
Old: "holds that Heaven and Earth are not humane: the Way gives life to each thing and does not possess it, and benevolence and righteousness appear when the Way is lost."
New: "holds that Heaven and Earth are not humane (*Daodejing* 5): the Way gives life to each thing and does not possess it (*Daodejing* 51), and benevolence and righteousness appear when the Way is lost (*Daodejing* 18, 38)."

**R4. C, Power and rank.**
Old: "And worth that follows nature, not strength, was taught outside Christianity too (the sacred order; the Dhamma)."
New: "And worth that follows nature, not strength, was taught outside Christianity too (the sacred order; the Way; the Dhamma)."

**R5. C, The Way: the Executioner.**
Old: "And since it never sends anyone to kill in the Executioner's place,"
New: "And since the Way commands no one, and so never sends anyone to kill in the Executioner's place,"

**R6. Evil, First: the Daoist sage.**
Old: "and so it cannot take her side against the past."
New: "and so it cannot take her side against the past. The Daoist sage, who rests in his own lot as what cannot be helped (*Zhuangzi* 4) and mourns even the enemies slain in war (*Daodejing* 31), can grieve with her; but nothing in his Way promises that she is kept."

**R7. W3: Zhuangzi.**
Old: "Zhuangzi answers gaily that it is good to be human now"
New: "Zhuangzi answers with an easy heart that it is good to be human now"

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**New insights**
1. ***Daodejing* 79 as a witness for A3.** When a great grievance is reconciled, some grievance always remains. A non-theist text admits the unpayable residue, and the book can set it beside A3 and W1(1). The Daoist sees what the book sees, and chooses to set the tally down rather than look for a Keeper. That sharpens where the two really part.
2. **The useless tree and A6.** Zhuangzi saw that being valued for use is the danger: what is useful gets cut. The Catholic counterpart is that being willed for one's own sake is what exempts a person from use (GS 24). The two meet in rejecting the instrumental calculus and part over whether exemption needs a Will. This makes "persons may not be spent" vivid for Eastern readers.
3. ***Zhuangzi* 5 and A4.** A whole chapter of amputees, hunchbacks and the deformed who have "virtue complete" (verify the chapter title's translation). It is perhaps the strongest pre-Christian text that worth doesn't follow capacity or beauty, and it fits A4's line about "the healthy and the beautiful."
4. **Possible convergence on end-of-life care (verify carefully).** Zhuangzi praises finishing the years Heaven allotted (*tian nian*, *Zhuangzi* 4, 6), and the true person does not "use the human to help Heaven" (*Zhuangzi* 6, verify). Read one way, this cautions both against ending a life early and against straining to prolong it, which loosely parallels CCC 2278–2279. But Daoism is ambivalent here: in *Zhuangzi* 18 the skull prefers death, and some hermits in *Zhuangzi* 28 drown themselves rather than accept a throne. Use it only as a hedged witness, if at all, and not in A6's list beside the Vinaya.
5. **Religious Daoism moved toward a Someone (Kohn, verify).** The Celestial Masters (from the 2nd century CE) had the sick confess their faults in "quiet chambers." They sent written petitions to the Three Officials of Heaven, Earth and Water, one burned or placed on a mountain, one buried, one sunk in water. By late Han, Laozi was deified as Lord Lao (Taishang Laojun), a personified Way who reveals and saves. Later ritual included rites to release the dead from the underworld. This parallels the book's point about Confucius's Heaven being "nearer a Someone": when a living tradition grew around the impersonal Way, it reached for confession, pardon and care for the dead. Offer it as a clue in W1(1) or W3, not as proof, and keep the C row's limit to Laozi and Zhuangzi.
6. **The Mother image (*Daodejing* 20, 52) and W3.** The book's line "a child is loved long before he can say who loves him" is exactly the Daoist image of being fed by the Mother. The book's answer, that the mother knows the child even when the child does not know her, is the right one and could be stated in W3 explicitly.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "Where we say God wanted *you*, it says the highest giving does not want."
- "Heaven and Earth give life to things; God wanted *you*."
- "it rears and does not rule"
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it."
- "Its claim is reach, not count."
- "The rebel can take her side; he cannot keep her."

## 8. Score

**8 / 10.** The text states the Way accurately and generously. It concedes, without hedging, that the Way bears the weight of evil more easily and draws the killing line more absolutely. It wins only what it fairly can: the Way does not know or keep *her*. It falls short of 9 because of a missed Daoist ground for A4 (*Zhuangzi* 4, 5, 17), one overstatement about pardon, and thin citations. With repairs R1–R7 I would score it 9.

**Ceiling for any theistic case against my school: 9.** The Daoist can consistently decline the Someone. He holds that wanting, knowing and keeping are falling-offs from the highest giving, not perfections of it. No argument can force him off that standoff. A case can only show, as this one does, what he gives up by holding it.
