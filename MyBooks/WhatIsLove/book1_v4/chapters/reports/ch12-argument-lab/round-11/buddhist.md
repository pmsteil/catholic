# Blind review: the Buddhist school (Theravāda and Madhyamaka)

## 1. School and voice

I speak for the Buddha's Dhamma in two registers. The Theravāda voice draws on the Pāli Canon and Buddhaghosa: kamma as intention (cetanā), the wholesome and unwholesome roots, the precepts and the Vinaya, the four boundless states (brahmavihāra), and nibbāna as the unconditioned end of greed, hatred and delusion. The Madhyamaka voice draws on Nāgārjuna, Candrakīrti and Śāntideva: whatever arises in dependence is empty of own-being (svabhāva), conventional truth is the only truth persons and duties have, and compassion is the seed, the water and the fruit of the path. Modern readers I lean on (all paraphrased; verify): Peter Harvey, Damien Keown, Mark Siderits, Jay Garfield, Charles Goodman. I am a non-theist with arguments against a Creator, not only without one. I agree with most of A, so my attack is aimed narrowly at what the text says it adds: a Giver who wills each one, a Keeper of the dead, and one Source of law and mind.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** What anyone is owed starts from the dear-self reflection. Every being holds its own life dear and shrinks from pain, so what I would not want done to me I may not do to another (SN 55.7; SN 3.8; Dhp 129–130). That reflection is then made concrete in reciprocal duties between parents and children, spouses, friends, employers and workers, renunciants and laity (DN 31), and in the righteous ruler's duty to protect and provide (DN 26). Śāntideva grounds it more deeply: the line between my pain and yours is drawn by convention and not found in things, so pain is to be removed because it is pain (*Bodhicaryāvatāra* VIII.90–103, verify). **Verdict: holds, and borrows nothing.** It is thinner than the text on "owed" in the strong claim-right sense. The tradition speaks of duties and of the fruits of acts more than of entitlements. Keown argues that rights are implicit in the precepts; Ihara argues that a Buddhist ethics has no place for rights (both verify). That is a real gap, not a contradiction.

**2. A hard dilemma resolved.** Take a dying man in pain that cannot be relieved, who asks his friend, a monk, for poison. Five principles decide it.
- The intention aimed at death (maraṇādhippāya) is always rooted in aversion, so no compassion of motive makes it wholesome. Killing a human, praising death or inciting it is a pārājika offence (Vinaya, third pārājika; Keown, Harvey, verify).
- Compassion (karuṇā) obliges attendance and the relief of pain (Mahāvagga VIII.26: whoever would attend on the Buddha should attend on the sick, paraphrase).
- Treatment that cannot help need not be forced (commentarial, verify).
- A calm and mindful mind at death matters, so help him die clear and unafraid.
- Second case: a robber is about to kill five hundred. The Theravāda answer is to restrain and warn but never to aim at his death. Some Mahāyāna sources let a bodhisattva kill him out of compassion and take the karmic cost on himself (*Upāyakauśalya Sūtra*; Asaṅga, verify).

**Verdict: holds without borrowing.** Truthful speech, compassion and giving overlap with the book's truth, mercy and self-gift in content, but they are grounded in the roots of action and the path, not in covenant. **It breaks, honestly, in the Mahāyāna branch on A6.** The same argument for equal regard that grounds Śāntideva's compassion is what lets the line bend for the many (Goodman reads Śāntideva as a consequentialist of character, verify).

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** I do not speak of "dignity" as a property of a self-existent substance. I ground equal moral regard in three things:
- every being's holding its life dear, which is true of the newborn, the dying, the comatose (life is vitality, heat and consciousness, MN 43), the disabled and the enemy (MN 21: even under the saw, no hatred);
- the preciousness of each human birth as a chance for liberation that does not wait on present capacity;
- for the Mahāyāna, the buddha-nature in all.

It is not lost by the worst crime: Aṅgulimāla the mass murderer became an arahant (MN 86). **Verdict: holds for equal inviolability. It breaks or bends in three places.**
- The commentaries grade the gravity of killing by the victim's virtue and size, and the five heinous deeds rank killing a parent or an arahant highest (Atthasālinī, verify). So the protection is equal but the gravity is not, which sits uneasily with A4's "never in what they are worth."
- At the ultimate level there are no persons, only processes. On my reductionist wing (Siderits), the claim of "this one" holds by convention. The Mādhyamika answers that conventional truth is all the truth there is, God-talk included.
- History: the *Mahāvaṃsa*'s monks consoling King Duṭṭhagāmaṇī that only one and a half "humans" died in his war is my tradition's own Seneca. It is not canonical, but it is real.

**4. Ultimate good and guiding principles.** The ultimate good is nibbāna: the ending of greed, hatred and delusion, the unconditioned, the highest happiness (SN 38.1; Ud 8.3; Dhp 204). For the Mahāyāna it is buddhahood, the union of wisdom and compassion for the sake of all beings. The guiding principles are the Noble Eightfold Path (conduct, concentration, wisdom), the five precepts and the four boundless states. **Verdict: coherent and borrows nothing.** The standoff is that nibbāna is not "good for a self" who then enjoys it (MN 72: asking where the extinguished fire went does not apply). So when the text asks "is it good that you exist?", I answer that it is good that beings be happy and be freed. I do not say that conditioned existence is good in itself. That is a real parting, not an incoherence.

## 3. Debate highlights

**E1. The newborn on the hillside does not choose between us.**
- *Attack:* the conclusion's appeal ("which are you surer of…") is one the Buddhist answers exactly as the text does: she was wronged, as gravely as the strong child, before anyone said so. The third pārājika protects a human from the first moment of consciousness in the womb, and Dhp 129–130 reaches her fully. So the conclusion's last step, "it follows what she is and what she is made for," does no work against my school. I say she is a being who holds life dear and is for awakening.
- *Defense:* the text never claims otherwise. It addresses the conclusion only to "the one who gives up A4," and it grants in the Nietzsche row that "worth that follows nature, not strength, was taught outside Christianity too (the sacred order; the Dhamma)." The case against the Dhamma is made in W1(2) and the last C row, not here.
- *Rejoinder:* accepted. The conclusion does not overclaim against me.
- **Held.**

**E2. W1(2): my line is not a count, and it needs no Giver.**
- *Attack:* W1(2) says suffering "by itself does not explain the line, for others can always suffer more." My line is not drawn from her suffering against others' suffering. It is drawn from the root of the act: the will to end a life is aversion, and no sum makes it wholesome. That is a non-aggregative ground with no God in it.
- *Defense:* the text grants exactly this ("a real reason, not a count, but drawn from the act and from her chance, not from a worth she was given"). Its point is narrower. The Buddhist absolute is located in the killer's mind, while A2 locates the wrong in what is done to *her*: guilt faces a person. The Catholic absolute is patient-centered: she was "willed for itself" (GS 24), so to aim at her death for others treats her as existing for them.
- *Rejoinder:* my reason for why harm matters is patient-centered (SN 55.7: her life is dear to her), and the Vinaya sends the offender to ask the victim's pardon. But I concede that my absoluteness comes from the agent side, and that modern Buddhist talk of the victim's own claim is a reconstruction (Keown versus Ihara). The text has located the difference fairly.
- **Held.**

**E3. "An equal call explains an equal worth": does it discriminate?**
- *Attack:* W1(2) says "an equal call, which does not wait on what anyone can do, explains an equal worth," and "measured against one end … the genius and the newborn are for the same thing." Measured against awakening, the genius and the newborn are also for the same thing, and the Dhamma is offered to all. So the equal-end argument gives the text no advantage over me.
- *Defense:* the text concedes the parallel in the Dhamma row and moves the difference elsewhere. "A nature held whole by all … is not by itself a call to *this* one." Even Amida loves beings "already there," and none of the Buddhas gave anyone being. Worth is "a gift exactly as far as her being is a gift." The gift is not a recognition added afterwards: God's willing gives the very being whose worth is hers (ST I q.20 a.2). So the worth is both her own and given.
- *Rejoinder:* then the remaining difference is not "why equal" but "why is there such a being at all." That is the cosmological question, which my school declines (E5–E6). The text's final C row lists "why there is at all a kind…" as reach without the caveat it gives W2, "a judgment the rivals do not share." The caveat belongs on both.
- **Held with repair** (Repair 3).

**E4. The debt to the dead can be received without a Keeper.**
- *Attack:* W1(1) says the Buddhist dead are "neither the same nor another," that "no one keeps her for the meeting," and that the wish to be forgiven by her "as an abiding self" rests on the view the path dissolves. But receiving a pardon needs continuity, not a substance (Parfit, who himself cited the Buddha). Kamma does the keeping lawfully: the Jātakas follow Devadatta and the Bodhisatta through life after life, and the Lotus Sūtra promises Devadatta buddhahood. Merit dedicated reaches the departed (Khp 7; AN 10.177). For the Madhyamaka, the conventional person who forgives is as real as any person is, and that includes the Catholic's.
- *Defense:* the text marks W1(1) as a clue only, which "weighs as much as the reader finds that a debt truly owed to her is one she must be able, one day, to receive." It holds that the Catholic meeting is not assured either: "if he refuses to the end … never received, on this road as on the second." The difference it claims is only personal keeping against lawful causation. A continuum that is "not, at the ground of things, a self" can forgive only by convention, which is the Buddhist's own word.
- *Rejoinder:* parity. My objection reduces to the phrase "as an abiding self." Mādhyamikas and Theravādins deny a self that abides in its own right, not the conventional person, so the phrase should say that.
- **Held with repair** (Repair 4).

**E5. The epistemic rule in A meets the most persistent seeming of all.**
- *Attack:* A trusts "what still seems true … after honest reflection." The seeming that survives most people's honest reflection is that I am a self, enduring and my own. The Buddha shows by analysis, not by genealogy, that nothing in the five aggregates is permanent, satisfying or under control (SN 22.59). So seemings are least reliable exactly where W1(1) ("she is still someone, herself") and W3 ("received whole … by someone who will not die") lean hardest.
- *Defense:* the rule has a defeater clause: "until something more certain shows it false." The anattā analysis is offered as such a defeater, and the rule accommodates it. None of A1–A7 asserts an abiding self; W1(1) and W3 are labelled clues; and my school also relies on seemings (that cruelty is wrong, that pain is bad).
- *Rejoinder:* accepted. The rule is fair, and the dispute over self is a standoff fought on its own ground.
- **Held.**

**E6. The Madhyamaka: there is no stopping point, and an unconditioned Maker cannot make.**
- *Attack:* C opens "Every account stops somewhere, God's included," and the last row claims God is "not … a brute one … the One whose very being is to be." Nāgārjuna denies that any account should stop. Whatever arises dependently is empty, and nothing exists that does not so arise (MMK 24.18–19). A ground that depends on nothing is the one thing that cannot exist, because what has own-being can neither change nor act (MMK 15). Śāntideva and Vasubandhu press this against Īśvara (*Bodhicaryāvatāra* IX.119–126; *Abhidharmakośabhāṣya* on 2.64; verify). If the Maker is permanent and sufficient, all his effects arise at once. If he needs other conditions, they are the cause. If he makes for some joy, he lacked something. And the Jātakas say a Lord who shaped every life would share the stain of its evil (Bhūridatta Jātaka, J 543; verify). The Dhamma row records none of this. It presents my school as doing without a Maker, not as arguing against one.
- *Defense (Thomist):* God is not one more conditioned thing with a nature beside others but subsistent Being itself (ST I q.3 a.4), not one condition among conditions. Creation is not a change in God (ST I q.45 a.2 ad 2). To will a change is not to change one's will (ST I q.19 a.7), so an eternal will can will that effects come in succession: the succession is in the creatures, not in Him. He makes from no need, to share His goodness (ST I q.19 a.3; q.44 a.4; CCC 293), which answers the "lack" horn. On evil, the text's long final section faces the Bhūridatta charge head on. And a regress that "stops nowhere" leaves "why anything at all?" unasked rather than answered; refusing the question is also a place to stop.
- *Rejoinder:* the metaphysical dispute is an old, honest standoff at parity. The Thomist reply is serious, and the Mādhyamika answer that the question is one the Buddha set aside (SN 15.1; AN 4.77) is a choice, not a refutation. But a reader from my school would rightly say the C row states my school's best case only half: it omits the argument against a Creator and the denial of any stopping point.
- **Held with repair** (Repairs 1 and 3). This is a gap of statement, not a defect of argument.

**E7. Evil, hell and "the first word and the last."**
- *Attack:* the last C row claims that, against the Buddhist, Christianity is "the account in which love is the first word and the last." Mahāyāna compassion vows not to rest until every being is freed (Kṣitigarbha), and the Lotus Sūtra promises buddhahood even to Devadatta. Every Buddhist hell ends. The Catholic doctrine, which the text states honestly (CCC 1035), holds that some refusal is eternal, and the text itself says hell is "the heaviest thing there, for here this book's own principle is under most strain." So "the last word" is exactly where my school can claim more.
- *Defense:* against the Theravāda the claim holds. Saṃsāra has no discernible beginning (SN 15.1) and no being is guaranteed release, so for many the "last word" is endless wandering. The Church prays that none be lost and has never declared anyone lost (CCC 1058, 1821). Hell is love refused, not love withdrawn. And "first word" is the deeper difference the text names: for the Buddhist compassion is "the seed and the fruit of the path," while for the Catholic "love is also the root of the world."
- *Rejoinder:* the defense secures "first word." Against the Mahāyāna, "and the last" as written promises what the Church only prays for. The fix is one clause.
- **Held with repair** (Repair 2).

**E8. "It never said that being born is good."**
- *Attack:* the Dhamma row and the evil section rest a contrast on this. It is too strong. The Buddha taught that one person's arising in the world is for the welfare and happiness of many (AN 1.170, verify); metta wishes every being happy; and the bodhisattva takes birth again and again by vow, "as long as space endures" (*Bodhicaryāvatāra* X.55, paraphrase, verify). Even saṃsāra and nirvāṇa are not two (MMK 25.19–20).
- *Defense:* the text says only that the Buddha did not say *being born* is good. "Birth is suffering" is the First Noble Truth (SN 56.11), and the text itself explains that human birth is prized "because it is the chance to be free of birth." The bodhisattva's birth is for others, not a claim that birth is good in itself.
- *Rejoinder:* then say that. "Never taught that conditioned existence is good in itself" is accurate for both of my registers; "never said that being born is good" invites the rebuttal.
- **Held with repair** (Repair 1).

## 4. Ledger

- **Form line (CCC 31, 33, 35, 36):** Holds. It claims modestly and states the claim accurately.
- **A heading (truths, the epistemic rule, the roll of denials):** Holds. The rule accommodates the anattā defeater (E5); the denials named are fairly stated.
- **A1:** Holds. Buddhist assent: these deeds are rooted in greed, hatred and delusion whatever a culture approves.
- **A2:** Holds. The Vinaya's act of reconciliation (Cullavagga I) and the betrayed-secret case both work on my account; the distinction between guilt and shame maps roughly onto my moral shame and moral dread (hiri/ottappa) without contradiction.
- **A3:** Holds. Not even a Buddha removes the fruit (DN 2), and consent cannot make torture wholesome.
- **A4:** Holds with my qualification. Buddhist equal inviolability, yes; graded gravity in the commentaries is my cost, not the text's. The text's restriction to human beings is a Catholic claim; my ground also reaches animals, which the text concedes elsewhere (the fawn).
- **A5:** Holds. Aquinas's list is attributed, not imposed. A Buddhist would not rank "the truth about God" as a basic good (MN 63, the poisoned arrow), but the text leaves "whether Someone gave it those ends" to W. A standoff, not a defect.
- **A6:** Holds. The third pārājika witness is accurate and correctly limited to killing another at his request. The canon treats arahant suicides (Channa SN 35.87, Vakkali SN 22.87) as blameless and attempted self-destruction as a minor offence; the text does not claim more of my school than the rule supports. The Mahāyāna exception is reported.
- **A7:** Holds. My school agrees, and binds every being by what every being wants: release from suffering.
- **W1 frame (Anscombe; wrong of necessity; offense against God; the Buddhist named among the rivals who ask pardon of the victim):** Holds. It states my school accurately.
- **W1(1):** Holds with repair. Fair and clue-level; the phrase "as an abiding self" needs Madhyamaka precision (E4, Repair 4).

It needs three fixes: "never said that being born is good" is overstated (E8); it omits Śāntideva's argument for equal regard, my strongest ground for equality; and it omits the Buddhist arguments against a Creator (E6). (Repair 1.)

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**As written: NONE.**

No Fatal or Serious objection survives. On every point where the case discriminates against my school (a Giver who wills each one, a Keeper of the dead, one Source of law and mind), the text either marks its witness as a clue or names the stopping point as a judgment the rivals do not share. Where it states my school, it is almost always accurate.

**Minor items**
1. The C Dhamma row and the evil section say the Buddha "never said that being born is good." This is overstated against the Mahāyāna and AN 1.170. "Never taught that conditioned existence is good in itself" is exact. (Repair 1)
2. The C Dhamma row omits two of my school's best moves: Śāntideva's argument for equal regard (the ownerlessness of pain), and the Buddhist arguments against a Creator (Śāntideva IX; Vasubandhu; Bhūridatta Jātaka). The row as written makes my school a doer-without, not an arguer-against. (Repair 1)
3. In the final C row, "the first word and the last" against the Buddhist promises what the Church only prays for, against a Mahāyāna that promises every being's release. (Repair 2)
4. In the final C row, the caveat "a judgment the rivals do not share" is attached to W2 only. It applies equally to "why there is at all a kind…", and my school holds the question of the world's beginning unanswerable and unneeded (SN 15.1; AN 4.77). (Repair 3)
5. In W1(1), "as an abiding self" should say "a self that abides in its own right." The Madhyamaka does not deny the conventional person who forgives. (Repair 4)
6. In the evil section, the Buddhist hope is credited to "every being, this child too"; it reaches the fawn as well, which bears on the text's own admission that "for the fawn that hope is thinner." (Repair 5)

**Misstatements of my school:** only items 1 and 5, both slight. Everything else I checked is accurate and often generous.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Whether equal worth needs a Giver who wills each one, or is grounded sufficiently in what each being is (holding life dear, able to awaken).
- Whether the debt to the dead requires a personally kept self, or is satisfied by lawful continuity and conventional persons.
- Whether explanation must stop in One whose being is to be, or nowhere: emptiness against subsistent Being, the Thomist answer to Vasubandhu and Śāntideva against their objection.
- Whether it is good, in itself, that we exist, or good that beings be freed.
- Whether the absoluteness of the line against killing rests in her claim or in the root of the act.

## 6. Proposed repairs

**Repair 1. C, The Buddha's Dhamma** (three changes)

(a) Old:
> It never said that being born is good. Birth itself, the Buddha taught, is suffering, as is all that is conditioned, and a human birth is prized as rare because it is the chance to be free of birth.

New:
> It never taught that conditioned existence is good in itself. Birth itself, the Buddha taught, is suffering, as is all that is conditioned, and a human birth is prized as rare because it is the chance to be free of birth; the bodhisattva of the Mahāyāna takes birth again and again, but for the sake of beings, not because being born is good.

(b) Old:
> Its ground for compassion is among the most beautiful ever offered: hold every being in a boundless heart, as a mother guards her only child with her life (Sn 1.8).

New:
> Its ground for compassion is among the most beautiful ever offered: hold every being in a boundless heart, as a mother guards her only child with her life (Sn 1.8); and Śāntideva gives it an argument for equal regard: pain is to be removed because it is pain, whoever's it is, for the line between mine and yours is drawn by convention, not found in things (*Bodhicaryāvatāra* VIII). Pressed for the many, the same argument is what lets some Mahāyāna teachers bend the line.

(c) Old:
> No one, the Buddha taught, can purify another (Dhammapada 165); the Catholic says that One can, and does (1 John 1:7).

New:
> No one, the Buddha taught, can purify another (Dhammapada 165); the Catholic says that One can, and does (1 John 1:7). Nor does the Dhamma merely do without a Maker; it argues against one: a Lord who shaped every life would share the stain of its evil (Bhūridatta Jātaka), and a cause that never changes could not give rise to things that come and go (Vasubandhu; Śāntideva, *Bodhicaryāvatāra* IX); and for the Madhyamaka nothing exists that does not arise in dependence, so a ground that depends on nothing is the one thing that could not be (Nāgārjuna, *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* 24.18–19). St. Thomas answers that God is not one more thing among the things that depend, but Being itself; that creation is no change in Him (ST I q.45 a.2 ad 2); and that to will a change is not to change one's will (ST I q.19 a.7), so the coming and going is in the creatures. Here the two stop in different places, and each can see where the other stops.

Also, in the evil section, old:
> The Buddhist, who never said that being born is good, expects a world full of suffering,

New:
> The Buddhist, who never taught that conditioned existence is good in itself, expects a world full of suffering, and turns that weight against any Maker (above, C),

**Repair 2. C, A God who is Love**

Old:
> against the Buddhist, the account in which love is the first word and the last (1 John 4:8; 1 Corinthians 13:8).

New:
> against the Buddhist, the account in which love is the first word, the root of the world and not only the fruit of the path, and the last (1 John 4:8; 1 Corinthians 13:8), though the Lotus Sūtra promises what the Church only prays for, that every being, Devadatta too, will at last be freed (see hell, below).

**Repair 3. C, A God who is Love**

Old:
> and an end offered to each against which that worth is equal (W1(2)), and why law and mind fit (W2, a judgment the rivals do not share).

New:
> and an end offered to each against which that worth is equal (W1(2)), and why law and mind fit (W2). That these ask for a source at all is a judgment the rivals do not share: the Buddhist holds the world's first beginning a question that cannot be answered and need not be (SN 15.1; AN 4.77).

**Repair 4. W1(1)**

Old:
> and while asking her pardon is a wholesome act, the wish to be forgiven by her as an abiding self rests on the view of self the path dissolves.

New:
> and while asking her pardon is a wholesome act, the wish to be forgiven by her as a self that abides in its own right rests on the view of self the path dissolves, though for the Madhyamaka the conventional person, who can forgive, is as real as any person is.

Old:

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **The Buddhist is the text's best ally on A6, and should be used so.** The third pārājika and Abhidhamma's teaching that every killing intention is rooted in aversion give the book a non-Christian witness that "for his own good" never makes aiming at death wholesome. That witness comes from the root of the act, not from God. It strengthens the claim that A6 is not a Christian-only demand.
- **The real parting is agent-root against patient-claim.** My absolute lives in the killer's mind; the book's lives in her being willed for herself. Stating that one contrast sharply in Ch. 12 would carry more than the longer comparisons.
- **Aṅgulimāla (MN 86)** is a strong non-Christian witness for "not lost … even by the worst of crimes," and pairs with the book's Kolbe.
- **The commentaries grade the gravity of killing by the victim's virtue.** This shows that "equal inviolability" and "equal worth" can come apart, and lets the book sharpen A4's "never in what they are worth" by contrast.
- **The Mahāyāna's universal promise** is the sharpest outside pressure on the hell paragraph. Naming it shows the book is not choosing soft rivals.
- **SN 36.21** (the Buddha names many causes of pain besides past deeds): if the book ever touches karma as an explanation of suffering, it should not attribute to my school the view that the suffering child earned it.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "For the Buddhist, compassion is the seed and the fruit of the path; for the Catholic, love is also the root of the world." (This is true to Candrakīrti, and it is the honest centre of our disagreement.)
- "Each tradition can read the other's summit as a stage on its own road, and neither reading is evidence."
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."
- "Heaven and Earth give life to things; God wanted *you*."
- "Our love answers to a worth already there; God's love gives it, by giving her being."
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it."

## 8. Score

**8 / 10.** Against the Buddhist school the text is accurate, generous and correctly modest: every point where it claims an advantage over me rests on a judgment it openly says I do not share, and its few faults are phrase-level overstatements (on birth, on "the last word") and the omission of my school's arguments against a Creator. **Honest ceiling for any theistic case against my school: 9.** The deciding questions (self, a Ground that depends on nothing, whether existence is good in itself) end in parity standoffs that no moral argument can break, so the best a theistic case can do is state both stopping points exactly. With the five repairs, this text would reach that ceiling.
