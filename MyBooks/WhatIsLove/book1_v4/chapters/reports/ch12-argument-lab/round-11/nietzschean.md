# Blind review: the Nietzschean school

## 1. School and voice

I speak for Nietzsche, read with Brian Leiter (*Nietzsche on Morality*: naturalism, a doctrine of types, anti-realism about value, and the charge that "morality in the pejorative sense" harms the highest human beings), Bernard Reginster (*The Affirmation of Life*: nihilism, and will to power as the overcoming of resistance), John Richardson (*Nietzsche's System*, *Nietzsche's New Darwinism*: values as the ends of drives that selection has shaped), and Maudemarie Clark (*Nietzsche on Truth and Philosophy*: the mature Nietzsche does not deny truth, and his perspectivism is no global skepticism). The school disagrees with itself. Leiter's Nietzsche is an anti-realist about value. Richardson's, and Clark and Dudrick's, gives value more standing. All four agree on what matters here. No value holds for everyone simply because each is human. Rank comes before equality. The unconditional will to truth is itself something whose value has to be questioned. And the deepest charge against Christian morality is that it is *bad for life*, which is more than calling it unfounded. Every paraphrase of Nietzsche or his readers below is mine and is marked "verify." I invent no quotations.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** Justice begins among parties of roughly equal power, as exchange and repayment (*Human, All Too Human* 92; *Genealogy* II.8, verify). At its height it is the strong man's eye, which stays fair even toward someone who has injured him (*Genealogy* II.11, verify). It ends by overcoming itself in mercy, which is the privilege of the most powerful (*Genealogy* II.10, verify). Its formula: equality for equals, inequality for unequals (*Twilight*, "Skirmishes" 48, verify). What someone is owed is what fits his rank and what he can enter into exchange for. **Verdict: it holds on its own terms. It breaks against A4, and does so on purpose.** Someone who can neither trade nor threaten, such as the newborn, is owed nothing *in her own right*. Nietzsche's real duties toward the weak (*Antichrist* 57) come from the giver's rank. She makes no claim of her own.

**2. A hard dilemma.** A young composer of rare promise has a mother who is dying slowly. She asks him to come home for her last years, and doing so would end his work. I do not settle this with a rule for everyone. I ask which choice expresses and raises the most integrated set of his drives, and which serves his "task" (become what you are: *Ecce Homo*, "Clever" 9, verify). I also ask whether he is being honest about his motives. Is it pity speaking, or fear of his own path? Even so, the exceptional owe the ordinary a tenderness beyond what they show their peers (*Antichrist* 57). For the man whose task is real, the answer may be to go. For a man of another type, it may be to stay. **Verdict: it holds, and it borrows one thing.** It relies on honesty, which is the book's "truth," but it holds honesty as *my* virtue and not as a law over every mind. It cannot tell the mother whether she was wronged, and I do not claim it can.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** I deny it. Worth is rank: it is reached, expressed and displayed. Nietzsche was blunt about the weak and the sick (the "first principle" of his kind of philanthropy, *Antichrist* 2; "morality for physicians," *Twilight*, "Skirmishes" 36, both verify). The most a Nietzschean can give the infant no one holds is the noble's tenderness and a judgment about the health of the community that exposes her. **Verdict: it breaks against A4, openly.** The text states this cost correctly.

**4. The ultimate good.** The enhancement of the type "man." Great health. Self-overcoming. Saying yes to life so fully that one would will its eternal return (*Gay Science* 341). Guiding principles: order of rank, the pathos of distance, honesty, the creation of values, *amor fati*. "Ultimate" here is relative to type, because there is no single highest good for all. **Verdict: it holds for the few. It has nothing to give the infant who dies before she can strive.** The affirmation is, as the text puts it, "said by me, not to me."

## 3. Debate highlights

**E1. Perspectivism against "wrong for everyone" (A heading, A1).**
*Attack.* There is only perspectival seeing (*Genealogy* III.12, verify). "Wrong for everyone" requires a standpoint above all standpoints, and none exists. The text's own rule ("what still seems true to *us*") is indexed to a community.
*Defense.* The text offers A as truths. It does not offer them as reports, and it says plainly that "none is beyond denial" and that the case "ends in a choice, not a proof." Global perspectivism also turns on itself. Clark, from my own camp, argues that the mature Nietzsche gave up the thesis that all knowledge falsifies, so perspectivism cannot carry a denial of moral truth all on its own.
*Rejoinder.* Granted. I fall back to Leiter's position: anti-realism about *value* in particular, argued from naturalism and from the shape of moral disagreement. That position stands, and the text names it as a denial it will not compel.
**Held** (parity at the premise, and the text says so).

**E2. Genealogy against A4.**
*Attack.* Equal worth won through the slave revolt in morals, the priests' revaluation, and impotence renamed forgiveness (*Genealogy* I.7, I.10, I.14). Its spread follows Christianity's. Its truth plays no part in explaining why we hold it.
*Defense.* The text's rule in A: a history defeats a belief only if the belief's truth plays no part in the best account of why we hold it. If God loves each person, a belief in equal worth that grew from belief in His love was answering to something real. The text applies the same restraint to itself: "nor can this book use that history to vindicate A4." It also notes that worth following nature, not strength, appears in the Neo-Confucian and Buddhist traditions too.
*Rejoinder.* The non-Christian parallels help less than the text hopes, because Nietzsche read Buddhism as the faith of exhausted, over-refined peoples (*Antichrist* 20–22). But by the fairness rule I may not treat my genealogy as decisive while denying the theist his explanation. Who wins "best account" depends on the whole case. That makes A4's weight exactly what the text says it is: how sure the reader is. The text does not overclaim.
**Held.**

**E3. Why should truth matter more than life?**
*Attack.* Faith in the unconditional value of truth is the last form of the ascetic ideal (*Genealogy* III.24–27; *Gay Science* 344, verify). The text's answer, "truth and life have one Source," does not answer the question. It restates that faith.
*Defense.* The text gives it as its own answer, not as a proof. It also puts a fair dilemma: either truthfulness binds every reasoning mind, which is W2's question, or it binds only those who hold it. In the second case the unmasking cannot show that anyone, Nietzsche included, *ought* to remain honest. The text admits the dilemma "does not settle" the matter. It also points out that Nietzsche himself needed art to make honesty bearable (*Gay Science* 107), which shows that on his account truth and life can come apart.
*Rejoinder.* I accept the second horn without cost: I never claimed that everyone ought to be honest. The text's reading of GS 107 is accurate, though, and Nietzsche admitted more than the text uses. He held that even godless seekers take their fire from the old faith that God is the truth (*Gay Science* 344, verify). That is his own concession that *unconditional* truthfulness depends on the faith he was dismantling. The text would be stronger if it cited this alongside *Twilight* "Skirmishes" 5.
**Held** (standoff at parity; see repair R4).

**E4. *Amor fati* and the child (W3; the section on evil).**
*Attack.* The text says the Nietzschean yes "cannot take her side against the past." Neither can the Christian. He holds that God allows an evil only if He brings good from that very evil (CCC 324). He sings of the happy fault, and with St. Leo he says that grace gave more than envy took (CCC 412). So he too blesses an order that contains her torment. My "thus I willed it" (*Zarathustra* II, "On Redemption," verify) is simply more candid.
*Defense.* Permitting is not willing. God gives the act its being but never its defect (CCC 311). The evil "never thereby becomes good" (CCC 312). God "says no to what was done to her, and yes to her" (Wisdom 1:13; CCC 413). The good hoped for is offered to *her*, within her own life, and is not paid to others at her expense. *Amor fati* has to will the event itself. The Christian wills that it be repaired.
*Rejoinder.* The asymmetry is real. Nietzsche refuses the distinction between willing and permitting, and the text keeps it without contradiction. What it costs him is a loss only if A1 and A2 are true, and I deny them. The text calls its answer "a hope, not a proof."
**Held** (standoff; the text's description of Nietzsche is accurate).

**E5. Hell, and *Genealogy* I.15.**
*Attack.* Nietzsche cited the claim from Aquinas's tradition that the blessed see the punishments of the damned so that their own bliss may please them more (Supplement q.94 a.1, verify). He read it as revenge in the costume of love. How can "God wills each of us for her own sake" survive that?
*Defense.* The Supplement was compiled after Thomas's death and is not magisterial. Its own answer is that the blessed rejoice in divine justice, not in suffering as such (q.94 a.3, verify). More to the point, the text uses none of it. It calls hell "the heaviest thing" on the scale and the place where its own principle "is under most strain." It records that the Church has never declared any person lost and prays that all be saved (CCC 1058, 1821).
*Rejoinder.* By the fairness rule I cannot use a genealogy of motive here unless I let the text use one against me. As a question of content, the text has already paid the price.
**Held.**

**E6. The value critique: equal worth harms the highest.**
*Attack.* My central charge is not that A4 lacks a ground. It is that A4 is *bad*: pity multiplies suffering and holds back the strong (*Antichrist* 7, verify), and equality levels the rare to the measure of the herd (*Genealogy*, Preface 6; *Beyond Good and Evil* 202, 228, verify). The text's Nietzsche row answers my genealogy and my question about truth. It never answers this. Read as a portrait of my school, that is an omission.
*Defense.* The book already contains the answer. "Equal worth is not equal holiness: the saints are higher than I am in how well they love, never in what they are worth" (A4). That is an order of rank in excellence alongside equality in worth, which is exactly what Nietzsche thought Christianity could not have. W3 adds that eros is healed, not poisoned (*Deus Caritas Est* 3–5). Nietzsche's own picture of mercy as the privilege of the strongest (*Genealogy* II.10) is close to the book's claim that mercy is strength.
*Rejoinder.* That reply is strong. It belongs in the Nietzsche row, not scattered elsewhere.
**Held with repair** (R2).

**E7. Conscience correcting itself (W2).**
*Attack.* W2 says that "what moves hearers against their own interest ... is reason holding itself to consistency with goods it already sees." But consistency works in both directions. One can remove an inconsistency by narrowing regard just as well as by widening it (the text's own Rorty row concedes as much). The direction comes from earlier values and from power. The many, made confident by priests, imposed one measure on everyone (*Beyond Good and Evil* 202, verify). Elsewhere the text insists that "a history settles neither." In this one sentence it lets history support its own side.
*Defense.* The sentence is hedged ("helped by sympathy, interest and force"), and W2 as a whole is presented as "a judgment about where explanation may stop."
*Rejoinder.* The hedge covers *why the wronged pushed*. It does not cover *why the hearers yielded*, and that is the half the text credits to the law of truth. Under the text's own fairness rule it needs the same "settles neither."
**Held with repair** (R3).

**E8. The creator who may destroy (W1(2), 1 Samuel 15).**
*Attack.* The text's absolute line binds creatures and not the Creator. "He who gives life its being ... does no one wrong when He calls a life home." That is an order of rank at the root of your morality: the one who makes may break (compare *Zarathustra* I, "On the Thousand and One Goals": the creator must also destroy, verify).
*Defense.* The right the text claims is grounded in being and in keeping, not in power. "The One who holds that right gave her being, willed her for her own sake, and does not lose her in death." The creator-destroyer in my picture does not keep what he breaks. The text also concedes that Kant and the Way draw the line more absolutely, and it "pays [that cost] openly."
*Rejoinder.* The difference is real: whoever is called home is kept. Whether that keeping is real is the whole question of the case.
**Held** (honest standoff, and the cost is stated).

**E9. The longing (W3).**
*Attack.* The longing to be "received whole ... by someone who will not die" is the need of the weak. Strong solitude does without it.
*Defense.* The text says "the longing alone settles nothing," that it is not evidence, and that we may not believe because a belief comforts (A7). It also notes that Nietzsche heard the same wish for "always" (all joy wants eternity, *Zarathustra* IV, verify) and answered it with recurrence.
*Rejoinder.* Fair. The longing appears in my own author, so I cannot dismiss it as merely plebeian.
**Held.**

## 4. Ledger

- **A heading.** Holds. The phenomenal-conservative rule (trust what survives reflection, including reflection on its own origin) applies to both sides. It also licenses the noble's reflective sense of rank, and the text accepts that result ("a choice, not a proof"). The attribution to Nietzsche (he denies the second half of A6, and denies that A1–A5 and A7 hold for everyone) is accurate. *Antichrist* 2's account of "good" as whatever heightens the feeling of power is a direct denial of A5.
- **A1.** Holds as a premise, and my denial is acknowledged. "Enslaving the weak" is exactly where Nietzsche bites the bullet (*Beyond Good and Evil* 257–258, verify).
- **A2.** Holds as phenomenology. Nietzsche's rival account of guilt as internalized debt (*Genealogy* II.4, II.16, verify) is a genealogy, so it is governed by the A rule.
- **A3.** Holds, given A1 and A2. I deny the frame, not the internal logic.
- **A4.** Holds as a premise, and it is the crux. The text is honest that history "does not decide," and the distinction between equal worth and unequal holiness is its best defense against rank (E6).
- **A5.** Holds, my denial acknowledged (values as the ends of drives, per Richardson).
- **A6.** Holds with repair. Its second half is correctly attributed to my denial, though for Nietzsche the many may be spent for the few rather than the few for the many (*Beyond Good and Evil* 258). Its first half ("always asks something of the giver") is false of the text's own God, who gives "without gaining anything from us," and it is contested by Nietzsche's gift from overflow (*Zarathustra* I, "On the Gift-Giving Virtue"). See R5.
- **A7.** Holds as a premise. My denial is correctly reported in C.
- **W1 frame.** Holds. Anscombe is stated accurately. Nietzsche agrees with her diagnosis (*Twilight*, "Skirmishes" 5), which the text could cite here as a second hostile witness.
- **W1(1).** Holds as a clue. Nietzsche's story that the debt to the ancestors grew into a debt to a god (*Genealogy* II.19–20, verify) is the obvious rival, and the A rule governs it. The text's insistence that God "does not take her place in giving the pardon that is hers" quietly escapes Nietzsche's mockery of the creditor who pays himself (*Genealogy* II.21, verify). That is a strength.
- **W1(2).** Holds. Grounding the Creator's right in giving and keeping rather than in power answers E8 at parity, and the cost is stated.
- **W1 closing ("This Lawgiver is not a commander").** Holds. It rejects voluntarism and so avoids the "will that invents values" that Nietzsche also held cannot bind anyone but its inventor.
- **W2.** Holds with repair (E7, R3). The debunking paragraph is fair to the naturalist Nietzsche (cf. *Gay Science* 111 on the survival origin of logic).
- **W3 (communion).** Holds. "Said by me, not to me" is accurate and fair. *Deus Caritas Est* on eros is correctly placed against *Beyond Good and Evil* 168.
- **W3 (hiddenness).** Holds. The concession is honest. Nietzsche put the same objection: a god who knows everything and can do everything, yet does not make sure his intention is understood (*Daybreak* 91, verify). The text answers its strongest form only by denying the "now," and it says so.
- **C intro.** Holds. The fairness rule is stated and observed.
- **C: Nothing really binds.** Holds.
- **C: Feeling, refined.** Holds.
- **C: Our best words, widening.** Holds. Its admission that consistency can shrink "we" is the basis of E7.
- **C: Reason's own law.** Holds.
- **C: What we owe each other.** Holds.
- **C: Care between persons.** Holds.
- **C: Well-being, counted impartially.** Holds.
- **C: Flourishing of our kind.** Holds.
- **C: Godless realism.** Holds.
- **C: Impersonal sacred order.** Holds.
- **C: The Way.** Holds.
- **C: God, or Nature.** Holds.
- **C: The Buddha's Dhamma.** Holds.
- **C: History and class.** Holds.
- **C: Power and rank.** Holds with repair. Its citations check out: BGE 227 on honesty as the virtue free spirits cannot leave behind; *Antichrist* 20–22; *Genealogy* I.7 and I.14; *Twilight* "Skirmishes" 5; *Gay Science* 107. The dilemma is fair, and so is "he can despise the man who tortures a child as base." It omits the value critique (E6), it gives the denied range as A1–A4 where the heading says A1–A5 and A7, and it misses *Antichrist* 57 (R1, R2, R4).
- **C: Freedom that makes its values.** Holds.
- **C: A God who is Love.** Holds. "Reach, not count" is the correct claim, and "clues of fit, not evidence" is honest.
- **Evil, paragraph 1.** Holds. "Prized [suffering] as the discipline behind every human greatness" is accurate (*Beyond Good and Evil* 225).
- **Evil, First.** Holds. It names "a god beyond good and evil who makes and breaks out of sheer fullness," and that is not a straw man: Nietzsche distinguished destruction from overfullness from destruction from hatred (*Gay Science* 370, verify). The text concedes that privation theory does not rule this god out and that only the whole case does.
- **Evil, Second.** Holds.
- **Evil, Third.** Holds. The Adorno and *Spe Salvi* point stands against every this-worldly hope, mine included.
- **Evil, invitation to the protester.** Holds. It does not reach me, since I do not protest on her behalf, and it does not claim to.
- **Conclusion.** Holds. "It cannot compel the one who gives up A4" is correct, and the claim has been scaled down from CCC 31 and 36 to "clues." "And what she is made for" goes beyond what the inference itself delivers, but it is flagged as "this book answers."

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**As written: NONE.**

The case never claims to compel a reader who gives up A4 and the unconditional value of truth, which is where a Nietzschean stands. Where I attack, the text has either already conceded the point or answers it at parity.

**Minor items**
1. **The value critique is missing from the Nietzsche row** (E6). The row answers my genealogy and my question about truth, but not my central charge that equal worth and pity harm the highest. The answer already exists in A4 (excellence ranked, worth equal), so the fix is to put it in the row.
2. **Inconsistent scope.** The A heading says Nietzsche denies that "A1 through A5 and A7 hold for everyone." The C row says "giving up A1 through A4." A5 is missing from the row (A7 is handled separately).
3. **W2's conscience sentence** lets history support the text's side in one place, where everywhere else the text says history "settles neither" (E7).
4. **A6's first half** ("always asks something of the giver") is false of the text's own God, who gives from no need, and Nietzsche's gift from overflow contests it. Limiting it to *us* fixes this.
5. **A missed asset:** *Antichrist* 57. Nietzsche held a real duty of the exceptional toward the ordinary, and it grounds that duty in the giver's rank, not the receiver's claim. This is the exact structural opposite of W1(2) and sharpens "What he cannot say is that the child was wronged."

**Misstatements of my school.** None of substance. The citations I checked are accurate (BGE 227, 168; GM I.7, I.14; AC 20–22; TI "Skirmishes" 5; GS 107). If anything the text is generous: "Nor need he praise cruelty" is true, but an honest Nietzschean must also own *Antichrist* 2 and *Twilight* "Skirmishes" 36. That is a cost to my school and not a defect in the text.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- How sure one is of A4 against the genealogy of A4. Which account "best accounts" depends on the whole case.
- The value of truth. The text's dilemma and my acceptance of its second horn.
- *Amor fati* against "God says no to what was done to her, and yes to her." This is a loss only if A1 and A2 are true.
- The Creator's right over life against the creature's absolute line. It is grounded in keeping, and the keeping is the thing in question.
- The longing for "always," which both Nietzsche and the text hear and which they answer differently.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact old → new)

**R1. C, Power and rank: *Antichrist* 57.**
Old: "Nor need he praise cruelty: he can despise the man who tortures a child as base. What he cannot say is that the child was wronged."
New: "Nor need he praise cruelty: he can despise the man who tortures a child as base, and he even held that the exceptional owe the ordinary a tenderness beyond what they show their peers (*Antichrist* 57). But that duty springs from the giver's rank, not from a claim that is hers. What he cannot say is that the child was wronged."

**R2. C, Power and rank: scope, plus the value critique.**
Old: "It stays consistent by giving up A1 through A4 as claims on everyone, and the second half of A6, openly, and asks the hardest question of all: why should truth matter more than life?"
New: "It stays consistent by giving up A1 through A5 as claims on everyone, and the second half of A6, openly, and asks the hardest question of all: why should truth matter more than life? Its deepest charge is not that equal worth is false but that it is bad: that pity and equality level the rare and hold back the highest (*Genealogy*, Preface 6; *Antichrist* 7). This book answers that equal worth is not equal excellence, for the saints are higher than I am in how well they love, never in what they are worth (A4), and that mercy is strength's privilege, not weakness renamed; Nietzsche himself called the mercy in which justice ends the privilege of the most powerful (*Genealogy* II.10)."

**R3. W2: the consistency sentence.**
Old: "but what moves hearers against their own interest, toward those who can never repay, is reason holding itself to consistency with goods it already sees: the law of truth again."
New: "but what moves hearers against their own interest, toward those who can never repay, is, this book holds, reason holding itself to consistency with goods it already sees: the law of truth again. The Nietzschean reads the same pressure as the power of the many over the few, and consistency can shrink regard as easily as widen it; here too the history alone does not decide (A4)."

**R4. C, Power and rank: Nietzsche's own concession about truthfulness.**
Old: "Nietzsche kept his own honesty, which he called the virtue his kind could not get away from, but he denied that it binds every mind."
New: "Nietzsche kept his own honesty, which he called the virtue his kind could not get away from, but he denied that it binds every mind; and he granted that its unconditional form still takes its fire from the old faith that God is the truth (*Gay Science* 344), and doubted that it could outlive that faith."

**R5. A6: scope the first half to us.**
Old: "**A6. Real good for another always asks something of the giver** (time, attention, comfort, sometimes far more)."
New: "**A6. Real good for another always asks something of us who give it** (time, attention, comfort, sometimes far more)."

**R6 (optional). W1(1): name the rival genealogy.**
Old: "That guilt asks proves nothing, for a mind made for living bonds would ask whether or not she hears;"
New: "That guilt asks proves nothing, for a mind made for living bonds would ask whether or not she hears, and Nietzsche told the story the other way, of a debt to the ancestors that grew until it was owed to a god (*Genealogy* II.19–20); by this case's rule (A), his story defeats the clue only if it best accounts for the debt;"

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **Nietzsche and Anscombe agree.** Both hold that the moral "ought" without God is a survival of faith (*Twilight* "Skirmishes" 5; *Gay Science* 344; Anscombe 1958). W1 can name Nietzsche as a second hostile witness to Anscombe's diagnosis. The witness is stronger for being hostile.
- **A duty with no claimant.** *Antichrist* 57 is the cleanest foil anywhere for W1(2). Nietzsche can produce duties toward the weak, but only out of the giver's rank. The book's whole claim is that the duty comes from *her*.
- **Two kinds of mercy.** Nietzsche's mercy forgives because the offender does not matter to the strong (*Genealogy* II.10: the creditor who can afford his parasites, verify). God's mercy forgives because she does matter. This could serve Chapter 8.
- **Two kinds of gift from fullness.** Zarathustra's gift-giving virtue gives from overflow, as God gives from no need. The difference is that Zarathustra seeks receivers worth his gift, while God gives the receiver her being. This answers Nietzsche on his own best ground.
- **The text already avoids Nietzsche's sharpest mockery of redemption** (the creditor who pays himself, GM II.21) by holding that God does not take her place in the pardon that is hers. That deserves to be said aloud.
- **Be ready for *Genealogy* I.15** (the Supplement passage on the blessed and the damned). The answer is in E5: it is non-magisterial, and q.94 a.3 gives its own reply.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "that yes is said by me, not to me"
- "What he cannot say is that the child was wronged."
- "Equal worth is not equal duty ... the saints are higher than I am in how well they love, never in what they are worth." This is the book's best answer to my order of rank. Use it against me explicitly (R2).
- "God says no to what was done to her, and yes to her."
- "A history settles neither."
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."

## 8. Score

**8 / 10.** Against the Nietzschean the text overclaims nowhere that matters. It states my school accurately and generously, it concedes that it cannot compel anyone who gives up A4 and the unconditional value of truth, and every exchange ends Held or Held with a small repair.

**Honest ceiling: 8.** No theistic case can do better against my school than parity at the premises plus advantages in what it can explain. A Nietzschean grants neither equal worth nor the unconditional value of truth, so the most any argument can show him is a tension with what he does grant: his honesty, his yes to life, his hearing that all joy wants eternity. With R1–R5 the text reaches that ceiling.
