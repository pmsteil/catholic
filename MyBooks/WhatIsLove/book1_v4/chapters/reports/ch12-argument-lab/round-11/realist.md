# Blind review: the non-naturalist secular moral realist

## 1. School and voice

I speak for robust, non-naturalist moral realism without God: moral and normative truths are objective, stance-independent, necessary, and not reducible to natural facts (Shafer-Landau, Enoch, Wielenberg, Huemer; Parfit's realism without ontology). Their necessity needs no mind to hold it. Where it needs grounding at all, it is grounded in the essences of pain, persons and promises (Fine on essence). Wronging is basic and directed: a bipolar nexus between agent and patient (Thompson, Wallace, Darwall). Constraints against harming one person to benefit others express each person's status (Kamm on inviolability, Nagel on the evil of aiming at harm). I come to this text as its nearest rival. I share nearly all of A, and I share its war on error theory, debunking and voluntarism. What I deny is that the law needs a Lawgiver. My job is to find where the text claims more against me than its own ledger supports.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** Persons have claims against one another because of what they are. The directed "ought" (what A owes B) is a basic normative relation, not something derived from aggregate value or from anyone's will (Thompson; Wallace). The first principle: each person is owed treatment that respects her standing as someone who can be wronged. She is not to be harmed as a means, and she is owed rescue and fair shares where we can provide them. Kamm adds that a world where she may not be spent is one where she has a higher status. **Holds.** It stops at a brute necessary truth, as the text says every account stops somewhere. It borrows nothing from theism. Darwall's version, which grounds claims in the standing to make demands of others, does not reach the newborn (the text is right about this). Wallace's and Thompson's versions do reach her.

**2. A hard dilemma resolved.** Take conjoined twins: Jodie can live if separated, and Mary cannot live without draining Jodie, who will die within months unless they are separated (the English *Re A* case, 2000, verify details). My principles are these. Each twin has her own claim. Harming someone as a means differs from harm that comes as a side effect of removing a threat. Under Kamm's Principle of Permissible Harm, harm that flows from a greater good, or from the means that are that good, may be permissible where harm used as a means is not. An innocent threat may in some cases be stopped. Proportionality decides the rest. Separation is permissible: Mary is not used to save Jodie, and the surgery removes Jodie from a threat that Mary poses through no fault of her own. **Holds without borrowing** truth, justice, mercy or self-gift in the book's covenantal sense. The weak point is honest: my method treats considered case intuitions as evidence (Huemer's phenomenal conservatism). That is defensible, and the text's own A heading uses the same epistemic principle. Catholic moralists divided on this case. The text's line in W1(2) on mother and child ("even when she would die whatever is done") shows where the two accounts would part.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** For the newborn, the dying and the enemy, my school grounds equal basic status in the essence of the kind: being a being whose nature is rational agency, whether exercised, not yet exercised or lost. Kagan's modal personism is a nearby variant: a being who could have been a person. This status cannot be lost by crime. Against agents my school is *more* unconditional than the text, because it recognizes no Lord-of-life exception (see Exchange 4). **Holds for most cases. It is brute, and contested inside my school, for the congenitally and permanently profoundly impaired.** For them we must either hold, as a brute necessary truth, that membership in the kind confers full status, which our own colleagues call speciesism, or with McMahan deny equal status. So there it **partly breaks**: holding equality costs a brute posit, and keeping a principled account costs equality. Honesty requires saying that the capacity-based majority of my school is less friendly to A4 than the text's C row suggests (see §5).

**4. Ending point for flourishing.** No single final end. The ultimate good for a human is a life rich in plural objective goods: knowledge, love and friendship, achievement, virtue, pleasure, appreciation of beauty, and self-direction, lived in right relation to others (an objective-list view; Parfit). The guiding principles are the directed constraints of challenge 1, together with impartial concern for well-being within them. **Coherent, borrows nothing, but stops short in two places.** It cannot offer the dead, or the infant who died in pain, any repair: her life went badly, and that stays true forever. And it has no account of a life's final unity or completion beyond its own span. That is a real cost of my view, not an inconsistency in it.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1. Euthyphro, second round.**
*Attack.* The text's own grounding helps me. A wrong is "wrong because of what it does to her" (W1). "Not even God makes cruelty right; He cannot." "A will that invents values ... cannot bind." So the features that make an act wrong lie in the creature. God's role reduces to three things: (i) creating beings who can be wronged, (ii) being the place where the law is held ("His own wisdom"), and (iii) being identical with the Good. Item (i) is causal and explains existence, not normativity. Item (ii) is a location my school says necessary truths do not need (Parfit). Item (iii) is a brute identity: what makes God's nature the standard, rather than one more exemplar of love and justice, except that it has the good-making properties in the highest degree? Then the properties are the standard.
*Defense.* The Good is not an exemplar measured against a further standard. God is subsistent being and goodness itself (ST I q.3 a.4; q.6). The good-making properties are the ways creatures participate in Him, so they are not a standard prior to Him. The point is not fewer first principles but one source for the law, the minds that read it and the beings it concerns (C, "A God who is Love").
*Rejoinder.* That is a coherent stopping point, and the text itself says so ("the realist says the same of his necessary truths"). The theist's single source unifies only by assigning one bearer two different relations: the law is *constituted* in His wisdom, and creatures are *caused* by His will. Whether that is a real explanatory gain or a single name for two relations is a judgment about where explanation may stop, as W2 says.
*Verdict:* **Held**, at parity, and the text concedes the parity accurately.

**Exchange 2. Fit and debunking (W2).**
*Attack.* My third-factor answer (Enoch; Wielenberg; Huemer on convergence toward liberal values) explains the fit: survival and flourishing are by and large good, so selection toward them correlates belief with moral truth, and general reasoning, once in place, reaches necessary truths in logic, number and morals. The theist's alternative, a Mind that made minds to read truths held in itself, only moves the access question: how does God's intellect relate to the necessary truths? By identity, which is brute. And theism predicted a more reliable conscience than we find. The text concedes that conscience fails exactly where self-interest pulls, and that "a world with no one behind it predicts it too."
*Defense.* Aquinas does not predict a uniformly reliable conscience. First principles are known to all, and the further conclusions are darkened by passion and custom (ST I-II q.94 a.4, a.6; CCC 1960). Self-correction toward those who cannot repay is reason holding itself to consistency with goods it already sees. The theist explains the fit "in logic and number as in right" from a single source.
*Rejoinder.* That is accommodation, not prediction. But the text never claims it as prediction: it calls the whole matter "a judgment about where explanation may stop, and the rivals do not share it." That is correct.
*Verdict:* **Held** as an honest standoff.

**Exchange 3. Kind versus capacity, and the call (W1(2), C final row).**
*Attack.* The text's surplus over my essentialism is "an equal call, which does not wait on what anyone can do." But Catholic teaching holds the supernatural call to be gratuitous, "a gift no nature could claim" (*Humani Generis* 26), and holds that God could have made intellectual creatures without it. Dilemma: in that pure-nature world, either humans would still have equal dignity, so the call is not what explains it, or they would not, so dignity depends on a free gift of grace that reason cannot see. That is voluntarism by another door, and it is a premise only Christians grant.
*Defense.* The first branch, rightly read. Ontological dignity belongs to the person "because he or she exists and is willed, created, and loved by God" (DI 7, paraphrase), and being in His image the individual has the dignity of a person (CCC 357). The image lies in the rational nature, which reason can see (DI 1). The call is the root reason of that dignity's fullness (GS 19), not a later addition to a dignity that would otherwise be missing. The text says as much: "the good that grounds her dignity ... a nature made in His image ... and with it a call."
*Rejoinder.* Then, as far as reason can see, the ground of equality is the nature: my ground. The theist's real surplus is narrower, and I grant that it is real: *each one willed for her own sake*. An intention directed at every member of the kind explains why the kind and not the capacity sets the worth, for the child who will never reason. I take that as a brute necessary truth or, with McMahan, deny it. The final C row, however, counts "an end offered to each" as reason's reach without saying that it is revealed.
*Verdict:* **Held with repair.** Separate what reason can weigh (willed for her own sake; a rational nature) from what faith adds (the supernatural end). See §6, Repair 5. On this point, honestly, theism has an edge over my school.

**Exchange 4. The Lord-of-life exception and A6.**
*Attack.* The text says the line binds us because "what protects her is her own worth ... to aim at her death for others is to treat one willed for her own sake as existing for them." That reason does not depend on who the agent is. Yet the text exempts God (1 Samuel 15) on a different ground: "He who gives life its being, and can give it back." That ground fits badly with the section on evil: "nor could a good paid her afterward" answer what was done to her. Worse, my school protects the innocent against every agent. On this axis theism grounds inviolability *less* unconditionally than godless realism does, which bears on the brief's own question.
*Defense.* The Thomist ground is not compensation but dominion and a universal decree: by His decree all die, and one who carried out His command would be His instrument, with no right of his own (ST I-II q.94 a.5 ad 2; II-II q.64 a.6 ad 1). The manner, "by these hands", is put on the scale as a cost, and the text concedes in so many words that Kant, Kamm, Nagel, Mencius and the Way draw the line more absolutely.
*Rejoinder.* The concession is honest, and it stands as a cost against the case at the weighing. But the phrase "can give it back" invites the reading that undercuts it. If dominion is the ground, the text should say so, and say that restoration is His promise, not His title.
*Verdict:* **Held with repair** (§6, Repair 3). The standoff favors my school on this one axis, and the text says so.

**Exchange 5. The debt to the dead (W1(1)).**
*Attack.* The witness rests on the premise that "a debt truly owed to her is one she must be able, one day, to receive." My school denies it. Directed duties can have claimants who will never receive: the permanently comatose, and promises to those who will never know. The text's own A2 shows that a wrong can be done with no experience of the wronged. The posthumous-harm literature (Feinberg, Pitcher; verify) needs no survival. Worse, the section on evil says these clues "could be answered only by a loving God." McTaggart's atheist idealism, a timeless community of selves bound by love with no Creator (*The Nature of Existence*; verify), answers the debt to the dead without God.
*Defense.* Wronging needs no experience on the victim's part, but forgiveness is an act, and only someone can perform it. The text does not claim the premise is shared. It weighs the clue exactly as heavily as the reader finds the premise true, and it says that for those who deny the premise it "weighs little." And W3's specific shape, being *given* being and told it is good that we exist, is not met by McTaggart, whose selves give one another no being.
*Rejoinder.* Agreed on W1(1)'s conditional framing and on W3. But the sentence in the section on evil overclaims: the debt-to-the-dead clue points to persons enduring, not to a Creator, and the text should name the godless form of that answer.
*Verdict:* **Held with repair** (§6, Repair 4).

**Exchange 6. Permitting evil "in order to" draw good, against "persons may not be spent."**
*Attack.* A6 forbids doing evil so that good may come (CCC 1789). CCC 412 says God permits evil in order to draw forth a greater good, and CCC 324 says that good comes from that very evil. If the good is drawn *from her suffering*, her suffering is a means in God's plan. For natural evil, Thomist concurrence makes God more than an allower: He gives the parasite's act its being. Doing versus allowing, which A6 relies on, is strained for a being who sustains every act.
*Defense.* Three points. (i) Kant's "merely": the text claims only that she is not spent as a *mere* means, since the good is offered to her as well. (ii) My own school supplies a distinction: Kamm's Doctrine of Triple Effect separates acting *because* a good will come from an evil from acting *in order to* bring that evil about. God intends the good and permits the evil. The evil is not His end or His chosen means. (iii) The text places the parasite and the pain of the ages before man explicitly "on this side of the scale" and calls the Fall's disordering of creation a teaching, "not something we see."
*Rejoinder.* (i) and (iii) hold. (ii) is a genuine help, and the text could use it. But the Catechism's own wording, "in order to", must stand, so the help works as a gloss and not a rewording. The cost from concurrence stays on the scale, as the text already keeps it.
*Verdict:* **Held.** An optional insight is noted in §7.

**Exchange 7. The final weighing against godless realism.**
*Attack.* Total the text's own verdicts against my school. On A1–A7 it accommodates me. The W1 frame "does not decide." W1(1) and W3 are "clues of fit, not evidence." W2 is "a judgment the rivals do not share." W1(2)'s surplus is real but modest (Exchange 3). Against all that, evil is "real weight, and it is heavy"; for the pattern of pain in nature, "the evidence is not weak"; and for the reader reasoning alone, 1 Samuel 15 is "a cost against the case." Evil weighs against theism and not against me. Realism makes no claim that anyone intended or permits anything, and pain falling "roughly where survival would put it" is what naturalism-plus-realism predicts. The section on evil lists the rivals who bear the weight more easily (the Buddhist, Nietzsche, the Way, Spinoza) and leaves out the one that bears it at least as easily as any of them: mine. On the text's own ledger, then, "the best explanation of love as we actually find it is a Lawgiver who is Love" does not follow against this rival.
*Defense.* The conclusion is a judgment offered, not a proof claimed: "An honest reader may weigh it differently." It is a converging case in Newman's sense, and convergence is not addition. Unity and reach are real explanatory virtues. The realist carries more brute necessities: the moral truths, the fit of minds to them, and the significance of the kind. And the realist cannot offer the child repair, which is part of "love as we actually find it."
*Rejoinder.* The hedge establishes that holding the conclusion is rationally permitted, not that it is the better explanation. A converging case still has to show convergence *against each rival*, and against mine this chapter shows parity-plus on the moral side and an admitted net debit on evil. Repair is a hope, not data; the text says so itself ("That is not evidence"). So the honest statement is that, against the realist, this chapter alone does not tip the scale, and whatever tips it must come from the rest of the book's case.
*Verdict:* **Failed as written; repairable** (§6, Repairs 1–2).

## 4. Ledger

- **A heading.** Holds. Its epistemic rule (trust what survives reflection, including reflection on its origins, unless its truth plays no part in the best explanation of the belief) is phenomenal conservatism plus Harman's test, which my school accepts. The list of deniers is accurate (McMahan correctly described).
- **A1.** Holds. Fully shared.
- **A2.** Holds. The secret-and-letter case is an excellent separation of wronging from worsening. Directed-duty theorists will accept it.
- **A3.** Holds. Shared, including the claim that no one can waive the claim in her place.
- **A4.** Holds with repair. The core is shared. The Catholic extras (the death penalty, "nor ... at his asking") are honestly labeled as this book's own. The ground for the profoundly impaired is where my school is weakest. See §5 on the generous C-row description.
- **A5.** Holds. The is–ought gap is shared. It leaves the source of nature's ends open, rightly.
- **A6.** Holds. The answer to Greene (calling the alarm false presupposes the answer) is the Berker/Kamm reply, and correct. "Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent" is shared. The absolute line at every number, and against consent, is labeled as this book's.
- **A7.** Holds. Shared. Epistemic normativity is as non-natural as moral normativity (Cuneo's companions-in-guilt argument), so A7 is an ally of both of us against Mackie and Nietzsche, not evidence for God over me.
- **W1 frame.** Holds. Anscombe is fairly stated. "Not wrong merely because it is forbidden" is good anti-voluntarism. It correctly concedes that neither unreleasability nor owed guilt decides between theism and realism.
- **W1(1).** Holds with repair. It is properly conditional, and its premise about receiving the debt is denied by my school at parity. The repair belongs to the matching line in the section on evil (McTaggart).
- **W1(2).** Holds with repair. The surplus "willed for her own sake" is real for the child who will never reason. The "can give it back" wording conflicts with the section on evil (Repair 3). The concession that Kant, Kamm and Nagel draw the line more absolutely is honest, but imprecise about Kamm (Repair 6).
- **W1 closing ("This Lawgiver is not a commander").** Holds. The anti-voluntarist argument ("a will that invents values ... cannot bind") is one my school uses itself. Against me it establishes no theistic surplus beyond Exchange 1's standoff.
- **W2.** Holds. My school's answer is stated accurately and in its best form, and the result is honestly called a judgment about where explanation stops.
- **W3 (longing).** Holds as a clue. It does not claim to be evidence ("That is not evidence"). Its specific shape, being given being by One who is no rival, is not met by godless immortality.
- **W3 (hiddenness).** Holds as an honest cost ("part of an answer, not a full answer"). The Church teaching cited is accurate.
- **C intro.** Holds. The fairness rule on origins is stated and kept.
- **C, Nothing really binds.** Holds. Fair to Mackie and Street. The pressure from A7 is the strongest move, and my school endorses it.
- **C, Feeling, refined.** Holds. Fair to the quasi-realist. "Might a heart refined as far as any could be still be wrong about her" is the right question, and one I ask too.
- **C, Our best words, widening.** Holds. Fair to Rorty and Wong.
- **C, Reason's own law.** Holds. Accurate on Kant (5:131; 7:63). The cost is paid openly.
- **C, What we owe each other.** Holds. Fair to Scanlon and Darwall, including the point that Darwall's addressability does not reach the newborn.
- **C, Care between persons.** Holds.
- **C, Well-being, counted impartially.** Holds. Hooker's rule consequentialism is fairly described.
- **C, The flourishing of our kind.** Holds. "A fair standoff, over gift and hope" is exactly right, and it is the right model for Repair 5.
- **C, Necessary truths that belong to no one (my school).** Holds with repair. My positions are stated accurately in the main: Parfit "needs no dwelling", essences, third factors. It is generous in saying that "its best defenders" ground worth in the essence of the kind. That is a minority view in my school (Repair 7).
- **C, An impersonal sacred order.** Holds.
- **C, The Way.** Holds.
- **C, God, or Nature.** Holds.
- **C, The Buddha's Dhamma.** Holds.

- **C, Freedom that makes its values.** Holds.
- **C, A God who is Love.** Holds with repair. Its comparison with me is accurate ("not fewer first principles but one source"). "An end offered to each" is counted as reason's reach without labeling it as revealed (Repair 5).
- **Evil, paragraph 1 ("On the other side of the scale").** Holds with repair. It is exceptionally honest on hell, 1 Samuel 15 and nature, and its Catholic statements are accurate. Its list of rivals who bear the weight more easily omits godless realism (Repair 2).
- **Evil, First (privation).** Holds. Correctly limited: it rules out a dark god, not an indifferent world. The line "could be answered only by a loving God" overclaims (Repair 4).

It is repairable by scoping the claim (Repair 1). The closing appeal about the newborn holds, and my school answers it the same way up to "what she is made for."

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**Serious: the conclusion's comparative verdict is unsupported against godless realism.** The premises are the text's own concessions: moral parity-plus (A shared, W1 frame undecided, W1(1) and W3 clues only, W2 a contested judgment, W1(2) a modest real surplus), against heavy evil, which on the text's own account weighs against theism and not against realism. It follows that this chapter does not show a Lawgiver to be the *best* explanation against this rival. The hedge ("an honest reader may weigh it differently") makes the conclusion rationally permitted; it does not make it better supported. The text cannot answer this at parity from within the chapter, though the book as a whole might. Repair: scope the claim and name the rival (Repairs 1–2).

No Fatal objection stands.

**Minor items**
- In the section on evil, "could be answered only by a loving God" (debt to the dead) overclaims. McTaggart's godless immortality of loving selves is a counterexample for W1(1), though not for W3's shape.
- In W1(2), "and can give it back" reads as compensation and clashes with "nor could a good paid her afterward" in the section on evil.
- In the final C row, "an end offered to each" is counted as reason's reach although it is a revealed, gratuitous end. That conflicts with the brief's rule against Christian-only premises unless it is labeled as such.

**Misstatements of my school (near-misses, not errors)**
- *Kamm and Nagel* "in their ways" draw the line more absolutely. That is true for binding every agent, God included. But Kamm has defended physician-assisted suicide (1999, verify), and Nagel treats constraints as non-absolute under catastrophe (*War and Massacre*; *The View from Nowhere*, verify). The parenthetical covers numbers but not consent.
- *"Its best defenders ground [worth] in the essence of the kind."* This is generous. Most non-naturalist realists ground status in capacities, which is why McMahan's revisionism is a mainstream position and not a fringe one. The text is more charitable to my school here than accuracy allows, and the correction slightly strengthens its own A4 point against me.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Where explanation may stop: one personal source versus brute necessary truths (W2; C final row).
- Whether a debt to the dead must be receivable (W1(1)).
- The Lord-of-life exception. Here my school grounds inviolability against agents *more* unconditionally, and the text concedes the cost.
- The kind-versus-capacity ground for the profoundly impaired. Here the text's "willed for her own sake" has a real edge over my brute essentialism, and I concede it.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact old → new)

**Repair 1 (Conclusion; Serious).**
Old: "Evil weighs against that conclusion, heavily; in this book's judgment it does not overturn it."
New: "Evil weighs against that conclusion, heavily; in this book's judgment it does not overturn it. Against its nearest rival without God, the realist who holds that these truths are real and need no Mind, the case is closest: what this chapter adds there is reach and fit, not a refutation, and on his account evil is no evidence at all, for no one permitted it. So against him this chapter does not decide the weighing alone; it is one of many signs that converge, and the rest of this book's case must carry the rest."

**Repair 2 (Evil, paragraph 1; part of the Serious repair).**
Old: "A Way that is not humane gives life and lets each thing go, and owes no one an answer; nor does Spinoza's God, which intends no one."
New: "A Way that is not humane gives life and lets each thing go, and owes no one an answer; nor does Spinoza's God, which intends no one. The realist who holds that these truths need no Mind bears it as easily as any: he can say, as fully as we, that what was done to her was wrong, and need give no account of why it was permitted, for no one permitted it; what he cannot offer her is repair (W1(1))."

## 7. New insights; lines worth keeping

**Insights for the book**
- **Name your nearest rival in the weighing.** The case is strongest against error theory, Nietzsche, relativism and voluntarism, and nearly all of that strength comes from A1–A7, which godless realism shares. Saying this openly makes the book more credible, not less. Against the realist, the chapter's honest yield is (a) "willed for her own sake" as the explanation of why kind and not capacity sets worth for the child who will never reason, and (b) one source for law, mind and the natures the law concerns. Point (a) is the sharpest thing in the chapter against my school. It deserves a sentence of its own in the realist row: "On the child who will never reason, his ground is a necessary truth he takes as given, or denies; ours is an intention toward her that explains it."
- **Recruit Kamm's inviolability.** Kamm's view that constraints express a higher status for each person, not a lower value on outcomes, supports A6's "what makes crossing it wrong is ... a claim that is *his*" from inside secular realism. It strengthens the text's case against utilitarianism.
- **Cuneo's companions-in-guilt argument** (epistemic norms are as non-natural as moral ones) supports A7 against Mackie and the debunker. It should be cited as shared ground, so that A7 is not mistaken for a theistic point.
- **Kamm's Doctrine of Triple Effect** ("because of" versus "in order to") can help the second answer in the section on evil explain God's permission without implying that the evil is His means. It can be offered only as a gloss on CCC 412, whose wording must stand.
- **The secret-and-letter case in A2** is the clearest separation of wronging from worsening I have seen in a popular text. Keep it, and consider giving it to W1(1) as well: it shows that a wrong needs no experience on her part, which sharpens why *forgiveness*, an act and not an experience, needs her.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."
- "Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent."
- "A will that invents values can command, but it cannot bind; even a promise binds only under a law no promiser invented."
- "Equal worth is not equal duty ... Nor is it equal holiness."
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it."
- "The rebel can take her side; he cannot keep her."
