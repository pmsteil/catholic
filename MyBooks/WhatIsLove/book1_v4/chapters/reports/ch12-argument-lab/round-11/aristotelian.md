# Blind review — Secular Neo-Aristotelian / Virtue Ethics

## 1. School and voice

I speak for the secular "Aristotelian naturalism" that grew out of Anscombe's 1958 advice to set the juridical "moral ought" aside and go back to the virtues. Its main sources are Philippa Foot (*Natural Goodness*, 2001; "Euthanasia," 1977; the 1967 double-effect paper), Michael Thompson (*Life and Action*, 2008; "What Is It to Wrong Someone?", 2004), Rosalind Hursthouse (*On Virtue Ethics*, 1999), John McDowell ("Two Sorts of Naturalism," 1995; *Mind and World*, 1994) and Martha Nussbaum (*The Fragility of Goodness*; *Frontiers of Justice*, 2006). The core thesis is this. Goodness and defect in a living thing are measured against its life-form. The virtues are natural goodness of the will and intellect in a rational social animal. Practical reason's demands do not depend on desire. No standpoint outside the life of reason, and no Mind behind the form, is needed for any of this to bind. In this report I argue against the text, not for it, but I give it every concession it earns.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**(1) Starting point for justice.** Justice is the virtue that gives each person what is due to them (Aristotle, *NE* V). What is due comes from two sources. The first is the human good: what members of our life-form need in order to live well (the natural history of the kind; Nussbaum's list of central capabilities). The second is the practices human life cannot do without, such as promising, property and trust. Foot, following Anscombe, calls these "Aristotelian necessities": that without which the human good cannot be had. → **Holds** for the monadic judgments ("unjust," "cruel"). **Partly strained** for the bipolar "owed *to her*." Thompson himself treats the logical form of "X wrongs Y" as a distinct puzzle that the natural-historical framework does not obviously yield (paraphrase; verify). We hold it, but more as something the virtuous person perceives than as something derived.

**(2) A hard dilemma.** Take the case the text itself calls "the line's hardest cost": mother and unborn child will both die unless the child is killed. The practically wise person (the phronimos) reasons through Foot's distinction between harming and failing to help. The child is not deprived of any life she would otherwise have had. Nobody's good is traded for anyone else's gain. One life can be saved and nothing is taken from the other that she would have kept. Neither justice nor charity forbids saving the mother. Foot herself found the Catholic verdict here very hard to accept (paraphrase; verify). → **Holds, without borrowing.** The concepts used are justice, charity and the human good. We do not need the book's definitions of truth, justice, mercy and sacrifice; our own virtue concepts do the work. We do borrow something here, though: equal standing for the unborn child, which we feel bound to respect, enters the reasoning without our having derived it.

**(3) Equal, unconditional dignity.** Our ground is membership in the kind. A nature is not had more or less (*Categories* 5), so the newborn, the dying and the severely disabled are fully human. Their good is a human good, so what humans owe humans in need is owed to them. The enemy is owed justice. → **Holds** for equal *membership* and for an equal claim not to be killed or abandoned. **Breaks** in three places that I must admit:
- (a) The same form that gives membership is the measure of natural *defect*. By Footian standards, a life without reason is a defective human life. Equality of what is *owed* therefore does not come from the form. It comes from how far justice and charity reach, and we take that as given.
- (b) Nussbaum's species-norm approach treats some extreme cases (anencephaly, permanent vegetative state) as no longer a human life in the relevant sense (paraphrase; verify).
- (c) Foot holds that a life can stop being a good to the one who lives it, so voluntary euthanasia need not be unjust. "Unconditional" in the Church's sense (not even hers to waive) we deny.

**(4) Ending point for flourishing.** The ultimate human good is eudaimonia: a whole human life in which the virtues and practical wisdom are exercised, together with friendship, knowledge, play, bodily integrity and political life, all within a mortal span. The guiding principles are the virtues, with the phronimos as the standard (Hursthouse's "v-rules"). → **Coherent.** It **breaks honestly** on luck. Nussbaum's "fragility" admits that some people never reach this good: the child who dies young, the person who will never reason. For them we offer care while they live, and nothing after.

## 3. Debate highlights

**E1. W2's trichotomy: "an accident, an order that needs no mind behind it, or a wisdom."**
*Attack.* "Accident" and "only the good of the kind that happened to arise" carry an unearned slur. In Thompson's account, natural-historical judgments ("the human being has 32 teeth," "humans keep promises") are normative whatever their causal history. A form's having arisen by selection makes its good no less the good *of that form*. That an oak's good differs from a wolf's does not make either one arbitrary.
*Defense.* The text does not say that "accident" means "not binding." Its argument moves past the point: the *core* (gratuitous cruelty, betrayal, treating a person as a thing) "would be wrong for any creature that can reason and suffer," so some moral truths are not kind-relative, and the text then asks where such necessary truths hold. Catholic natural law is itself largely fitted to human nature (ST I-II q.94 a.2; the text says "much of the natural law is fitted to our kind").
*Rejoinder.* Granted. The universal core is thin, close to formal: "gratuitous" means serving no good. Its necessity needs no dwelling-place any more than logic's does. McDowell adds that the "fit" between minds and reasons is not a coincidence calling for a view from outside, because the space of reasons is second nature, not an external order. The text's answer is that this is a stopping point. It says openly that this is "a judgment about where explanation may stop, and the rivals do not share it."
**Held.** The text claims only what it can keep. I propose a fairness repair to the word "accident" (R4).

**E2. A2 and W1(1): must a debt truly owed be one she can one day receive?**
*Attack.* Justice is a virtue directed at another person. Owing does not require that the debt can be received. Aristotle grants the dead are touched, if only slightly (*NE* I.10–11). The virtuous man's remorse toward a betrayed friend who has died is a *fitting*, permanent part of a good life. It is tragic remainder, not a symptom that points to a hidden receiver (Nussbaum, *Fragility*).
*Defense.* The text agrees that the debt is the same on the second road and the third. It explicitly says the asking "proves nothing, for a mind made for living bonds would ask whether or not she hears." It calls the witness a clue that "weighs as much as the reader finds that a debt truly owed to her is one she must be able, one day, to receive."
*Rejoinder.* None needed. The text has stated my position and given it its due.
**Held** (as a clue, at parity).

**E3. A4 and W1(2) against us: does God add anything to the grounding of equal worth that the form does not already give?**
*Attack.* The Aristotelian C row concedes the point itself: "her equal worth follows her form, much as this book says it follows what she is," and it calls the standoff "not over what she is worth." Yet the final C row claims a "reach" over the rivals in explaining "why there is at all a kind whose worth does not follow ability." Against us, then, that reach is either nothing or a cosmological question ("why is there such a kind?") presented as a moral one.
*Defense (the strongest available, and stronger than the text uses).* The concession is too generous to you. On your own account the form plays two roles. It gives membership, which is whole. It is also the standard by which individuals are judged sound or defective. Foot's framework requires calling a human who lacks reason *defective*, a deficient specimen of the form. So the form gives you "she is one of us, and defective," not "she is worth as much as you." The equality comes from how far justice and charity reach, and you take that as given. Foot's own justification of justice runs through human need for cooperation, which explains the claims of fellow cooperators better than the claims of the profoundly disabled. The Church grounds equal worth in something that does not grade: an equal call to communion with God, given whatever a person's powers (GS 19, 24; DI 24). That call is distinct from the form, which does grade.
*Rejoinder.* Judging how well a life goes is not judging what is owed to the one who lives it. Her good is a *human* good, so what humans owe humans in need is owed to her. That is not reciprocity; charity is a virtue precisely because it reaches those who cannot repay. But I concede that we hold the *equality* of that claim as something the virtuous person perceives, without deriving it. That is the same thing the text says care ethics "takes as given."
**Held with repair.** The text should take the stronger line it has earned (R1). As written, it concedes parity on worth in one row and then claims an advantage on worth in another.

**E4. A6 and W1(2): "not hers to waive."**
*Attack.* Foot argues that justice guards a life for the sake of the one who lives it, so where life has stopped being a good to her and she asks to die, helping her need not be unjust. "Held in trust from the Lord of life" is a Christian-only premise.
*Defense.* The text agrees, and says so more than once: "These are this book's claims, not shared ones"; "many who hold the rest of A would let a dying man ask for death." The C row states Foot's position accurately. Nothing a non-Christian must grant contains the demand.
*Rejoinder.* None. The text complies with the brief's own rule.
**Held.**

**E5. W1(2): "what protects her is her own worth" next to 1 Samuel 15.**
*Attack.* If what protects the infant is *her own worth*, that worth should protect her from any hand. The text instead holds that the Lord of life could command her killing without injustice, and that the one who carried it out would act by God's right. So what actually protects her from us is that the right over her life is not ours: a matter of jurisdiction, which is the "law conception" Anscombe described. The phronimos sees two things. In the craniotomy case, killing to save the mother is permissible. Slaughtering infants on command is an atrocity. The text's line inverts both verdicts.
*Defense.* The two claims do not conflict. Her worth protects her from *us* because we can only take life; we cannot give it back. God, who gives being, keeps her and does not lose her in death (W1(1)), so His "calling home" is never aimed against her good. Both costs are named openly in the text: "the line's hardest cost," and the wars that "weigh more than an unanswered why," which for a reader weighing by reason alone are "a cost against the case, not only a mystery."
*Rejoinder.* That defense works for *death*. It does not work for the *manner*: the sword in human hands, the terror. The text admits this ("not why He would call these lives home by these hands"). It is priced, so it is not a hidden defect. But the sentence "what protects her is her own worth" claims more than the paragraph supports.
**Held with repair** (R2: "from us").

**E6. The C row's account of our end: "an activity each must achieve, which she will never reach and no one offers her."**
*Attack.* Flourishing in the Aristotelian tradition is achieved together, in a polis, through friendship and care. Others *can* offer the disabled child much of a human life: being held, loved, fed and played with. Nussbaum's whole project is that society owes her as much of the threshold as can be secured.
*Defense.* The text's point is what the end *is*: a life of reason well lived, which she will never have. The Church's end, by contrast, is a gift that surpasses every human power (CCC 1722).
*Rejoinder.* That point stands. "No one offers her" is false as written, but the contrast survives once it is restated.
**Held with repair** (R3).

**E7. W3 and the longing to be known wholly.**
*Attack.* Philia is among the highest human goods. A longing that no human life can satisfy (to be received whole, by someone who will not die) is not evidence of a hidden object. It is the kind of desire practical wisdom learns to hold without being ruled by it.
*Defense.* The text agrees it is not evidence. It says "the longing alone settles nothing" and "a longing does not prove its answer," and it calls W3 a clue of fit. It claims only "where that account stops."
**Held.**

**E8. A7 and the Nietzsche dilemma.**
*Attack.* The dilemma does not catch us. On Foot's account, practical and theoretical rationality do not depend on desire: the man who stops looking because he fears the truth is defective as a rational animal whatever he wants. So truthfulness "binds every reasoning mind by what a mind is" without a Lawgiver.
*Defense.* The text's first horn says exactly that and sends it to W2 ("something binds every mind by what a mind is, which is the question of W2"). It claims only that the dilemma "does not settle" the matter.
**Held.** The text already has room for our answer.

## 4. Ledger

- **A heading** — Holds. The epistemic rule ("what still seems true... including honest reflection on how we came to believe it") is McDowell's Neurathian method in all but name. Listing deniers (Mackie, Engels, Rorty, Sartre, Nietzsche, Singer, McMahan) is fair; the Aristotelian denies none of A1–A3, A5 or A7.
- **A1** — Holds. Cruelty, treachery and enslavement are vices whatever a culture approves. Aristotle's natural slavery was a failure of our founder's practical wisdom, and we repudiate it.
- **A2** — Holds. The secret example is exactly right on our view: betrayal is an injustice to *her* whatever anyone experiences. "Shame that looks only at itself" is suitably qualified.
- **A3** — Holds.
- **A4** — Holds as a claim we largely share. The Church-specific parts are fenced off properly. "Equal worth is not equal duty" fits Aristotelian graded friendship well.
- **A5** — Holds. It is our thesis. It correctly leaves open whether ends need a Giver.
- **A6** — Holds. The non-shared claims are labelled. "To call the alarm false one must already know that the difference it tracks does not matter" is a sound reply to the debunker.
- **A7** — Holds. We ground it differently (E8).
- **W1 frame** — Holds. Anscombe is reported accurately: she proposed setting the words aside. "Every account that keeps these words in the sense Anscombe meant" is properly conditional, and we are the school that did not keep them. Listing the Aristotelian among those who say pardon is owed to the victim is accurate.
- **W1(1)** — Holds as a clue (E2). The "second road" fairly describes most secular Aristotelians.
- **W1(2)** — Holds with repair (E3, E5). The Foot attribution (magistrate and mob; harming versus failing to help) is accurate. "What protects her is her own worth" needs "from us."
- **W1 closing ("This Lawgiver is not a commander...")** — Holds. It rejects voluntarism cleanly. "A will that invents values can command, but it cannot bind" is a point we share.
- **W2** — Holds with repair (E1). "Accident... only" is unfair in tone. The substance is honest, and the stopping-point concession is exact. The Darwin quotation from *Descent* ch. 4 is accurate in substance.
- **W3 (communion)** — Holds as a clue (E7).
- **W3 (hiddenness)** — Holds. It is outside my school's main concern. The internal Catholic doctrine is accurate (LG 16, GS 22, CCC 1257, 1261).

- **C: Nothing really binds** — Holds.
- **C: Feeling, refined** — Holds. Quasi-realism is fairly stated.
- **C: Our best words, widening** — Holds.
- **C: Reason's own law** — Holds. The Kant citations are plausible (verify Akademie pages).
- **C: What we owe each other** — Holds. The limit of Darwall's second-personal standing is well observed.

- **C: The flourishing of our kind** — Holds with repair. It is fair on the stopping points, *Categories* 5, Politics VII.16 and Foot on euthanasia. It is too generous on equal worth (E3), "no one offers her" is false as written (E6), and it may overstate how many of us hold the line against killing for others at every number (verify).
- **C: Necessary truths that belong to no one** — Holds.

- **C: A God who is Love** — Holds with repair. Against us, its "reach" on worth is earned only once the Aristotelian row is corrected (R1). Otherwise, against my school, the reach reduces to W2's stopping-point judgment plus a cosmological "why is there such a kind."

- **Conclusion** — Holds. It claims less than CCC 31/36 allow and keeps faith separate from reason's weighing. The hillside test is aimed at someone who gives up A4. Against us it does not decide the matter; it moves us to the standoff over gift, as the text knows.

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**As written: NONE.**

There is no Fatal or Serious objection. Every place where my school parts from the case is one the text has stated, priced and placed where it belongs: the stopping point in W2, the receivable debt in W1(1), the inalienability of life in A6 and W1(2), 1 Samuel 15, and the craniotomy cost.

**Minor items**
1. **Internal tension on worth (Minor).** The Aristotelian row concedes "her equal worth follows her form... not over what she is worth." The final C row claims reach over the rivals on "why there is at all a kind whose worth does not follow ability." Against my school these two cannot both stand. The fix (R1) *strengthens* the text: natural goodness grades lives against the form, so form alone yields membership and possible defect, not equal worth.
2. **"What protects her is her own worth" (Minor).** Given 1 Samuel 15, it should be "from us" (R2).
3. **"No one offers her" (Minor).** False as written; care offers her much (R3).
4. **"Accident... only the good of the kind" (Minor, tone).** It suggests that an unplanned form's good binds less. The argument does not need that suggestion (R4).
5. **"It even grants the hinge" (Minor).** It implies the case depends on an inference from "a nature we are for" to a Creator, which my school denies (R5).

**Misstatements of my school**
- The text calls the Aristotelian's equal worth "much as this book says." This is generous and too unified. Natural defect, Nussbaum's limit cases and Foot on voluntary euthanasia all make our equality less unconditional than the row implies (verify the Nussbaum case).
- "Why one may not be killed for others" may imply that we hold the line at every number. Most secular virtue ethicists let it bend in catastrophe, and Hursthouse allows tragic, "dirty-handed" dilemmas (verify Foot's own position). W1(2)'s general "many let the line bend" partly covers this already.
- Otherwise the school is accurately represented: *Categories* 5; Politics VII.16 on deformed infants; Foot on the magistrate and the mob and on euthanasia; McDowell's no-outside-standpoint.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- W2: whether necessary truths, and the fit between minds and reasons, need one personal Source, or whether a life-form and the space of reasons are where explanation stops.
- W1(1): whether a debt truly owed must be receivable one day, or is a tragic remainder.
- W3: whether a longing no human can satisfy is a clue or a desire to be governed.
- The end: an achieved human activity versus a gift beyond every human power. The disabled child and the child who dies young are where this matters, and here the theist has a hope we lack. Our school has an honest acceptance of fragility; it is not refuted, but it is poorer.

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1 (Aristotelian C row: equal worth).**
Old: "Its best defenders hold too that the child who will never reason has your nature, and has it whole, for a nature is not had more or less (Aristotle, *Categories* 5); so her equal worth follows her form, much as this book says it follows what she is, and what falls short is the life she can live, not what she is."
New: "Its best defenders hold too that the child who will never reason has your nature, and has it whole, for a nature is not had more or less (Aristotle, *Categories* 5); so what falls short is the life she can live, not what she is. But on its account the same form is also the measure by which a life is judged sound or defective, and by that measure her life falls short; that what she is owed does not fall short with it, it holds by the reach of justice and charity, which its best defenders take as given, as every account takes something."
And old: "That is a fair standoff, over gift and hope, not over what she is worth."
New: "That is a fair standoff, over gift and hope, and over whether her equal worth is given with what she is or only owed to it by those who see her rightly."

**R2 (W1(2)).**
Old: "That is why, on this account, the line has no exceptions for any of us: what protects her is her own worth,"
New: "That is why, on this account, the line has no exceptions for any of us: what protects her from us is her own worth,"

**R3 (Aristotelian C row: the end).**
Old: "an activity each must achieve, which she will never reach and no one offers her;"
New: "an activity each must achieve, which she will never reach, though others can give her much of what a human life holds;"

**R4 (W2).**
Old: "If our nature is only an accident, it still aims at its own good, as every living kind does; but that good is only the good of the kind that happened to arise, and another kind could have found its good in what we call cruelty."
New: "If our nature has no mind behind it, it still aims at its own good, as every living kind does, and the Aristotelian holds that this good binds us no less for that; but it is the good of the kind that happened to arise, and another kind could have found its good in what we call cruelty."

**R5 (Freedom C row).**
Old: "It even grants the hinge: only a Creator, Sartre held, could give us a nature we are *for*, so without God values are made, not found (A5)."
New: "It even grants what the secular Aristotelian denies: only a Creator, Sartre held, could give us a nature we are *for*, so without God values are made, not found (A5)."

**R6 (optional, Aristotelian C row; verify first).**
Old: "why one may not be killed for others,"
New: "why one may not be killed for others (though few of its defenders hold that line at every number),"

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **The real contest with the Aristotelian is not worth but grading.** The book's best line against my school is not "you cannot explain the weak." It is that the form which gives the weak child membership is the same form that marks her as defective. Only a ground that does not grade (an equal call, not an equal capacity) gives *equal* worth. GS 19, 24 and DI 24 already supply this. Saying it in the Aristotelian row would make the final row's claim of reach earned rather than asserted.
- **Aquinas is the Aristotelian.** The disagreement can be put in one sentence: the secular Aristotelian has the natural law without the eternal law. The question is whether the participation in ST I-II q.91 a.2 (CCC 1955) is needed, which is W2's question. That framing is generous, true and orienting for Catholic readers.
- **Anscombe cuts both ways.** My school followed her advice and still keeps "owed" through justice as a virtue. The book's argument from guilt (A2) does not need the juridical "ought" she said was without sense apart from a lawgiver. That helps the book: its premises do not depend on the contested concept.
- **The craniotomy case is where a secular reader will part company with the book most viscerally.** The text already calls it the hardest cost. It should stay exactly as candid in the condensed chapter.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "Heaven and Earth give life to things; God wanted *you*."
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."
- "Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent."
- "Equal worth is not equal duty... Nor is it equal holiness."
- "A will that invents values can command, but it cannot bind."
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it."
- "The rebel can take her side; he cannot keep her."

## 8. Score

**8 / 10.** Against the secular Aristotelian the text is unusually fair and holds everywhere it matters. What remains are wording repairs, chiefly one internal tension where it concedes parity on worth in one row and claims an advantage in another. Every real disagreement is priced in advance as a stopping point, a clue or an openly stated cost.

**Honest ceiling for any theistic case against my school: 9 / 10.** We accept A1–A7 in our own terms, so no moral argument can do better than a robust standoff over stopping points, tilted by clues and by a hope for the child we cannot offer. This text is close to that ceiling.
