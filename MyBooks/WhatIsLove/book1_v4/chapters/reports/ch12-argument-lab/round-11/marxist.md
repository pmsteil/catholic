# Blind review: the Marxist school

## 1. School and voice

I speak for historical materialism and its moral heirs: Marx and Engels; the analytic Marxists on justice (G. A. Cohen, Norman Geras, Ziyad Husami against Allen Wood and Robert Tucker); Steven Lukes's *Marxism and Morality* (1985), which argues that Marxism rejected the morality of *Recht* (rights and justice) in favour of a morality of emancipation, and paid for it in the means-end disasters of the twentieth century; the Frankfurt School where it took up guilt toward the dead (Benjamin, Horkheimer, Adorno); and Terry Eagleton, who holds that Marx had a full idea of human nature and that its ethic is, at bottom, one of mutual flourishing, close to love (verify for all paraphrases). My weapon by trade is ideology critique: showing that a belief serves a ruling order. The fairness rule binds me hard. I do not get to say "that belief arose from class interest, so it is false" unless I let the text say "your belief in equality arose from Christianity, or from commodity exchange, so it is false." I use genealogy only as a rival explanation that has to win on the evidence, the same test the text sets in A.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** My honest answer splits along a known fault line. On Wood's reading, Marx has no theory of what anyone is *owed*. He condemns capitalism for domination, alienation and stunted lives, which are non-juridical goods lost, and he treats "justice" as the norm that fits each mode of production. On the Geras and Cohen reading, exploitation is unjust: taking unpaid labour time from someone who has no real choice but to sell it. The deepest principle is need: from each according to ability, to each according to needs. **Verdict: breaks or borrows, exactly as the text's dilemma says.** Wood's road cannot say the ancient slave was *owed* freedom. Cohen's road ends in fundamental principles that do not depend on facts (*Rescuing Justice and Equality*, verify), so it meets the realist's question in W2.

**2. A hard dilemma, resolved.** Case: a striking hospital worker's child needs a medicine he can pay for only by crossing his own union's picket line. My principles are need, solidarity, freedom from domination, and asking who built the trap. Resolution: the union owes him the medicine money from its strike fund. That is need first, and solidarity paid in money as well as words. If the union fails him and he crosses, he is not to be blamed; the blame falls on the relations that made his child's life a bargaining chip (Marx, *Capital* I, Preface, verify). **Verdict: coherent in practice, and it borrows at one point.** When I tell the comfortable scab who *wants* to cross that he owes his comrades loyalty, "owes" is borrowed. Class interest gives him reasons only if he shares it. Solidarity is self-gift under another name, and I cannot say why it binds a man who would rather not give himself.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** My best answer is Marx's humanism of 1843–44. The criticism of religion ends in the teaching that the human being is the highest being for the human being, and in the imperative to overthrow every relation in which a human being is debased, enslaved, abandoned or despised (Marx, Introduction to the *Critique of Hegel's Philosophy of Right*, verify). With Geras (*Marx and Human Nature*, 1983) I ground it in a shared human nature of needs and potentials. The newborn and the severely disabled are human beings in need. **Verdict: universal, yes; equal, only as equal standing, since Marx refused any single measure (Gotha); unconditional, asserted but not grounded.** Why membership in the species carries that standing, I take as given, as the contractualist and the Aristotelian do. The enemy is where my tradition broke in practice: the class enemy was spent. Its humane heirs restored the line, and the restoration borrows from Kant.

**4. Ending point.** A society in which the free development of each is the condition for the free development of all (*Manifesto*). Labour that is no longer alienated, rich individuality, and a realm of freedom beyond necessity (*Capital* III, ch. 48, verify). The guiding principles: abolish domination and exploitation, and provide according to need. **Verdict: coherent for the living and for those still to come. It breaks for the dead.** Horkheimer conceded, against Benjamin, that past injustice is complete and the slain are really slain (letter of 1937, verify). For the child whose powers will never develop, my ideal of "development" reaches her only as care for her needs.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1. The starting points are ideology (A, opening paragraph).**
*Attack.* A's method trusts "what still seems true to us after honest reflection." Ideology is exactly what survives armchair reflection inside a form of life. Theses on Feuerbach II: whether thinking reaches objective truth is a practical question, not a theoretical one (verify). The ruling ideas of every age are those of its ruling class (*German Ideology*, verify). A's seemings are trusted until defeated, so the burden falls on the critic, which favours whatever is already believed.
*Defense.* A's own clause admits the debunker's defeater: a belief loses its standing if "its truth plays no part in the best account of why we hold it." Ideology critique is welcome on those terms. It must then do the work, belief by belief. The fairness rule cuts both ways. My conviction that exploitation is an evil also rests on reflection that survived my own class's ideas. Cohen saw this: he noted that he would hold some of his philosophical beliefs differently had he trained at Harvard rather than Oxford, and he did not conclude that they were false (*If You're an Egalitarian, How Come You're So Rich?*, ch. 1, verify). And practice, Marx's own test, has largely confirmed A1: the practice of abolition, and the near-universal verdict on the footbridge cases across cultures.
*Rejoinder.* I grant it. The method is the one I use myself. One residue is a matter of selection, not error: every example in A1 is a wrong between persons, none of them a structural one. A2 repairs this.
**Held.**

**Exchange 2. Guilt individualizes what systems do (A2, W1).**
*Attack.* Guilt "faces a person." Marx would not hold individuals responsible for relations whose creatures they remain. The confessional model turns structural exploitation into private sin and private absolution, which leaves the structure standing. Engels called it social murder when a society knowingly keeps workers in conditions that foreseeably kill them (*Condition of the Working Class in England*, verify). The doctrine of double effect files those deaths as side effects to be weighed.
*Defense.* A2 states the structural case in full: wrongs "done by many hands, through customs and systems no one of us chose," shared by whoever takes part, approves or fails to hinder (CCC 1868), and named structures of sin (CCC 1869; *Sollicitudo Rei Socialis* 36–37). A6 refuses to let "weighing" become a licence: accepting murderous famines without efforts to remedy them is "a scandalous injustice and a grave offense" (CCC 2269). That is Engels's point in the Church's words. Marx's refusal to *blame* the individual capitalist is not a denial that the worker is *wronged*. He called the original accumulation robbery. Catholic teaching goes further than the text needs to: Scripture says that wages kept back by fraud "cry out" and reach "the ears of the Lord of hosts" (James 5:4, RSV), and the Church counts injustice to the wage earner among the sins that cry to heaven (CCC 1867).
*Rejoinder.* The remaining dispute is over direction of explanation. The Church says structures are the expression and effect of personal sins; we say personal vices are largely the expression of structures. That is a standoff in social theory. It does not bear on whether the moral argument for God goes through.
**Held.**

**Exchange 3. The text's dilemma against Marx is a false one (C, History and class).**
*Attack.* The text says: if justice is only what fits each age, the slave was not wronged; if principles hold in every age, Marxism meets the realist's question. There is a third road, historical naturalism (Geras, Sean Sayers, Eagleton, verify). Human nature has transhistorical needs and historically developing powers. Domination thwarted the slave's nature then as now, even though abolishing it was not yet historically possible. That is a transhistorical bad with no timeless juridical code.
*Defense.* The third road is the secular Aristotelian row with a theory of history attached. It inherits that row's stopping point: is our nature, and the good it carries, an accident or an order that needs nothing behind it (C, Flourishing; W2)? And "domination thwarted her nature" says she was harmed. To say she was *owed* freedom, the naturalist must step onto the dilemma's second horn. Wood's Marx would not say "owed," and Geras's Marx would, and then faces W2. The dilemma stands. The road I offered is a version of one of its horns.
*Rejoinder.* Conceded on the logic. On the text: "Marx condemned in the name of freedom, not of justice" is stated as fact, but it is Wood's reading, and Geras, Husami and Cohen contest it. The conditional "if" saves the argument, but the sentence should flag the reading (repair R1). The row also omits Engels's own claim of moral progress (*Anti-Dühring* I.9). Progress needs a measure no single age supplies, and that point strengthens the text's dilemma (repair R3).
**Held with repair.**

**Exchange 4. "Equal worth" is the commodity's equality (A4; C, History and class).**
*Attack.* Marx explains why Aristotle could not decode value: Greek society rested on slave labour and so on the inequality of persons. The equality of all human labour could be read only once human equality had become a fixed popular prejudice, which happens in a society of commodity exchange (*Capital* I, ch. 1, verify). Equal right measures unequal individuals by one standard, taking them from one side only, as workers (Gotha). Engels: the real content of the demand for equality is the abolition of classes, and anything beyond that is absurd (*Anti-Dühring* I.10). On this account A4's "same dignity" is a bourgeois abstraction, and need, not equality, is the true principle.
*Defense.* Three replies. (a) A4 already says "equal worth is not equal duty." Equal worth does not prescribe equal portions. Provision according to need is what equal worth asks when needs differ. Marx's objection to measuring a person "from one side only" is exactly A4's point: worth follows the whole of what she is, not her productive powers. (b) Meeting *each* person's needs, the disabled child's included, presupposes that each one counts. That is equal standing, and the Gotha critique presupposes it. (c) By the fairness rule, the commodity genealogy does not debunk equality. Marx himself does not say the belief is false. He says a social form made a truth about labour legible, and A4 likewise says the truth came late (CCC 37–38; DI 10–13).
*Rejoinder.* Conceded. Marx's anti-egalitarianism targets equal *measure*, not equal *standing*. One caution on the text: putting Engels's "absurd" next to "why she counts as much as you" invites the reader to think Engels denied equal moral worth. His target was equality as a political-economic programme beyond class abolition. The sentence is accurate. Its placement leans (see the minor items).
**Held.**

**Exchange 5. The debt to the dead (W1(1)).**
*Attack.* My tradition can walk the third road without God. Benjamin: the past is not closed. Each generation has a weak messianic power, and the struggle of the oppressed redeems the dead by completing their cause (Theses on the Philosophy of History II, verify). What the dead are owed is victory, not a private pardon. Asking that *she* forgive is the bourgeois-Christian privatizing of a collective debt.
*Defense.* The tradition's own record supports the text. Horkheimer answered Benjamin that past injustice is complete, the slain really slain, and that to take its unfinished character seriously one would have to believe in the Last Judgment. Benjamin replied that remembrance forbids us to think of history as wholly without theology, though we may not write it in theological concepts (*Arcades Project*, N8,1, verify). Adorno, cited by the text through *Spe Salvi* 42, held that true justice would require undoing the irrevocable past. Completing the cause of the dead is the second road, which Xunzi walks in the text. It finishes well what death broke off, but it does not reach *her*. The text claims no more than a clue, weighed "as much as the reader finds that a debt truly owed to her is one she must be able, one day, to receive."
*Rejoinder.* I cannot do better than Horkheimer, and he gave the text its premise. The text holds. Its modesty is what makes it hold.
**Held.**

**Exchange 6. Longing as "the sigh of the oppressed creature" (W3).**
*Attack.* Marx accepted Feuerbach's result. The attributes given to God are the human species' own love and knowledge, projected and estranged. The longing to be "known and loved wholly ... by someone who will not die" is the heart of a heartless world. Abolish the conditions that need illusions and the longing will be met by human community, or will fade. There is evidence: religiosity falls as existential security rises (Norris and Inglehart, *Sacred and Secular*, 2004, verify).
*Defense.* (a) This is a genealogy. By A's rule it defeats W3 only if its story, and not the truth of what is longed for, best accounts for the longing. If there is a God who is Love, a longing that grows sharper in want still answers to what is there. Nor may the text cite that sharpening as proof, and it does not. (b) The data measure the decline of religious practice. They do not measure the longing W3 names: to be received whole, across every role and year, and to have the dead kept. That is untested, and prosperous secular societies report loneliness, not its end. (c) The Church took up Marx's concept and turned it around: a person is alienated if he refuses to transcend himself and to live self-giving and authentic community oriented to his final destiny, which is God. Such alienation is found in market societies too (*Centesimus Annus* 41). (d) W3 claims only fit: "a longing does not prove its answer."
*Rejoinder.* The security data weigh slightly against W3 *as a clue*. If the longing tracks insecurity, it says less about our nature. That weight is light and already priced in by "clue, not evidence." It is parity.
**Held.**

**Exchange 7. Hope as opium (evil section, Third; Conclusion).**
*Attack.* The promise that "the last word over the suffering child is not the suffering" reconciles the living to the conditions that kill children. The Conclusion admits that the believer's assent "does not wait on" the argument. So the argument is ideology in the strict sense: a belief held on grounds other than the ones it gives.
*Defense.* The text closes off quietism in so many words. "His permission never excuses ours (CCC 2269)." The hands that pull a child from the fire are "part of what He made real." *Gaudium et Spes* 21 teaches that hope in a life to come does not lessen the weight of earthly duties but adds new motives for them. On the second point, A7 binds both sides. Whether a belief consoles or pacifies is no evidence against its truth, just as the Marxist's commitment, which also "does not wait on" argument, is no evidence against his. The text names its own grounds for assent openly. That is the opposite of ideology's concealment. And its description of my school is exact: we work for a world in which no one is spent for another's gain, and for the children already spent we have memory, not repair.
*Rejoinder.* Conceded.
**Held.**

**Exchange 8. Tu quoque: History's goal and the Lord of life (A4, A6, W1(2), C).**
*Attack.* The text charges that where Marxism "spent the innocent for history's goal, it gave up A6." But W1(2) holds, with St. Thomas, that a man who killed the infants of Amalek at God's command acted rightly, as the instrument of God's right. The structure is the same: an authority that cannot err wills a death, and a human hand carries it out on that authority. The commissar too claimed no right of his own, only History's. A4 says the innocent are "never ... to be deliberately killed by any of us." On the text's own position, that "never" has an exception.
*Defense.* The two cases differ in what the authority is. History did not give her being, cannot give it back, does not will her for her own sake, and does not keep her in death. The text claims all four for God (W1(2), W1(1)). History has no right over life because it is no one. And the text holds that no man may now claim such a command (CCC 67). It pays the cost openly: Kant, Kamm, Nagel, Mencius and the Way draw the line more absolutely than this book does, and the wars "weigh more than an unanswered why," a real cost for a reader weighing by reason alone.
*Rejoinder.* The distinction is sound on the text's premises, and the cost is disclosed, so the charge of a double standard fails. Two pieces of wording remain. A4's "never ... by any of us" is not quite the book's position, which is "never by any right of our own." And the C row should say that the difference is a difference of *who* holds the right, so the reader sees the parallel faced, not dodged (repairs R4, R5).
**Held with repair.**

**Exchange 9. Engels's answer to the fit question (W2).**
*Attack.* W2 asks why minds built for survival grasp necessary truths at all. Engels answered long ago. Thought is a product of the brain, the brain a product of nature, so its products do not contradict the rest of nature's connections but correspond to them (*Anti-Dühring* I.3, verify). Mind and law share one nature, as in Spinoza, whom Plekhanov treated as the ancestor of materialism (verify).
*Defense.* That explains why thought matches *this* nature. It does not explain why it grasps what holds for *every possible* nature, in logic and number, or in the claim that cruelty would be wrong for any creature that can reason and suffer. Engels himself held that eternal truths are few and trivial (*Anti-Dühring* I.9). So he denies the premise W2 needs rather than explaining it. The text already lists the denier, the debunker, and calls the rest "a judgment about where explanation may stop, and the rivals do not share it."
*Rejoinder.* Parity. The text would be fairer to name Engels's answer in W2's parenthesis of rivals, since it is the Marxist position on the very question.
**Held** (an omission, not an error).

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**"As written: NONE."**

**Minor items**
1. *C, History and class:* "which Marx condemned in the name of freedom, not of justice" states Allen Wood's reading as settled. Geras, Husami and Cohen contest it. The argument survives because the horn is conditional, but the sentence should say whose reading it is (R1).
2. *C, History and class:* "right, he held, can never be higher than the economic order of its age" has no citation (*Critique of the Gotha Programme*, I) (R2).
3. *C, History and class:* putting Engels's "absurd" (*Anti-Dühring* I.10) beside "why she counts as much as you" can be read as Engels denying equal moral worth. His target was equality as a programme beyond the abolition of classes. Adding his I.9 claim of moral progress corrects the lean and strengthens the dilemma (R3).
4. *A4 against W1(2):* "never, if innocent, to be deliberately killed by any of us" is stricter than the book's own position, which allows a man to act as the instrument of God's right in 1 Samuel 15. The parenthesis "why the line binds us and not the Lord of life" does not cover the human hand (R5).
5. *C, History and class:* "Where it spent the innocent for history's goal, it gave up A6" is true, but it invites a tu quoque that the text answers only in W1(2). One clause here faces it on the spot (R4).
6. *W2:* Engels's answer to the fit question (thought as nature's product, so in correspondence with nature) is the Marxist position on that question and is not named. This is an omission, not an error.
7. *"Much of this the Church grants (W2)":* W2 grants that conscience fails where self-interest pulls. It does not grant that moral codes answer to modes of production. The cross-reference slightly overstates the agreement.

**Misstatements of my school**
None of substance. The *Capital* Preface (agents as creatures of relations), Gotha (one-sided standard; right not higher than the economic structure), *Anti-Dühring* I.9–10 and Lukes's thesis are all paraphrased fairly. The only lean is items 1 and 3 above.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- The direction of explanation between personal sin and social structure (CCC 1869 against historical materialism).
- Where explanation may stop: one Source, against Engels's or Spinoza's single nature (W2).
- Whether the longing of W3 is a sign of our nature or a symptom of insecurity: the data on practice do not reach the longing itself.
- Equal standing grounded in what she is: I stop at the human species and its needs; the text adds what she is made for. Neither side can compel the other.
- The debt to the dead: Horkheimer against Benjamin is a standoff within my own tradition, and the text weighs it correctly as a clue.

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **The Marxist tradition's own witness for W1(1).** Horkheimer to Benjamin, 1937: past injustice is complete, the slain are really slain, and to take its incompleteness seriously one would have to believe in the Last Judgment. Benjamin's reply: remembrance forbids us to think of history as wholly without theology (*Arcades Project* N8,1, verify both). This is stronger than the Adorno line already used, because it is a Marxist naming the fork in W1(1) in the book's own terms. It belongs on the second road as its most honest walker. Suggested insertion after the Xunzi sentence: "and Max Horkheimer, who told Walter Benjamin that the slain are really slain, and that to hold their wrong still open one would have to believe in the Last Judgment (verify)."
- **The wage withheld from the dead.** CCC 1867 and James 5:4 (RSV: the withheld wages "cry out; and the cries of the harvesters have reached the ears of the Lord of hosts") join Marx's exploited worker to W1(1). The anonymous dead of history's workshops, whom no one remembers by name, are heard by Someone. This answers the text's own worry that "many of the dead have no one left to remember them by name," on ground a Marxist reader already stands on.
- **Alienation turned around.** *Centesimus Annus* 41 adopts Marx's word and relocates the wound: alienation is refusing self-gift and communion oriented to God, found in market societies too. It fits W3 and the book's definition, and it answers "the sigh of the oppressed creature" without contempt for it.
- **A4 reaches the workplace.** "Never to be used as a mere means" condemns what Marx condemned. The Church rejects both atheistic collectivism and the absolute primacy of the market over human labour (CCC 2425), and holds that profit as the exclusive norm is morally unacceptable (CCC 2424; *Laborem Exercens* 12). The book's case would gain with Marxist readers if one sentence in A4 or A6 said so.
- **Engels's moral progress** (R3) is a ready-made instance of the Rorty question, turned on my school.
- **Lukes's thesis is the book's best secular ally on A6.** A movement that rejected rights as bourgeois lost the means to refuse atrocity. That is the cost the text says A6 prevents.
- **Eagleton** (a Marxist raised Catholic) reads creation as gratuitous, made for the love of it and from no need (verify). That matches the text's "freely and from no need" (ST I q.19 a.3) and makes the God of the text the opposite of the ruling-class sovereign my school expected to find.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "A will that invents values can command, but it cannot bind." (The text's best defence against ideology critique of law as command.)
- "Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent. Weighing is not a license."
- "His permission never excuses ours."
- "The last three are hopes for those who come after; to the children already spent they can offer memory and rites, not repair." (Painful, and exact.)
- "The rebel can take her side; he cannot keep her." (It applies to my school word for word.)
