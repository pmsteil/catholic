# Blind review: the utilitarian school

## 1. School and voice

I write for the classical and contemporary utilitarians. That means Henry Sidgwick's self-evident axiom of rational benevolence (the good of any one counts for no more, "from the point of view of the universe," than the like good of any other), J. J. C. Smart's act utilitarianism, R. M. Hare's two-level universal prescriptivism, Brad Hooker's rule consequentialism (with some priority to the worst off), and Peter Singer and Katarzyna de Lazari-Radek's objectivist hedonism (*The Point of View of the Universe*, 2014). Two things I hold together: I am a moral realist about reasons, and I deny that a person has an inviolability that no amount of good can outweigh. I am a non-theist. I find the text's account of my school mostly accurate and often generous. My quarrel is with its premises A4 and A6 in their strong forms, and with the weight it gives evil.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

1. **Starting point for justice.** Equal consideration of interests. Bentham's maxim is that each counts for one and none for more than one. Justice is the name for the rules and institutions that protect the most vital parts of well-being, and so the ones we most need everyone to keep (Mill, *Utilitarianism* ch. 5, paraphrase, verify). What anyone is owed is what the impartially best rules, or the critical-level verdict, assign to him. **Holds** as a coherent theory. It **breaks** with the text's directed sense of "owed" (A2–A3), because for us being owed is derived, not basic.
2. **A hard dilemma.** Take the text's own case: mother and unborn child will both die unless the child is killed. We say save the mother, because one life is better than none and the child loses nothing she would otherwise have had. Our method is critical-level reasoning from impartial benevolence, plus intuitive-level rules for ordinary life (Hare). **Holds** without borrowing the book's covenant definitions. It does share one thing with the book: Sidgwick's benevolence is "to will the good of another" (ST I q.20 a.2) applied to everyone impartially. What we leave out is willing anyone *for her own sake*.
3. **Equal, unconditional dignity.** We **deny** it in the text's sense of equal inviolability and equal wrongness of killing. We affirm equal consideration. The newborn's pain counts exactly as much as the genius's, the enemy's as much as a friend's, and an animal's as much as a man's. That equality does not follow ability. The wrongness of *killing* does vary with what the being loses, so killing a newborn is less grave than killing a self-conscious adult (Singer, *Practical Ethics*, verify). This is coherent and does not borrow. It **breaks** at exactly the point where the text's conclusion presses, and we pay for that openly.
4. **Ultimate good and guiding principles.** For de Lazari-Radek and Singer, the ultimate good is well-being, meaning pleasant conscious states and the absence of suffering. Hare counts satisfied preferences, and Hooker uses an objective list. The guiding principles are impartial benevolence and rational prudence. Sidgwick could not reconcile those two without a moral order of the world (see §7). His heirs resolve the conflict by debunking egoism. There is no final end beyond experienced lives, and no completion after death. This **holds** for coherence. It **borrows nothing**, but it leaves the longing of W3 unanswered, which we accept.

## 3. Debate highlights

**E1. Selective debunking (A6).**
- *Attack.* A6 answers the debunker by claiming parity: "the regard for the many that would spend the one has a history too, in sympathy learned among kin." That misdescribes our best argument. De Lazari-Radek and Singer (following Singer, "Ethics and Intuitions," 2005, and Greene's dual-process work) argue the debunking is *asymmetric*. Constraints against spending the one are just what an evolved alarm against up-close harm would produce. Greene's studies suggest the alarm responds to "personal force," which both sides disown as irrelevant. Impartial benevolence, by contrast, runs against kin and kind, so evolution would not have built it. On the text's own rule (A heading: a belief is defeated when "its truth plays no part in the best account of why we hold it"), only our axiom survives.
- *Defense.* (a) Berker (2009): the neuroscience does no normative work. The argument turns on a premise about which features are irrelevant, and the deontologist does not grant that the means/side-effect distinction is irrelevant. (b) Kahane ("Evolution and Impartiality," 2014): impartial benevolence is debunkable too, as kin sympathy overgeneralized by a reasoning capacity selected for other ends. (c) A6's cases have no personal force. A city that secretly hangs a man and a surgeon who could kill by quiet injection push no one, yet the verdict holds. (d) The line is held by reasoned principle across unrelated traditions (Mencius, the Vinaya, Kant), and that cross-cultural agreement fits with its truth playing a part.
- *Rejoinder.* (c) and (d) are good and (b) is live. The parity is real once these points are made. But the text as written makes none of them, and it states our position in a form we do not hold.
- **Held with repair** (R1).

**E2. Wronging versus worsening (A2).**
- *Attack.* Under preference utilitarianism (Hare), the told secret and the blown letter frustrate the same wish. What separates them is *agency*: wrongness belongs to choices, and the wind does not choose. So the letter case shows that acting badly differs from a bad event, which we grant. It does not show a directed relation, "to her," over and above the badness grounded in her.
- *Defense.* The text already grants this (C, Well-being: "counting wishes … it can call the telling wrong"). Its point is elsewhere. Against hedonism, the telling is a wrong with no worsening at all. Against the preference view, the issue is who may forgive. Only the one wronged can release, and no total can (Darwall's second-person standing). Our account can endorse a *practice* in which the victim's forgiveness counts, because such practices are optimific. It cannot say that she alone *in fact* has the standing.
- *Rejoinder.* Correct. For us the standing is a useful convention, not a fact. That is a real cost, and we pay it.
- **Held.**

**E3. Equal worth, the newborn and the species line (A4, conclusion).**
- *Attack.* If worth does not follow ability, why does it follow the human kind? A chimpanzee outdoes an anencephalic infant on every capacity the text sets aside. Grounding worth in "what she is" either puts the line at species, which is arbitrary (Singer's "speciesism"), or extends it to every sentient being. The conclusion's test also hides a gap. It grants "however differently one counts what each of them lost" and still asks whether she was wronged *no less*. On our view a wrong is not separable from a loss, so the comparative claim is a theory, not something one sees.
- *Defense.* The text does not deny that animals count (CCC 2416–2418 forbids causing them needless suffering). It denies only that any other earthly creature is willed for itself (GS 24). A natural kind is not a race. It fixes the form and end of the individual (Aristotle, *Categories* 5: a nature is not had more or less). The text says outright that it "faces too" the question of why the kind carries such worth before reason is used, and it answers with a call that does not wait on powers (W1(2)). As for the comparative test, it is posed as "which are you surer of," and the text concedes it "cannot compel the one who gives up A4." Even McMahan, no utilitarian, sides with us here (A heading), so the text is not cornering a crank position.
- *Rejoinder.* That is a fair standoff over first principles. The text, however, never states the species question, which is our most famous challenge to A4 (R3).
- **Held** (standoff at parity), with a recommended addition.

**E4. "Not hers in her own right" turned around (C, Well-being; W1(2)).**
- *Attack.* The text faults us because her claim "weighs as one part of the total, not as hers in her own right." On the text's own account, though, her life is "not hers to dispose of" but "held in trust" from the Lord of life. And it is no injustice for Him to command that Amalek's infants be killed by human hands (1 Samuel 15:3, with St. Thomas). So on the text's view too, her claim against being killed is not ultimate. It yields to another's right, exercised (by 15:2) for reasons about others.
- *Defense.* Her claim is supreme against every creature. God's right is not a rival interest placed on a scale against hers. It is the Giver's right, exercised for her own sake. He gave her being and keeps her (W1(1)), so calling her home is not taking. On our view any agent with large enough numbers may override her, and no one keeps her. The text also names the 1 Samuel cost openly, against Kant, Mencius and the Way.
- *Rejoinder.* That locates the difference correctly: no total is ever weighed against her, and the only one with a right over her life also restores it. But that is not what the C row's phrase "in her own right" says. It claims more than the text's own doctrine allows.
- **Held with repair** (R4).

**E5. Rule worship (A6; W1(2), "the line's hardest cost").**
- *Attack.* Smart's charge: a rule that lets mother and child both die rather than one, when the child gains nothing, protects the agent's clean hands, not her worth. "Without it, no innocent is safe, in principle" is itself a consequentialist argument about security. A two-level view delivers the same security in practice (Hare).
- *Defense.* The text calls this case the hardest cost and does not soften it. Its ground is not outcomes but what aiming at her death makes of her: one willed for her own sake treated as existing for others. It permits treatment aimed at the mother's illness, and it marks the absolute at every number as "this book's claims, not shared ones." The security line is offered as a consequence, not as the ground.
- *Rejoinder.* Agreed. This is a clash of first principles, stated honestly by both sides.
- **Held** (standoff).

**E6. Sentient suffering and the indifference hypothesis (evil section).**
- *Attack.* For my school, suffering is the central disvalue, and the record of hundreds of millions of years of animal pain is our strongest evidence. Draper's argument: the distribution of pain and pleasure is far more probable if nature is indifferent than if it is governed by a God who loves. The text concedes this ("not where justice would"; "there the evidence is not weak"). By its own account, W1(1) and W3 are clues an indifferent world "would leave just as we find them," and W2 rests on "a judgment about where explanation may stop." So for any reader who does not already hold A4 strongly, the ledger tips to naturalism.
- *Defense.* The text says nearly exactly this. It cannot compel one who gives up A4. Its positive weight is A4's grounding and the reach of one Source. The conclusion is "this book's judgment," offered to a reader who "may weigh it differently." The text does not use privation (First) against the indifference hypothesis, and says so.
- *Rejoinder.* The honesty is complete. What remains is a difference in weighing, which the text owns.
- **Held** (standoff).

**E7. Hell, and the Mary concession (evil section).**
- *Attack.* Eternal loss is the greatest disvalue there is. The text concedes that God "can bring a free creature to say yes without overriding her." So permitting a final refusal is permitting an avoidable, infinite loss to one He wills for her own sake. That looks less like a strain on W1(2)'s principle and more like a conflict with it.
- *Defense.* Permitting a free refusal does not *spend* her: no one gains by her loss, so the text's "never spends" principle is not broken. The text says reason cannot show how the permission is for her sake. The Church declares no one lost and prays for all (CCC 1058, 1821). The text calls this "the heaviest thing" on the scale and holds its claim "in that hope and under this weight."
- *Rejoinder.* It is not a strict contradiction, and the text claims no answer. It stays as owned weight.
- **Held** (owned cost).

## 4. Ledger

- **A heading:** Holds. The defeat condition is the same test Sidgwick's checks for self-evidence and Singer's debunking use. The list of deniers is fair. "Singer, parts of A2 through A7" is accurate, if understated on A4.
- **A1:** Holds. We grant it in every realistic case, and at the intuitive level. The text is generous: a strict hedonist must, in principle, weigh the pleasure of a large sadistic crowd. See §7.
- **A2:** Holds. Against hedonism it is a clean case of wronging without worsening. Against the preference view, the letter contrast separates agency, not directedness, and the claim about standing is what does the work (E2). Optional repair R7.
- **A3:** Holds as an offered truth. Mill himself denies that anyone can sell himself into slavery (*On Liberty* ch. 5, verify). We deny that the dead are owed anything beyond surviving preferences.
- **A4:** Holds as a premise, stated honestly with its deniers. It is the crux against my school (E3).
- **A5:** Holds. Hare adds universalizability to wants and so does not derive ought from want alone. The basic goods overlap with Hooker's objective list.
- **A6:** Holds with repair. It misdescribes the history of utilitarian regard and does not meet the asymmetric debunking argument (E1, R1). Its statement that "what makes crossing it wrong is still the good of all" is exact.
- **A7:** Holds. Realist utilitarians grant that epistemic reasons bind every mind. Our dissent concerns lying, which C states correctly.
- **W1 frame:** Holds. Anscombe is correctly reported. The "wrong of necessity" claim holds for us at the level of the first principle and of acts like torture for fun.
- **W1(1):** Holds as a clue, honestly weighted. Its list of readers it does not reach omits the rule utilitarian (R5).
- **W1(2):** Holds. "That she can suffer … does not explain the line, for others can always suffer more" states our limit exactly. The 1 Samuel cost is owned. Its contrast with us should be tightened in C (R4).
- **W1 closing:** Holds. Its anti-voluntarism is one Sidgwick shares.
- **W2:** Holds at parity. De Lazari-Radek and Singer are among the godless realists it describes, and they give the "survival and reason" answer the text reports.
- **W3:** Holds as a clue. It concedes that longing settles nothing.
- **W3 hiddenness:** Holds. It is honest, and denies only the "now."
- **C intro:** Holds. The fairness rule is kept, except at A6's history line (R1).
- **C Nothing really binds:** Holds.
- **C Feeling, refined:** Holds.
- **C Our best words:** Holds. James, *Pragmatism* Lecture III, is used fairly.
- **C Reason's own law:** Holds (outside my school; nothing I contest).
- **C What we owe each other:** Holds.
- **C Care:** Holds.
- **C Well-being:** Holds with repair. The replaceability claim misstates the preference form (R2). It omits equal consideration's reach to animals and the species challenge (R3). "In her own right" overclaims (R4). The point about telling the secret is incomplete for two-level views (R7). Everything else, including Hooker's disaster clause, the subordination of truthfulness, and the credit to Mill and Singer, is accurate.
- **C Flourishing:** Holds.
- **C Necessary truths:** Holds. Insight: today's leading utilitarians belong here too (§7).
- **C Sacred order, The Way, God or Nature, Dhamma, History and class, Power and rank, Freedom:** Hold. These are outside my school and I find nothing to contest from it.
- **C A God who is Love:** Holds. "Reach, not count" is the right claim. Its cost is the evil section.
- **Evil, first paragraph:** Holds. The Draper-type concessions are accurate and honestly heavy.
- **Evil, First:** Holds. A hedonist denies that pain is a mere privation, and the text sensibly does not use privation against indifference.
- **Evil, Second:** Holds. Under the order of nature, animals are given one to another. That is the species line again, at God's level, and the text leaves it on the scale.
- **Evil, Third:** Holds. The protest paragraph slightly overstates what the protester must assume (R6).
- **Conclusion:** Holds. The newborn test is valid as stated, with "however differently one counts what each of them lost". It is the right place for the case to rest against my school.

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**"As written: NONE."**

No Fatal or Serious objection stands. Every major utilitarian objection is either answered at parity or named by the text as an owned cost: the separateness of persons, rule worship, replaceability, voluntary death, the strong form of A4, sentient suffering, and hell.

**Minor:**
1. A6 misdescribes the utilitarian's asymmetric debunking argument ("sympathy learned among kin") and leaves it unanswered. The answer exists (E1).
2. C, Well-being: the replaceability sentence is false of prior-existence preference utilitarianism, which holds that self-conscious persons are *not* replaceable (and grants that newborns are).
3. C, Well-being: it omits the species challenge to A4, which is my school's best-known objection to the premise the conclusion rests on.
4. C, Well-being: "not as hers in her own right" claims more than the text's own trust doctrine and its reading of 1 Samuel 15 allow (E4).
5. W1(1) parenthesis: the rule utilitarian reader is missing from those for whom "owed" names something else.
6. Evil, the protest paragraph: the utilitarian protester needs only A1 and equal consideration, not A2–A4 in their strong forms.
7. A2 and C: against preference and two-level views, the letter contrast and the line about the telling need a phrase each (R7).

**Misstatements of my school:** items 1 and 2.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**
- equal consideration versus equal inviolability (A4);
- side constraints versus two-level security (A6);
- the victim's standing to forgive as a fact versus a practice (A2);
- whether a self is irreplaceable (W3);
- voluntary death (A6, W1(2));
- where explanation may stop for necessary normative truths, which we share with the godless realist (W2);
- the weight of sentient suffering and of hell against the case (evil).

## 6. Proposed repairs (old → new)

**R1 (A6).**
- Old: "and the regard for the many that would spend the one has a history too, in sympathy learned among kin. A history settles neither."
- New: "Its ablest utilitarian critics answer that their own principle, that the good of any one counts no more than the like good of any other, is just what evolution would not have built, since it runs against kin and kind, so that only theirs survives the test. But reason's reach beyond kin has a history too, in sympathy learned among kin and widened by the habit of consistency; and the alarm sounds where there is no push and no blood, at the secret hanging and the quiet injection, and in traditions that never met. A history settles neither."

**R2 (C, Well-being).**
- Old: "It can say that it is good that you exist, because your life goes well, but not that you could not be replaced without loss by another life that goes as well (W3)."
- New: "It can say that it is good that you exist, because your life goes well. In its preference form it holds that one who wants to go on living cannot be replaced without loss, but it finds no such want in the newborn, and so holds her replaceable (Singer, verify); in its hedonistic form it values your good for its own sake, not you, and cannot say that you could not be replaced without loss by another life that goes as well (W3)."

**R3 (C, Well-being, opening).**
- Old: "counts every pain equally, refuses to let the stranger count for less, and is right that consequences matter;"
- New: "counts every like interest equally, whoever has it, the newborn's pain as much as the genius's and, it adds, an animal's as much as a man's; refuses to let the stranger count for less; and is right that consequences matter. It asks A4 its sharpest question: if worth does not follow ability, why does it follow the human kind? This book answers with what she is and what she is for (W1(2)); it does not deny that animals count (CCC 2416–2418), only that any other creature on earth was willed for itself (GS 24);"

**R4 (C, Well-being).**
- Old: "but her claim still weighs as one part of the total, not as hers in her own right."
- New: "but her claim still weighs as one part of the total, on one scale with everyone's, not as a claim no total can outweigh. (On this book's account too her life is not hers to dispose of, and the Lord of life holds a right over it (W1(2)); the difference is that no sum of others' good is ever set against her, and the One who holds that right gave her being and keeps her.)"

**R5 (W1(1) parenthesis).**
- Old: "(It weighs little for a reader who holds that \"owed\" voices refined feeling or names what a people's laws assign,"
- New: "(It weighs little for a reader who holds that \"owed\" voices refined feeling, or names what a people's laws assign or what the rules best for all would assign,"

**R6 (evil, the protest paragraph).**
- Old: "But it stands on the ground this case starts from: that what was done to her is really wrong, whoever approves it, that no one on earth can make it right, and that she was worth as much as anyone, though she could give nothing back (A1–A4)."
- New: "But it stands on ground this case starts from: at the least, that what was done to her is really wrong, whoever approves it (A1); and for most who make it, that no one on earth can make it right, and that she was worth as much as anyone, though she could give nothing back (A3, A4)."

**R7 (C, Well-being).**
- Old: "Counting experiences, it finds no wrong in the telling itself, though it condemns the man disposed to tell, for no one feels the betrayal (A2);"
- New: "Counting experiences, it finds no wrong in the telling itself, for no one feels the betrayal, though in its two-level form it would have us hold the telling wrong and condemns the man disposed to tell (A2);"

## 7. New insights; lines worth keeping

**New insights:**
- **Sidgwick's concession.** The greatest systematizer of my school found that practical reason backs both one's own good and the good of all. He could not reconcile the two without a moral order of the world, and wrote in the first edition that otherwise the "Cosmos of Duty" is reduced to a "Chaos" (*Methods of Ethics*, concluding chapter, verify edition and wording). De Lazari-Radek and Singer resolve this "dualism of practical reason" by debunking egoism with the same evolutionary argument the text meets in A6 and W2. This is a fair, non-genealogical point that belongs in C (Well-being) or W2: a founder of the rival school saw the gap the Lawgiver fills.
- **A precise contrast** for the row and for W3: *it values her good for its own sake; it does not value her for her own sake.* That one sentence carries replaceability, separateness, and "God wanted *you*."
- **The utilitarian point of view.** Our best defenders describe the moral standpoint as one no human occupies: Sidgwick's "point of view of the universe," and Hare's archangel with complete knowledge and no partiality. The book may note, as an observation and not an argument, that it holds there is Someone whose point of view that is, and who sees each one and not only the sum.
- **Allies from my school**, which strengthen the text: Mill against contracting oneself into slavery (A3); Singer's drowning child ("Famine, Affluence, and Morality," 1972) beside CCC 2269 and "weighing is not a license" (A6), where the utilitarian saw first.
- **A sharper A1 test** against hedonism, if wanted: torture of one child for the pleasure of a vast crowd. Hedonism must in principle weigh it, and two-level views can only say we should never think that way.

**Lines worth keeping:**
- "Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent."
- "That she can suffer, or holds her life dear, explains why harming her matters; by itself it does not explain the line, for others can always suffer more."
- "A law or an order gives a nature to every member of a kind; it does not want any one of them."
- "the parasite takes from the child; God does not give her to it."
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it."

## 8. Score

**8/10.** The text states my school accurately apart from two repairable slips. It concedes every real cost, and it rests where the disagreement actually lies: on A4 and A6 against equal consideration and impartial benevolence, where it is at parity and cannot win outright. **Honest ceiling for any theistic case against utilitarianism: 8.5.** Our dispute bottoms out in which first principles one trusts. Evil, especially sentient suffering, weighs heavier on our scale than on any other school's, so no theistic case can do better than a well-argued standoff plus explanatory reach.
