# Blind review: contractualist / second-personal school

## 1. School and voice

I speak for the view that morality, in its central sense, is what we owe each other. An act is wrong when any principle allowing it is one that someone could reasonably reject, and the reasons that count are each person's own, not totals (Scanlon). Moral obligation is a matter of accountability between persons with equal standing to make claims on one another (Darwall). Wrongs are relational: they are done to someone, who holds a claim against the wrongdoer (Wallace). Justice is the set of principles free and equal persons could accept, and it takes seriously that persons are separate (Rawls). I am not a theist. My metaethics is Scanlon's reasons fundamentalism: normative truths about reasons are basic, and they need no metaphysical home. I come to this text expecting to be met and not caricatured, and I was. The text states my school more fairly than most of my school's critics do. Below I test whether its comparison actually holds against the best form of my view.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**(1) Starting point for justice.** Justifiability to each person. What anyone is owed is fixed by principles for the general regulation of conduct that no one affected could reasonably reject, judged from each person's standpoint taken one at a time, with no summing across persons. For the basic structure of society, Rawls's original position applies the same idea. *Verdict: holds.* It rests on one primitive: the value of standing with others in mutual recognition, which is what makes justifiability matter. I hold that as a truth about reasons, not something derived from anything else. That is the point the text names (C, contractualist row), and it is accurate.

**(2) A hard dilemma and its resolution.** Take the obstetric case the text calls its own "hardest cost": mother and unborn child will both die unless the child is killed. Compare principles. A principle forbidding the procedure costs the mother her life (and her other children their mother) and gains the child nothing, because the child dies anyway within a short time. A narrowly drawn principle permitting it costs the child, speaking through a trustee, only that short stretch of life. The child's trustee cannot reasonably reject the permissive principle; the mother can reasonably reject the forbidding one. So it is permitted. Scanlon's World Cup case points the same way at scale: we must stop a broadcast to spare one man an hour of agonizing shocks, however many viewers lose their enjoyment, because no viewer's loss compares with his. *Verdict: holds, with an honest admission.* "Reasonable" is already a moral notion, so the procedure organizes moral judgment rather than generating it from nothing. This is the "redundancy" worry raised by Pettit, Blackburn and others (verify the attributions). I do not borrow the text's definitions of truth, justice, mercy and self-gift. I do borrow a substantive idea of what persons can fairly ask of one another.

**(3) Ground of equal, unconditional dignity.** Equality is structural in my view. Anyone within the scope of justification gets an objection that is heard on its own, and no one's objection counts for less. Scope is the hard part. Scanlon extends it to those who cannot speak for themselves, through trustees, and to all who are born of human beings, because of that relation and not because of their powers (WWOTEO ch. 4, verify page). Darwall's version, which grounds dignity in the standing to make demands, reaches least far. Rawls grounds equality in a capacity for moral personality held at or above a threshold (TJ §77, verify), so his account reaches only as far as that capacity does. *Verdict: holds for the newborn and the enemy, and bends at the edge.* Including every human by relation rather than by capacity is a normative primitive. McMahan and Singer call it arbitrary, and I have no deeper derivation for it. I do not deny unconditional dignity. The "unconditional" part, for those who never will have reason, rests on a stopping point that is less secure than my others.

**(4) Ending point for flourishing.** By design my view does not name one. Scanlon denies that well-being is a master value: lives go well through success in rational aims, relationships, understanding and enjoyment. Rawls leaves the conception of the good to each citizen. The moral good my view does name is living with others on terms each could accept. *Verdict: coherent, but it declines to answer.* At the grave it has nothing to offer the one who was wronged. That is not a contradiction. It is a silence, and the text is right to mark where that silence falls.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1: scope.**
*Attack.* The row says my school "takes as given" why being born human brings the infant within the circle of those owed justification. The theist takes just as much as given, namely that a divine call confers equal worth. Every account stops somewhere.
*Defense (text and Catholic teaching).* The text says exactly that: "as every account takes something." Its claim is "reach, not count." It also has a structural edge. Its ground is a feature of *each* individual, an end offered to her, against which every human is equal (W1(2); GS 19, 29). Mine is a membership relation, and membership relations are what speciesism charges target. A call is not open to the charge that it favors one's own kind, because it is addressed to each one.
*Rejoinder.* The call cannot be observed, any more than the relation can be. But I concede the structural point. The relational move is exactly where McMahan presses Scanlon, and the theist's ground does not invite that pressure.
**Held** (an honest standoff, with a real edge to the text on scope).

**Exchange 2: scope versus equality.**
*Attack.* The row runs together two different things: "why that relation, and not her powers, gives her *full and equal* standing, it takes as given." Equality is not a second thing I posit. Once someone is in scope, the procedure gives each standpoint its own unweighted objection. What I take as given is (a) the relation that sets the scope and (b) the value of justifiability, and the row already names (b).
*Defense.* The equal objection rests on the value of mutual recognition, which is taken as given, so nothing the row says is false.
*Rejoinder.* Agreed, nothing is false. But as written, the row implies a third posit that I do not make, and so it slightly overstates how much my view takes on trust.
**Held with repair** (R1).

**Exchange 3: is God idle in what makes the wrong a wrong?**
*Attack.* W1 concedes that the wrong is wrong "because of what it does to her." W1(2) explains the exceptionless line this way: "to aim at her death for others is to treat one willed for her own sake as existing for them." Remove "willed" and what remains is Rawls's separateness of persons, or Scanlon's rule that only individuals' reasons count. On wrong-making, the divine will adds nothing.
*Defense.* The text agrees about wrong-making and never claims otherwise ("not wrong merely because it is forbidden"; "Not even God makes cruelty right"). God's role in the text is grounding, not wrong-making. He explains why there is a kind whose members each have such standing, why it holds of every member necessarily, and what each is for. That claim is confined to W2 and to the final row, and both are marked as a judgment "the rivals do not share."
*Rejoinder.* Then the dispute is about where explanation may stop, and the text says so in exactly those words.
**Held** (parity, and correctly claimed only as parity).

**Exchange 4: the aim/foresee line against "answering to each."**
*Attack.* The book's hardest verdict is that both may die because the child's death may not be aimed at. That verdict can be justified to no one. The mother loses everything. The child gains nothing. No third party has a reason. My central test therefore turns against the book's line at exactly the point where the book says the line costs most. Scanlon (*Moral Dimensions*) and Thomson argue that intention does not settle permissibility (verify), and they reach the transplant and scapegoat verdicts without relying on it.
*Defense.* The text does not count this verdict as shared ground. It calls it "the line's hardest cost," and it marks the absolutes in A6 as "this book's claims, not shared ones." The Catholic rule is that one may never do evil so that good may come (CCC 1789). The defense can also turn the generic-reasons machinery against me. A principle that makes "those who will die anyway" killable gives strong grounds of rejection to the dying, the condemned and the hospice patient, all of whom stand in that class.
*Rejoinder.* A principle drawn narrowly (death of both imminent and certain, no one else served) answers that, and Scanlon does not let a slippery slope count unless the principle as written invites it. The verdict remains a cost the book pays openly. One flaw does sit in the text, though. In A6, "Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent" is offered as common ground, and on that reading I accept it. W1(2) then cites A6 for "Her death foreseen and not aimed at may be weighed (A6); aimed at, it may not be." That reads "spent" as "aimed at," and the shared datum does not carry that reading.
**Held with repair** (R3). The verdict itself stays on the scale as a disclosed cost.

**Exchange 5: whose claim, if she cannot waive it?**
*Attack.* The text says her claim is "truly her own," and also that her life is "not hers to dispose of," because it is held in trust from the Lord of life. A claim that its holder cannot waive looks like a claim held by someone else. Rawls and Scanlon both signed the 1997 "Philosophers' Brief" in support of a right to assisted death (verify), for this very reason.
*Defense.* Inalienable claims are coherent; Kant grounds one in the humanity in one's own person, and the text cites him for it. The text separates two relations. Against *others*, the claim is hers, because no number of others has any right to take her life. Toward *herself*, ending her life turns against her own good, against "the just love of herself as well as against the love of God" (CCC 2281). It also says outright that this is the book's claim, and it names the many who hold the rest of A and would allow assisted death.
*Rejoinder.* The Kantian route makes the claim hers. The trust route makes it partly God's. The text uses both and admits the second is not shared.
**Held** (labeled book-specific; no overclaim).

**Exchange 6: the debt to the dead.**
*Attack (Darwall).* A second-personal debt does not need anyone to receive it in order to be real. The wrong was complete when it was done, and the victim's standing is fixed in that address. Asking that every debt be receivable is asking for cosmic closure. That is a wish, not a datum.
*Defense.* The text agrees that the debt is "the same on the second road and the third." It claims only a clue ("it weighs as much as the reader finds..."), and it says outright that the clue weighs little for those who hold certain views.
*Rejoinder, then a probe.* For my school the clue weighs little, which the text allows. A sharper probe: on the Catholic view God forgives guilt "whole," so does He override the victim's standing? *Reply:* no. The text separates guilt before God from the bond with her, which only she can heal (Spe Salvi 44). Pardon she gives out of perfected charity is more truly hers, not less. That is coherent.
**Held.** In fact this exchange produced an insight for the book (§7).

**Exchange 7: the Lord of life and 1 Samuel 15 (the strongest attack).**
*Attack.* W1(2) gives two grounds for the line. One is agent-neutral: "what protects her is her own worth...; to aim at her death for others is to treat one willed for her own sake as existing for them." The other is agent-relative: "no number of others has any right of their own." The exception for God disposes only of the second. If a command to kill the Amalekite infants aims at their deaths for Israel's sake (and 1 Sam 15:2 gives a punitive reason, against Ezek 18:20), then the first ground condemns it, whoever gives the command. Separately, the text says God "never spends her as a mere means against her own good." For an infant who dies unbaptized, the text itself can only hope (CCC 1261, cited in W3 and the Aristotelian row).
*Defense.* St. Thomas's position, which the text adopts, is that death comes to all, "the righteous with the sinners," by God's decree. Calling a life home executes that decree. It does not subordinate her to Israel. The human hand acts by God's right, not by its own. She is kept (W1(1)). And the text does not hide the cost: "reason can see only that He does no wrong in calling a life home, not why He would call these lives home by these hands," and a reader may be "surer that those infants were wronged than of any argument that they were not."
*Rejoinder.* The Thomist answer is coherent, but the text leaves out the one clause it needs, namely that God's calling-home does not aim at her death for anyone else's sake. Without that clause, the agent-neutral ground appears to bind God. And "never spends her..." is stated as fact where, for some, the text's own doctrine offers only hope.
**Held with repair** (R4, R5). The case stays a heavy, disclosed weight against the argument. It is not a hidden contradiction.

**Exchange 8: the evil section under my test.**
*Attack.* The text adopts my test for God: "whether it could be justified to her," with no good paid afterward, only "a good brought from that very evil within her own life." For an infant who dies of a painful disease at two days old, there is no such good she could make her own. By the text's own standard, the case fails for her.
*Defense.* The text says this is "a hope, not a proof." It says "the evidence is not weak" about the child "whom the good of her own freedom does not serve." Its resource for that hope is the risen Christ who "kept His wounds": what was suffered is transfigured, not just compensated (W3; CCC 324).
*Rejoinder.* Whether transfiguration differs from compensation I cannot settle. But I grant that the text claims nothing beyond hope here, and it weighs the case against itself.
**Held** (a disclosed weight, at parity).

**Exchange 9: Rawls's restraint.**
*Attack.* The grounding question is idle for justice. Rawls's conception of the person is political, not metaphysical, so free and equal citizenship needs no ground beyond what citizens can share.
*Defense.* The text's question is about truth, not about public justification ("offered as truths"). Its pragmatist row already meets the claim that grounds do no work, by asking what keeps a claim true when no one says it.
*Rejoinder.* That is fair. Rawls's restraint is a principle of public reason, not a denial that the question exists, and the text never says otherwise.
**Held.**

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**"As written: NONE."**

No Fatal or Serious objection survives. Every place where my school bites hardest (the obstetric verdict, unwaivability, 1 Samuel 15, the infant who dies of disease, hell) is already on the text's own scale as a disclosed cost. None is presented as shared ground or as a win.

**Minor:**
1. *A6 to W1(2) shift.* A6 offers "persons may not be spent" as shared. W1(2) cites A6 for the aim/foresee line, which is not shared (Scanlon and Thomson draw the line without it, verify). Repair R3.
2. *W1(2), agent-neutral ground versus the divine exception.* The text has the Thomist answer available but does not state the clause that God's calling-home is not aimed at her death for anyone else's sake. Without it, the text's own ground appears to bind God. Repair R4. (If the book could not affirm that clause, this would become Serious.)
3. *W1(2), "who never spends her as a mere means against her own good."* This is stated as fact, but for those who die unbaptized before they can choose, the text's own doctrine offers hope (CCC 1261). Repair R5.

**Misstatements of my school:**
- One slight overstatement. The C row says we take "full and equal standing" as given. Equality is the form of the test; what we take as given is the relation that sets the scope (R1).
- An omission, not a misstatement. The row does not say that the same test which reaches the abandoned child also leads its defenders to reject the book's verdicts in the obstetric case and on assisted death (R2). Adding this makes the row fairer to both sides.
- Rawls is not named. That is acceptable. His basis of equality (a capacity above a threshold) is more exposed to the text's "powers" point than Scanlon's is, so leaving him out does not help the text.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**
- Where explanation may stop. My view stops at reasons fundamentalism plus the value of mutual recognition; the text stops at one Source (W2, final C row).
- Scope by relation versus scope by an individual call. The text has a real but not decisive edge here.
- Whether a debt to the dead must be receivable (W1(1)). For my school the clue weighs little.
- The obstetric case and assisted death. These are a real disagreement about verdicts, and the book pays for its side openly.
- God's right as Lord of life and the infant's justification under my test. This is a disclosed weight that the text does not claim to lift.

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1 (C, What we owe each other).**
Old: "why that relation, and not her powers, gives her full and equal standing, it takes as given."
New: "why that relation, and not her powers, brings her within that circle, it takes as given; once she is within it, her standing is equal by the form of the test itself, for each one's objection is heard on its own and none counts for less."

**R2 (C, What we owe each other).**
Old: "It reaches the abandoned child as surely as the wanted one, for it asks what could be justified to *her*, not whether anyone chooses to speak for her."
New: "It reaches the abandoned child as surely as the wanted one, for it asks what could be justified to *her*, not whether anyone chooses to speak for her. The same test leads many of its defenders to part from this book where holding the line serves no one, as when mother and unborn child will both die unless the child is killed, and where a dying man asks for death (A6, W1(2))."

**R3 (A6).**
Old: "Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent."
New: "Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent. (That the line falls between what is aimed at and what is only foreseen is this book's reading, with St. Thomas; some who hold the line, among them T. M. Scanlon and Judith Jarvis Thomson, draw it without that distinction, and so part from this book in its hardest case, W1(2).)"

**R4 (W1(2)).**
Old: "He who gives life its being, and can give it back, does no one wrong when He calls a life home; we, who can do neither, could only take it."
New: "He who gives life its being, and can give it back, does no one wrong when He calls a life home; He does not call her home as though she existed for others, for by His decree all die, and He does not lose her by it. We, who can do neither, could only take it."
(Note: the stated reason in 1 Samuel 15:2 is punitive, which makes this clause hardest to hold for those pages. The evil section already weighs that cost, and the repair makes the principle consistent with it.)

**R5 (W1(2)).**
Old: "and who never spends her as a mere means against her own good."
New: "and who never spends her as a mere means against her own good (where she dies before she can choose and unbaptized, the Church holds this in hope: CCC 1261)."

## 7. New insights; lines worth keeping

**Insights for the book:**
- *My school's own grammar supports W1(1) better than the text uses it.* On Darwall's account, an apology or a request for pardon is a second-personal address, and it presupposes a "you" who can take it up. Asking pardon of someone who no longer exists is an address with no addressee. It cannot be completed, only performed. So the third road is the one account on which the second-personal practice of apology can ever be completed, rather than just owed. This stays a clue, not proof, because my school can still say the debt is real without a receiver. But the book could say in a single sentence that its rivals' own account of apology asks for an addressee.
- *The evil section's best move is mine.* The text already says that only a good brought from the evil within her own life could answer "could it be justified to her," and that "only what is offered to the child herself could answer" Ivan. Those two sentences are the individualist restriction applied to God. The book can tell contractualist readers plainly that it accepts their test, and that only a God who keeps her could ever pass it.
- *Scope is the book's real edge against my school.* The edge is not grounding in general. A call addressed to each individual cannot be charged with arbitrary membership. That is where to press, briefly.

**Lines worth keeping:**
- The posthumous-secret example in A2, the cleanest case of a wrong done to someone without making anything worse.
- "Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent." (Keep it, with R3.)
- "Equal worth is not equal duty."
- "A will that invents values can command, but it cannot bind; even a promise binds only under a law no promiser invented."
- "Our love answers to a worth already there; God's love gives it, by giving her being."
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."
- "The rebel can take her side; he cannot keep her."
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it."

## 8. Score

**8.5 / 10.** The text states my school accurately and even generously, claims only parity where parity is all it has, and puts every verdict my test condemns openly on its own scale. What remains are three small consistency repairs, not a broken argument.

**Honest ceiling for any theistic case against my school: 9.** Reason alone can bring a theistic case to parity on grounding and give it an edge on scope and on the dead. But any Catholic case must carry verdicts my central test cannot justify to the one who bears them (the obstetric case, the commanded killings, hell). Those costs can be disclosed; they cannot be removed.
