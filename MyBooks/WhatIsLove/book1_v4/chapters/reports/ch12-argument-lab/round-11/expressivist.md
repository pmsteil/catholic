# Blind review: the Expressivist / Quasi-Realist school

## 1. School and voice

I speak for the sentimentalist line that runs from Hume and Adam Smith through Ayer and Stevenson to Simon Blackburn's quasi-realism and Allan Gibbard's norm-expressivism. Moral judgment comes from sentiment and attitude, corrected by a general or impartial point of view. Moral words express attitudes, or the acceptance of norms for guilt and resentment, rather than describing a moral realm. Having said that, the quasi-realist then earns, from inside the practice, the right to call moral claims true, to say they do not depend on us, and to say we might be wrong. I am not a subjectivist and not an error theorist. I hold A1 in full, including that torturing a child would be wrong even if every culture approved. For me that counterfactual is itself a moral judgment, and I make it: what makes cruelty wrong is what it does to the child, not what we feel about it. My question for the text is narrower than "is morality real?" It is this: once the quasi-realist can say nearly all of A in his own voice, what is left that only a Lawgiver explains?

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** Smith's is the best: justice is the negative virtue of not injuring another, and what a person is owed is what an impartial spectator, entering fully into her situation, would resent on her behalf if it were withheld (TMS II.ii.1, verify). Gibbard sharpens this: an act is wrong when guilt in the agent and resentment in others would be apt (*Wise Choices, Apt Feelings*, verify). **Holds** for anyone a spectator can enter into. **Breaks in Hume's own version**: Hume admitted that creatures too weak to make us feel their resentment would be owed "gentle usage" by humanity, but not justice (EPM 3.1, verify). Smith's spectator, who resents on behalf of the helpless, is the repair, and it is ours to make, not borrowed.

**2. A hard dilemma resolved.** Take the dying patient in intractable pain who asks for help to die. I weigh sympathy with her suffering, respect for her own say over her life, Hume's "general rules" (what policy, kept steadily, protects the vulnerable from pressure), and the impartial spectator's verdict on each party. Most of my school would allow assisted dying with strong safeguards. Truthfulness, fairness, compassion and sacrifice all appear, but as qualities useful or agreeable to self or others (Hume's catalogue), not as parts of covenant love. **Holds without borrowing.** The result parts from A6's absolute against consent, which the text correctly labels "this book's claims, not shared ones." On the surgeon case I hold the line, but on general-rule and spectator grounds, so at vast numbers many of us would let it bend. **Coherent, and honest that the line is not exceptionless.**

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** I avow it. Every human being is a subject who can be wronged, someone's child, one of us. I reject grading worth by capacity, because on reflection I find such grading intolerable, and because its history is self-serving. Smith himself condemned the exposure of Greek infants as a custom that had corrupted sentiment (TMS V.2.15, verify). **Holds as a commitment I make and defend; does not "ground" in the book's sense.** I deny that a commitment needs a ground beyond the reasons we give for it. Where it **strains**: "equal" for the permanently unconscious or anencephalic is a stance my school can adopt but cannot show that refined sentiment requires. Some of us, Singer-influenced, refuse it. The school is neutral at exactly the point the book cares most about.

**4. End of human flourishing.** There is no single ultimate good. Hume's answer is a life of qualities useful and agreeable to self and others: friendship, conversation, honest work, justice, and the peace of a mind that can bear its own survey. Smith adds that we want not only to be loved but to be worthy of love (TMS III.2.1, verify). My guiding principles are sympathy, impartiality and the general point of view. **Coherent, but it stops at the grave.** For the child who never lives a life, my account calls the loss a tragedy and offers no repair. I do not pretend otherwise.

## 3. Debate highlights

**E1. The frame.** *Attack:* A says its claims are offered "as truths: not as reports of feelings people happen to share." That does not exclude me, since expressivism is not the view that moral claims report feelings. So the case either begs the question against a strawman or has to find the difference somewhere else. *Defense:* It finds it somewhere else. The text knows the quasi-realist "can say nearly everything in A in its own voice" and puts the real disagreement in C. *Rejoinder:* Agreed. But the A parenthesis sends "the one who says these truths are real but need no Mind" to W2, and W2's own parenthesis then exempts me ("need no meeting of law and mind; theirs is the question in C"). That is a misdirection, not a flaw in the argument. **Held with repair** (R1).

**E2. Euthyphro moved over to worth.** *Attack:* W1(2) says God's love "gives" her worth, and that she may not be spent because she is "willed for her own sake." Either God's willing makes her worthy, which is voluntarism, which the book rejects. Or He wills her because of a nature that already merits it, and then the nature does the normative work and God's will adds only a causal story. *Defense:* That is a false dilemma. Worth follows being ("If her worth lies in what she is, then it is a gift exactly as far as her being is a gift"). God does not confer worth on something that already exists without it; He gives the being, and being and good go together. The standard is not above Him and not invented by Him: He is the Good (Ch. 3; *Veritatis Splendor* 36, 40). The "trust" structure (CCC 2258, 2280) adds something real, an explanation of why the line holds even against her own consent. *Rejoinder:* Granted, this is not voluntarism. But "the being who is Being itself is the standard of good," like "being called to an end that is God confers equal worth," is a substantive evaluative principle the theist holds as first. Taking anything as the standard of goodness is itself an act of evaluation, so his foundation ends in a judgment, as mine does. *Defense:* The text says exactly this: "Every account stops somewhere, God's included", "a judgment about where explanation may stop, and the rivals do not share it", "Its claim is reach, not count." **Held: a standoff at parity, and correctly reported as one.**

**E3. Fundamental error, the place where the C row puts the difference.** *Attack:* The "Feeling, refined" row says the difference is "whether a heart refined as far as any could be might still be wrong about her," and that "its critics doubt that its account can say so, and its defenders call the doubt idle." That misreports my school's leading defender. In his reply to Andy Egan, Blackburn does not call the worry idle. He argues the quasi-realist can coherently say his own stable, improved views might be fundamentally mistaken. To call them mistaken is only to say their denial holds, and no a priori guarantee protects any sensibility, his included (Blackburn, "Truth and A Priori Possibility: Egan's Charge against Quasi-Realism," 2009, verify). If he is right, the stated difference is not a difference in what each side can say. *Defense:* The difference survives in another form. On the theist's account, the possibility of error is not just "the denial might hold." It is departure from a standard that exists in a Mind, apart from every human heart. The theist also has a channel beyond the heart: what reason can know is also revealed, so that it can be known "with firm certainty and with no admixture of error" (CCC 38), and a darkened conscience can be corrected from outside. *Rejoinder:* That is the true location: whether moral truth has a home beyond the first-order reasons ("what it does to her"). The quasi-realist calls that further demand idle, and it is there, not at the bare possibility of error, that "idle" belongs. Revelation is a real asymmetry, but reason's weighing in this case does not use it. **Held with repair.** The parity holds, but the row misstates the school at the point where it places the difference (R2).

**E4. The debt to the dead.** *Attack:* W1(1) needs the premise that a debt truly owed to her is one she must be able, one day, to receive. I deny it. On Gibbard's account, guilt toward the dead can be apt with no receiver. Smith gives the psychology: we sympathize with the dead by lodging our living selves in their situation (TMS I.i.1.13, verify). So the demand for her pardon is what a creature built for living bonds would feel whether or not she hears. *Defense:* The text concedes that word for word: "That guilt asks proves nothing, for a mind made for living bonds would ask whether or not she hears." It claims only a clue, and it says the clue "weighs little" for my school. Under its own rule (A), a genealogy defeats a belief only if the belief's truth plays no part in the best account, and I accept that rule for my own beliefs. I am not using Smith to debunk, only to show the receivability premise is optional. *Rejoinder:* The text's reason for why the clue weighs little for me is slightly off. It says "owed" "voices refined feeling," but the C row has already let me say "my guilt is owed to her" in the full sense. The real reason is that I can say "truly owed" and still deny that the debt must be receivable. **Held with repair** (R3).

**E5. How conscience corrects itself (W2).** *Attack:* W2 says that what moves hearers against their own interest, toward those who can never repay, "is reason holding itself to consistency": "the law of truth again." That is a rationalist psychology Hume and Smith deny. Reason alone moves nothing. What widens regard is sympathy corrected by the general point of view and the impartial spectator. *Defense:* The text lists sympathy among the helpers. The spectator's own demand is impartiality, which is a demand for consistency. And W2 exempts sentimentalists from its question anyway. *Rejoinder:* Granted, the two descriptions meet. But as written, the sentence presents a contested theory of moral motivation as fact in order to bring in A7. A disjunctive wording costs nothing. **Held with repair** (R4).

**E6. Evil, and Philo's fourth hypothesis.** *Attack:* Hume's Philo (*Dialogues* XI, verify) holds that a world this mixed is best fitted by a first cause with neither goodness nor malice. Against my school, the positive moral side of the case is at parity (E2, E3), W2's moral half exempts me, and the clues fit an indifferent world equally well, which the text itself admits. On reason alone, then, the weight of evil should tip the balance toward indifference. *Defense:* The text never claims otherwise as proof. It names the indifferent source and says only the whole case answers it. It concedes that "a dark god or an indifferent world would leave them just as we find them." It keeps the pattern of pain in nature, the parasite, the fawn, hell and 1 Samuel 15 on the far side of the scale. It ends: "this book judges," and "An honest reader may weigh it differently." What remains on its side against indifference is W1(2), a worth willed for each one's own sake, and W2's fit for logic and number, which my school does not dissolve. *Rejoinder:* Then against me the verdict is a judgment, not a result, and the text says so. **Held: an honest standoff, not a defect.**

**E7. The stopping point.** *Attack:* Hume's Demea and Cleanthes exchange (*Dialogues* IX, verify): whatever we can conceive as existing we can conceive as not existing, so "the One whose very being is to be" is no better a stopping point than the realist's brute necessities. *Defense:* Aquinas denies that God's existence is self-evident to us (ST I q.2 a.1). His necessity is reached a posteriori, and conceivability is a poor guide to possibility once a posteriori necessities are admitted. The text also claims no advantage in count: "The realist says the same of his necessary truths." *Rejoinder:* Accepted. **Held.**

**E8. The newborn test in the conclusion.** *Attack:* The test does not separate us. I am as sure as the author that the infant left on the hillside was wronged, and wronged no less than the strong one. I can even say the last line in my own voice: what rises in me is not the source of her worth but my answer to it. That is Blackburn's denial of mind-dependence, almost word for word. The step to "it follows what she is *and what she is made for*" adds a teleology the test does not supply. *Defense:* The conclusion addresses only "the one who gives up A4" and says it "cannot compel" even him. The teleological step is marked "this book answers," an answer offered, not an inference claimed. *Rejoinder:* Correct. The line is shared ground, and the book loses nothing by sharing it. **Held.**

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**As written: NONE.**

I looked hard for a Serious objection. The obvious candidates are the Euthyphro moved over to worth, the Humean argument from evil, Hume on necessary being, and the debunking of the debt to the dead. The text answers each at least to parity, and in most places in its own words before I could press it. It never claims that the moral argument proper beats quasi-realism. It claims parity on grounding, an advantage of "reach" that it labels a contested judgment, clues it labels as clues, and a weighing that it labels "this book judges."

**Minor items:**
1. **Misstatement of my school (the most important minor item).** "Its defenders call the doubt idle" misreports Blackburn's published reply to Egan, which argues that quasi-realism *can* say even a fully improved sensibility might be wrong. "Idle" belongs one step further on: to the demand that moral truth have a home beyond the first-order reasons. As written, the row places the difference at a point my school says is no difference (R2).
2. **Misstatement (lumping).** Hume and Smith are sentimentalists about where moral distinctions come from. The semantic thesis that "moral words voice our attitudes" belongs to Ayer, Stevenson, Blackburn and Gibbard. "Made informed, consistent and sympathetic" sounds like an ideal-observer analysis of what moral words mean, which Blackburn rejects (R2).
3. **Omission.** The row does not give my school's strongest resource on A2: Gibbard's analysis of "wrong" in terms of apt guilt and resentment, which builds in the orientation toward the one wronged (R2).
4. **Routing in the A parenthesis** (R1).
5. **The W1(1) parenthesis** gives the wrong reason why the clue weighs little for me (R3).
6. **W2's motivation sentence** states rationalist moral psychology as settled (R4).

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**
- What grounds equal worth: the theist's first principle ("He is the Good") against my avowed commitment. Each ends in something held as first (E2).
- Whether moral truth needs a home beyond what it is about. The theist says it is held first in a Mind; I say the demand is idle (E3).
- The weighing of evil against W1(2) and W2 (E6).
- Persons at the grave. This is a dispute about the metaphysics of persons, not about metaethics. My school is usually naturalist here, but expressivism as such is silent, and the text's "joined as it usually is" is exactly right.

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1. A heading, routing parenthesis.**
Old: `(For the one who says these truths are real but need no Mind, W2 takes up the claim;`
New: `(For the one who says these truths are real but need no Mind, W2 takes up the claim, and C the one who says them in his own voice and holds that they need no ground beyond the reasons that support them;`

**R2. C, "Feeling, refined," opening and the error sentence.**
Old: `(Hume, Adam Smith, and today's quasi-realists: moral words voice our attitudes, made informed, consistent and sympathetic) can say nearly everything in A in its own voice, even "her worth does not depend on us" and "my guilt is owed to her."`
New: `(Hume and Adam Smith, for whom moral judgment springs from sentiment corrected by a general or impartial point of view; and today's expressivists and quasi-realists, Simon Blackburn and Allan Gibbard among them, for whom moral words express attitudes, or the acceptance of norms for guilt and resentment, rather than describe a moral realm, and who then earn, from within, the right to call them true) can say nearly everything in A in its own voice, even "her worth does not depend on us" and "my guilt is owed to her"; Gibbard makes guilt its center, holding that to call a deed wrong is to hold that guilt in the doer and resentment on behalf of the one wronged would be fitting.`

Old: `but whether a heart refined as far as any could be might still be wrong about her: on ours it might, for the truth is held first in the Mind that made her; its critics doubt that its account can say so, and its defenders call the doubt idle.`
New: `but whether the truth about her has any home beyond what is done to her. Its critics doubt that it can even say that a heart refined as far as any could be might still be wrong about her; its ablest defender answers that it can, for to call such a heart wrong is only to say that what it holds is false, and nothing guarantees any heart against that. On ours, the truth is held first in the Mind that made her, from which every heart might depart; its defenders call that further claim idle, and here the two part.`

(Check the Gibbard and Blackburn paraphrases against *Wise Choices, Apt Feelings* ch. 3 and Blackburn 2009 before final.)

**R3. W1(1), closing parenthesis.**
Old: `It weighs little for a reader who holds that "owed" voices refined feeling or names what a people's laws assign,`
New: `It weighs little for a reader who holds that "owed" names what a people's laws assign, or who, with the quasi-realist, will say "truly owed" and still hold that a debt can be truly owed to one who will never receive it,`

**R4. W2, conscience correcting itself.**
Old: `but what moves hearers against their own interest, toward those who can never repay, is reason holding itself to consistency with goods it already sees: the law of truth again.`
New: `but what moves hearers against their own interest, toward those who can never repay, is reason holding itself to consistency with goods it already sees (sympathy corrected by an impartial standpoint, Hume and Smith would say, and impartiality is that same demand for consistency): the law of truth again.`

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights:**
- **Smith passes the hillside test, and the book can say so.** Smith condemned the Greek exposure of infants as a custom that had corrupted natural sentiment (TMS V.2.15, verify). Crediting him in A4 or the conclusion would show that the newborn test is not where theist and sentimentalist part. It would also make the conclusion's real target, the one who gives up A4, clearer.
- **Hume's own admission on A4.** Hume's passage on creatures too weak to make their resentment felt, owed "gentle usage" but not justice (EPM 3.1, verify), is the sentimentalist's sharpest self-confessed cost on equal dignity. It belongs beside the sensible knave, which the text already handles well, and it would strengthen the care row's point about standing that rests on power.
- **Smith on sympathy with the dead** (TMS I.i.1.13, verify) is the exact sentimentalist source for the concession in W1(1) that "a mind made for living bonds would ask whether or not she hears." Citing it would show the concession is made to the best opponent, not a vague one.
- **The impartial spectator as a clue.** The sentimentalist's best tool is an imagined someone who sees the infant no one holds, and Smith himself let the shaken spectator appeal to the all-seeing Judge (already cited in W3). The book could note, as a clue and no more, that it holds such a Spectator is not only imagined. That would be one sentence in W3 or C, offered as fit, not evidence.
- **Where the disagreement with my school really lies.** It is not whether we can say "true," "independent of us," or "we might be wrong," which the quasi-realist can say. It is whether moral truth has a home beyond the first-order reasons. The book's strongest honest asymmetry against my school is revelation as a channel beyond the heart (CCC 38). It is right to keep that out of reason's weighing, but it could say once that this is where its own "might be wrong" draws its meaning.

**Lines worth keeping:**
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it." (Shared ground. Keep it, knowing the quasi-realist says it too.)
- "No argument compels such a man, ours included; the difference is what he is missing."
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."
- "That tells us how we long, not whether there is Someone to long for."
- "It is not wrong merely because it is forbidden."
- "Heaven and Earth give life to things; God wanted *you*."
- "The rebel can take her side; he cannot keep her."

## 8. Score

**8/10.** Against expressivism and quasi-realism the text is sound as written. It does not overclaim, it concedes my first-order points in its own words, and it ends its weighing as a labeled judgment. The only flaw from my school's side is a misreport of Blackburn on fundamental error at the exact point where the C row places the difference.

**Honest ceiling for any theistic case against my school: 8/10.** The moral argument proper can reach only parity with a well-built quasi-realism. Any advantage must come from the metaphysics of persons, from the fit of mind with logic and number, or from revelation, which reason's weighing sets aside. This text is essentially at that ceiling. The repairs make it more accurate but do not raise it.
