# Blind review: the Spinozist / naturalist pantheist school

Note on sources: references to Spinoza's *Ethics* use E + part + proposition (P), corollary (C), scholium (S), demonstration (D), appendix (App), definition of the affects (DefAff); TTP = *Theological-Political Treatise*; TP = *Political Treatise*; Ep. = letters. Each paraphrase of Spinoza or a modern reader is marked (verify). No direct quotations from copyrighted translations.

## 1. School and voice

I speak for Deus sive Natura: one substance, existing necessarily and causing itself, with infinitely many attributes, two of which we know, Thought and Extension. Everything follows from its nature with the same necessity as a triangle's properties follow from the triangle (E1P16, E1P17S, E1P29, E1P33). God has intellect and "will" only as the necessary working of thought (E1P32C2, E2P49C). He does not choose and does not act for ends; final causes are a human projection (E1App). Good and bad are relative to the striving (*conatus*) by which each thing persists in its being (E3P6–7, E3P9S, E4Pref). Freedom is acting from the necessity of one's own nature, understood adequately (E1D7, E4P66–73). Beatitude is the intellectual love of God, which is virtue itself (E5P32–42). I argue with modern readers: Edwin Curley (a God read as the most general laws of nature), Michael Della Rocca (the principle of sufficient reason pressed to necessitarianism), Don Garrett (necessitarianism, singular essences, the eternity of the mind), and the "cosmic religion" of Einstein, who said he believed in Spinoza's God, revealed in the harmony of what exists, and not in a God concerned with human fates and actions (paraphrase, verify). I use the word "God" with full seriousness. I deny that God is a person who wills anyone.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**(1) Starting point for justice.** Each thing strives to persist, and the mind strives to understand (E3P6–7, E4P26). Reason tells us that nothing is more useful to a human being than another human who lives by reason (E4P18S, E4P35C1). So the rational person wants for every other human the good he wants for himself (E4P37). *Justice* in the strict sense, giving each person his own, exists only under civil law, and there is no wrong or sin in the state of nature (E4P37S2; TTP 16; TP 2.18–23; verify). What anyone is owed is therefore (a) whatever reason's common good requires, and (b) whatever the commonwealth assigns. **Verdict: holds as a system.** It does not borrow. It **breaks** against A1–A2 as the text means them: cruelty is *bad* by reason whatever any culture approves, but it is a *wrong done to someone* only under law.

**(2) A hard dilemma resolved: the surgeon and the five, in secret.** The free man never acts deceitfully, even to save his own life (E4P72 and scholium). A secret killing is deceit through and through, so it is ruled out. Further, a commonwealth whose physicians may spend patients destroys the mutual trust that makes it more powerful than its members alone (E4P73; TP 3.6, verify). The principle that decides: act from adequate ideas toward what increases the shared power of acting and understanding, never from fear, hope or pity (E4P50, E4P63). **Verdict: holds** for this case without borrowing. My ground is the agent's integrity and civic reason, not *her* claim. Where good faith is not at stake, for example a sovereign openly sacrificing an innocent for the people's safety, my answer weakens to prudence, and I do not hold the line at every number. Mercy is replaced by *generositas*, the desire to aid others from reason alone (E3P59S). Self-gift is replaced by the same thing. **Borrows nothing, but thin** exactly where the book is thickest.

**(3) Equal, unconditional dignity.** I deny it as a metaphysical endowment. Every human being, newborn and dying included, is a necessary expression of God's infinite power, and God's intellect holds an idea of the essence of *this* body, not merely of a kind (E2P8, E5P22). Nothing in nature is contingent or superfluous. Reason also asks that the commonwealth protect all, and that hatred be repaid with love, the enemy included (E4P46). But perfection *is* power (E2D6, E3P7), and Spinoza says outright that one who, like an infant, has a body capable of very few things has a mind conscious of almost nothing, whose eternal part is small (E5P39 and scholium, verify). **Verdict: breaks.** I can give equal civil protection and a necessity that no human can be replaced. I cannot say the infant no one held was worth as much as the sage, in any sense beyond what reason and law decree.

**(4) The end of human flourishing.** Blessedness is the intellectual love of God that arises from the third kind of knowledge. It is the mind's own activity, eternal in its eternal part, and it is virtue itself, not its reward (E5P27, E5P32–33, E5P36, E5P42). The guiding principles are: seek your true advantage, which is understanding; join with others in reason; and accept what is necessary, the *acquiescentia* that understanding brings (E4App 32). **Verdict: holds and coherent.** It **breaks** on equality, because the end is reached by power and is "as difficult as it is rare" (E5P42S, paraphrase). It offers the newborn, and the man who will never reason, almost nothing.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1: final causes and anthropomorphism.**
*Attack.* The case turns on "willed for her own sake" and "God wanted *you*" (W1(2), C "Sacred order", conclusion). That is a final cause read into the infinite. If God's intellect and will are what the Thomist says, identical with His simple essence, they resemble ours no more than the Dog Star resembles the barking dog (E1P17S). Then "wanted" names no content we can use to explain anything.
*Defense.* The text is not anthropomorphic. It denies passion and need in God: He wills "from no need" (C, A God who is Love), "nothing harms God" (Job 35:6–8, in W1), and to love is to will another's good (ST I q.20 a.2). Aquinas's analogy is not equivocation: perfections such as knowing and willing the good belong to God properly and first (ST I q.13 a.3, a.5), "more truly than in us" (end of W2). The text also refuses voluntarism: "not a commander who picks rules" (W1 closing). Finally, the E1App diagnosis is a genealogy, the claim that men imagine purposes because they themselves act for ends. By the fairness rule I may not use that genealogy unless I let the text use one against me.
*Rejoinder.* Granted on the genealogy, which I withdraw. What remains is conceptual: the more remote the analogy, the less "wanted" explains. But the text claims only explanatory *reach* and calls its own decisive items clues. **Held.**

**Exchange 2: divine freedom and the necessity of creation (the Della Rocca line).**
*Attack.* The text's whole difference from me is that God wills each of us "freely and from no need" (C, A God who is Love). Take a simple, immutable God whose essence is to exist (ST I q.3 a.4, cited) and whose will is His essence. Then either His willing of this world is as necessary as His essence, which is my position, or the fact that this world exists rather than another, or none, has no sufficient reason. The text needs a principle of explanation to run W2 ("where explanation may stop"). Here, at the top, it stops at a brute choice. Mine stops at nothing contingent.
*Defense.* The Church affirms against exactly this view that God created by a will free from all necessity, not as necessarily as He loves Himself (*Dei Filius* ch. 1 and can. 1.5). Aquinas: God wills His own goodness necessarily and creatures not necessarily, since His goodness is whole without them (ST I q.19 a.3). The reason for creating is His goodness, a reason that suffices without forcing (q.19 a.5). His act of will is one and necessary; what is contingent is what that act brings about (the "extrinsic" model; verify for its modern defenders). And necessitarianism pays its own price: the child's torture could not have been otherwise, which most readers find less credible than a free creation.
*Rejoinder.* That is a real answer, and the price I pay is real (Della Rocca accepts it; many Spinozists do not like it). But the text as written does not mention the trade at all. It says only that its God is "not a brute" stopping point. Against me in particular, it should say that its free creation leaves one thing without a further why, where mine leaves none. **Held with repair** (Repair R4).

**Exchange 3: does W2 tell my God from the text's?**
*Attack.* Law, mind and the natures the law is about, all from one source: that is E2P7, the order of ideas being the order of things. My God is Mind too (infinite intellect, E1P31, E2P3). So W2 cannot count for the text against me.
*Defense.* The text says so itself. Spinoza "agree[s] with this witness and part[s] from this book in W1 and W3" (W2 parenthetical). The C row "A God who is Love" places the difference where it belongs, in willing each of us for her own sake.
*Rejoinder.* Agreed, with one exception. The evil section's *First* paragraph lists "a source... from whose fullness the ruin follows with the rest, as Spinoza held" and then says such sources are answered "by the law and the mind that reads it (W2)." Against me, W2 answers nothing; the text's own W2 says so. **Held with repair** (Repair R1).

**Exchange 4: "No order wants any one of them" (W1(2)).**
*Attack.* The text assumes an impersonal order is a law of *kinds*. It "gives a nature to every member of a kind; it does not want any one of them." But my God's intellect holds the singular essence of this very body (E5P22). Her conatus *is* God's power (E4P4D, which the text grants). And because nothing could have been otherwise, she cannot be replaced, more firmly than under a God who might have made someone else instead. The text charges utilitarianism with failing to say "you could not be replaced without loss" (C, Well-being). I say you could not be replaced at all.
*Defense.* Necessity is not regard. Spinoza's God is as much the power of the parasite that eats the child as of the child's striving to live (E4Ax). Nothing in that order is *for* her against what destroys her. The text's evil section draws exactly this line: "the parasite takes from the child; God does not give her to it." And on my own account her worth is graded. Perfection is power, and the infant's eternal part is small (E5P39S, which the text cites correctly in the C row). The text's ground for equal worth is an end offered equally to every human being whatever her powers (W1(2), C God who is Love). My school has nothing that does not wait on power.
*Rejoinder.* I hold that her being and necessity are her full reality (E2D6). But I concede that I cannot say what A4 and the conclusion say, and that most honest readers are surer the abandoned infant was wronged no less than the strong one than they are of E5P39. The text's lever, "which are you surer of," works against me here. I do note that the equal *call* is revealed content (*Humani Generis* 26). It earns its weight only as explanatory fit, and C presents it as exactly that. **Held.**

**Exchange 5: inviolability and the Lord of life (1 Samuel 15).**
*Attack.* The text says the line has no exceptions for us because "what protects her is her own worth." Yet the same worth does not protect her from God's command to kill the Amalekite infants (W1(2), with ST II-II q.64 a.6 ad 1). So her protection lies in *jurisdiction*: the right is His, not ours. That is the image of God as a prince with prerogatives, which Spinoza thought the ignorant made up (E2P3S; TTP 4, where divine decrees are eternal truths and only the ignorant take them as commands).
*Defense.* The text's Lord is not a prince who owns lives as goods. He gives being, wills her for her own sake, and does not lose her in death (W1(1)–(2)). So His "calling home" does not spend her against her own good; we can only take a life, never give it back. The text also refuses every present claim to such a command (CCC 67). The law is His wisdom, which is close to TTP 4's eternal truths.
*Rejoinder.* Then the absolute against killing the innocent rests on her worth *together with* the killer's inability to restore what he takes. That is coherent. But it means worth alone does not draw the line, and Kant, Mencius and the Way draw it more absolutely. The text concedes all of this ("the line's hardest cost"; "a cost against the case, not only a mystery"). My genealogy of the prince image is barred by the fairness rule, so only the conceptual point stands, and it is already paid for. **Held (acknowledged cost; a standoff, not a defect).**

**Exchange 6: "said to no one" (evil section, *First*).**
*Attack.* The text says a calm that understands the deed had to happen "cannot take her side against the past," while "God says no to what was done to her, and yes to her." But the theist's God also leaves the past in place. He eternally permitted what He could have prevented. His "no" did not stop it. My sage takes her side where sides can be taken: in the present and future, in law, in generosity, in making sure it does not happen again.
*Defense.* "God says no" is the doctrine that God did not make death and does not will evil for its own sake (Wisdom 1:13; CCC 311, 413; ST I q.19 a.9). It is not a claim that He stopped the deed. The real difference lies in what follows: a good drawn "from that very evil within her own life" (CCC 324), resurrection, and her being kept. The text marks this as "a hope, not a proof."
*Rejoinder.* Then the difference is eschatological, not attitudinal, and the text says so in its *Third* paragraph and in its Adorno passage. I grant that my *acquiescentia* offers the dead child nothing. **Held.**

**Exchange 7: is the intellectual love "answered by no one"? (W3)**
*Attack.* W3 says that if the highest good "loves us only in loving itself, wanting none of us," then love "is answered in the end by no one." That states my position at its weakest. Spinoza holds that God's love toward human beings and the mind's intellectual love toward God are one and the same (E5P36C). The love is answered by the infinite intellect, insofar as it makes up my own mind. It is answered more intimately than by any second party.
*Defense.* Identity is not communion. A love that is my own love of God, seen from the other side, does not turn to me as another, and Spinoza himself says God, strictly, loves no one (E5P17C). The text makes the same point against Mou Zongsan in the C row "Sacred order": "it knows as my own deepest heart, not as another who turns to me." Love wills the good *of another* (ST I q.20 a.2).
*Rejoinder.* That is the honest parting. The text should state my best form before it parts from me, as it does for Mou. "By no one" should read "by no one besides ourselves." **Held with repair** (Repairs R2, R3).

**Exchange 8: the duty to truth (A7, Nietzsche dilemma).**
*Attack.* I ground A7 without a Lawgiver. A mind's power is its adequate ideas (E3P1, E3P3). Its striving is to understand (E4P26–27). So every mind, by what a mind is, is bound to the truth, and a comforting falsehood is a loss of power, not a gain.
*Defense.* That is the first horn of the text's Nietzsche dilemma, "something binds every mind by what a mind is," which sends the question to W2. W2 grants that Spinoza agrees, so the text claims no win here.
*Rejoinder.* None needed. **Held.**

## 4. Ledger

- **A heading (epistemic rule, list of deniers):** Holds. The rule (trust what survives reflection unless its truth plays no part in the best account of why we hold it) is fair, and it applies to both sides equally. Spinoza is not on the list of deniers, but the list says "and others," and C treats him accurately.
- **A1:** Holds. Spinoza grants that cruelty is bad by reason whatever a culture approves. He denies only that it is a wrong done to someone outside law, which A2 carries.
- **A2:** Holds as a premise. The secret-and-letter pair is the strongest case I know against reducing wronging to a worse state of affairs. Spinoza can explain the difference only on the agent's side (bad faith, E4P72), not as *her* being wronged. That costs us.
- **A3:** Holds. Spinoza denies a debt to the dead. The premise is at least as plausible as the denial.
- **A4:** Holds. This is where my school breaks (E5P39S). The text's handling of how late the belief came (Seneca, Christians slow on slavery) is honest.
- **A5:** Holds. It is modest: wants and abilities do not *by themselves* settle what we ought to do. Spinoza says we judge things good because we desire them (E3P9S). He also says desire guided by adequate ideas is what reason dictates, so the premise as worded stands.
- **A6:** Holds, as the book's own claim, clearly labelled. Spinoza supports the absolute against bad faith (E4P72) and holds that no one seeks his own death from his own nature (E4P18S, E4P20S). He does not hold the line against the sovereign at every number.
- **A7:** Holds. Spinoza agrees fully, from what a mind is.
- **W1 frame:** Holds. It is accurate that any account keeping Anscombe's words treats wrong as necessary. Aquinas's point that God is offended by us only because we act against our own good (SCG III.122) is one Spinoza would welcome.
- **W1(1):** Holds, as the clue it says it is. Spinoza is correctly placed on the first road. The concession that "a mind made for living bonds would ask whether or not she hears" heads off my imagination-based debunking (E2P17) before I can make it.
- **W1(2):** Holds. It states accurately that Spinoza's God knows each one eternally as herself and that God's power is her striving. The differential claim (an equal end offered, being willed for her own sake, being kept) survives Exchange 4. The 1 Samuel 15 cost is paid openly.
- **W1 closing ("not a commander who picks rules"):** Holds. It converges with TTP 4 (decrees are eternal truths) while keeping a personal Author. Its non-voluntarism is consistent.
- **W2:** Holds. By its own admission it does not tell theism from Spinoza, and it says where the difference lies.
- **W3, first paragraph:** Holds with repair. Spinoza's E5P19 is reported accurately, and the reply (to want to be wanted by One who lacks nothing) is good. But "answered in the end by no one" understates E5P36C (R2).
- **W3, second paragraph (hidden seekers):** Holds. My school says divine hiddenness is just what one would expect. The text treats it as a real weight and does not overclaim.
- **C, Nothing really binds:** Holds; no Spinozist objection.
- **C, Feeling, refined:** Holds; no Spinozist objection.
- **C, Our best words, widening:** Holds; no Spinozist objection.
- **C, Reason's own law:** Holds. The admission that Kant draws the line more absolutely is honest.
- **C, What we owe each other:** Holds; no Spinozist objection.
- **C, Care between persons:** Holds; no Spinozist objection.
- **C, Well-being, counted impartially:** Holds. The point about being replaceable is fair against utilitarianism. Spinoza would answer it differently, by necessity (Exchange 4).
- **C, Flourishing of our kind:** Holds; no Spinozist objection.
- **C, Necessary truths that belong to no one:** Holds. The question of "fit" is framed as a judgment about where explanation may stop, not as a proof.
- **C, Impersonal sacred order:** Holds. The Mou Zongsan sentence ("my own deepest heart, not as another") is the model for repairing W3.
- **C, The Way:** Holds; no Spinozist objection.
- **C, God, or Nature:** Holds with repair. Every claim is accurate: E1P16–17S, E4P72S, E1App, E4P37S2, E4P54/E5P6, E5P21–23S, E5P39S, E5P36C. The clause "Spinoza's wills no one" is slightly loose, since Spinoza grants God a will identical with intellect and denies only choice. The row also leaves out two points that help the text: the identity of God's love and ours (E5P36C), and the fact that God is equally the power of what destroys her (R3).
- **C, The Buddha's Dhamma:** Holds; no Spinozist objection.
- **C, History and class:** Holds; no Spinozist objection.
- **C, Power and rank:** Holds. Its dilemma is friendly to Spinoza, and the text admits it does not settle the matter.
- **C, Freedom that makes its values:** Holds; no Spinozist objection.
- **C, A God who is Love:** Holds with repair. It does not mention the freedom-or-necessity trade, which is the price of "freely and from no need" against Spinoza (R4).
- **Evil, paragraph 1 ("On the other side of the scale"):** Holds. It correctly says Spinoza's God, which intends no one, bears this weight more easily. Hell, 1 Samuel 15 and the unbaptized infant are held as costs, not dissolved.
- **Evil, *First* (privation; dark or indifferent sources):** Holds with repair. It cites W2 against Spinoza's "fullness" source, which W2 cannot answer (R1). "Said to no one" is fair but leaves out E5P36C, which matters less here.
- **Evil, *Second* (permission is not a trade):** Holds. The doctrine is accurate (CCC 311, 312, 324, 412; ST I q.22 a.2 ad 2). The concession on the parasite and on the ages before humans is honest.
- **Evil, *Third* (God who suffers with; hopes compared):** Holds. The comparison of hopes is fair. A Spinozist hope (understanding, a freer commonwealth) belongs with the "hopes for those who come after," and the text's verdict on such hopes is correct.
- **Conclusion:** Holds. It claims less than CCC 31/36 permits and does not overclaim. Its final lever is exactly where my school must give way.

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**As written: NONE.**

I tried each of my school's strongest lines: final causes, the necessity of creation, singular essences, the identity of loves, jurisdiction against worth, and *acquiescentia* against "taking her side." Each either meets an answer at parity, or targets a cost the text already names and pays, or reduces to a defect of wording. None is valid, with premises at least as plausible as the text's, and beyond the text's power to answer at parity.

**Minor items**
1. *Evil, First:* "by the law and the mind that reads it (W2)" is offered against Spinoza's source, contradicting W2's own admission that Spinoza agrees with it (R1).
2. *C, A God who is Love:* the freedom-or-necessity trade is not acknowledged. "Not... a brute" stopping point is accurate relative to the realist, but against Spinoza a free creation leaves exactly one thing without a further why (R4).
3. *W3:* "answered in the end by no one" states Spinoza's position below its best (E5P36C) (R2).

**Misstatements of my school** (all slight)
- *C, God, or Nature:* "Spinoza's wills no one." Spinoza ascribes will to God as the necessary working of intellect (E1P32C2, E2P49C). What he denies is free choice and ends. Better: "chooses no one, and wills nothing for anyone's sake" (R3).
- *W3:* "loves us only in loving itself, wanting none of us" omits that, for Spinoza, God's love of us *is* our intellectual love of Him (E5P36C) (R2).
- Everything else said of Spinoza is accurate, including E5P39S on the infant, which is often missed. That includes W1(1) on repentance, W1(2) on singular knowledge and conatus, W3 on E5P19, C on E4P37S2, E4P72S and E5P23S, and the evil section on fullness.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Necessity against free creation: each side pays a different price, and neither refutes the other.
- Whether communion requires an Other, or whether identity in God is the highest answer to love.
- Whether "it is good that she exists" requires someone who says it, or whether it is simply her degree of reality (E2D6).
- Whether worth can differ from power, which turns on whether essence is conatus (E3P7). Most readers' certainty about A4 favours the text; my school accepts the cost.
- Whether the line against killing the innocent rests on worth alone or on worth together with the killer's inability to restore. The text pays for this one in its 1 Samuel 15 treatment.

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1 (Evil, *First*).**
Old: "Those are answered only by the whole case: chiefly by a worth that is each one's own and not to be spent for others, which this book holds is wanted for her own sake (W1(2)), and by the law and the mind that reads it (W2);"
New: "Those are answered only by the whole case: chiefly by a worth that is each one's own and not to be spent for others, which this book holds is wanted for her own sake (W1(2)); and, against a source with no mind at all, though not against Spinoza's, which is Mind too, by the law and the mind that reads it (W2);"

**R2 (W3).**
Old: "If the highest good knows and loves no one, or knows each of us only as a truth it holds, and loves us only in loving itself, wanting none of us, then love, the best thing in us, is answered in the end by no one."
New: "If the highest good knows and loves no one, or knows each of us only as a truth it holds, and loves us only in loving itself, so that, as Spinoza held, its love for us and our love for it are one and the same, wanting none of us as another, then love, the best thing in us, is answered in the end by no one besides ourselves."

**R3 (C, God, or Nature).**
Old: "and its God, in whom each of us is known eternally, loves men in loving Himself. Aquinas too roots God's love of creatures in His love of His own goodness (ST I q.19 a.2), but his God also wills each person for her own sake; Spinoza's wills no one (W3)."
New: "and its God, in whom each of us is known eternally, loves men in loving Himself, so that His love of us and our understanding love of Him are one and the same. Aquinas too roots God's love of creatures in His love of His own goodness (ST I q.19 a.2), but his God also wills each person for her own sake; Spinoza's has a will only as the necessary working of its intellect, chooses no one, and is as much the power of what destroys her as of her own striving (W3)."

**R4 (C, A God who is Love).**
Old: "it is the one that wills each of us for her own sake, freely and from no need, to share His goodness (ST I q.19 a.3; q.44 a.4; CCC 293, 295), a claim hell strains most (below),"
New: "it is the one that wills each of us for her own sake, freely and from no need, to share His goodness (ST I q.19 a.3; q.44 a.4; CCC 293, 295; against a creation by necessity, *Dei Filius* ch. 1 and can. 1.5), a claim hell strains most (below). Against Spinoza that freedom is a trade, not a gain: his account leaves nothing contingent and so nothing without a further why, at the price that nothing, the child's suffering included, could have been otherwise; this one holds that God's goodness is reason enough to create but does not force Him, and that His willing has no cause beyond itself (ST I q.19 a.5)."

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**New insights**
- **Spinoza as a partial witness for A6's "even at his asking."** Spinoza holds that no one neglects his own preservation, or kills himself, from the necessity of his own nature, but only when overcome by causes outside him. He calls such a person weak-minded, and his example is Seneca, ordered by Nero to die (E4P18S, E4P20S; verify). This is a non-theistic witness that a will aiming at its own death is not acting from its own good. It agrees with CCC 2281 on the point and with CCC 2282 on diminished responsibility, though Spinoza has no free will to diminish. It could sit in A6 beside the Vinaya. Mark it "partial": Spinoza says nothing explicit about another person killing at someone's request.
- **E4P72 handles the secret surgeon.** Spinoza's absolute against bad faith covers A6's "even if no one would ever know" without counting totals. This strengthens the text's claim that the line is widely seen, and it shows exactly where a non-theistic absolute stops: at the agent's integrity, not at *her* claim.
- **"Irreplaceable" from necessity.** The text could pre-empt my strongest move in Exchange 4 with one clause: a necessity that no one could have been otherwise is not a regard *for* her, because the same necessity is the power of what destroys her. The evil section's parasite sentence already contains the answer. Linking it to W1(2) would make the reply to Spinoza explicit.
- **TTP 4 converges with W1's closing.** Spinoza argued that divine decrees are eternal truths, which the ignorant take as a king's commands. The text's "not as a will that invents it, but because it is His own wisdom" is closer to that than many readers will expect. The book can note that it agrees with Spinoza against voluntarism and parts from him only over whether that Wisdom is Someone.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "Guilt faces a person: I did this to *her*." (A2)
- "A law or an order gives a nature to every member of a kind; it does not want any one of them." (W1(2))
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive." (W1(1))
- "But to love is to will another's good... to long for His love is to want to be wanted by One who lacks nothing." (W3; the best short reply to E5P19 I have seen.)
- "The parasite takes from the child; God does not give her to it." (Evil, *Second*)
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it." (Conclusion)

## 8. Score
