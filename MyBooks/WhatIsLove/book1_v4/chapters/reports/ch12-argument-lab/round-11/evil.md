# Blind review: "A GOOD GOD WOULD NEVER ALLOW THIS"
Reviewer school: the problem of evil and divine hiddenness at full strength (Mackie, Rowe, Draper, Schellenberg, Maitzen, Stephen Law, Marilyn Adams and Talbott on hell, Morriston and Wielenberg on the divine-command killings, Ivan Karamazov).

## 1. School and voice

I argue from the theist's own premises, not from mine. The problem of evil doesn't need my metaethics. It needs the text's: that the child on the hillside was really wronged (A1, A4), that a wrong must be answered for the one it was done to and not just outweighed (Second), and above all that God "wills each of us for her own sake" and "never spends her as a mere means against her own good" (W1(2)). The more the case says about love, the sharper my objection gets. Where I need a positive view (section 2), I speak as a godless robust realist in Wielenberg's manner (verify): necessary ethical truths that rest on the natures of persons and pain, with nothing behind them. The text names this view as its nearest rival.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

1. **Starting point for justice.** Each person is a subject whose life can go well or badly *for her*, and that standing is a necessary truth with nothing behind it. What she is owed comes from it: not to be harmed for others' gain, to be told the truth, to be helped when helping costs us little. → **Holds**, at a stopping point. The text names this point honestly (W2, "Necessary truths") and calls it a judgment about where explanation may stop. That's a standoff, not a borrowing.

2. **A hard dilemma.** Take the text's own hardest case: mother and unborn child will both die unless the child is killed. I say saving the mother wrongs no one. The child loses nothing she would otherwise have had, the mother keeps her life, and no one's claim is set back for anyone else's gain. My principles are claims, the duty to rescue, and the rule that you may not make anyone worse off for others. → **Holds** without borrowing the book's covenant sense of justice or mercy. I use the plain senses everyone shares. The text admits this is where its line costs most (W1(2)). We part at parity: it holds that aiming at an innocent's death is wrong in itself, and I hold that a line which protects nothing of hers isn't grounded in her good.

3. **Equal, unconditional dignity.** I affirm it. My whole argument depends on it, because if the hillside infant weren't wronged there'd be no problem of evil. I ground it as the Aristotelian and the godless realist do in the text's own C rows: in the nature she has whole, not in her powers. → **Holds, with a strain I admit.** Why that kind carries such worth before reason is ever used, I take as given. The text takes God as given there, and its C rows concede the shape is the same ("much as this book says").

4. **Ultimate good.** Persons flourishing in love, knowledge and achievement, free from needless suffering, in finite lives. My guiding principles are to reduce suffering, honor persons, and tell the truth. → **Breaks at the grave.** I can offer the child already spent memory and rites, not repair (Third). That's a real cost. It isn't evidence, though, since A7 forbids believing for comfort, and the text says so itself.

## 3. Debate highlights

**E1. Mackie, revived on the text's own premise.**
*Attack.* The text says God "kept Mary from every sin by grace without forcing her yes ... so He can bring a free creature to say yes without overriding her," and then says free will does not by itself answer why He permits evil "since He can, as with Mary, bring a free will to its yes." That is Mackie's premise, granted outright. A world of free creatures who all freely love was open to God, so moral evil isn't the price of freedom.
*Defense.* The text never offers moral evil as the price of freedom. It falls back on CCC 412 (with St. Leo, Christ's grace gave more than sin took away) and on the good of a life in which love can be refused (Chs. 1, 4). It calls this "a good, not a necessity," and leaves for reason the question whether it is worth this world's cost. The logical problem needs only a *possible* sufficient reason, and the redemption is offered as one.
*Rejoinder.* The logical form is answered to parity. But by putting the Thomist claim in a universal form, the text throws away the Molinist free-will defense (transworld depravity), which the Church allows. It makes the evidential burden heavier than its doctrine requires.
**Held with repair (R2, R3).**

**E2. Draper and Rowe: the pattern of pain.**
*Attack.* Pain in nature follows biological usefulness, not justice. Draper's hypothesis of indifference predicts that pattern far better than a God who loves each creature. Rowe's fawn dies slowly, unseen, and serves no good it shares in.
*Defense.* The text grants all of this ("pain in nature falls roughly where survival would put it"; "So, roughly, does conscience"; the parasite and the child). It gives a setting, not an explanation: a world on its way, the dignity of real causes (CCC 306, 310). It separates two questions. Not seeing how each evil is *repaired* is weak evidence that it isn't, since repair would come at the end. Whether a world of real causes *needs* this much pain is a question where the evidence "is not weak." For the fawn, "that hope is thinner ... and this book does not pretend otherwise."
*Rejoinder.* That's the most exact handling of skeptical theism I have seen in a book for general readers. It doesn't claim more than it has.
**Held** (as a weight honestly laid on the scale).

**E3. Stephen Law's evil god.**
*Attack.* Every theodicy has a mirror. A malevolent maker would give us real worth so that violating it is worse, knowledge of the law so that we know when we break it, and a longing to be loved wholly that he never answers. The same world is evidence for him.
*Defense.* The privation thesis: evil is always a good harmed, there can be a supreme Good but no supreme Evil (ST I q.49 a.3), and a will that lacks nothing has nothing to grasp at. The text admits this "rests on what evil is."
*Rejoinder.* That rules out a *metaphysically supreme* evil. It doesn't rule out a vast malevolent maker, and it doesn't break the symmetry of the evidence. For a reader who rejects privation, A4, W2, W1(1) and W3 all point both ways. The text concedes this for W1(1) and W3 but not for A4 and W2.
**Held with repair (R6).**

**E4. Schellenberg and Maitzen: hiddenness.**
*Attack.* A perfectly loving God ensures that no nonresistant person capable of relationship with Him is ever without belief that He exists. Belief doesn't coerce love ("Even the demons believe—and shudder," James 2:19, RSV). Yet whether one comes to know Him "depends far more on where and to whom one was born than on how honestly one sought," as the text grants. Maitzen's point is about how God would behave if He existed, not a genealogy of belief: a parent who lets his children's knowledge of him depend on geography isn't loving them equally. The book's own definition (a love that "wills to be known back") strengthens the premise, and so does 1 Timothy 2:4: God "desires all men to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth."
*Defense.* The text denies only the *now*. The choice made in this life can be made before the name is known, because the Spirit offers everyone a share in the Paschal Mystery (GS 22), and the knowing is completed at the end. God makes Himself known through persons ("a mother's love before a creed") and has entrusted us with making Him known (CCC 848). The child is loved before he can say who loves him. The text calls this "part of an answer, not a full answer."
*Rejoinder.* The child analogy fails where it matters: the child *experiences* the one who loves him, while the honest seeker in the dark experiences no one. GS 22 is a teaching of faith, not a reason, so for the reader weighing by reason alone the premise stands. The text knows this and puts hiddenness on the scale. Its denial of the *now* should say what it rests on.
**Held as a weight; Minor repair (R9).**

**E5. Adams and Talbott: hell.**
*Attack.* Here is the argument from the text's own premises.
- (P1) God wills each person for her own sake and permits nothing against her good unless the good is offered to her (W1(2), Second).
- (P2) God can bring any free creature to say yes without overriding her (the Mary sentence).
- (P3) To permit a final refusal you could have won without overriding is to permit what is against her good when you could have prevented it at no cost to her freedom.
- So no one is finally lost.

But the Church doesn't let universal salvation be taught as certain (CCC 1035; the anathemas against apokatastasis, 543, verify). Without P2's universality, the text has to choose between two options. One is certain universalism, which is excluded. The other is the Thomist account of reprobation: God shows His goodness in some by mercy and in others by justice, for the order of the whole (ST I q.23 a.5 ad 3; cf. q.23 a.7 ad 3, "those who are saved are in the minority"). That account is the structure of being spent for the whole, which P1 denies. Adams's own standard (an evil must be *defeated* within the life of the one who suffers it, which the text adopts in Second) led her to universalism for exactly this reason. And Matthew 26:24 says it would have been better for one man not to have been born.
*Defense.* Respecting a final refusal isn't spending the one who refuses. The good was offered him. The Church has never declared anyone lost, she prays that no one be lost (CCC 1058, 1821), and the text holds its claim "in that hope and under this weight." The text separates those who suffer from the one who refuses (Second), and responsibility is measured by what one knew and chose freely (CCC 1735, 1859–1860).
*Rejoinder.* That defense works if God *cannot* win every will without overriding it. The Molinist may say so (transworld damnation), and the Church has left that debate open (*de auxiliis*). The text as written says the opposite. With P2 asserted universally, respecting a refusal God could have won isn't love, and the hope that none is lost turns into a certainty the Church forbids.
**Failed as written; Held with repair (R2, R4).** Even after the repair, hell stays the heaviest weight, as the text says.

**E6. Morriston and Wielenberg: 1 Samuel 15.**
*Attack.* The text grounds the absolute line in *her* worth ("what protects her is her own worth, which no total can make it right to aim against"). Yet it holds that God could rightly command Amalek's infants killed. Either her worth doesn't protect her, and the line rests on the agent's lack of dominion rather than on her, or the command wronged them. Worse, by the text's own epistemic rule (trust what survives honest reflection until something more certain overturns it), Saul's soldier should have been surer that killing infants is wrong than that Samuel spoke for God.
*Defense.* The line binds "any of us," and no one "can under any circumstance claim *for himself* the right" (CCC 2258). God, who gives life its being and can give it back, does no one wrong in calling a life home. One who obeyed would act by God's right, not his own (ST I-II q.94 a.5 ad 2; II-II q.64 a.6 ad 1). No one may claim such a command now (CCC 67). Scripture is read by its literary forms and in the light of Christ (DV 12, 15; VD 42). And the text pays the cost openly: Kant, Mencius and the Way draw the line more absolutely, and for a reader weighing by reason alone, being surer that those infants were wronged is "a cost against the case, not only a mystery."
*Rejoinder.* The position hangs together. Her worth protects her against every agent who can't give back what he takes, and that is a principled restriction. The epistemic point is the text's own, and it concedes it.
**Held** (cost paid openly; standoff).

**E7. Ivan Karamazov.**
*Attack.* Ivan returns his ticket even if the harmony is real. He goes further and says the mother has no right to forgive the torturer for the child's sufferings even if the child herself forgives him (paraphrase; verify). His refusal is aimed at the *permitting* and at the price, not at whether the child is later made whole.
*Defense.* Only a good brought from that very evil within her own life, so that what was done to her is answered and not merely outweighed, could meet him. And the God being weighed suffered with her (Third).
*Rejoinder.* The first half answers the weighing, not Ivan, who refuses even that. The second half is Alyosha's answer, the One who can forgive everything because He gave His blood, and it does meet him, but as faith, not as a reason. The text should say which answer meets which challenge.
**Held with repair (R5).**

**E8 (decisive). From Source to Love: the ledger turned on itself.**
*Attack.* Take the text at its word.
- A4 is shared by the godless realist, the Aristotelian and the sacred order, each of which grounds worth in nature, not ability ("much as this book says").
- W2 supports a Source of law and mind, but by the text's own parenthesis Spinoza "agree[s] with this witness," and his God loves no one. So W2 doesn't deliver Love.
- W1(1) and W3 are clues that "a dark god or an indifferent world would leave ... just as we find them."
- W1(2)'s "wanted" is the hypothesis, not a datum. The datum is equal worth, which the rivals keep.

So by the text's own scoring, everything that points specifically to *Love* adds up to clues whose likelihood is about equal on both sides. Meanwhile evil, hiddenness and hell bear on exactly that attribute and, as the text grants, not on the impersonal rivals ("Such an order is free of the problem of evil only if it intends no one in particular"). The conclusion still says that reason's weighing finds the best explanation to be "a Lawgiver who is Love," and that evil "does not overturn it."
*Defense.* (a) The Source of the moral law is the Good itself, and to love is to will another's good (ST I q.20 a.2). So Love follows from W2 together with the identity of the Source with the Good, without needing separate evidence. (b) Reach: one source for law, minds and the persons the law is about, against several brute facts. (c) Newman: certainty comes from many signs converging, not from a single calculation.
*Rejoinder.* (a) is metaphysics that W2 doesn't establish. The text itself lets Spinoza accept W2 and part company only at W1 and W3. And if Love follows from the metaphysics, the evidence of evil bears on the metaphysics. (b) Reach is a real virtue, but it explains A without needing the Source to love. (c) Convergence needs independent signs pointing to *Love*, and the text has graded its Love-signs as clues that fit, not as evidence. The judgment can stand as the author's. It can't stand as what reason's ledger, as the text itself keeps it, delivers.
**Failed as written; Held with repair (R1).**

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**S1. Serious: the verdict outruns the text's own ledger on Love.** By the text's own scoring:
- A4 is shared with the nearest rivals.
- W2 delivers a Source that Spinoza can accept.
- W1(1) and W3 have likelihoods "just as we find them" under an indifferent world.
- "Wanted for her own sake" is the hypothesis, not a datum.

Evil, hiddenness and hell bear specifically on Love, and the impersonal rivals don't carry them. So "reason's weighing" can't deliver "a Lawgiver who *is Love*" with evil not overturning it. The author may keep that as his judgment, but the text has to say where the scale is thinnest. Repair: R1.

**S2. Serious: the Mary sentence, read universally, sets the central principle against hell.** (P1) willed for her own sake + (P2) God can win any free yes without overriding + (P3) permitting a refusal you could have won is not for her good. Together these entail certain universal salvation, which the Church excludes. The only other way out is Aquinas's account of reprobation (ST I q.23 a.5 ad 3), which is the being-spent-for-the-whole structure the text denies. The same sentence throws away the Molinist free-will defense. Repair: R2, R3, R4. After repair, hell remains a heavy weight at parity, not a contradiction.

**Minor**
- M1. Stephen Law's symmetry: for a reader who rejects privation, A4 and W2 also point both ways (R6).
- M2. "The good hoped for is offered to her as well" is stated categorically. For the child who dies unbaptized, who is the paradigm hillside victim, the Church can only hope (CCC 1261), as the Flourishing row correctly says (R7).
- M3. CCC 324 is placed so that it seems to teach that the good comes "within her own life." That's the book's (Adams's) standard, not the Catechism's (citation fix in R7's sentence).
- M4. Hiddenness: the denial of the *now* should say it rests on faith's teaching (R9).
- M5. "One mystery, and a mystery that explains" omits the mystery the account brings with it (R10).
- M6. "God says no to what was done to her" should keep His permission in view, which W1(1) already does. No wording change is needed if R1 stands.

**Misstatements of my school**
- Ivan: the text implies that what is offered to the child herself could answer him. He anticipates even the child's forgiveness and refuses anyway (R5).
- The conclusion's turn ("that a child left to die was wronged, or that a good God could have reasons to let her die") is put in a form a theist can answer with "both." The real question is Rowe's: are you surer that no good could justify a loving, all-powerful God in permitting *this*, or that such a God exists? (R8)
- Otherwise my school is stated accurately and at full strength: Draper's pattern, Rowe's fawn, Maitzen's distribution, Schellenberg's "now," Morriston's 1 Samuel 15 and Adams's defeat standard (used without being named).

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- The logical problem, once R2 and R3 restore the Molinist option.
- The evidential problem of natural evil, as a weight the text lays on the scale itself.
- Whether reach (unification) outweighs a strong disconfirmation of one attribute.
- Privation against the evil god, for a reader who accepts privation.
- 1 Samuel 15: the dominion principle against Kant.
- Skeptical theism limited to "repair."
- Hiddenness, once R9 makes clear that the denial rests on faith.

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1 (conclusion; S1).**
Old: "Weighing both sides, this book judges that the best explanation of love as we actually find it is a Lawgiver who is Love. Evil weighs against that conclusion, heavily; in this book's judgment it does not overturn it."
New: "Weighing both sides, this book judges that the best explanation of love as we actually find it is a Lawgiver who is Love. Reason's share in that judgment is not even. That the law, the minds that read it and the persons it is about have one Source is a judgment about where explanation may stop (W2, W1(2)); that this Source is Love, willing each of us for her own sake, rests on clues that fit and do not prove (W1(1), W3), and it is exactly there that evil and God's hiddenness weigh, heavily, on us and not on the impersonal rivals. In this book's judgment they do not overturn it; but that is where an honest reader is most likely to weigh it otherwise, and this book does not claim that reason alone tips the scale there."

**R2 (hell; S2).**
Old: "so He can bring a free creature to say yes without overriding her; why one will is won by His grace and another refuses it to the end, the Church does not explain, and she lets her theologians differ over it,"
New: "so He can bring a free creature to say yes without overriding her. Whether He could so win every will in every circumstance, and why one will is won by His grace and another refuses it to the end, the Church does not explain; she lets her theologians differ over it (the controversy *de auxiliis*, which Paul V left open in 1607, verify),"

**R3 (free will; S2, E1).**
Old: "it does not by itself answer why He permits it, since He can, as with Mary, bring a free will to its yes."
New: "it does not by itself answer why He permits it, since He can, as with Mary, bring a free will to its yes (whether He could do so for every will, the Church leaves open, above)."

**R4 (Aquinas on reprobation; S2).**
Old: "He wills the order of the whole, never the loss for its own sake (ST I q.19 a.9), and within that order He governs each person for her own sake (*Summa contra Gentiles* III.112)."
New: "He wills the order of the whole, never the loss for its own sake (ST I q.19 a.9), and within that order He governs each person for her own sake (*Summa contra Gentiles* III.112). (St. Thomas also explains why some are permitted to be lost by the showing of God's justice in the whole, ST I q.23 a.5 ad 3; this book's claim, that no one is spent for the whole, does not rest on that explanation, and for the one who refuses to the end it is held only in the hope named above.)"

**R5 (Ivan).**
Old: "and Ivan Karamazov, who would hand back his ticket even if the harmony bought with one child's tears were real, refuses the weighing itself, and only what is offered to the child herself could answer him (below)."
New: "and Ivan Karamazov, who would hand back his ticket even if the harmony bought with one child's tears were real, refuses the weighing itself, and refuses it even if the child herself should forgive; what is offered to her (below) answers the weighing, not his refusal, which faith meets only with the One who suffered and can forgive (*Third*, below)."

**R6 (evil god; M1).**
Old: "That rests on what evil is; a reader who holds that malice could be as full and real as love must weigh a dark god against the whole case."
New: "That rests on what evil is; a reader who holds that malice could be as full and real as love must weigh a dark god against the whole case, and for him much of the case cuts both ways, since a maker who wished us harm would also give us a worth to violate, a law by which to know we break it, and a longing he would not answer (Stephen Law's challenge, verify)."

**R7 (offered / CCC 324; M2, M3).**
Old: "and the good hoped for is offered to her as well."
New: "and the good hoped for is offered to her as well (for the child who dies unbaptized, the Church can only hope that it is, CCC 1261)."
Old: "only a good brought from that very evil within her own life (CCC 324), so that"
New: "only a good brought from that very evil (CCC 324), and within her own life (this book's claim), so that"

**R8 (conclusion's turn; misstatement).**
Old: "which is he surer of, that a child left to die was wronged, or that a good God could have reasons to let her die?"
New: "which is he surer of: that no good could justify a loving and all-powerful God in letting this child die so, or that such a God exists?"

**R9 (hiddenness; M4).**
Old: "this book denies only the *now*:"
New: "this book denies only the *now*, on the Church's teaching and not by any reason it can show:"

**R10 (one mystery; M5).**
Old: "It does not remove all mystery; it is one mystery, and a mystery that explains;"
New: "It does not remove all mystery; it is one mystery, and a mystery that explains, though it carries a second that it does not explain, why Love permits what lies on the other side of the scale;"

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- The case's deepest honest admission should be stated outright: *the attribute that does the most work in the case (Love willing each one for her own sake) is the attribute evil bears on most.* Saying so makes the case more credible to a reader like me, not less.
- The Mary sentence is a trade-off the author should make on purpose. Stated universally (the Thomist reading), it costs the free-will defense and sharpens hell into a dilemma. Stated with the *de auxiliis* hedge, it keeps the Molinist reply (transworld depravity and transworld damnation) open, as the Church allows.
- Second adopts Marilyn Adams's standard (defeat, not balance) without naming her. Name her: she is a Christian philosopher, and she drew universalism from exactly this standard. Showing why the book stops short of her conclusion is part of the hell section's honesty.
- 1 Timothy 2:4 joins salvation to "the knowledge of the truth." That verse is the hiddenness objection's strongest scriptural premise, and the book should face it on purpose.
- In the both-die case the line protects nothing of hers. That's where a reader like me will say the absolute is grounded in the agent's act, not in her good. The book already calls it the hardest cost; it may want to say this one sentence more.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "Not seeing how each evil is repaired is weak evidence that it is not; it is no evidence that each was needed."
- "For the fawn that hope is thinner than it is for the child, and this book does not pretend otherwise."
- "Such an order is free of the problem of evil only if it intends no one in particular."
- "A yes said to whatever happens ... is said to no one."
- "Whoever protests against God on the suffering child's behalf does not refute himself; his protest is this weight at its strongest."
- "He did not keep her from what was done to her; that weight is on the other side of the scale."
- "That is a cost against Kant, and this book pays it openly."
