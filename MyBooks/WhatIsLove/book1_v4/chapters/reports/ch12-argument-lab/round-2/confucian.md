# Argument Lab, Round 2 — Confucian opponent vs. v2

## 1. School and voice

The opponent speaks for the Confucian tradition at full strength, and this round he leads with its two most philosophical forms. The first is Neo-Confucian principle (*li* 理): Zhang Zai, the Cheng brothers, Zhu Xi. The second is Mou Zongsan's "immanent transcendence," in which the moral heart-mind is itself the creative infinite. Behind them stand the *Analects*, Mencius (the nature is good; the sprouts), Xunzi (the nature must be shaped; Heaven is the regular course of nature), the *Doctrine of the Mean* and the *Great Learning*. He grants most of what the book says about love. He denies that the Source of it is Someone.

*Accuracy notes.* *Analects* numbering follows Slingerland; Legge is often one off. Every passage is paraphrased; nothing here is a direct quotation of a modern author. The personal or impersonal character of Heaven (*Tian*) is honestly contested. It runs roughly from personal (Zhou religion, *Shangdi*, much of the *Analects*), through ambiguous (Mencius), to naturalized (Xunzi) and principle-centered (Zhu Xi). Both sides use that range below.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges (Round 2: new material only where Round 1 was thin)

**Challenge 1. Starting point for justice.**
*Answer.* Rightness (*yi*) grows from the heart's sprout of shame and disdain (*Mencius* 2A6). What is owed is fixed by the relationship. Reciprocity governs everything (12.2, 15.24). The Neo-Confucian adds the ground beneath the roles: one principle, many expressions (*li yi fen shu*, from Cheng Yi on Zhang Zai's *Western Inscription*). The same principle that makes me owe my father reverence makes me owe the stranger humaneness, in a different measure. Justice starts from a principle that is one and is fully present in every person.
*Verdict: Holds, and it is stronger than in Round 1.* "One principle, many expressions" answers the Round 1 worry about the stranger who holds no role. It does not borrow the book's terms. It assumes that the principle has authority. It does not explain that authority, and that is the C question.

**Challenge 2. A hard case, resolved.**
*Case.* An adult daughter cares for her father, who has advanced dementia and no longer knows her. Her siblings urge her to stop: he is "no longer there," and the cost is ruining her marriage.
*Answer.* Feeding a parent is not enough; even dogs and horses are fed, and without reverence what is the difference (2.7)? Serving one's parents is the greatest of services (*Mencius* 4A19). His failing to know her changes nothing she owes him, because what she owes rests on what he *is* to her, not on what he can still do. Weighing (*quan*) governs the *how*. Her marriage is also a bond she owes, so she may share the care, bring in help, or move him to good hands. She may not abandon him, and she may never end his life to end the burden. Mencius says the sages would not kill one innocent person even to gain the whole world (2A2). If he had no daughter, the duty would pass to the ruler and the community. King Wen's government cared *first* for widowers, widows, orphans and the childless (1B5).
*Verdict: Coherent, and it reaches the book's verdict without the book's words.* It does lean on reverence (*jing*) for a person whose capacities are gone. Reverence is a posture toward something sacred. That is A4 by another route, and it is evidence *for* A, not against it.

**Challenge 3. What grounds equal, unconditional dignity?**
*Answer (new, Neo-Confucian).* Zhu Xi, following Zhang Zai, separates the original nature (the nature of Heaven and Earth) from the nature as embodied in each one's endowment of *qi*. The original nature is identical in every human being, because it *is* principle, whole and undivided: every person has the whole of the Supreme Ultimate (*taiji*) within. People differ only in how clear or clouded their *qi* is. So the severely disabled child, the newborn and the man in a coma carry exactly the nature that the sage carries. Only its showing is hindered. Worth follows nature, not capacity. Late-imperial Confucian gentry acted on this: they condemned infanticide and founded foundling homes.
*Verdict: Holds for equal worth, and it is the best capacity-free ground any non-theist school has offered this lab.* It wobbles at exactly one point. What has the worth is the *nature* present in each one. When the *qi* disperses at death, principle is unharmed, and nothing in the system holds *this one*. The ground is equal and unconditional for the nature, but not *for her own sake*. See Exchange 3.

**Challenge 4. The ultimate human good.**
*Answer.* To become a sage, forming one body with Heaven, Earth and the myriad things (Wang Yangming), and forming a triad with Heaven and Earth (*Doctrine of the Mean* 22). Mou Zongsan adds that the moral heart-mind, fully realized, *is* the creative reality of the cosmos: finite, and yet able to be infinite. Death is met with serenity. Zhang Zai's *Western Inscription* ends, in paraphrase: while I live I serve and follow; when I die, I am at rest.
*Verdict: Coherent, and the noblest non-theist summit on the panel.* It stops at communion. Forming one body with all things is *union*: the self enlarged until nothing is outside it. It is not being known and loved back by Someone. The tradition's own founder still wept beyond what ritual allowed for Yan Hui (11.9–10) and said it was Heaven that knew him (14.35). Zhang Zai's rest is real peace, but it answers the fear of death, not love's refusal of death.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1: did v2 fix Round 1?**
*Audit.* Mostly yes. The sacred-order row now exists in C (it was the Round 1 omission). W1 no longer rests on "not laid by any human, so laid by Someone." A2 names shame alongside guilt. A4 separates equal worth from equal duty. W2 now claims one Source and leaves personhood to W1 and W3. Two Round 1 repairs did *not* carry over: W3 lost the clause for the relational self, and the sacred-order row was written in a form that a Confucian reader can show to be inaccurate in three places (Exchanges 2–4).
*Result: Held.* v2 is a real improvement. The remaining faults are new or are accuracy faults.

**Exchange 2: "it has no problem of evil to answer" is false for the Heaven that matters.**
*Opponent.* "Your row grants us one advantage, and you have given it to the wrong Heaven. Xunzi's Heaven, the bare course of nature, has no problem of evil. But Xunzi's Heaven is also the one that cannot ground A3 or A4, as you argued last round. The Heaven that *sees as the people see* (*Mencius* 5A5), that gave Confucius his virtue (7.23) and his mission (9.5), faces exactly your problem. Sima Qian, the great Han historian, wrote the Chinese problem of evil in his biography of Bo Yi and Shu Qi (*Shiji* 61): the good starve, Yan Hui died young, the robber Zhi lived out his years, and he asks whether the Way of Heaven is just or not. The Stoics, whom your row also lists, wrote whole treatises on why the good suffer (Seneca, *On Providence*). Your concession is inaccurate."
*Defense.* "Granted, and the correction helps the book. The rival now faces a fork. The more impersonal Heaven is, the less it owes anyone an answer for suffering, and the less it can say why *each one* matters or who forgives. The more it cares for each one, the more it carries the same weight we carry. No version of the sacred order gets both a free pass on evil and a ground for unconditional worth."
*Opponent rejoinder.* "Then we will stand with Zhu Xi: a Heaven with the heart of giving life, and no deliberate providence. That carries a lighter problem of evil and still grounds A4."
*Defense.* "That moves us to Exchange 3."
*Result: Held with repair.* The row must stop listing the Stoic Logos as simply impersonal. It must give the no-problem-of-evil advantage only to the indifferent form, and it must state the fork. See §6.

**Exchange 3: "indifferent to whether each one lives or dies" is false for Neo-Confucianism.**
*Opponent.* "Zhu Xi's essay on humaneness says the mind of Heaven and Earth is to give life to things. The Cheng brothers saw *ren* in the spring growth of every plant. That is not indifference. And every person holds the whole of principle, however clouded his *qi*. This grounds A4 without any capacity test, which is more than Kant or Scanlon could do in Round 1. Your row attacks Xunzi and labels him Confucian Heaven."
*Defense.* "The correction is fair, and the row must change. But look at what 'gives life' gives life *to*. It produces things, kinds and the endless renewal of life, and when a person's *qi* disperses at death, principle loses nothing. Each one shares the whole nature. No one is wanted *for her own sake*. The Church says that the human person is the only creature on earth that God willed for itself (*Gaudium et Spes* 24). That is the difference between being produced and being wanted. A4 says that *this* child matters without condition, not only that the nature she carries does."
*Opponent rejoinder.* "Your own Church grounds dignity in human nature too: ontological dignity belongs to every human being by being human. You also ground it in kind."
*Defense.* "In kind, *and* in being willed and loved one by one. Most days the two grounds give the same verdict. They part at two places. The first is death: does this one still matter when her *qi* is gone? The second is the singular: is she loved as herself, or as one more case of the nature? There the Neo-Confucian ground falls silent and ours speaks."
*Result: Held with repair.* Theism stays ahead on A4 against this rival, but narrowly, on "for her own sake" and "for good," not on equality or capacity. The row must say so, and must credit the rival with what it does well. See §6.

**Exchange 4: "its own great teachers reach for personal words" misdescribes the history.**
*Opponent.* "Three faults. (i) *Genealogy.* The personal words are the inherited idiom of Zhou religion, in which Heaven and *Shangdi* were worshipped as deities. Our great teachers did not *reach for* them under pressure. They inherited them and spent two thousand years refining them away: Xunzi naturalized Heaven, and Zhu Xi made it principle. The trajectory runs opposite to your claim. (ii) *Selection.* Against your few passages (3.13, 9.12, 14.35) stand the Master's silence on the Way of Heaven, which his disciples say they could not get him to discuss (5.13). He would not speak of prodigies or spirits (7.21). Heaven does not speak, yet the seasons turn (17.19). (iii) *Ambiguity.* *Ming* means decree, and it also means lot or fate. Mencius says that nothing is not *ming* (7A2). The Round 1 claim that *Tianming* is command language rests on one sense. And 3.13 has a standard naturalizing reading: if you go against Heaven, no petition to lesser powers will save you from the consequences."
*Defense.* "Some of that must be conceded. 'Reach for' implies that your teachers were driven to personal words at the gap points. That is not the history, and the *Tianming* argument from Round 1 is weak. But the inherited idiom was never fully dropped, and where it survives tells us something. Confucius used personal words for Heaven precisely when his life was in danger (7.23, 9.5), when no one understood him (14.35), and when he was accused of deceit (9.12). These are not lectures on cosmology. They are the moments when a man needs to be known. And when the most careful of your teachers was pressed directly, he did not simply say 'metaphor.' Zhu Xi said that Heaven and Earth both have a mind and do not: no deliberating, yet not without a heart. He also taught that principle itself has no intention, no deliberation, and does no making. So the accurate claim is this: where the order would have to know, forgive or love, its teachers either fall silent or speak as if it could, and the most careful of them refused to give it a mind and refused to deny it one. That is a sign of strain at exactly A2, A4 and W3."
*Opponent rejoinder.* "'Has a mind and has none' is not strain. It is precision. It refuses to anthropomorphize."
*Defense.* "Then it should be judged by what it affirms. A principle with no intention cannot be offended (3.13), cannot be deceived (9.12), and cannot know a man (14.35). If those passages mean anything, it is more than such a principle can do. If they mean nothing, A2 and W3 have no answer in your system."
*Result: Held with repair.* The C row's last sentence fails as written; it is inaccurate as history. It is repaired by stating the accurate claim: silence, or speaking *as if*, at the points of knowing, forgiving and loving, plus Zhu Xi's "has a mind and has none." See §6.

**Exchange 5: analogy cuts both ways.**
*Opponent.* "Your own Aquinas says every name we give God falls short of Him, and that we know of God more what He is not than what He is. The Thomist God is not 'a someone' among someones, a large person with moods. He is subsistent being itself. Then your 'Someone and not something' (W2, God row) is the crude contrast your own theology forbids, and the distance between Aquinas's God and Zhu Xi's Supreme Ultimate is smaller than your row pretends. Both are one, beyond all things, the source of all things, and spoken of in personal words that fall short."
*Defense.* "Analogy removes the creaturely *mode* of a perfection, never the perfection itself. Aquinas says that names like wise and good belong to God more properly than to us, as to what they signify (*ST* I q.13 a.3, a.6). He says 'person' is said of God because it signifies what is most perfect in all of nature (I q.29 a.3). God knows (q.14) and wills (q.19) more truly than we do. Zhu Xi does the opposite: he denies intention, deliberation and making to principle, and so he removes the perfection, not just the mode. Our negations make God *more* than personal. His make principle *less*. That is a real difference, and it is exactly the difference A2 and W3 need."
*Opponent rejoinder.* "Then stop saying 'Someone' as though you meant a big man."
*Defense.* "Fair. The book should say 'not less than personal' once, where it first claims personhood."
*Result: Held with repair.* The God row and W2 need the short clause. See §6.

**Exchange 6: Mou Zongsan, neither an order nor a Person.**
*Opponent.* "Your row fights Zhu Xi, whom Mou called a side branch of the tradition because his principle is static and outside the heart. The main line, from Mencius to Lu Xiangshan to Wang Yangming, says the original heart-mind *is* principle. Mou calls this a moral metaphysics: the creative reality of the cosmos and the moral heart are one, transcendent and also immanent. Mou held that the human heart has the intellectual intuition Kant denied it. This is not an impersonal order, and it is not a Person over against me. It breaks two of your arguments.
(a) W1(2) says that a worth which is ours, unearned and not conferred, is *best explained as received*. There is a fourth option: it is *ours by being the infinite*. It is neither earned nor conferred nor received; it is what the deepest self is.
(b) Mou's charge against Christianity is that its external transcendence makes man a permanent debtor who can never become what he worships. Christianity says no one can become Christ. Mencius says anyone can become Yao or Shun (6B2). Your 'received worth' is a lesser dignity than ours."
*Defense.* "To (b), first, because it misreads us. The Church teaches that the Son of God became man so that we might become partakers of the divine nature (CCC 460, citing 2 Peter 1:4 and St. Athanasius). Christianity does not say no one can be holy as God is holy. It says we become so by gift. And a received gift is not less ours. A child receives her language whole from her mother, and it is wholly hers. That is Chapter 4's point: love is given and becomes truly the beloved's own.
"To (a), the fourth option has three costs. First, if the law-giving heart is mine at its deepest, then my guilt toward the dead woman whose ashes I buried in a field is owed to my own original heart. Before my own heart I can feel shame. I cannot be pardoned by it, and she is not served by it. Second, the gap between sprout and sage is where every one of us actually lives. Mou himself granted that man is finite even though he can be infinite. Your system names the gap and leaves us in it. Third, union is not communion. To form one body with all things is to have nothing outside oneself. To be loved is to have Someone outside oneself who says, 'it is good that you exist.' Augustine kept both halves Mou wants: God is more inward than my inmost self *and* higher than my highest (*Confessions* III.6.11). Immanence, yes. But only the second half makes forgiveness and being loved back possible."
*Opponent rejoinder.* "Then we are at parity on autonomy, and you are ahead only on forgiveness and communion."
*Defense.* "Yes. That is what a converging case should admit."
*Result: Held with repair.* W1(2) must not slide from "received" to "Someone" (this is the Round 1 fault in a new form). C should give Mou's variant one sentence. See §6.

**Exchange 7: W1(1) contradicts A3, and our ancestors already answer it.**
*Opponent.* "A3 says no one else can forgive in the victim's place: not friends, not a nation, not the human race. W1(1) then says that when the victim is dead, no one on earth is left, but God can forgive. Either God forgives *in her place*, which A3 forbids, or He forgives only the wrong done to Himself, and the victim's own debt is still unforgiven. If you answer that she is not really gone, that she lives and can forgive, then you have left God out of the argument and joined us. We address the dead in the ancestral rite. Our *Book of Rites* takes the dead father's claim so seriously that a son may not live under the same sky as his killer. The dead are creditors. Your argument shows at most that guilt is owed to persons who outlast death. It does not show that one of those persons is God."
*Defense.* "The inconsistency is real, and W1(1) must be reworded. God forgives the part that is His. Every wrong against a person is also a wrong against the One who made her precious (Psalm 51:4; CCC 1850). The victim forgives her own part. But your rite shows something too. Even a tradition without a personal God addresses guilt to *persons* beyond death, not to a principle: the shape of guilt is personal all the way down. And the rite has a famous gap. It reaches the dead who have descendants. The orphan, the childless, the infant left to die have no one to sacrifice to them. Your own Ming state had to build county altars for the unattended dead. Your system knows that some debts have no one to receive them and no one to make the victim whole. The Church says there is no one whom no one remembers: 'Can a woman forget her nursing child? Even these may forget, yet I will not forget you' (Isaiah 49:15). And only a Maker can raise the murdered child and make her whole, so that she can forgive."
*Opponent rejoinder.* "That is hope, not evidence."
*Defense.* "It is fit, not proof. Guilt has the shape of a debt to persons who outlast us, and some of them are remembered by no one on earth. Only a Person above them all could see every such debt received and settled. W1(1) should claim that much and no more."
*Result: Held with repair.* As written, W1(1) is internally inconsistent with A3. This is a Serious fault, and the repair is easy. Repaired, it stands as a strong clue, ahead of the ancestral answer at the kinless and the unknown victim. See §6.

**Exchange 8: Anscombe's fork has a road between.**
*Opponent.* "W1(3) presents a fork: law with a Lawgiver, or no law and only the excellences of our kind. We are the road between. Neo-Confucians speak constantly of *Heaven's principle* (*tianli*), which binds as strictly as any law, belongs to no lawgiver, and is not the mere excellence of a species, since it is the principle of every thing. We are also the counter-case to Anscombe's prediction. Our tradition never had the juridical 'ought,' and its moral life did not decay into confusion; it lasted two thousand years."
*Defense.* "The road between is real, and it is exactly the sacred-order row in C. So W1(3) is not an independent reason. It hands the question to C, where the question is whether a pattern can hold a debt. Notice also that Xunzi, your most law-minded teacher, *supports* the first horn: rites and rightness, he says, were made by the sage-kings. That is law with lawgivers, and human ones. Then A3 bites: what sages made, later sages could remake. Xunzi answers by saying the core of the Way did not change across all the former kings (ch. 17). He is grounding the rites in something no king made."
*Result: Held with repair.* W1(3) must stop presenting the fork as exhaustive. See §6.

**Exchange 9: the hope argument and the gatekeeper.**
*Opponent.* "Your evil paragraph ends by saying that the account without God removes hope for evil's victims. A gatekeeper once described our Master as the man who knows a thing cannot be done and does it anyway (14.38). We do good without needing hope of reward. Mencius put rightness above profit from his first page (1A1). The Ming critics of Ricci charged that Christian ethics runs on heaven and hell, and so is mercenary."
*Defense.* "The book agrees about motive. Perfect contrition comes from love of God for His own sake, not fear of punishment (CCC 1452). The hope in that paragraph is not the doer's wage. It is hope *for the victim*, and no gatekeeper's resolve can raise Yan Hui. v2 already says this is not evidence."
*Result: Held.* No repair needed. The tradition's 14.38 is a fine line to honor in the chapter.

**Exchange 10: "one stopping point instead of many."**
*Opponent.* "The God row claims simplicity: one stopping point instead of many. That beats the realist's many brute truths. It does not beat us. Principle is one; that is our first word."
*Defense.* "Granted. Against the sacred order, the claim is not one against many. It is one that knows and loves against one that does not."
*Result: Held with repair.* See §6.

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**Against v2 as written:**
1. **W1(1) is inconsistent with A3.** God either forgives in the dead victim's place, which A3 forbids, or the victim's own debt is left unforgiven. If the victim survives to forgive, the argument does not separate God from the ancestors. — **Serious.**
2. **The impersonal-sacred-order row is inaccurate in three places**: "no problem of evil" (false for Mencian Heaven and the Stoics), "indifferent" (false for Neo-Confucianism), and "reach for personal words" (it reverses the tradition's history). A Confucian or Stoic reader would dismiss the row, and with it the comparison. — **Serious.**
3. **W1(2)'s "received" slides to "Someone."** This is the Round 1 "laid by" gap in new form. Confucians say the nature is received from Heaven, and Mou offers a fourth option: ours by being the infinite. — **Serious.**
4. **C omits Mou Zongsan's immanent transcendence**, the leading modern Confucian position, which is neither an order nor a Person. — **Minor** (the Kant row's "cannot forgive" and W3's union-is-not-communion answer it; it needs one sentence).
5. **W1(3) presents Anscombe's fork as exhaustive.** — **Minor.**
6. **The God row's "one stopping point instead of many"** fails against "principle is one." — **Minor.**
7. **A4's citation placement** implies the Catechism discusses infant exposure; "it is good that you exist" is uncredited (Pieper). — **Minor.**

**After the repairs in §6:** NONE that our side cannot answer at least to parity. The honest balance against the strongest Confucian (Zhu Xi or Mou):
- **Level:** A1, A3, A5, A6, A7, W2, and equal worth and freedom from capacity tests in A4.
- **Theism ahead:** A2 and W1(1), on forgiveness and the kinless dead. A4, on "for her own sake" and "for good." W3, on communion against union.
- **Sacred order ahead:** evil, but only in its indifferent form, and that form pays for the advantage at A4. Mou's autonomy objection stands at parity, answered by Chapter 4's logic of gift and by CCC 460.
- **At parity, not relied on:** Ricci's causal principle (whatever lacks understanding cannot give rise to beings that understand). Emergence theorists contest it, and the case should not rest on it.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**A4, last sentence:**
> Reason can see this (DI 1), yet history shows how easily it is lost (CCC 37–38): the same ancient world that taught that slaves are men also left weak and deformed infants to die.

**W1(1):**
> (1) *Forgiveness.* A wronged person needs forgiving, and only she can forgive the wrong done to her; no one can do it in her place (A3). So when she is dead, or never knew, the debt remains, and no one on earth can lift it. If the dead are simply gone, it can never be lifted. Peoples all over the world have refused to believe that, and go on speaking to their dead. Yet many of the dead have no one left to speak to them, and no one on earth can make the murdered child whole. The Church says that every wrong against a person is also a wrong against the One who made her precious (Psalm 51:4; CCC 1850), and that part only He can forgive. And He forgets no one (Isaiah 49:15): He can raise her and make her whole, so that she herself can forgive. This does not prove that the dead live. It shows that guilt has the shape of a debt to persons who outlast us, and that only a Person above them all could see every such debt received and settled. A truth can be true; only a person can forgive.

**W1(2):**
> (2) *A standing that is ours but not ours to cancel.* The victim's claim is truly his own. Yet he did not make it, cannot sign it away, and it belongs as fully to the newborn who cannot speak as to the man who can. No one earned it and no one on earth conferred it. Wise traditions say it was received with our nature. But a law or an order gives a nature to every member of a kind; it does not want any one of them. The worth in A4 is the worth of *this* child, for her own sake. That is best explained if she was wanted for her own sake, and only someone can want. The Church says the human person is the only creature on earth God willed for itself (Gaudium et Spes 24).

**W1(3):**
> (3) *Law or no law.* The Catholic philosopher Elizabeth Anscombe argued that if morality is a law, it has a Lawgiver; and if there is no Lawgiver, we should stop calling it law and keep only the excellences of our kind. Some great traditions walk a road between: a sacred pattern that binds like law and has no lawgiver (see C). But if A2 and A3 are real (a debt still owed when the one we wronged is gone), morality has the shape of law, and the question becomes whether a pattern can be owed a debt. C weighs that.

**W2, last sentence:**
> W1 and W3 show why that Source is Someone and not something: not less than personal, because knowing and loving are in Him more truly than in us.

**W3, after "not even ourselves":**
> Even if we are made of our relationships, the longing remains: to be received whole, across every role and every year, by someone who will not die.

**W3, the Pieper line:**
> When grasping love is purified, what remains is the love that says to another, in Josef Pieper's words, "it is good that you exist."

**C, replace the "impersonal sacred order" row:**
> - **An impersonal sacred order** (Heaven as Xunzi and Zhu Xi understood it; karma) is the strongest rival a book about love can face. It explains a great deal (A1, A3, A5, A6, W2). In its Neo-Confucian form it even says that every person, however clouded his body or mind, carries the whole of Heaven's principle, so that worth follows nature, not capacity (A4). Its sages say the heart of Heaven and Earth is to give life. But it gives life to kinds and lets each one go. It can say that each one shares the nature, not that *this* one is wanted for her own sake (A4). An order cannot be wronged or forgive (A2), and it cannot know or love anyone (W3). Its most careful teacher, Zhu Xi, said as much: principle has no intention and does no deliberating, and Heaven both has a mind and has none. Yet the older teachers, in danger and at the edge of grief, spoke of a Heaven that knows them and can be offended (Analects 14.35, 3.13). The modern Confucian Mou Zongsan places the infinite inside the moral heart itself; then the law is mine at my deepest, but no one is there to forgive me or love me back. Such an order has no problem of evil only if it cares for no one in particular. The more it cares, the more it faces our question: the historian Sima Qian, watching good men starve and robbers live long, asked whether the Way of Heaven is just. (The Stoic Logos sits nearer a Person: it was prayed to as Providence, and the Stoics, like us, had to answer for evil.)

**C, replace the "A God who is Love" row:**
> - **A God who is Love** is also a stopping point. Against the realist it is one stopping point instead of many. Against the sacred order, which is also one, it is the one that knows and loves. It explains A1–A7 together, and it is Someone (not less than personal), which is what the shape of guilt, forgiveness and communion suggests.

## 7. New insights for the book

1. **The Confucian tradition independently confirms A6's hardest clause.** Mencius says the sages would not commit one wrong or kill one innocent person even to gain the whole world (2A2). That is the surgeon case, two thousand years early, and it belongs in Ch. 9 or Ch. 12 as evidence that Romans 2:14–15 holds.
2. **The book's closing move is Mencius's move.** "Look at the newborn in front of you" is what Mencius did with the child about to fall into a well (2A6). He did not argue; he asked the hearer to look. The chapter can say that the oldest moral teachers outside Israel used the same test.
3. **Being produced vs. being wanted.** This is the cleanest way to state the difference between a life-giving order and a loving God: "Heaven and Earth give life to things. God wills each one for its own sake" (GS 24). It fits Ch. 4 (gift) and Ch. 10 (communion).
4. **Union is not communion.** The highest non-theist summit (forming one body with all things) enlarges the self until nothing is outside it. Love needs Someone outside oneself. Augustine's "more inward than my inmost, higher than my highest" holds both halves. Ch. 10.
5. **The kinless dead.** The ancestral rite shows that guilt and love are addressed to persons beyond death even without a personal God. It also exposes the gap: the orphan and the abandoned infant have no one to remember them. Isaiah 49:15 answers that gap exactly. Ch. 12 or Ch. 8 (mercy).
6. **Received worth is not lesser worth.** Against Mou's charge that Christian dignity is borrowed: CCC 460 (partakers of the divine nature), and a child's language, which is received whole and is wholly hers. Ch. 4.
7. **Sincerity as the Way of Heaven** (*Mean* 20; *Great Learning* 6) is a non-Christian witness for A7: being true when no one would ever know.
8. **For Further Study:** Matteo Ricci, *The True Meaning of the Lord of Heaven* (1603). Its Chinese critics (*Poxie ji*, 1639) are useful to know: their charges (anthropomorphism, mercenary motive) are the ones this chapter now answers.
9. **Chapter-text flags still open from Round 1:** "every attempt ... collapses" (the Confucian tradition stopped short; it did not collapse); the Atheist's Dilemma still omits the sacred-order option; "laws require lawgivers," stated bare, meets *Analects* 17.19.

**Lines worth keeping (short, plain, warm):**
- "Heaven and Earth give life to things. God wanted *you*."
- "Union is not communion. To be loved, there must be Someone besides you."
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."
- "Many of the dead have no one left to speak to them. God forgets no one."
- "A received gift is not less yours. A child's language is her mother's gift, and wholly hers."
- "The more Heaven cares for each one, the more it has to answer for each one's suffering."
