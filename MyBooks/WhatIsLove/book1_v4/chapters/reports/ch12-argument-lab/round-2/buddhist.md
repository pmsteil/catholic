# Argument Lab, Round 2: Buddhist opponent vs v2

## 1. School and voice

The same composite voice as Round 1: the Theravada Pali Canon and its commentaries (Buddhaghosa), and Indian Madhyamaka (Nagarjuna, Candrakirti, Santideva). This voice holds that there is no lasting self (anatta), that craving (tanha) rooted in ignorance causes suffering, that karma is intention ripening by its own order with no one appointing it, that boundless kindness and compassion are what the awakened mind is naturally like, and that nirvana, the unconditioned, is the highest good. In Round 2 it argues against v2 as it now stands, checks whether v2 took in Round 1's findings, and presses on new ground. The Church "rejects nothing that is true and holy" in it (Nostra Aetate 2), and this voice is argued in that spirit.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

These are fresh answers, not repeats of Round 1.

**1. Starting point for justice.**
*Their answer:* Justice starts from non-harm, and Buddhism frames non-harm as a *gift*. The Buddha calls the five precepts "great gifts" (AN 8.39): whoever refrains from killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, lying and intoxication gives countless beings freedom from danger, hostility and oppression, and has a share in that freedom himself. Every being is owed that gift, because every being trembles at the rod and holds life dear (Dhp 129–130). A king is owed obedience only while he looks after the poor (DN 26).
*Verdict:* **Holds, and comes strikingly close to the book.** Justice as a gift given to all beings, never earned by them, sits close to Chapter 4's "gift of covenant." The weak point is the one Round 1 found, sharper now. "What I owe you" becomes "what I give you." The giver stays pure, and the receiver never holds a *claim* he could press. The victim's standing to demand ("you owed me this") has become the doer's generosity. That matters, because A2's whole force is that the victim was *wronged*, not merely left without a gift.

**2. A hard dilemma: the dying patient who asks to be killed.**
*Their answer:* A man dying in great pain asks his doctor for a lethal injection. Theravada says no. The third *parajika* rule of the monastic code (Vinaya, Parajika 3) arose when monks, disgusted with the body, sought death and found someone to kill them. The Buddha made it a gravest offense to take a human life, to supply the means, or even to *praise* death to someone. Pain relief is right even if it may shorten life, as long as the intention is to relieve pain and not to kill. The Abhidhamma reason: an intention to end a life arises with aversion, here aversion to the pain or to the condition, so "compassionate killing" is not compassion. What is owed instead is care, presence, and help toward a calm and clear mind at death. (Damien Keown's work on Buddhist bioethics argues this at length.)
*Verdict:* **Holds, does not borrow, and reaches the Church's verdict** (CCC 2277–2279, including the line between intending death and accepting it as a foreseen side effect). The ground is the analysis of intention, not any Lawgiver. One reason it gives, the state of mind at death because of rebirth, belongs to Buddhist doctrine alone. The main reason does not.

**3. Ground of equal, unconditional dignity.**
*Their answer:* There is no being, the Buddha says, who has not at some time been your mother, father, brother or sister in the beginningless round (SN 15.14–19). That reaches the enemy at once. The stream of mind runs on in the newborn, the comatose and the severely disabled, so none of them falls outside compassion for lack of ability. The mind is by nature luminous; its defilements are visitors (AN 1.51–52). Mahayana adds buddha-nature in every being.
*Verdict:* **Holds better than most secular schools. Bends in three places, does not break.** Credit first: this ground reaches all four test cases (newborn, dying, disabled, enemy) without any test of reasoning ability, which Kant, Scanlon and Singer cannot do. The three bends: (i) it is equal *compassion* toward all sentient beings, not the equal dignity of *persons*; (ii) under karma the present birth, with its abilities and limits, is the fruit of past deeds (MN 135), so worth is not "prior to any deed"; (iii) anatta leaves no one who keeps that worth *as himself* from one life to the next. The luminous mind and buddha-nature are the nearest thing to the book's "worth no deed confers," and that resemblance helps W1(2) (Exchange 4).

**4. Ultimate good and guiding principles.**
*Their answer:* Theravada: nirvana, the unborn, unbecome, unmade, unconditioned, without which there would be no escape from what is conditioned (Ud 8.3). It is not annihilation. The Buddha refused to say the liberated one "does not exist" after death, as he refused to say he exists; he is deep and hard to fathom, like the ocean (MN 72). Madhyamaka: emptiness. At the final level samsara and nirvana are not two (MMK 25.19–20). The guides are the Eightfold Path, the four boundless states (kindness, compassion, joy in another's good, equanimity), and the perfections, truthfulness among them.
*Verdict:* **Holds; coherent.** Two notes the book needs. (a) Theravada has its own *unconditioned* stopping point. It is not "dependence all the way down"; that is Madhyamaka. So C must not describe the Buddhist ultimate as karma. Karma belongs to the round one is escaping. (b) The tension Round 1 found still stands: the road is love of beings, and the goal is beyond persons.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1. W1's fork again: is the debt owed, or does it ripen?**
*Buddhist:* W1 says there are "two ways": a moral law that belongs to no one (necessary truths, a standing persons have, the shape of our kind) or a Good who is Someone. Karma is still not on the first list. It is not a necessary truth, not a standing, not a nature; it is an *order of cause and fruit*. Look at what that does to W1(3). You argue that if there is "a debt still owed when the one we wronged is gone," morality is a law, and so has a Lawgiver. But a debt that outlives the victim is exactly what karma is. The deed ripens in the doer whether or not the victim lives, or whether he forgives (Dhp 165). Our tradition calls it a law (*dhamma niyama*, the fixed order of things, which stands whether or not Buddhas arise: SN 12.20), and it has no giver. Anscombe's fork, "law, so a Lawgiver, or no law," is the old gap "laws require lawgivers" with a famous name attached. Anscombe herself argued only that the *juridical* ought makes sense inside belief in divine law. She did not argue from the ought to the Lawgiver.
*Patrick's side:* Then we have to say what kind of debt it is. A ripening debt is owed to no one. It is a seed. Our guilt does not feel like a seed. It feels *owed*: I did this to *her*. Your own tradition shows that shape. Ajatasattu goes to a person, and is accepted by a person (DN 2).
*Buddhist rejoinder:* We also know the doer's remorse (*vippatisara*). The Buddha says the whole purpose of good conduct is freedom from remorse (AN 10.1). Remorse is healed by purity, confession and restraint, not by pardon from above.
*Verdict:* **W1 framing and W1(3) fail as stated.** Neither the persistence of the debt nor the word "law" separates theism from karma. **Held with repair.** W1 must name karma among the impersonal options. W1(3) must move from "a debt persists" to "a debt is owed *to* someone." Then the reader can weigh whether guilt is owed to someone or only ripens. That is the real difference, and on the plain experience of guilt it leans our way (Repair W1-intro, W1(3)).

**Exchange 2. The dead victim is not gone.**
*Buddhist:* W1(1) says that when the one we wronged is dead, "no one on earth is left who can" forgive, and God steps in. On our view the victim is not gone. Her stream of mind goes on. Enmity and reconciliation reach across lives. The commentary on Dhammapada 5 ("hatred is never ended by hatred") tells of two women whose feud ran through several births until the Buddha ended it. Meanwhile the wrongdoer is not helpless. He can dedicate merit to the departed (the Tirokudda discourse, Khp 7), and turn his life to kindness toward her wherever she is. So "no one is left" is your premise, not a datum. And your answer, that God "can raise the victim and make her whole," is the resurrection. It is an article of faith. A witness that is meant to be "reason's witness" (CCC 35) cannot use it to beat its rivals.
*Patrick's side:* Agreed: neither rebirth nor resurrection is something we can see, and W1(1) should not lean on either. But look at what people do, Buddhists included. The Tirokudda offerings go to the dead *as persons*. In Japan, many Buddhist families hold memorial rites for children lost before birth, and those rites often include an apology to the child. The datum is not a doctrine of the afterlife. It is that guilt goes on speaking to the person wronged, even when that person cannot be seen. An order of cause and fruit can purify the doer or let the deed ripen. It cannot be the one he speaks to.
*Buddhist rejoinder:* Merit-dedication is a gift, not a request for pardon. And the Japanese rites are a late practice, much debated even inside Japanese Buddhism.
*Verdict:* **W1(1) fails as stated** (a revealed premise, and a contested "no one is left"). **Held with repair.** Argue from the shape of guilt (it is addressed to someone) and drop the resurrection as a premise. The Japanese example is supporting evidence, not something the case rests on; it should be checked against scholarship before use (William LaFleur's study of these rites is the standard one).

**Exchange 3. A3 against W1(1): who may forgive in the victim's place?**
*Buddhist:* This is new, and it is internal to v2. A3 says: "No one else can forgive in his place: not his friends, not a nation by vote, not the whole human race." W1(1) then says God can forgive, "without wronging the victim again." Which is it? If God forgives the wrong done to the *victim*, then someone other than the victim forgives in his place, and A3 is broken. If God forgives only the wrong done *to Himself* (Ps 51:4), then the victim's part is still unforgiven, and the theist is exactly where we are: the debt to the victim waits on the victim, beyond this life.
*Patrick's side:* The second. And it is Catholic teaching, not a retreat. Sin wounds both God and neighbor. Forgiveness of the offense against God does not remove what is owed to the neighbor, which calls for amends (CCC 1459). God does not forgive in her place. What theism adds is not a substitute forgiver. It is that the victim herself is real enough to be owed and, in the end, to forgive, and that the One who made her precious will not let her be lost. That is the hope; it is not the premise.
*Buddhist rejoinder:* Then W1(1)'s whole weight rests on one question: are persons real enough to be owed and to forgive? That is anatta against the person. It is not "forgiveness against no forgiveness."
*Verdict:* **Held with repair, and the repair is needed for consistency.** As written, A3 and W1(1) contradict each other. Repaired, they agree: no one forgives in the victim's place, God forgives what was done against Him, and the case for a Person rests on the reality of persons. That ties W1(1) to W1(2) and A4, which is where it belongs (Repair W1(1)).

**Exchange 4. W1(2): "received" from Someone, or from conditions?**
*Buddhist:* W1(2) says a worth "that we did not earn and no one conferred is best explained as received." Received is our word too. On dependent origination, *everything* is received: from causes and conditions, with no one giving it. And under karma the worth *was* earned: a human birth is the rare fruit of past good deeds (MN 129, the turtle and the yoke). So "received" does not separate us, and "did not earn" we deny.
*Patrick's side:* Two answers. First, causes and conditions explain what a thing *is*; they do not explain what it is *owed*. That is the book's own A5: describing what we are never by itself yields an ought. A standing that binds everyone else is not one more condition among conditions. Second, grant karma, and see where it leaves the newborn. An order of deeds and their fruits, however exact, can only return to each what each has done. It cannot say why someone matters *before* he has done anything. Yet you say the mind is luminous by nature, its stains only visitors (AN 1.51), and Mahayana speaks of buddha-nature, which no deed creates and no deed destroys. Something in your own tradition reaches for a worth that comes before every deed.
*Buddhist rejoinder:* The luminous mind is a ground of *possibility*, not a worth owned by a self. Candrakirti would call buddha-nature a teaching for those who fear emptiness.
*Verdict:* **Held with repair.** "Received" is ambiguous and should go. The point that survives is a worth *prior to any deed* and *owed by everyone*. Karma cannot explain that by its structure. Conditions explain facts, not claims. The Buddhist rejoinder holds to parity at the level of Madhyamaka metaphysics, not beyond (Repair W1(2)).

**Exchange 5. C's line on "an impersonal sacred order": does it describe karma truly?**
*Buddhist:* The line puts karma beside Confucian Heaven and the Stoic Logos, and every clause of it misses us.
(a) *"Sacred."* Karma is not sacred to us. It is the machinery of the round we seek to leave. Our ultimate is nirvana, or emptiness, not karma.
(b) *"Has no problem of evil to answer."* Karma *is* an answer to the problem of evil. Max Weber called it the most consistent answer ever devised. It explains the child's cancer and even the fawn, since animal birth is itself the fruit of deeds. On the scale of evil, karma explains more than your own C does. Your C admits it cannot explain the fawn.
(c) *"An order indifferent to whether each one lives or dies."* Karma is the opposite of indifferent. It weighs every intention exactly.
(d) *"Its own great teachers reach for personal words."* Not for karma. When our teachers speak in personal terms, they speak of the Buddha and the bodhisattvas, who are persons who love but did not create, never of karma.
*Patrick's side:* Each correction is fair, and the book should make them. But (b) and (c) cut both ways. Take (c): if karma weighs every intention and returns a fitting result, across lives, into bodies and worlds, it is a law that reads hearts. Gravity does not read intentions. A law that sorts by the heart and returns what fits has the shape of judgment, and judgment is the work of a mind. That was Round 1's strongest point against traditional karma, and v2 dropped it. Take (b): karma explains the child's cancer by what *she* did before. The Buddha himself refused to say that everything a person feels comes from past deeds. He named bile, phlegm, wind, the seasons, careless conduct and assault alongside karma (SN 36.21). So even your canon will not let karma account for every pain. And where it does account for one, it pays with A4: the child's suffering becomes her desert.
*Buddhist (Madhyamaka) rejoinder:* "A law that reads hearts" assumes the heart and the law are two. Mind conditions mind; the reading *is* the ripening, with no reader. And karma is empty too, a conventional truth. The real stopping point is emptiness, not a judge.
*Verdict:* **C's line fails as written for this school.** It is inaccurate on all four counts. A Buddhist reader would stop trusting the comparison at that line. **Held with repair.** Give the Buddha's Dhamma its own line. Describe karma accurately, as the most moral impersonal law ever proposed and an answer to evil that has a price. Restore the heart-reading argument. Name the Buddhist ultimate (the unconditioned, or emptiness). Leave Confucian Heaven and the Logos to their own line (Repair C-Dhamma). Against Madhyamaka, the stand-off over stopping points holds at parity, as in Round 1.

**Exchange 6. W3: what remains when grasping love is purified?**
*Buddhist:* v2 says that when grasping love is purified, "what remains is the love that says to another, 'it is good that you exist.'" Our tradition has spent twenty-five centuries purifying love, and that is not all that remains. Four boundless states remain, and the fourth is equanimity (*upekkha*): the even mind that sees each being as heir to its own deeds and lets each go. Your list of what is left leaves out the one state that answers your grief. Then W3 still reasons from the craving: "known and loved wholly and *for good*," "can *hold us forever*." That is the craving for continued existence (*bhava-tanha*), kept in your premise one sentence before you say craving is purified. The arrow discourse (SN 36.6) calls grief the second arrow, the one we shoot into ourselves. And your closing line, "if the highest good is no one," misdescribes nirvana. The Buddha refused to call the liberated one nothing (MN 72). Finally, purified love seeks no answer. Kindness that needs to be "answered" is still trading.
*Patrick's side:* Equanimity is ours too. Ignatius asks for an indifference that holds all created things with open hands, and John of the Cross asks for detachment from everything. But Christian equanimity rests on trust that the beloved is held. It does not rest on the beloved being empty. So equanimity does not decide between us; it is compatible with both. You are right that purified love seeks no reward. Charity gives without counting. But friendship is *mutual* by its nature (ST II-II q.23 a.1), and your Buddha called good friendship the whole of the holy life (SN 45.2). The question is not whether love needs to be paid. It is whether what love affirms, *this one, good, really there*, is true at the ground of things or only conventionally true. That is the fork, and it is an honest one. We accept that "no one" was the wrong word. Nirvana does not *know or love* anyone. That is all the argument needs, and it is accurate.
*Buddhist rejoinder:* At the final level no being is led to nirvana (the Diamond Sutra). The love is real; its object is empty.
*Verdict:* **W3 as worded: held with repair, at parity.** Take the craving-form sentence out of the premise or reframe it. Name equanimity honestly. Replace "is no one" with "knows and loves no one." The fork that remains, whether persons are real at the ground of things, is answered to parity and no further (Repair W3).

**Exchange 7. Hiddenness: the contemplatives who looked and found peace, not a Person.**
*Buddhist:* v2 faces hiddenness with "honest seekers look for God and do not find Him." Our case is stronger and different. For two and a half thousand years, monks and nuns have spent whole lives in disciplined inward search. It is perhaps the most careful introspective inquiry in history. They report the unconditioned, peace, the deathless, and the absence of a self. They do not report a Person at the ground. That is not "failing to find"; it is finding something else. And W3's answer, that such a person "is already following a light without yet knowing its name," names our light for us. It is kind, and it assumes what it is arguing.
*Patrick's side:* We owe that testimony respect, and the book should put it on the scale, not around it. Three things weigh against reading it as evidence against God. First, what the contemplatives report, an unborn, unmade, unconditioned (Ud 8.3) and a peace beyond grasping, does not contradict God as classical theism understands Him: not a thing among things, known in the end by unknowing. Our own mystics speak of a cloud, a darkness, a *nada* on the way to Him. Second, contemplative reports are shaped by the framework the seeker brings, on both sides; Christian contemplatives, as disciplined, report a Person. Third, the "light without a name" line is not a premise of the argument. It is the Church's hope for those outside it (LG 16), stated as hope.
*Verdict:* **Held to parity with repair.** The testimony belongs on the other side of the scale, by name and with honor. The hope in LG 16 should be marked as hope, not as a reading of what Buddhists experience (Repair Evil-scale insert).

**Exchange 8. The evil paragraph: "the account without God removes hope for evil's victims."**
*Buddhist:* This sentence is simply false about us. The whole teaching is hope for those who suffer: the third noble truth says suffering can end. Mahayana vows that *every* being will be freed. And "evil is always a good thing harmed" does not fit what we see. Aging, sickness and death are not accidents that happen to good things. They are the nature of everything conditioned. Those were the Buddha's first sights.
*Patrick's side:* On hope, you are right, and the line must change. But look at what each hope is. Yours is the hope that suffering ends, by each stream's own long work, with no self who comes through it. Ours is the hope that *this child, herself*, is raised and made whole and her tears wiped away (Rev 21:4). Only an account with a living God who raises the dead can hope for that. On impermanence: change and passing away are not evil. The Church teaches that God made a world "in a state of journeying" toward its final perfection, where building and destroying forces of nature exist together (CCC 310). Suffering in such a world is real, and its weight is admitted. But impermanence is the condition of a world still on its way, not proof that the world is bad.
*Verdict:* **Fails as worded on "removes hope"** (a factual overclaim against a major tradition without God). **Held with repair** once the hope is stated precisely and CCC 310 is added. The privation claim holds.

## 4. Ledger for v2 (every numbered item)

- **A heading ("what almost everyone finds they cannot give up").** **Holds.** Round 1's overclaim is fixed.
- **A1. Some things are really wrong, for everyone.** **Holds.** Buddhism affirms it fully. The precepts bind gods and humans alike, so "not merely wrong for creatures shaped like us" is shared. It does no work against this rival, and it need not.
- **A2. Guilty, not merely mistaken or flawed (the ashes case).** **Holds with repair.** The datum holds. Buddhism also calls the act unwholesome, not a mere error. But the ashes case is equally explained by intention (greed and deceit), so it shows the wrong is real, not that a person is wronged. The last two sentences overclaim. "We go back to that person for pardon" is not the central Buddhist remedy (MN 61: confess to the Teacher or wise companions, and restrain in future), and "every serious moral tradition knows this" claims too much. See Repair A2.
- **A3. No one on earth can make the wrong right; no one forgives in the victim's place.** **Holds.** Buddhism agrees outright (Dhp 165). But as written it contradicts W1(1) (Exchange 3), and the repair must be made in W1(1).
- **A4. Equal dignity, whatever he can or cannot do.** **Holds.** Buddhism affirms equal compassion that reaches all four test cases, and its Vinaya rule against praising death fits "not signed away when life grows hard." The difference with this school (persons, prior to any deed, not lost) is argued in W1(2), which is the right place.
- **A5. What we are able to do does not settle what we ought to do.** **Holds with minor repair.** Buddhism agrees: craving is also an inclination "we did not give ourselves," and it is beginningless. So say which inclination: the inclination to our proper end (ST I-II q.91 a.2; q.94 a.2), not just any inclination.
- **A6. Real good asks something of the giver; some things must never be done (the surgeon case).** **Holds.** Generosity (*dana*) and the simile of the saw (MN 21). The Mahayana ship's captain is not a counterexample: he kills a would-be murderer to prevent mass murder, not an innocent for his organs.
- **A7. We are bound to the truth.** **Holds, strongly.** Truthfulness is one of the perfections. The Dhammapada says there is no evil that a man who lies, and scorns the world beyond, would not do (Dhp 176). The Kalama discourse (AN 3.65) makes testing by evidence a duty.
- **W1, opening ("two ways"; the wrong is necessary; three things favor a Person).** **Holds with repair.** Karma must be on the list of impersonal options, and v2 should say plainly that the release test does not decide between them.
- **W1(1) Forgiveness.** **Fails as stated.** It contradicts A3, uses revealed premises (Ps 51:4 and CCC 1850 as premises, resurrection as the deciding fact), and asserts "no one is left," which rebirth denies. **Holds with repair:** argue from the addressed shape of guilt; God forgives what was done against Him and does not forgive in the victim's place; resurrection is the hope, not the premise.
- **W1(2) A standing ours but not ours to cancel.** **Holds with repair.** "Received" is ambiguous (dependent origination: everything is received from conditions) and "did not earn" is denied under karma. Rest it on a worth *prior to any deed* and *owed by everyone*. An order of deeds cannot explain that, and conditions explain facts, not claims.
- **W1(3) Law or no law (Anscombe).** **Fails as stated against this school.** A debt that outlives the victim is just what karma is, and "law" does not imply a giver in the Buddhist sense of the word. **Holds with repair:** "owed to someone, or only ripening?"
- **W1, closing (not a commander; the Creator's authority).** **Holds.** It answers Round 1's Exchange 3 (a creator who determines everything) and keeps clear of voluntarism.
- **W2. The law and the mind that reads it.** **Holds.** It is modest now and hands personhood to W1 and W3. The Buddhist reply (mind is conditioned by the order it reads; ignorance is our first condition, not wisdom) holds only to parity, and the book has its own account of why truth is hard for us (CCC 37). Minor repair: "never require us to do evil (1 Corinthians 10:13)" uses Scripture as evidence. Keep it as what Christians hold on God's promise.
- **W3, first paragraph (communion; purified love).** **Holds with repair, at parity.** It still reasons from the craving form ("hold us forever"), it leaves out equanimity, and "is no one" misdescribes nirvana.
- **W3, second paragraph (hiddenness; LG 16).** **Holds with repair.** The contemplative testimony needs a place on the scale, and the "light without a name" should read as hope, not as a reading of other traditions' experience.
- **C, opening (every account stops somewhere; accommodate vs explain).** **Holds.**
- **C: Nothing really binds.** **Holds.** Not at issue for this school.
- **C: Feeling, refined.** **Holds.** Not at issue.
- **C: Reason giving itself the law.** **Holds.** Not at issue.
- **C: What we owe each other.** **Holds.** Not at issue.
- **C: Well-being, counted impartially.** **Holds.** Not at issue. (Santideva's impartiality is not aggregation: he asks the self to bear the cost, and does not trade others.)
- **C: The flourishing of our kind.** **Holds.** Not at issue.
- **C: Necessary truths that belong to no one.** **Holds.** One note: "why worth follows kind rather than capacity" has a third answer, sentience, which the Buddhist gives. That answer belongs on the Buddhist line, not here.
- **C: An impersonal sacred order (Confucian Heaven, the Stoic Logos, karma).** **Fails as written for karma.** "Sacred," "no problem of evil," "indifferent" and "teachers reach for personal words" are all inaccurate for karma, and the heart-reading argument is missing. **Holds with repair:** take karma out of this line and give the Buddha's Dhamma its own (Repair C-Dhamma).
- **C: Power and rank.** **Holds.** Not at issue.
- **C: A God who is Love.** **Holds with repair.** "One stopping point instead of many" answers the realist, not the Buddhist. Name the Buddhist stopping point and say where the two accounts part.
- **Evil and hiddenness ("on the other side of the scale").** **Holds with repair.** It is honest and heavy, as it should be. "Removes hope for evil's victims" is false against Buddhism. Add CCC 310 for suffering in a world still on its way. Add the contemplatives. Minor: "not seeing a reason" understates the other side, since we *see* the suffering. What we do not see is God's reason.
- **Conclusion.** **Holds.** The modesty is right, and it is what lets the case stand against this school. The closing appeal to the newborn does not touch the Buddhist, who does not give up A4.

## 5. Remaining sound objections

Against v2 **as written**:

1. **A3 and W1(1) contradict each other.** One says no one forgives in the victim's place; the other has God do it. **Serious.** Answered by Repair W1(1).
2. **W1(1) decides the case with revealed premises** (resurrection; Ps 51:4 and CCC 1850 as premises), against the chapter's own "reason's witness" frame. **Serious.** Answered by Repair W1(1).
3. **W1's list of impersonal rivals still leaves out karma, and W1(3) repeats "law, so Lawgiver."** A debt that outlives the victim is just what karma explains without a giver. **Serious.** Answered to parity by Repairs W1-intro and W1(3).
4. **C's karma clause is inaccurate on four counts** and drops Round 1's strongest argument against karma. **Serious** (it is the line where a Buddhist reader stops trusting the comparison). Answered by Repair C-Dhamma.
5. **"The account without God removes hope for evil's victims" is false against Buddhism.** **Serious** (a factual overclaim). Answered by Repair Evil.
6. **W3 still reasons from the craving form, leaves out equanimity, and calls nirvana "no one."** **Serious → parity** after Repair W3.

**After the repairs below: NONE below parity.** What remains is the fork Round 1 named, now drawn more sharply. Are persons real enough, at the ground of things, to be owed, to forgive and to be loved for good? Or is "person" a true convention over a stream that is empty at the last? The theist side answers it to parity: it explains the personal shape of guilt and of love's affirmation better, while Madhyamaka answers with a principled stopping point of its own (dependence without ground, and the emptiness of emptiness itself). Against *traditional* karma, the repaired case does better than parity: a law that weighs intentions and returns what fits across lives is better explained by a mind, and karma's answer to evil costs A4.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**A2, replace the last two sentences** ("Guilt points to a person, and we go back to that person for pardon. Every serious moral tradition knows this, whether it speaks of guilt or of shame.") **with:**
> Guilt faces a person: I did this to *her*. That is why, in every age, people confess, make amends and ask pardon, even of the dead. Traditions differ on what heals guilt. Almost none treats it as a mere mistake.

**A5, last sentence, replace with:**

**W1, opening, replace** ("There are two ways ... But three things favor a Person.") **with:**
> **W1. Wronging points to Someone.** There are two ways to account for A1–A4. One says the moral law belongs to no one: a set of necessary truths, a standing persons simply have, the shape of our kind, or an order of cause and fruit like the Buddhist's karma, which no one laid and no one can cancel. The other says the Good that binds us is Someone. On one point both agree: the wrong is necessary, and no one can cancel it. Not even God makes cruelty to a child right; He cannot, because He is the Good (Chapter 3). So the fact that no one can release us does not decide between them. But three things favor a Person.

**W1(1), replace with:**
> (1) *Forgiveness.* A wronged person needs forgiving, and only a person can forgive. An order of cause and fruit can purify the one who did wrong, or let his deed ripen. It cannot pardon him, and it is not the one he wronged. Notice what people do when the one they wronged is dead: they speak to her, at the grave or in prayer, and ask her pardon, as David did after Uriah was dead (Psalm 51). Guilt keeps its personal shape even when no one on earth is left to answer it. That is what we should expect if the Good is Someone: every wrong against a person is also a wrong against the One who made her precious, and He can forgive what was done against Him. He does not forgive in her place (A3). What Christian faith adds, and reason alone cannot show, is the hope that He will raise her, so that her own pardon is not lost. A truth can be true; only a person can forgive.

**W1(2), replace with:**
> (2) *A worth that comes before any deed.* The victim's claim is truly his own. Yet he did not make it, cannot sign it away, and it belongs as fully to the newborn who has done nothing as to the man who has done much. An order of deeds and their fruits, however exact, can only return to each what each has done; it cannot say why anyone matters before he has done anything. Causes and conditions cannot say it either: they explain what a thing is, not what it is owed. A worth that is ours before every deed, and that everyone owes us, is best explained as given, by Someone who wills each of us to be, for our own sake.

**W1(3), replace with:**
> (3) *Owed, or only ripening?* The Catholic philosopher Elizabeth Anscombe saw that the language of moral law and moral debt came from belief in a divine Lawgiver, and argued that whoever gives up that belief should give up the language too. The Buddhist keeps a law without a giver: a deed ripens in the one who did it, and the debt outlasts the victim. But a ripening debt is owed to no one. Our guilt is owed: to her whom we wronged, and beyond her. If that is how it is (A2, A3), morality is not only a law of consequences but a law between persons, and a law between persons is best explained by a Person.

**W2, replace** "but they never require us to do evil (1 Corinthians 10:13)" **with:**
> but they never require us to do evil (CCC 1789); Christians hold this on God's own promise (1 Corinthians 10:13)

**W3, first paragraph, replace with:**
> **W3. Communion is a clue that points to One who is Love.** Only a person can love us back; a law, a force, an order or an idea cannot. We long to be known and loved wholly and for good. The Buddhist calls part of that longing craving, and about the part that grasps, he is right: both traditions say that grasping love must die. We part over what is left. The Buddhist says what is left is boundless kindness, compassion, joy in another's good, and an even-minded peace that lets each one go. The Catholic says all of that is real, and that beneath it is love's first word to another: "it is good that you exist." Love does not say that for a while only, and it does not say it to a convention. Such love does not need to be paid back. But it does claim something true: that the one we love is good, and really there. A longing does not prove its answer, but it shows what answer would fit: a Person who said that word to each of us first, who knows us wholly, and who loved us first (1 John 4:19). If the highest good knows and loves no one, then love, the best thing in us, is answered in the end by no one.

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **Justice as gift has a Buddhist counterpart.** The Buddha calls the five precepts "great gifts" that give all beings freedom from fear (AN 8.39). That fits Chapter 4's "gift of covenant" and could serve Chapter 7 (justice) as honest common ground. The difference is just as useful: a gift the doer gives is not yet a claim the victim holds.
- **The dead-victim case is the real test of W1, and rebirth is the Buddhist answer to it.** The book should know that answer (the victim's stream continues, merit is dedicated to the dead, feuds are healed across lives) and argue from the *shape* of guilt rather than from "no one is left."
- **A3 and W1(1) have to be consistent.** God forgives what was done against Him. He does not forgive in the victim's place. This is Catholic teaching (CCC 1459: forgiveness does not remove what is owed in amends), and it makes the argument stronger, because the case for a Person then rests on the reality of persons.
- **The best one-line test that separates theism from karma:** is guilt *owed to* someone, or does it only *ripen*? Plain, fair, and both traditions can recognize it.
- **Karma is not the Buddhist ultimate.** Theravada has its own unconditioned (Ud 8.3); Madhyamaka has emptiness. Never call nirvana "nothing" or "no one" (MN 72). Say "knows and loves no one," which a Buddhist will accept as accurate.
- **Restore "gravity does not read intentions."** It is still the strongest argument in the lab against traditional karma, and v2 lost it.
- **Karma answers evil, at a price.** That is a fair point for the evil paragraph: an account that explains the fawn and the child by desert pays for it with A4. The Buddha's own refusal to trace every pain to past deeds (SN 36.21) lets the book say this without disrespect.
- **Buddhist ethics on the dying converges with the Church** (Vinaya Parajika 3 forbids even praising death; intending to relieve pain versus intending to kill). That is good evidence for A4's "not signed away when life grows hard" and for W2's "one law, read everywhere."
- **"Every being has been your mother" (SN 15.14–19)** is one of the most beautiful non-theist grounds for loving the enemy anywhere. If the book quotes it, it should do so with honor.
- **Equanimity is shared.** Ignatian indifference and Buddhist *upekkha* both hold things with open hands. The difference is what each rests on: trust that the beloved is held, or seeing that the beloved is empty.
