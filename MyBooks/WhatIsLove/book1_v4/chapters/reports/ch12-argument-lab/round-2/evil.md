# Round 2: The Problem of Evil and Divine Hiddenness, against v2

Version under test: v2 (EVOLUTION.md). Also checked: round-1/evil.md, round-1/synthesis.md, chapter_12_current.md. Catechism wording on evil was checked against vatican.va this session: CCC 306, 309–314, 324, plus 412.

## 1. School and voice

This is the staunch "a good God would never allow this" atheist, at full strength. J. L. Mackie supplies the logical problem: he is an error theorist who runs the argument purely as an internal test of theism. William Rowe supplies the evidential problem: the fawn in the forest fire, and the inference that suffering we can find no reason for probably has none. Paul Draper supplies the hypothesis of indifference: pain and pleasure fall where survival would put them, not where a loving God would. J. L. Schellenberg supplies hiddenness: a perfectly loving God would always make a conscious relationship available to anyone not resisting it. Stephen Law supplies the evil-god mirror. Ivan Karamazov supplies the refusal: even if the harmony comes, he hands back his ticket.

His charge in Round 2: v2 took Round 1's repairs, but its new evil paragraph rests on three reasons. The first answers the wrong opponent. The second compares the wrong things. The third is the conclusion restated as a reason. And the "greater good" sentence turns v2's own A6 against God.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.**
*His answer:* The school splits. Rowe and most of the evidential camp are secular realists: agony is bad in itself, and no one may be made to suffer for nothing. Mackie, the school's founder, denies objective values altogether. For him justice is a human invention, a device for checking our limited sympathies so we can live together.
*Verdict: Holds as a position, and stops at a brute fact (Rowe) or at invention (Mackie).* Neither needs our premises in order to run the argument, since both run it internally. But Mackie's version carries a quiet cost: if nothing is objectively wrong, then "a good God would not allow this" is a charge of inconsistency, not of cruelty. It loses its moral force and keeps only its logical force. Rowe's version keeps the force by borrowing exactly what A1 asserts.

**2. A hard case.** A newborn with an untreatable condition and pain that cannot be relieved, as in the Groningen protocol.
*His answer:* Many in this school permit ending her life. The principle is that pointless suffering should be ended by whoever can end it, even if that means ending the sufferer. Others stop at maximal palliation.
*Verdict: Holds (coherent, borrows nothing), but it shows something.* His case against God and his ethics share one premise: whoever can end pointless suffering should, if necessary by ending the one who suffers. The book denies the last clause (A4, A6; CCC 2277–2279 forbid direct killing while permitting palliation and declining disproportionate treatment). It is therefore no surprise that the God of A4 does not "solve" suffering by ending sufferers. Such a God must bear with them and bring them through. This does not refute the argument from evil. It shows that the demand "remove all pointless suffering, whatever it takes" is not neutral, and that the book's God answers to the book's ethics, not to the opponent's.

**3. Ground of equal, unconditional dignity.**
*His answer:* Mackie says dignity is invented. Rowe says it is a brute fact. The sentientist majority grants equal consideration of interests, not equal dignity, and admits that capacities come in degrees.
*Verdict: Breaks (Mackie, the sentientists) or stops at a brute fact (Rowe).* The irony from Round 1 still stands. The problem of evil is at its heaviest when the suffering child has unconditional worth, and that is our premise, not his. He may borrow it to test us, but his own view weighs the dying infant's agony less than ours does.

**4. Ultimate human flourishing.**
*His answer:* Love, knowledge and the relief of suffering, inside mortality. Schellenberg adds an honest twist: he is a skeptic, not a dogmatic naturalist, and holds that some ultimate reality might yet be found in deep time. What he rejects is the personal, loving God of theism.
*Verdict: Coherent and bounded.* The victims of history are, on his view, forever unanswered. He grants this and calls it the tragic truth. Fair enough. That fact does not count as evidence for God, and v2 rightly says so.

### Exchange 1. The "greater good" sentence turns A6 against God

*Attack:* v2's A6 says one may never do evil for a better outcome, and a surgeon may not kill one to save five. C faults utilitarianism because it makes every person tradeable for better totals. Then the evil paragraph says God permits evil "only because He can bring a greater good out of it." That is the utilitarian's own reasoning, given to God: the child's agony is the price of a larger good. Either God does what A6 forbids, or A6 is not the absolute v2 says it is.

*Reply:* Start with the citation, because v2 has it wrong. CCC 311–312 do not say "greater good," and they do not say "only because." CCC 311 says God permits moral evil because He respects the freedom of His creatures and knows how to bring good from it. CCC 312 adds that evil never thereby becomes good. The "only because" line is Augustine (Enchiridion 11, used at ST I q.2 a.3 ad 1). The "greater good" line is Aquinas, quoted at CCC 412, and it concerns the Incarnation after the Fall. The summary the Catechism actually gives is CCC 324: faith gives the certainty that God would not permit an evil unless He brought a good from *that very evil*. That is not a bigger total bought with her.

Then make two distinctions the book already owns. (a) Permitting is not doing. Ch. 11 asks who does the evil, and the murderer's act is his own. (b) For a person, the good God brings from an evil she suffers is, in the Church's hope, a good for *her*. She is raised, healed, and given a share in the very good drawn from her suffering. She is not a coin spent for others. Eleonore Stump, a Catholic Thomist, defends exactly this condition: a permitted suffering is justified only by a good for the sufferer herself. So no one is the price of someone else's good, not even in God's hands.

*Rejoinder:* An omnipotent being always has an alternative. Double effect requires that there be no better way, and CCC 310 itself concedes that "with infinite power God could always create something better." So the proportionality test fails by your own Catechism.

*Second reply:* The concession cuts the other way. If for every world there is a better one (ST I q.25 a.6), there is no best possible world. Then "He could have done better" convicts no one, because it would convict God of every world He could make. The live question is not whether a better world was possible but whether *this* evil is gratuitous. That is Exchange 3.

*Final rejoinder:* Granted that "could have done better" proves nothing alone. But v2's sentence as written still reads as a greater-good trade, and a skeptical reader will set it beside the surgeon.

**Held with repair.** As written, the sentence is Serious: v2 contradicts itself and miscites the Catechism. Repaired, it gives CCC 324 and 312, says permitting is not doing, and says the good is for the one who suffered. That reaches parity. Animals fall outside A4's equal dignity, so for the fawn the question is gratuity (Exchange 3), not instrumentalization.

### Exchange 2. Privation answers one opponent, not this one

*Attack:* Three problems with the first reason.

(a) The fawn's agony is not an absence. Its nerves work perfectly, and the pain is vivid. Calling evil "nothing of its own" sounds like calling her pain unreal.

(b) Grant privation entirely. It defeats a supreme *evil* god. It does nothing against Draper's hypothesis, which is not a dark god but no one at all: an indifferent world where good things wound good things. Yet v2 offers privation as one of three things that keep "that weight" from overturning the case.

(c) Even against Law's challenge, privation is not enough. The evil-god theorist does not need evil to be a substance. He needs a creator of maximal power and knowledge whose will aims at our harm. Malice may be a defect in the will, but that does not make the being weaker.

*Reply:*
(a) Correct, and the wording must say so. In Aquinas the pain is the real felt awareness of a real harm. Privation says what evil *is* (good things harmed). It does not say that harm is unreal or that it hurts less. "Nothing of its own" means nothing it did not take from a good, not nothing at all.
(b) Conceded. Privation answers the dark-god mirror only. v2's own clause ("not evidence of a dark god") is scoped correctly, but the framing ("three things keep that weight from overturning") overclaims. The rest of the case, not privation, answers the indifferent world.
(c) Ch. 1 already holds the missing step. Sin is choosing a lesser good over the greatest Good. Every choice of evil reaches for some good the chooser lacks. A will that knew everything and lacked nothing would have nothing to grasp at. A perfectly knowing, perfectly powerful will aimed at harm is therefore not a coherent rival.

*Rejoinder:* That is classical theism, imported.

*Second reply:* It is the book's own Ch. 1 definition, and the evil-god challenge is a claim of *symmetry*. Showing that one side of the mirror is incoherent is a fair answer to a symmetry claim.

**Held with repair.** As written, the reason is Minor: its conclusion is scoped right, but its framing and "nothing of its own" invite misreading. Repair (c) is the strongest new line: "A will that lacks nothing has nothing to grasp at."

### Exchange 3. "Seeing a love" against "not seeing a reason" compares the wrong things

*Attack:* v2 puts our *data* (the love we see) on one side and our *ignorance* (God's reasons) on the other. But the other side has data too: we *see* the agony. And what we see on your side is *human* love, not God's love. God's love is inferred, just as God's reasons would be. The honest comparison sets the love and the agony we both see against the inference each side draws from them. The rhetorical asymmetry hides that.

Worse, skeptical theism answers Rowe's step (we see no reason, so probably none), but Draper's argument has no such step. It compares how well two hypotheses predict the *pattern*: pain tracking biological usefulness, agony spread over deep time without regard to desert. Not seeing particular reasons does not touch a pattern argument.

And v2 is now inconsistent. It calls the weight "heavy," then offers a reason that, if it worked fully, would make the weight nothing. Which is it?

*Reply:* Most of this is right, and the paragraph should be rebuilt around it.

(1) Restate the asymmetry honestly. Both sides see love and agony. The question is whether Rowe's inference from "no reason we can see" to "no reason" is strong. It is weak *when the good in question is completed only at the end* (CCC 314; CCC 324: by ways we shall fully know only in eternal life). That is a principled reason, not an ad hoc one: the Catholic hypothesis says the defeat of evil is eschatological, so it predicts that we would not see it now. Note also that W1–W3 make a different kind of judgment. They reason from what God *is* (Love gives itself, so He would make creatures able to love) to the general shape of creation. They do not reason to which permission serves which good. Skeptical theism weakens the second kind of judgment, not the first.

(2) Concede Draper's pattern. Our best answer is CCC 306 and 310. God made a world still on its way to its perfection, and gave creatures "the dignity of acting on their own, of being causes" for each other. A world of real causes that can fail is what that teaching predicts, and the teaching is not invented for the problem of evil. But it makes the pattern *compatible* with God; it does not make the pattern *expected*. On this datum, indifference predicts better.

(3) So resolve the inconsistency: the asymmetry blocks Rowe's inference. Draper's pattern still weighs. The weight is heavy, and the rest of the case is what outweighs it, in the book's judgment.

*Rejoinder:* Then say exactly that, and stop letting "not seeing a reason is weaker evidence than seeing a love" carry more than it can.

**Held with repair.** As written, the reason is Serious: the comparison is rigged, the paragraph contradicts itself, and the pattern argument goes unaddressed. Repaired, it reaches parity on gratuity. On the pattern it does not, and it owns that as weight on the scale.

### Exchange 4. The Cross is the conclusion restated as a reason

*Attack:* The case is "reason's witness." Its third reason for why evil does not overturn the case is that God entered suffering on the Cross and will raise the dead. That counts only if Christianity is true, which means only if God exists. Placed on reason's scale, it is circular. And v2 admits it "does not explain why." Then why is it listed as a reason the weight does not overturn the case?

*Reply:* It belongs on the scale, but not as an independent reason. It belongs as *the content of the hypothesis being tested*. The argument from evil must test the God actually proposed, not a distant designer. Marilyn McCord Adams pressed this, and so does Salvifici Doloris: the God proposed is one who bears suffering with His creatures and will not leave them in the ground. Whether this world fits *that* God is a different question from whether it fits a bare designer. This is not padding the hypothesis to rescue it. "God is Love" plus "God permits horrors" already implies that He does not leave the victim undefeated; the Cross is the Christian claim that He has begun to act on it.

*Rejoinder (Ivan):* A God who suffers alongside, while holding the power to stop it, is like a surgeon who could give the anesthetic and instead chooses to feel the pain beside you. Co-suffering is not justification. I hand back my ticket.

*Second reply:* The Cross is not God feeling pain beside us while He holds the anesthetic. It is the Innocent bearing the evil others do and turning it into their rescue. CCC 312 points to exactly this: from the greatest moral evil ever done, God brought the greatest good, and the evil did not thereby become good. And Ivan's refusal is love's own voice. The Church does not silence it; Job, who protests, is vindicated over the friends who explain.

*Final rejoinder:* Coherent. Still not evidence.

**Held with repair.** As written, the reason is Serious because it sits on reason's scale as a reason. Repaired, it is "what the God being weighed actually is," and the line "That does not explain why" stays. Parity as a defense; nothing as evidence, and the text should say so.

### Exchange 5. If hope is on the scale, so is hell

*Attack:* v2 says the account without God "removes hope for evil's victims." Fine, you say it is not evidence. But a Catholic paragraph that lists the resurrection and every tear wiped away, and is silent on the Church's teaching that a person can be lost forever, is selective bookkeeping. On atheism there is no eternal loss. On yours there may be. That is an evil your hypothesis adds.

*Reply:* Granted that it must be named. What the Church teaches is narrower than the objection supposes. Hell is "definitive self-exclusion" (CCC 1033), not a fate imposed on the innocent. God predestines no one to it (CCC 1037). The Church prays that all be saved and names no one as lost. It follows from Ch. 4: a gift forced is no gift, so a love that can be given can also be refused for good. And it is not the fate of victims, which is what this paragraph is about.

*Rejoinder:* An eternal refusal is still a vast loss that God permits.

*Second reply:* Yes. The account without God has its own permanent loss, every person ending in nothing, though it is loss, not pain. Fairest is to name both in one line and move on.

**Held with repair** (Minor). Add one clause naming it.

### Exchange 6. W1(1) "only He can make the victim whole" contradicts A3

*Attack:* Three problems.

(a) A3: "No one else can forgive in his place: not his friends, not a nation by vote, not the whole human race." W1(1): God can forgive the wrong done to the dead victim. Your reply is that every wrong is also against God. Then God forgives *His* share. The share owed to the child is still hers, and by A3 no one can forgive it in her place. Ivan's mother may forgive her own pain, not her child's. Neither may God.

(b) A3: "when the one we wronged is gone, the debt is not." To whom is it owed? Either she is simply gone, so there is no creditor and the claim is false, or the debt persists because she persists, and you have assumed survival to argue for the One who secures it.

(c) "Make her whole" sounds like the wrong is undone, which trivializes it. And "only He can raise the victim" is revelation, offered inside reason's witness.

*Reply:* All three land, and the repair makes W1(1) stronger.

(a) God does not forgive over her head. He forgives what was done against Him, and He raises her, so that she is there to be answered to, to be healed, and to forgive in her own name. That keeps A3 whole: no one forgives in her place, not even God. It also gives W1 a sharper point than before. Only the Creator can make it *possible* for a dead victim to forgive her debtor at all. St. Maria Goretti forgave her murderer before she died, and the Church holds up that forgiveness as hers to give.

(b) Reword A3 to the datum everyone recognizes: when she is gone, we still feel we owe her something we can no longer pay. The inference then belongs in W1, honestly abductive. Such a debt makes sense only if she is not simply gone, and only Someone could keep her.

(c) Say "could," not "will," for reason's part: reason can see that the Creator of a person *could* raise her, and faith says He will. Say that the wrong is not undone (A3: nothing makes it right; CCC 312: evil never becomes good). What is undone is the victim's being left in the ground while her debtor walks free.

*Rejoinder:* With that wording the internal contradiction is gone. You are still left with a hope, not a demonstration.

*Final reply:* That is all a converging witness claims.

**Held with repair.** As written this is Serious, an internal contradiction between A3 and W1(1). Repaired, it holds and improves: the resurrection becomes justice for victims, not only mercy for sinners.

### Exchange 7. Hiddenness: v2's answer changes the subject

*Attack:*
(a) LG 16 is about *salvation*. Schellenberg's premise is about a *conscious relationship now*. "He does not withhold what is needed for salvation" answers a question I did not ask.
(b) W3 now pulls against itself. Its first half says the longing to be *known and loved*, consciously and reciprocally, fits a Person who loves us. Its second half says that Person's love may stay unrecognized for a whole life. If unrecognized love is enough for Him, why does the longing for recognized love point to Him?
(c) Isaiah 45:15, "a God who hides Himself," is the datum restated, not a reason.
(d) "Every child is loved before he can say who loves him" is false of many children: abandoned, abused, unwanted. It is true only if God loves them, which is the conclusion.
(e) Availability tracks birthplace. Whether one can consciously know this God depends on where one is born (Maitzen's demographic point). A loving parent does not ration his face by geography.

*Reply:*
(a) Partly granted. The Catholic claim is not only that salvation is available. It is that the relationship is already real in the turning of the will toward the good conscience shows: a light followed before it is named. But conscious relationship is a real good, and for many it comes late. Say that.
(b) Resolve it with the end. What W3 longs for, to be known face to face, is promised at the end (1 Corinthians 13:12). For now it is begun, often without a name. A longing for a completion not yet given fits a love that is begun now and finished later. It does not fit a love that was never there.
(c) Granted. Pair the verse with a reason or let it go. The reason the tradition gives: God makes Himself known mostly *through persons*. A mother's love comes before a creed, a neighbor's mercy before a name (Matthew 25:40). And He has entrusted us with making Him known to one another (CCC 848). He shares the work of revelation with His creatures as He shares causality with them (CCC 306).
(d) Granted. Make it an analogy: "A child is loved long before he can say who loves him."
(e) The reply to the last objection is the same as (c). The Church calls this unequal knowledge a summons, not a fact to be satisfied with: she "still has the obligation and also the sacred right to evangelize all men" (CCC 848, already quoted in the chapter). Meanwhile God does not hold against anyone what he could not know (LG 16; CCC 1260).

*Rejoinder:* So a child's conscious knowledge of God hangs on whether a missionary was faithful. That is a costly way for Love to run things.

*Final reply:* Costly, yes. It is also how love runs everywhere in this book: through persons, at a price (A6). The cost is real and stays on the scale.

**Held with repair.** As written this is Minor to Serious: it answers a nearby question, and one line is false as a universal claim. Repaired it reaches parity, with the owned cost that conscious communion comes late, or only at the end, for many non-resisting people.

### Exchange 8. The only One who can forgive is hidden

*Attack:* W1(1) says guilt sends us back to a person for pardon, and for the dead victim only God is left. W3 concedes God is hidden from honest seekers. So the guilty atheist has nowhere to go. A God who alone can forgive, and then hides, makes the forgiveness argument cruel.

*Reply:* The turn of the heart away from the wrong, out of love for the good and for the one wronged, is already a turning toward Him, even unnamed. LG 16 covers exactly this. God's forgiveness does not wait on a correct theory of who forgives. The father runs to the son while he is still far off (Luke 15:20). This is W3's "light without yet knowing its name" applied to guilt.

*Rejoinder:* Coherent. It needs one sentence in W1 or W3, or the reader will ask.

**Held with repair** (Minor).

### Exchange 9. The verdict, and two Round 1 replies v2 dropped

*Attack:* (a) The conclusion says that "weighing both sides honestly," evil does not overturn the case. You concede you have no account of the fawn or of Draper's pattern. Then the verdict is your judgment, and calling it what honesty yields implies that a reader who weighs it the other way is dishonest. (b) v2 dropped Round 1's answers to "why not start with heaven, where the saints are free and cannot sin?" and to "any human bystander who could stop this and didn't would be guilty." A lay reader asks both first.

*Reply:* (a) Granted. The case is offered for weighing (CCC 31, 35), and the version should say whose judgment it is. (b) The bystander answer is restored by the repaired paragraph: permitting is not doing, and only the Creator can raise the victim. "Why not heaven first" is Ch. 1's answer (love freely given must first be freely given) and can be referenced there.

**Held with repair** (Minor).

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**Against v2 as written:**
1. **The "greater good" sentence and its miscitation** contradict A6 and C's utilitarian line, and the Catechism text cited does not say it. **Serious.**
2. **W1(1) has God forgiving in the victim's place,** against A3's "no one else can forgive in his place"; and A3's "the debt is not [gone]" assumes a creditor. **Serious** (an internal contradiction).
3. **The second reason compares our data with our ignorance,** leaves Draper's pattern argument untouched, and conflicts with calling the weight "heavy." **Serious.**
4. **The third reason (the Cross) is on reason's scale as a reason,** so it is circular there. **Serious** as framed; it is answered by reframing it as the hypothesis under test.
5. **The first reason (privation) answers only the evil-god mirror** but is framed as lifting the whole weight, and "nothing of its own" can read as "pain is unreal." **Minor.**
6. **The hiddenness paragraph answers salvation, not present relationship,** uses Isaiah 45:15 as if it were a reason, and makes a false universal claim ("every child is loved"). **Minor to Serious.**
7. **Hell goes unnamed** in a paragraph that names the resurrection. **Minor.**
8. **The conclusion presents a judgment as what honesty yields,** and the bystander and "heaven first" replies were dropped. **Minor.**

**Against v2 with the repairs in section 6:**
- **Draper's pattern** (pain distributed as indifference predicts). CCC 306 and 310 make it compatible with God, not expected. **Owned weight, not a refutation: Minor** against the repaired version, because the version claims only that the weight does not overturn the case, and says the judgment is the reader's too.
- **Lifelong non-resistant non-belief** (Schellenberg, Maitzen). It is answered to parity as a defense ("through persons," LG 16, the end), with a real cost: conscious communion comes late or only at the end for many. **Minor**, owned.
- **Ivan's refusal.** It is not an argument the case must defeat. It is love's protest, and the book honors it. **No rating:** it does not attack a premise.

**No Fatal objection.** The case never claims proof, it owns the weight, and with the repairs every remaining objection is either answered to parity or carried openly on the other side of the scale.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**A3: replace the last two sentences** ("And when the one we wronged is gone, the debt is not. Forgiveness heals the bond; it does not remake the law.") with:
> And when the one we wronged is gone, the wrong does not go with her: we still owe her something we can no longer pay. Forgiveness heals the bond; it does not remake the law.

**W1(1): replace in full:**
> (1) *Forgiveness.* A wronged person needs forgiving, and only a person can forgive. When the one we wronged has died, or never knew, no one on earth is left to forgive us, and still we owe her (A3). If the Good is Someone, two things follow that no other account offers. Every wrong against a person is also a wrong against the One who made that person precious (Psalm 51:4; CCC 1850), so He can forgive what was done against Him. And what was done against her, He does not forgive over her head. Her Creator could raise her, so that she is there to be answered to, to be healed, and to forgive in her own name, as St. Maria Goretti forgave the man who killed her. That would not make the wrong right; nothing can (A3). It would mean the wronged are not left in the ground while their debtors go free. A truth can be true; only a person can forgive, and only a person can give the dead back.

*(Check before entering the book: Maria Goretti's deathbed forgiveness, as recorded in her canonization.)*

**W3, first paragraph: after "and loved us first (1 John 4:19)." insert:**
> That knowing is promised in full only at the end, when we shall know as we are known (1 Corinthians 13:12); for now it is begun.

**W3, hiddenness paragraph: replace from "She says that knowing God by reason alone" through "Every child is loved before he can say who loves him." with:**
> She says that knowing God by reason alone is hard for us as we are (CCC 37). She says that He makes Himself known mostly through persons: through a mother's love before a creed, through a neighbor's mercy before a name (Matthew 25:40), and that He has entrusted us with making Him known to one another (CCC 848). And she says that He does not withhold what is needed for salvation from anyone who, through no fault of his own, does not yet know Him and, by grace, tries to live a good life (*Lumen Gentium* 16). Such a person is already following a light without yet knowing its name, and when he turns from a wrong out of love for the good, he is already turning toward the One who forgives. A child is loved long before he can say who loves him. That is a real answer, and it has a real cost: for many honest seekers the conscious knowing comes late, or only at the end. The Church does not hide that cost; she calls it a summons to mission.

*(Isaiah 45:15 may stay as an epigraph to the paragraph, but not as part of the answer.)*

**C, the evil paragraph: replace in full:**
> **On the other side of the scale is real weight, and it is heavy.** Children suffer horribly. Animals suffered for hundreds of millions of years before any human chose anything, and pain in nature falls roughly where survival would put it, not where justice would. Honest seekers look for God and do not find Him. And the Catholic faith adds a weight of its own: it teaches that a person can refuse God forever (CCC 1033), though God predestines no one to that refusal (CCC 1037). Free will (Chapter 1) answers much of the evil we do to one another; it does not answer the fawn in the forest fire or the child with cancer. For those, the Church gives a setting, not an explanation: God made a world still on its way to its perfection, and gave His creatures the dignity of acting on their own and being causes for each other, so they can fail and harm (CCC 306, 310). Beyond that she claims no explanation. Only Christian faith as a whole answers it (CCC 309). Faith gives the certainty that God would not permit an evil unless He brought a good from that very evil (CCC 324), though the evil never thereby becomes good (CCC 312). And we will know His ways fully only at the end (CCC 314).
>
> Three things keep that weight from overturning the case, though they do not make it light. First, evil has nothing of its own: it is always a good thing harmed (Chapter 1). That does not make pain less real; it says what pain is the loss of. There can be a Supreme Good but not a supreme Evil (ST I q.49 a.3). A will that knew everything and lacked nothing would have nothing to grasp at, since every choice of evil reaches for a lesser good the chooser lacks (Chapter 1). So the world's darkness is not evidence of a dark god. Second, God's permission is not a trade. No one may do evil that good may come (CCC 1789), and God does not do it either. Permitting what free creatures do, or what a world of real causes brings about, is not doing it (Chapter 11). And for a person who suffers, the good the Church hopes for is a good for her, not a price paid with her for someone else. A good finished only in eternal life (CCC 314, 324) is not one we would expect to see now, so not seeing God's reasons is weak evidence that He has none. It is not no evidence: the pattern of pain in nature still weighs. Third, the God weighed here is not a designer watching from a distance. He entered suffering Himself on the Cross, and Christian faith hopes He will raise the dead and wipe away every tear (Revelation 21:4; *Salvifici Doloris*). This is not a further proof; it is what the God being weighed actually is. The question is not whether a distant designer fits this world, but whether a God who suffers with it and will not leave it in the ground does. That does not explain why. It means that, if He is, the last word over the suffering child is not the suffering. The account without God does not remove evil; it removes hope for evil's victims. That is not evidence, but it is where that account stops.

**Conclusion: replace the first two sentences** ("Weighing both sides honestly, ... does not overturn it.") with:
> Weighing both sides, the best explanation of love as we actually find it is a Lawgiver who is Love. Evil weighs against that conclusion, heavily; in this book's judgment it does not overturn it. An honest reader may weigh it differently, and should still weigh it.

**Chapter text (for the redline; outside v2):** apply Round 1's line 131 repair. "Not because the evidence is insufficient" now contradicts v2's own CCC 37. Also limit line 109 ("Every time an atheist says 'That's wrong!'") to the person who denies that anything is really wrong; it must never stand as a reply to the problem of evil.

## 7. New insights and lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **The Catechism's own summary is better than v2's paraphrase.** CCC 324 says God would not permit an evil unless He brought a good *from that very evil*. That is not a bigger total bought with someone. It answers the instrumentalization charge by itself, and it says this is known by *faith*, which keeps reason's witness honest.
- **A3, kept strict, makes the resurrection a matter of justice.** If no one can forgive in the victim's place, not even God, then the God who forgives sinners must give the victims back, so that they can be answered to and can forgive. The book's Chs. 7–8 (justice and mercy one in God) land here.
- **Ch. 1's definition of sin answers the evil god.** Sin is reaching for a lesser good. A will that lacks nothing has nothing to reach for. The book already owns the strongest reply to Law.
- **Skeptical theism helps only where the hypothesis predicts we would not see.** The Catholic hypothesis does predict it: the defeat of evil is finished at the end. Said that way, the reason is principled, not a shrug.
- **The demand "end all pointless suffering, whatever it takes" licenses ending the sufferer.** The God of A4 will not. The opponent's ethics and his argument share a premise the book denies. Use this carefully, as an observation and not as a rebuttal.
- **Hiddenness and mission are one teaching.** God shares revealing Himself with persons, as He shares causality with creatures (CCC 306). The unequal knowledge of Him is real, and the Church treats it as her summons, not as God's indifference.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "He does not forgive over her head."
- "No one is the price of someone else's good, not even in God's hands."
- "A will that lacks nothing has nothing to grasp at."
- "Only a person can give the dead back."
- "A child is loved long before he can say who loves him."
- "The question is not whether a distant designer fits this world, but whether a God who suffers with it and will not leave it in the ground does."
- From Round 1, still good: "The last word over the suffering child is not the suffering."
