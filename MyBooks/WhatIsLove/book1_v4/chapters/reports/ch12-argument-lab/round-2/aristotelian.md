# Argument Lab, Round 2: Secular Neo-Aristotelian Naturalism / Virtue Ethics vs. v2

## 1. School and voice

The voice is again the school of Philippa Foot (*Natural Goodness*, 2001), Rosalind Hursthouse (*On Virtue Ethics*, 1999), Michael Thompson (*Life and Action*, 2008; "What Is It to Wrong Someone? A Puzzle about Justice," 2004), John McDowell (second nature, moral perception) and Martha Nussbaum (*The Fragility of Goodness*, 1986; "Transcending Humanity," 1990; *Frontiers of Justice*, 2006). Goodness in ethics measures a living thing against the form of life of its kind; a virtue is what a rational, social, dependent animal needs to live well. In Round 1 this school pressed W1's invalid step and C's straw man. In Round 2 it accepts what v2 fixed and goes after what v2 *added*: the new forgiveness argument, Anscombe's fork, the evolutionary dilemma, and the claim that the Creator "established what we are for."

This remains the rival nearest the book. Aquinas built on Aristotle, so most of the fight is about the source of a moral order both sides largely share.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

*(Round 1 answers stand. These add what Round 2 sharpened.)*

**Q1. Starting point for justice.**
*Their answer:* Justice is the virtue that gives another what is due. Its structure is *bipolar*: the unjust man does not only fail at a standard; he wrongs *someone*, and that someone has a complaint against *him* in particular (Thompson). Aristotle already had the shape: in corrective justice the wrongdoer gains and the victim loses, and a judge restores the equality between them (NE V.4). Aquinas took it over: justice is always toward another (ST II-II q.58 a.2), and injustice calls for restitution (II-II q.62). What is due is fixed by what members of a human community need and by the practices they share (promising, property, trust).
*Verdict:* **Holds, and it is stronger than v2 credits.** The school has "debt," "complaint" and "repayment," not merely "flaw." It borrows only at one point, as in Round 1: the *equality* of every party comes from the Stoic, Christian and Kantian inheritance, not from natural goodness. Aristotle's own justice was proportioned to worth.

**Q2. A hard case.**
*Case (new for Round 2):* Conjoined twins (the Manchester case, 2000). The weaker twin lives only by the stronger twin's heart and lungs. Left joined, both die within months. Separated, the stronger lives and the weaker dies at once.
*Their answer:* Ask what a just, charitable and practically wise surgeon would do (Hursthouse). Justice: the weaker twin is not being used as a means; nothing is taken from her for another's benefit; a dependence that is killing both is ended. Charity: both lives are goods, and one can be saved. Practical wisdom: the surgeon who refuses because "his hands stay clean" is acting from a false purity, not justice. Most of the school would permit the separation, with grief and with "remainder": a good person who does it will not come out of it unmarked.
*Verdict:* **Coherent, borrowing nothing, and it does not break.** Honesty requires saying that Catholic moralists themselves divided on this case: the parents and the Archbishop of Westminster opposed separation, while other Catholic moralists argued that double effect permits it. A case on which the Church's own tradition is divided favors neither side. The lesson for the book: the divide between this school and the Church is not in ordinary hard cases. It is at the point where a person's life is weighed *against his own good* (Foot on euthanasia, Round 1), which is A4's ground.

**Q3. Ground of equal, unconditional dignity.**
*Their answer:* Membership in the rational kind. Every human is a member of it from conception to death, so equal belonging is guaranteed. On the newborn: she is a human infant, a stage of the rational life-form, not a candidate for it.
*Verdict:* **Holds for equal belonging; does not reach "may never be spent."** Round 2 adds a point the book has missed: kind-membership explains *every* feature v2's W1(2) lists (ours, unearned, conferred by no one, uncancellable, belonging equally to the newborn). An essence is not earned or conferred, and you cannot sign away being human. What kind-membership does not explain is the *height* of the worth: why one innocent human may never be traded for five, or ended when his life "is no longer a good to him." Foot held there are strong negative duties, but grounded their strength in the shape of the human virtue of justice, and permitted voluntary euthanasia. So the school grants equality and stops short of the unconditional.

**Q4. Ultimate human good.**
*Their answer:* *Eudaimonia*, a complete life of virtuous activity with friends and community, bounded by death. Round 2 adds Nussbaum's strongest point: the human virtues (courage, generosity, love itself) take their shape from our finitude. Odysseus is right to refuse Calypso's offer of endless life, because to want to be immortal is to want to stop being human, and with it to lose the goods we actually love.
*Verdict:* **Coherent, and it now cuts against W3.** It is the strongest argument any secular school offers that the longing of W3 is not a longing of human nature at all. See Exchange 9.

### 3b. Exchanges

**E1. Attack on A5: "who made us for it" is a loaded question, and v2 never answers it.**
*Opponent:* Your A5 ends: the "ought" lives in an end written into our nature "as an inclination we did not give ourselves." I grant every word, and that is the problem. "We did not give it ourselves" is true of the acorn's growth toward an oak too. Nobody gave it; it is what an oak is. "Not given by us" does not mean "given by someone." This is v1's broken W1 step ("not laid by any human, so laid by Someone") moved to a new address. Your Round 1 repair at least asked the question openly ("who made us for it?"), but "who" presupposes a someone, so even that was a complex question. Honest wording asks *whether anyone* made us for it. And v2 has quietly dropped the question entirely, while W1's last paragraph *asserts* the answer: "His authority is the Creator's: to create, to establish what we are for." Patrick's whole account of God's authority rests on that phrase, and nothing in A or W argues for it. Finally, you cite ST I-II q.91 a.2 inside a premise you say almost everyone grants. That article says the inclination is our share in the *eternal law*. You have put your conclusion into the footnote of your premise.
*Defense:* The criticism of the citation is right: A5 should cite the article on the order of our inclinations (q.94 a.2), which is descriptive, and leave q.91 a.2, which is about participation in God's law, for W2. The criticism of the dropped question is also right. But the question has a real answer in the case, and it runs through W2. An end inside a nature needs no maker *if the nature is merely there*. The dispute is about why there is a nature whose end is *good* in a way that binds a rational being, not merely a function it performs. A heart's function does not oblige the heart. A rational animal's end obliges *him*, and he can refuse it.
*Opponent's rejoinder:* Foot closed that gap. Practical rationality is itself part of natural goodness: to act well as a human *is* to act for the reasons a good human acts on. "Why should I be a good human rather than a successful one?" is asked from nowhere. McDowell: a person brought up into the space of reasons sees the reasons; there is no further, external justification, and there needn't be.
*Defense:* Then the rival also stops: at a nature that binds just because it is ours. That is a stopping point, not an explanation, which is all the book claims. The question "whether anyone made us for it" does not settle the matter; it marks the exact place where the two accounts part, and W2 must carry it.
*Result:* **Held with repair.** A5 itself holds (this school grants it). But v2 has a real gap: the claim that the Creator "established what we are for" is asserted, not argued. Fix the citation, make A5 hand the question to W2 openly, and let W2 answer it (Repairs A5, W2).

**E2. Attack on A3 and W1(1): the forgiveness argument contradicts itself.**
*Opponent:* A3 says: "No one else can forgive in his place: not his friends, not a nation by vote, not the whole human race." Note the first clause is unrestricted: *no one else*. Then W1(1) says God forgives the wrong when the victim is dead. Either God forgives *in the victim's place*, and A3 is false; or God forgives only the wrong done to *Himself* (CCC 1850), and the wrong done to the victim is still unforgiven, which is what A3 said. On the second reading the dead-victim case does not point to God as forgiver at all. It points to the *victim* surviving death, since only she can forgive what was done to her. You have shown, at most, that guilt toward the dead makes sense only if the dead are still someone. That is an argument about the soul, not about a Lawgiver.
*Defense:* The contradiction is in the wording and must be fixed: A3 should say "no one else *on earth*." But the opponent's second reading is the Catholic one, and it is stronger than he thinks. The Church holds both things at once: every wrong against a person is also an offense against God, which only God can forgive (Psalm 51:4; CCC 1850), and the wrong against the person is owed to the person, who does not cease to exist at death (CCC 366, 1022). So the dead-victim case points two ways, and both ways lead past matter. First, the debt persists because the victim is still someone. Second, the reason the wrong against her was never hers alone is W1(2): she did not make her own worth. And if there is such a God, He forgives what was done against Him, and He alone could make the victim whole so that she can forgive us herself. Faith names that the resurrection. Reason sees only that it would fit.
*Opponent's rejoinder:* On my view the dead are not someone, so the debt is a debt to no one: what remains is a fitting remorse and, where possible, restitution to heirs (Aristotle himself allows, cautiously, that the dead can be affected by what happens to those they loved: NE I.10–11). You still need W1(2) to reach God.
*Defense:* Agreed. W1(1) does not reach God on its own; it reaches "the victim is still someone, or the debt is owed to no one." The opponent takes the second, which is the Round 1 standoff (remorse vs. debt). That is parity, not defeat.
*Result:* **Held with repair.** As written, A3 and W1(1) contradict each other, and W1(1) also leans on the resurrection, which reason cannot supply. The repair makes the argument honest: the case points first to the victim's survival, then (through W1(2)) to the One against whom every wrong is also done (Repairs A3, W1).

**E3. Attack on W1(3), Anscombe's fork: wrong exegesis, false dilemma, and a missing third party.**
*Opponent:* Three faults. **(a) The fork is not Anscombe's as you state it.** In "Modern Moral Philosophy" she argued that the special moral "ought" is a survival from a divine-law conception, and that those who do not believe in a divine lawgiver should drop it. She did *not* argue "so there is a Lawgiver." And she said, in the same essay, that the virtue words do the work: if you ask whether procuring the judicial execution of the innocent is excluded, "unjust" answers at once. By her own lights, the second tine keeps the absolutes of A6. **(b) Her own account of promising is naturalistic.** In her later work on promises (paraphrasing) she explained the binding force of a promise by what she called an "Aristotelian necessity": something is necessary when some human good cannot be had without it, and a promise binds because the practice that secures that good does. That is a godless account of exactly the "debt" in your ashes case, from your own witness. **(c) The leap from two to three.** A debt is *bipolar*: it runs from the wrongdoer to the one wronged (Thompson). Your fork says that if the debt is real, morality is a "law," and a law has a Lawgiver. But a debt between two people is not thereby a debt to a third. "If A2 and A3 are real, morality is a law" adds a third party to a relation that has two, and "law → Lawgiver" is the bare premise Round 1 already flagged.
*Defense:* (a) Fair. The book must not credit Anscombe with an argument for God that she did not make; what she showed is that the juridical "ought" without a lawgiver is like "criminal" after criminal law has been abolished: the word goes on carrying force it no longer has a ground for. That is the fork's real content: *keep the debt and ask what grounds it, or keep only the virtues and accept that "owed" means less than it says.* (b) Her "Aristotelian necessity" explains why the *practice* of promising binds: the good of trust hangs on it. It does not explain the debt in the ashes case, which is built so that no good hangs on keeping *this* promise. This school answers that the just person keeps it anyway, because justice is a settled disposition, not a calculation. Granted, and that answers the utilitarian, not the book. (c) Granted: the fork alone does not get from two to three. Only W1(2) can do that: if the victim's worth is something she *received*, then a wrong against her is also a wrong against the One who gave it.
*Opponent's rejoinder:* Then W1(3) is not a third reason. It is a frame whose antecedent is A2–A3 and whose force depends entirely on (1) and (2).
*Defense:* Yes.
*Result:* **Failed as an independent reason; holds as the frame.** Repair: Anscombe's choice introduces W1, stated as she meant it, and W1 has two reasons, not three (Repair W1).

**E4. Attack on W1(2): "no one conferred it, so it was received" is the old invalid step again.**
*Opponent:* "A worth that is ours, that we did not earn and no one conferred, is best explained as received." Every feature you list is explained by kind-membership (Q3 above). An essence is not earned; no one confers it; you cannot sign it away while you live; and the newborn has it as fully as the philosopher. "Received" presupposes a giver, which is what you are trying to show. As abduction, it ignores the rival that explains all four features at no cost.
*Defense:* Conceded on equal belonging: the book should say so plainly, and credit the rival. But W1(2) is pressing the wrong feature. What kind-membership does not explain is the *height* of the worth: that no total, no vote, and no grief can make it right to trade one innocent life for others, or to end a life because it has stopped being a good to the one living it. Natural goodness grades members against their kind. It yields "good human" and "defective human." It does not yield "may never be spent." The school's own record shows this: Foot permitted voluntary euthanasia; Nussbaum placed some profoundly impaired lives outside a human life in her sense.
*Opponent's rejoinder:* "Height" is not a property. There is no such thing as worth beyond the requirements of the virtues. The absolutes come from justice, which forbids harming the innocent; the strength of negative duties is a feature of the human virtue of justice.
*Defense:* Then the rival grounds the absolute in a disposition of *agents* rather than a worth in the *victim*, and has to explain why our form of justice is shaped so that a newborn may not be killed to save five adults, though the five would flourish and she would not. It can only say: that is what human justice is. Both sides stop. Ours stops at a worth given to each by Someone who loves each for her own sake; the rival's stops at the shape of a virtue. Which explains A4 better is the question for the reader.
*Result:* **Held with repair, at parity.** W1(2) must stop arguing from "unconferred" (which the rival explains) and argue from "may never be spent" (which it does not) (Repair W1).

**E5. Attack on W2's dilemma: it cuts the Thomist as deeply as the Aristotelian.**
*Opponent:* W2's first horn: if the human form is a product of evolution, its law "could have been otherwise," and A1 shrinks to "wrong for creatures shaped like us." But your own Aquinas holds that what is good for a creature follows from its nature, and that God could have made other natures with other goods. Angels have no precepts about marriage; a rational creature of another body would have other secondary precepts. So on Thomism too, much of the moral law "could have been otherwise" and holds "for creatures shaped like us." Darwin's rational hive bees are as much a problem for you as for me: could God have made them? If so, would killing their brothers be their duty? Your second horn ("fixed for any rational mind") is equally available to me: Thompson treats the *rational* life-form as a category known a priori. What holds for any rational animal as such holds for any; what holds for us as mammals holds for us. That is exactly Aquinas's distinction (the common principles are the same for all; the particular conclusions vary with nature, q.94 a.4), and it needs no Mind. And "very near what the Church means by God's wisdom" is a slide: an a priori order is near divine wisdom in *content*, not in being a mind.
*Defense:* The opponent is right that "could have been otherwise" is the wrong test: both sides say natures are contingent and their goods follow from them. The real question is whether the nature is an *accident* or a *wisdom*. On the evolutionary horn, nothing in the process had to aim at the good at all. If the rational bees had come about, fratricide would have been their virtue, and no one could say it was a worse form of rational life, only a different one. On the Thomist view, some things hold for any rational nature God could make (seek the truth, do good, harm no innocent), because every rational nature is a share in the one eternal law (q.91 a.2). As to the a priori form: it is real content, but it is not a cause. It does not explain why, in a world of matter and selection, there came to be animals that are instances of it and that read it truly.
*Opponent's rejoinder:* They read it truly because to be an instance of a rational life-form *is* to be aware of one's form and its norms. The fit between mind and law is not luck and needs no third cause: the mind is one more instance of the form whose norms it reads (Thompson, McDowell). As for why such animals exist at all: evolution produced them.
*Defense:* Then the rival says that blind selection produced creatures that instance an a priori order which holds for every possible rational animal. That is either a coincidence of the first rank or a sign that the order and the process that reached it share one Source. The rival can accept the coincidence. That is a coherent stopping point, and the book should say so.
*Result:* **Held with repair, at parity.** As worded, the dilemma is symmetric and the Thomist side has to answer its own Darwin bees. Recast it: not "could have been otherwise," but "accident or wisdom," and end on the question the rival leaves open: why there are, in a world of matter, minds made to read an order that holds for every rational mind (Repair W2). Move "not merely 'wrong for creatures shaped like us'" out of A1, where it begs the question, into W2, where it is argued (Repair A1).

**E6. Attack on C's Aristotelian line: two new misstatements.**
*Opponent:* (a) "It turns guilt into a flaw to fix rather than a debt to forgive." False of Aristotle. Corrective justice treats a wrong as a gain to the wrongdoer and a loss to the victim, to be repaid between them (NE V.4); Aquinas takes restitution from there. We have debt. What Aristotle lacks is *forgiveness* of a voluntary wrong: his pardon is for what was done in ignorance or under compulsion (NE III.1). Say that, not "flaw." (b) "A worth measured as 'good for its possessor' can be lost." Foot does not measure the *person's* worth as good-for-the-possessor. She asks whether *life* is still a good to him. His worth as a human being is untouched; what charity owes him changes. You have confused the worth of the person with the value of his life to him.
*Defense:* Both conceded, and (b) can be turned. The distinction Foot needs, between the person and the life that may be "no longer a good to him," is one the Aristotelian should be the last to accept. For Aristotle the soul is the form of a living body (*De Anima* II.1): a person does not *have* a life the way he has a house; his living is his being. Then to judge that his life is no longer worth continuing is to judge *him*, and the euthanasia argument treats a person as the owner of his life rather than the one living it. The Church's teaching on the unity of body and soul makes the same point (CCC 365; *Veritatis Splendor* 48–50).
*Opponent's rejoinder:* Hursthouse would reply that the virtuous person grieves at the death and still ends the suffering; nothing in hylomorphism forbids mercy.
*Defense:* Nothing forbids mercy. The question is whether mercy may take the form of ending the one it serves. The hylomorphist who says yes must explain how a person can be "relieved" of himself.
*Result:* **Held with repair.** C must credit corrective justice, name forgiveness as what is missing, and press the euthanasia point as the cost (Repair C).

**E7. Attack on the A-list's new loaded clauses.**
*Opponent:* v2's heading says these are things "almost everyone finds they cannot give up." Three of v2's new clauses fail that test. (a) A1's "and not merely 'wrong for creatures shaped like us'" is a philosophical thesis about counterfactual species; almost no one has an opinion about it, and it is the very point W2 is meant to argue (E5). (b) A4's "not to be... signed away when life grows hard" builds the Catholic verdict on voluntary euthanasia into a premise. Majorities in several countries, and Foot herself, reject it. The brief forbids putting contested Christian conclusions into premises a non-Christian must grant. (c) A2's "Every serious moral tradition knows this, whether it speaks of guilt or of shame" is false or equivocal. Shame looks to a self seen by others and calls for hiding or reform, not for "going back to that person for pardon." Aristotle says shame befits the young and that the good man will not do what calls for it (NE IV.9).
*Defense:* (a) and (b) conceded: both belong in the argument, not in the data. Weaken A4 to what the school *does* grant ("not lost when life grows hard"); then C can show that a worth not lost, yet allowed to be ended, is an unstable position (E6). (c) Conceded: replace with a modest, true claim.
*Result:* **Held with repair** (Repairs A1, A2, A4).

**E8. Attack on the evil paragraph through teleology: the Creator who "established what we are for" designed the wolf for the fawn.**
*Opponent:* Your evil paragraph says evil "has nothing of its own: it is always a good thing harmed." Aristotelians invented privation; I agree with it, and that is my problem with your use of it. For the naturalist, the fawn's death in the jaws of the wolf is the wolf's flourishing and the fawn's failure. Nobody designed it. But you insist the Creator "established what we are for," and therefore what *every* living thing is for. Then He made the wolf *for* killing the fawn, and the parasite *for* eating the child's eye from inside. Here the harm is not an accident that befalls a good design; it is the design: one form's good is built out of another's loss. The more weight W1 and A5 put on the Creator establishing ends, the more of predation and parasitism the Creator owns. On this point teleology makes the problem of evil heavier for you, not lighter.
*Defense:* Aquinas faces it squarely: the perfection of the whole requires things that can fail, and the life of the lion requires the death of the animals it eats (ST I q.22 a.2 ad 2; q.48 a.2). God does not will the loss for its own sake; He wills the order of the whole, to which the loss is attached (ST I q.19 a.9). That is an answer about *what* God wills, not a justification the sufferer can feel. v2 already says the Church claims no full explanation (CCC 309) and points to the Cross and the promise of the end. What v2 must add is that teleology belongs on the heavy side of the scale, not only privation on the light side.
*Opponent's rejoinder:* Then the theist has more to explain about the fawn than the naturalist does.
*Defense:* About the fawn, yes. About the child who is tortured for fun, no: the naturalist must still say why that is wrong for any rational mind (E5). Each account carries the weight it carries. The book should name this one.
*Result:* **Held with repair (owned weight).** The first of v2's "three things" is true of each harm but silent about an order that builds some goods on others' losses. It must say so (Repair Evil).

**E9. Attack on W3: Nussbaum's argument that the longing for lasting love is a longing to stop being human.**
*Opponent:* W3 says we "grieve as though love were meant to last," and that love, purified, says to another, "it is good that you exist." Aristotle's friendship already says that: the friend wishes the friend's good for the friend's own sake (NE VIII.2; IX.4), and a mortal friend can say it truly. W3's further step, that the longing must be answered by Someone who can hold us forever, meets Nussbaum. Odysseus refuses Calypso's endless life because the goods he loves (Penelope, courage, risk, home) have their shape from his finitude. To want to be held forever is to want to be something other than a human being, and so to lose the very love that wants it.
*Defense:* Nussbaum's argument is sound against what Calypso offers: endless *more of the same*, cut off from the mortal life one loves. That is not the Christian hope. The Church does not promise escape from human nature but its fulfilment: grace perfects nature, it does not destroy it; the hope is the resurrection of *this* body and the communion of *these* persons, made whole (CCC 1024, 1042). Love that says "it is good that you exist" asks, by its own logic, that the beloved not cease to exist: the Catholic philosopher Gabriel Marcel wrote, in substance, that to love someone is to say to them, "you will not die." Aristotle's friend can say "it is good that you exist" and mean it. He cannot say "it is good that you go on existing" and promise it. The longing is natural; its fulfilment is not in our power.
*Opponent's rejoinder:* "Natural" is exactly what I deny. The longing for *forever* is a Christian formation of the longing for *more*.
*Defense:* Then the rival must explain why grief across every people treats death as a wrong done to love, not only a loss. W3 is a clue, not a premise; it stays one.
*Result:* **Held at parity.** W3 should answer Nussbaum in one sentence (Repair W3).

**E10. Brief: A6 and A7.**
*Opponent:* A6 is ours (Foot on doing and allowing; Anscombe on never doing evil that good may come; Aristotle on acts one should rather die than do, NE III.1). A7, being bound to truth, is ours too: truth is the good of the intellect (NE VI.2), and a rational animal needs it to deliberate. So neither does work against *me*; W2 cannot use A7 to show that the mind needs a source beyond the form.
*Defense:* Agreed: against this school, A6 and A7 are common ground. Their work is against the utilitarian (A6) and the debunker and Nietzschean (A7). W2's use of A7 should say that the best account without God explains it (one nature for mind and law) and then ask its real question (E5).
*Result:* **Held.**

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**Against v2 as written:**

1. **A3 and W1(1) contradict each other** ("no one else can forgive in his place" vs. God forgives the dead victim's debt), and W1(1) relies on the resurrection inside a witness of reason. *Serious.* Answered by Repairs A3 and W1.
2. **W1(3) misstates Anscombe and does not get from a two-party debt to a third party.** *Serious* (a careful reader will check Anscombe; she gave a naturalistic account of promissory obligation herself). Answered by Repair W1 (fork as frame, two reasons).
3. **W1(2) and A5 repeat v1's invalid step** ("not given by us / not conferred, so received / given"), and the Creator's "establishing what we are for," central to Patrick's account of God's authority, is asserted, never argued. *Serious.* Answered by Repairs A5, W1, W2.
4. **W2's dilemma is symmetric against Thomism** as worded ("could have been otherwise"). *Serious.* Answered to parity by Repair W2 (accident vs. wisdom).
5. **C's Aristotelian line misstates the rival twice** (corrective justice; person vs. life). *Serious* for credibility. Answered by Repair C.
6. **A1 and A4 carry contested theses inside "almost everyone" premises; A2's "guilt or shame" overclaims.** *Minor.* Answered by Repairs A1, A2, A4.
7. **Teleology adds weight to the problem of natural evil** that v2's privation reply does not touch. *Serious as owned weight*, not a refutation. Answered to parity by Repair Evil, which names it.

**After the repairs: NONE.** What remains are standoffs at parity, which a converging case (CCC 31) should expect and say openly:
- whether a nature that binds needs a source, or simply binds because it is ours (E1, E5);
- whether guilt toward the dead is a debt to someone or a fitting remorse owed to no one (E2, carried from Round 1);
- whether the absolute in A4/A6 lives in a worth given to each victim or in the shape of the human virtue of justice (E4);
- whether the longing of W3 is natural to us or a Christian formation of the longing for more (E9);
- whether the theist's heavier load on the fawn is outweighed by the naturalist's lighter grip on the tortured child (E8).
In each, this school has a coherent position, and our side answers it without overclaiming.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**A1.**
> **A1. Some things are really wrong, for everyone.** Torturing a child for fun, betraying a friend for gain, enslaving the weak. Not merely disliked: wrong even if every culture approved.

**A2.** (replace the last sentence only)
> Moral traditions name this in their own words, but most send the wrongdoer back to the one he wronged.

**A3.** (replace the fourth sentence only)
> No one else on earth can forgive in his place: not his friends, not a nation by vote, not the whole human race.

**A4.**
> **A4. Every human being has the same dignity, whatever he can or cannot do.** The newborn, the dying, the severely disabled, the enemy: equal, prior to anyone's recognition, not to be traded away for better totals, and not lost when life grows hard (*Dignitas Infinita* 1, 15). Equal worth is not equal duty: we owe more to our own parents and children than to strangers, but no one is worth less. Reason can see this (DI 1), yet history shows how easily it is lost: the same ancient world that taught that slaves are men also left weak and deformed infants to die (CCC 37–38).

**A5.**
> **A5. What we are able to do does not settle what we ought to do.** We are capable of truth and lies, justice and injustice, mercy and cruelty, giving and grasping. Listing what we can do never by itself tells us which to choose. The "ought" lives in what we are *for*: an end we find already written into our nature, as an inclination we did not choose (ST I-II q.94 a.2). Whether anyone wrote it there is the question W2 takes up.

**W1.** (replaces the whole of W1)
> **W1. Wronging points to Someone.** The Catholic philosopher Elizabeth Anscombe saw the choice that runs under this chapter. Either we keep the language of debt (I *owe*, I am *guilty*, I must be *forgiven*) and ask what can ground it, or we keep only the excellences of our kind and accept that "owed" means less than it says. The best account without God takes the second road honestly, and keeps a great deal: justice is owed to another, a wrong must be set right, and no vote can make cruelty good. On one point both roads agree: the wrong is necessary. Not even God makes cruelty to a child right; He cannot, because He is the Good (Chapter 3). The question is what grounds the debt. Two things favor Someone.
> (1) *The debt that outlives every human creditor.* A wronged person needs forgiving, and only a person can forgive. When the one we wronged is dead, no one on earth can forgive in her place. Either the debt died with her, and what we feel is only remorse, or it did not, and then she is still someone to whom it is owed. And the wrong was never hers alone: every wrong against a person is also a wrong against the One who made that person precious (Psalm 51:4; CCC 1850). If there is such a One, He can forgive what was done against Him, and He alone could make the one we wronged whole, so that she can forgive us herself. Faith gives that hope its name: the resurrection. A truth can be true; only a person can forgive.
> (2) *A worth no one on earth could give.* Being human explains why the newborn belongs as fully as the man who can speak. It does not explain why what belongs may never be spent: why no total, no vote and no grief can make it right to trade one innocent life for others, or to end a life because it has grown hard. A worth that high, which we did not earn and no one on earth conferred, is best explained as given by Someone who loves each one for her own sake.
> This Lawgiver is not a commander who picks rules. A will that invents values can command, but it cannot bind. His authority is the Creator's: to create, to establish what we are for, and to define what love must be submitted to, because He is Himself the Good He asks of us.

**W2.** (replaces the whole of W2)
> **W2. The law and the mind that reads it point to one Source.** We are bound to truth (A7), and the best account without God says why: mind and law belong to one nature, and the good of a rational animal includes seeing truly. That is right as far as it goes. Then ask where that nature came from. Both sides agree that what is good for a creature depends on what it is, and that other creatures could have had other goods. So the question is not whether our nature could have been otherwise, but whether it is an accident or a wisdom. If it is only a product of evolution, nothing in it had to aim at the good at all. Darwin himself imagined rational creatures raised like hive bees who would think it a sacred duty to kill their brothers; on that view, that would simply have been their virtue. Yet no one really believes that torturing a child for fun is wrong only because of how our kind turned out. If instead some of the law holds for any rational mind whatever (seek the truth, do good, harm no innocent), then there is an order of ends that holds before any creature exists and that minds can know. That is very near what the Church means by God's wisdom ordering all things, in which the natural law is our share (ST I-II q.91 a.1–2). The best rival can call that order a fact of reason that needs no mind. Then it must still say why, in a world of matter and chance, there came to be minds made to read it. And this law is one: its demands can be very hard (Chapter 11), but there is always a way that does no evil, though it may cost everything (*Veritatis Splendor* 52; 1 Corinthians 10:13). A law and a mind made for each other are best explained by one Source who made both. W1 and W3 show why that Source is Someone and not something.

*(If the realist and debunker panels still want the third-factor sentence, it can stand after "seeing truly"; against this school it is not needed.)*

**W3.** (insert after "loved us first (1 John 4:19).")
> Some wise friends of nature answer that a human life must end to be human, and that to want more is to want to stop being human. But the Christian hope is not more of the same years. It is that the one we love will go on being herself, body and all, made whole (CCC 1024). Love that says "it is good that you exist" asks, by its own logic, that she not stop existing.

**C: the flourishing line.**
> **The flourishing of our kind** (secular Aristotelian) sees the most: what we need, why virtue is good for us, why justice is owed to another and a wrong must be repaid, why the weak still belong. But it must say that our nature, and the good it carries, is either an accident of history or an order it cannot explain. It knows debt and repayment, but not forgiveness, and when the one we wronged is gone it has no one left to repay. And it can say that a person's worth is never lost, yet allow that his life may stop being a good to him and be ended, as though a person owned his life rather than being the one who lives it.

**C: the God line.**
> **A God who is Love** is also a stopping point. Against the realist, it is one stopping point instead of many; against the flourishing view, which also stops at one, it reaches further. It explains A1–A7 together, including what the best rivals leave out: forgiveness, a worth that may never be spent, and a longing no one on earth can answer. And it is Someone, which is what the shape of guilt, forgiveness and communion suggests.

**Evil paragraph.** (insert after "not evidence of a dark god.")
> Honesty adds the hard part. In the world God made, one creature's good often comes through another's loss: the wolf lives by the fawn. Aquinas says plainly that God wills such an order for the good of the whole, not the loss for its own sake (ST I q.19 a.9; q.48 a.2). That belongs on the heavy side of the scale, and the book does not hide it.

**Chapter text (carry forward from Round 1, still open):** the fish-in-water line ("And the fish did not make itself for water") and naming the Aristotelian rival in "The Atheist's Dilemma." Note for the redline: if W2 is recast as above, the fish line should become "Someone made it for water, *and made water for it*," since the point is now the fit of the creature and its world, not only the creature's end.

## 7. New insights and lines worth keeping

**New insights**
- **The forgiveness argument has two halves, and v2 fused them.** The dead-victim case first shows that the debt makes sense only if the victim is still someone; only then, through her received worth, does it reach God. Said in that order, it is stronger and honest, and it ties Chapter 12 to the Church's teaching on the soul (CCC 366). Said v2's way, it contradicts A3.
- **Anscombe is still the hinge, but she must be quoted for what she said.** She diagnosed the juridical "ought" as a survival from divine law; she did not argue from it to God, and she gave a naturalistic account of promising. The book's honest use: she shows the choice, and the cost of the second road, not the conclusion.
- **Kind-membership explains equal belonging completely.** The book's best ground against this school is not that worth is unconferred (the rival explains that) but that it may never be spent (the rival cannot). "Belonging" vs. "never spent" is the line.
- **"Could have been otherwise" is the wrong test, on both sides.** Thomists and Aristotelians both say goods follow from natures and natures are contingent. The real question is *accident or wisdom*. This is a better question for the book and fits Patrick's ruling about the Creator who establishes what we are for.
- **Teleology is double-edged.** The more the book says the Creator established what every creature is for, the more of predation it owns. Saying so, with Aquinas, is better than having the reader find it.
- **Aristotle against Foot on euthanasia.** If the soul is the form of the living body, a person does not own his life; he is the one living it. The Aristotelian has the best tools to refuse Foot's conclusion, and the book can hand them to him.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "Being human explains why the newborn belongs. It does not explain why what belongs may never be spent."
- "The question is not whether our nature could have been otherwise, but whether it is an accident or a wisdom."
- "Either the debt died with her, or she is still someone."
- "A person does not own his life; he is the one living it."
- "Aristotle's friend can say 'it is good that you exist.' He cannot promise 'you will go on existing.'"
- "The Christian hope is not more of the same years."
