# Round 2: Humean Sentimentalism / Quasi-Realist Expressivism vs. v2

## 1. School and voice

The voice is David Hume (*Treatise* Book 3; *Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals*; *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion*), Adam Smith (*Theory of Moral Sentiments*), Simon Blackburn's quasi-realism and Allan Gibbard's norm-expressivism. On this view moral judgments voice attitudes and plans, not beliefs about moral things. The quasi-realist then earns, step by step, the right to the realist's words: "true," "wrong even if everyone approved," "guilty, not mistaken," "her worth does not depend on us." He adds nothing to the world, God's or anyone's. In Round 1 he said A1 to A4 were his too. In Round 2 he answers the new W1 and the new C line written against him, and he presses on what v2 adds: the forgiveness argument, the word "received," Anscombe's fork, "inclination," and the closing appeal to the newborn.

---

## 2. The four standing challenges (Round 2)

**Challenge 1: starting point for justice.**
*Their answer.* Hume treats justice as an "artificial" virtue: conventions about property and promises that arise among people of limited generosity facing moderate scarcity, and that we come to approve through sympathy with the public good. Smith adds that justice in the strict sense is mostly a *negative* virtue. It rests on fitting resentment of injury and is satisfied by not harming (paraphrase; verify TMS II.ii.1). The modern form says a person is owed what the norms we would endorse after full information and sympathetic reflection require.
*Verdict: still BREAKS at the edge, and the school admits it.* Hume's passage on creatures too weak to make us feel their resentment (Enquiry §3 pt 1) still stands. Such creatures are owed gentleness under "the laws of humanity," but not justice. Smith's version ties justice to *resentment*. The newborn and the permanently unconscious can be wronged, but they cannot resent. The school widens justice to them by choice, and it is coherent to do so. But a widening chosen is not a debt found. This is unchanged from Round 1, and v2's A4 and C line rightly lean on it.

**Challenge 2: a hard case resolved (new case).**
*Case.* An infant is born with a profound brain malformation. She will never be conscious, and she will live days or weeks. The parents ask whether her organs may be taken *before* death, while they are still viable, to save other infants.
*Method.* Take Smith's impartial spectator. The spectator feels the parents' grief and their wish that something good come of it. He feels strongly the claims of the infants who could be saved. Toward this child he feels what Smith calls our sympathy even with the dead: by imagination we lend feelings to someone who cannot have them, and Smith himself calls this an illusion of the imagination (paraphrase; verify TMS I.i.1.13). The spectator also feels the danger to the practice. If doctors may kill the dying for parts, every frightened family loses trust.
*Verdict: HOLDS in result, and its reason shows where it breaks.* Most refined sentimentalists answer no: wait for death. But the reasons they actually give are the parents' feelings, trust in the practice, and a sympathy their founder calls an illusion. None of them is the child's own worth. A sentimentalist of equal refinement who weighs the infants who could be saved more heavily, as Peter Singer's followers do, passes every test of refinement and answers yes. The method resolves the case, but the case is resolved for the child's sake only through her survivors' feelings and the general practice. Parity on method; the gap shows up again at Challenge 3.

**Challenge 3: equal, unconditional dignity.**
*Their answer.* "Every human being has equal worth" voices a stance of equal respect that we demand of everyone, hold in every possible world, and hold *as independent of our attitudes*. Blackburn reads "independent of us" as an attitude about how to judge worlds where our attitudes differ. Equality corrects a sympathy that is naturally graded.
*Verdict: COHERENT, AND IT DECLINES TO GROUND, now more precisely located.* The quasi-realist can *say* every word of A4, including "prior to anyone's recognition." What he cannot say is the explanatory "because": that we value her *because* her worth is there, rather than that "worth" is the name our valuing gives. Aquinas places the difference in one sentence the chapter already quotes: our love is not the cause of a thing's goodness, but God's love is (ST I q.20 a.2). That is where the line falls, and v2's C line puts it in the wrong place (see Exchange 5).

**Challenge 4: the ultimate good.**
*Their answer.* Happiness is plural (Hume's essay "The Sceptic"): a calm mind, friendship, useful work, and being able to look back on one's own conduct with approval. The longing of W3 is honored as real and read as love outrunning its objects.
*Verdict: COHERENT, AND SMALLER.* No change from Round 1. Its honest answer to W3's last line ("answered in the end by no one") is: yes, and that is the grown-up sorrow. It does not borrow, and it does not explain the longing away.

## 3. Debate highlights

### Exchange 1. A3 against W1(1): who may forgive in the victim's place?

*Expressivist:* A3 says no one can forgive in the victim's place, "not his friends, not a nation by vote, not the whole human race." Then W1(1) says God can forgive the wrong done to a dead victim, and "only He can forgive without wronging the victim again." Either God forgives *in her place*, which A3 forbids to anyone, or He forgives only the offense against Himself, and then her claim is still unpaid and W1(1) has shown nothing about her. Your own tradition knows this objection by heart. Ivan Karamazov says the mother of the tortured child has no right to forgive the torturer the child's suffering. Using Psalm 51:4 ("against you, you only") here makes it worse. David says it after having Uriah killed, and read alone, the verse seems to make Uriah drop out of his own murder.

*Patrick's side:* The collision is real in v2's wording, and the Church has already resolved it. Benedict XVI, in *Spe Salvi* 44, praises Dostoevsky for protesting against a heaven where grace is "a sponge which wipes everything away." He says "grace does not cancel out justice. It does not make wrong into right," and that evildoers do not sit at the eternal banquet beside their victims as though nothing had happened (verified on vatican.va). So God does not forgive in the victim's place. He forgives the wrong done to Himself (CCC 1850). For the wrong done to her, He does something no one else can do: He raises her, so that the one who was wronged can herself be faced, repaid and, if she wills it, can forgive. *Spe Salvi* 43 goes further: the question of justice is "the essential argument, or in any case the strongest argument" for eternal life.

*Expressivist:* Then W1(1) depends on the resurrection of the dead, which is revealed and not something reason can see.

*Patrick's side:* It depends on it as the answer that would *fit*, not as a premise. What reason sees is the shape of the guilt: it seeks a person, and it keeps seeking after everyone on earth who could answer is gone. That is a clue of the same kind as W3, and it should say so.

*Verdict: W1(1) as written FAILED (it contradicts A3). Repaired W1(1) HELD as a clue.* Use *Spe Salvi* 42–44 in place of Psalm 51:4 here. It is magisterial, it uses Dostoevsky with the Church's own approval, and it turns the opponent's best objection into the argument.

### Exchange 2. W1(1) is the longing argument dressed as morality.

*Expressivist:* The book's own record (v0, Option 3) says "longings correspond to real goods" fails as a law, and v2 itself calls W3 only "a clue." W1(1) runs the same inference: a felt need for pardon implies a Pardoner. If W3 is a clue, W1(1) is a clue, and calling it one of three things that "favor a Person" applies two standards inside one case. And I can tell you how guilt is laid down with no one to forgive it. The norm for apt guilt includes its own end. Admit the wrong, repair what can be repaired, change your life. Then the guilt rightly gives way to *remorse*: a lasting sorrow that the thing was done, without lasting self-punishment. That is not forgiving yourself in her place. It is ceasing to be fit for blame while remaining fit for sorrow.

*Patrick's side:* That distinction is real, and it is humane. But notice what it leaves. The guilt ends; the bond stays broken forever. Remorse is the name for a wound that will never be healed by the one it was done to. Ordinary moral experience holds that reconciliation, not only remorse, is what a wrong between persons calls for (A2: "a wronged person needs forgiving"). Your account ends at a permanent break, and ours does not. That is an explanatory difference, not a refutation.

*Expressivist:* And "the bond calls for healing" is again a felt need.

*Patrick's side:* Yes. So W1(1) should claim exactly what W3 claims: it shows what answer would fit.

*Verdict: HELD WITH REPAIR.* W1(1) is demoted openly to a clue of W3's weight. Its strength comes from converging with W3, not from standing alone.

### Exchange 3. W1(2): "received" smuggles in a Giver.

*Expressivist:* "A worth that is ours, that we did not earn and no one conferred, is best explained as received." The choices are not exhausted by earned, conferred and received. A fourth is *had in virtue of what one is*. Her having two eyes was not earned or conferred, and to call it "received" is to assume a Giver. If her worth lies in what she is (a living human who can be harmed, one of us), it is "had," not "received," unless what she *is* was received. That is the cosmological question, not the moral one. And "cannot sign it away" is still the content of the norm. I disapprove of anyone's waiving it, and that disapproval reaches all the way up. This is Round 1's staircase applied to a new premise.

*Patrick's side:* The trichotomy is too quick, granted. But the fourth option gives us the honest form of the argument. If her worth is what she is, then her worth is as much a gift as her being is. Aquinas says exactly this, and the chapter already quotes him: God's love "infuses and creates goodness" (ST I q.20 a.2). The moral question and the question of creation are not two questions here; they meet. W1(2) should say so plainly instead of hiding it in the word "received."

*Expressivist:* Then W1(2) borrows its force from an argument outside the chapter.

*Patrick's side:* It borrows from the converging case, as every witness does (CCC 31). That is what convergence means.

*Verdict: W1(2) as written FAILED (it begs the question in one word). Repaired W1(2) HELD WITH REPAIR,* stated as the place where worth and being meet.

### Exchange 4. W1(3): Anscombe's fork has a third road, and "necessary" concedes binding without a Person.

*Expressivist:* First, the fork. Either a law with a Lawgiver, or only the excellences of our kind. There is a third road, and it runs through my school. Peter Strawson's reactive attitudes, on which Gibbard builds, give morality its juridical shape: blame, resentment, guilt, demands we hold one another to. Hume's conventions give it the shape of a law laid by no one in particular. "A debt still owed to the dead" is a second-personal relation among persons, living and remembered. It is not the shape of a statute. Second, Anscombe did not use her fork to argue for God. She argued that the "moral ought" should be *dropped* until we have a sound philosophy of psychology. Citing her as if she supported W1 overstates her. Third, W1 itself concedes that the wrong is *necessary*, and that not even God can make cruelty right. Then the binding comes from the Good's necessity and not from anyone's will. "Lawgiver" is earned only through forgiveness and communion, not through binding at all.

*Patrick's side:* On Anscombe, the point is fair. The book should say she saw the choice, not that she drew our conclusion. On the third road: accountability among persons is real, and the book affirms it (A2). But it is accountability *among the living*. A3's debt that outlives every creditor on earth, and A4's standing that no one on earth conferred, are exactly what an agreement or practice among the living does not reach. That is why the fork bites at A3 and A4 and nowhere else. On necessity: the objection asks the right question, and the answer is Patrick's own ruling. The wrong is necessary *given what we are*. What we are is the Creator's choice. He did not choose whether cruelty is evil, since goodness is His nature (Ch. 3). He chose to make creatures who can be wronged, and He established what they are for. The Creator's authority is not to make the necessary true; it is to make the creatures to whom it applies, and to make them for Himself.

*Expressivist:* That makes "Lawgiver" mean "Creator of the ones bound." It is a smaller claim than the chapter's rhetoric.

*Patrick's side:* It is the claim Aquinas makes. The natural law is the rational creature's share in the eternal law (ST I-II q.91 a.2), and the eternal law is God's own wisdom. It is smaller only than voluntarism, which the book rejects.

*Verdict: W1(3) HELD WITH REPAIR.* Restate Anscombe accurately, aim the fork at A3 and A4, and add the sentence reconciling "necessary" with "Lawgiver." Without that sentence, W1's own concession invites the reader to conclude that the binding needs no one.

### Exchange 5. The C line: "a commitment we make, not a worth that is there."

*Expressivist:* This line misstates me at the level of what I say. I do say "her worth is there." I say it is there whether or not anyone recognizes it, and I would say she is wronged in a world where all recognition stopped. "Commitment versus worth that is there" is a contrast my first-order voice rejects. That is Round 1's lesson, and v2 forgot it. And companions in guilt runs in a new direction now. On your view, too, her worth is grounded in a *love*: God's (ST I q.20 a.2). So your shape is mine with a grander lover: worth grounded in someone's loving stance.

*Patrick's side:* Both points help us place the line correctly. The difference between us is not in what can be *said* but in what explains the saying. On your account, at the bottom of the explanation, our love does not answer to her worth; the word "worth" answers to our love. On ours, our love answers to her worth, and her worth is there because God's love made *her*. His love is not a stance toward something already there. It is the act by which there is a *her* at all. The chapter already has the line: "Your love responds to goodness... God's love *creates* goodness." That is the disanalogy, and it answers the companions-in-guilt charge. A creature's love can only meet worth or project it. The Creator's love gives it by giving being.

*Expressivist:* At the level of explanation, yes: I say our sensibility is the source of the word's application, and you say a Creator's love is the source of the thing. I grant that this is where we part.

*Verdict: The C line as written FAILED (it is a straw man at first order). Recast at the level of explanation, it HELD.* This is the single most important repair for this school. See §6.

### Exchange 6. "Refined": refined by what?

*Patrick's side (our attack):* "Feeling, refined." Refinement means more information, more consistency and wider sympathy. Those are formal tests. Take two fully refined sensibilities: one holds equal worth for the infant who will never be conscious, and the other holds worth graded by capacity, as Singer does. Both are informed, both are consistent, and both are imaginative. On your account, what makes the first right?

*Expressivist:* Nothing beyond what I say when I say it: Singer is wrong, and the infant matters. I say it from where I stand, and you say yours from where you stand. Gibbard and Sharon Street both discuss an "ideally coherent Caligula": a man who is consistent and informed and still values others' agony. The honest expressivist says no argument from coherence alone refutes him. Neither does yours. The book itself says its case "cannot compel the one who gives up A4."

*Patrick's side:* Agreed: neither of us compels Caligula. The difference is what each of us can say *about* the disagreement. For us, Singer and the Church disagree about something, and one of them is wrong about the infant. For you, the disagreement is a clash between two refined sensibilities, and the word "wrong" is what each calls the other. You may say "Singer is wrong," but on your own account nothing makes it so beyond the saying. Ordinary people who say "the infant matters as much as you" mean more than that. The plain sense of A4 is our explanatory advantage. It is not a refutation, and the book should claim no more.

*Verdict: HELD AT PARITY on proof, ADVANTAGE to our side on explanation.* This exchange, not "a commitment, not a worth," is the honest substance of the C line.

### Exchange 7. The book's last line is Hume's method.

*Expressivist:* Your conclusion: "look at the newborn in front of you, and ask whether you can." That is my entire method. Put the object before the heart and let sympathy do what argument cannot. Rorty argued that human-rights culture has grown through sad stories and sentimental education, not through proofs. Your closing move concedes that feeling carries the case.

*Patrick's side:* The Catholic tradition never set the heart against reason. Dietrich von Hildebrand's "value-response" holds that affections can be *adequate or inadequate* to their object: joy can fit good news, and indifference can fail a child's suffering. Karol Wojtyła's personalistic norm (*Love and Responsibility*) holds that a person is the kind of good to which love is the only fitting response (paraphrase; verify). Pascal's "the heart has its reasons" belongs to the same tradition. So we agree with Hume that the heart sees. We disagree about whether there is something there to be seen. To look at the newborn is to let the heart *answer* her, and an answer can be right or wrong.

*Expressivist:* "Fitting" and "adequate" are norms, and I express them.

*Patrick's side:* Then we are back at Exchange 6, and the reader can judge which account makes better sense of what he sees when he looks.

*Verdict: HELD WITH REPAIR.* Add one sentence to the conclusion so that the closing appeal reads as seeing, not stirring. The sentimentalist's greatest strength then becomes the book's: feeling, rightly ordered, answers to a worth that is there.

### Exchange 8. A5: unchosen inclinations include disordered ones.

*Expressivist:* A5's first half is Hume's is/ought gap, and v2 kept it, thank you. Its second half says the "ought" lives in "an end written into our nature as an inclination we did not give ourselves." An inclination is a fact about what we are, so this is the very gap A5 just stated, reopened. Worse, "we did not give ourselves" cannot tell the good inclinations from the bad. We did not give ourselves our disordered desires either. Your book's own account of human nature lists "good and disordered desires," and Catholic teaching calls the inherited tilt toward sin concupiscence. So "unchosen inclination" is the wrong test for what we are *for*.

*Patrick's side:* Correct, and it is a sharp catch. In Aquinas the "ought" does not sit in the inclination as a brute tendency. It sits in reason's first grasp that good is to be done and pursued (ST I-II q.94 a.2); the natural inclinations show reason *which* goods are ours. The test is not "unchosen" but "good, as reason sees it," and it takes the virtues (Ch. 11, prudence) to sort ordered from disordered desire. And whether we are *for* anything at all is not common ground. Hume denied it. That half belongs in W, where it is argued.

*Verdict: A5 as written FAILED (the second half contradicts the first and cannot separate ordered from disordered desire). Repaired A5 HELD.*

### Exchange 9. W2's hive bees, and A7.

*Expressivist:* W2 says: if the pattern of a good life is a passing product of evolution, then the law is local custom and A1 becomes "wrong only for creatures shaped like us." I deny the "then." Had we evolved like Darwin's hive bees, we would have *judged* fratricide a duty, and we would have been *wrong*. I say that from my present standards, which apply to every possible creature. The explanation of my judgments can be contingent without their content being parochial. As for A7, Gibbard is an expressivist about epistemic norms too. "Follow the evidence" is a plan I accept, so nothing is sawn off. W2's "a mind made to read the law" has no target here, since on my view conscience does not read a law. Round 1 asked for one scope sentence, and v2 did not add it.

*Patrick's side:* Fair. Against this school the honest question is the one in Exchange 6: what makes your present standards, and not the hive bees', the right ones? "By my lights" is your answer. W2 answers the realist. Against you, the question is C's.

*Verdict: W2 HOLDS against the realist and MISSES this school again.* Minor; repaired by the one sentence Round 1 proposed.

### Exchange 10. Hume's own fourth hypothesis on evil.

*Expressivist:* Your evil paragraph argues that evil is a good harmed, so there can be a Supreme Good but no supreme Evil, and the world's darkness is "not evidence of a dark god." Philo, in Hume's *Dialogues* Part XI, never proposed a dark god. He set out four hypotheses about the first causes: perfectly good, perfectly malicious, mixed, or *indifferent*, having neither goodness nor malice. He argued that the mixed world we see fits indifference best (paraphrase; verify). The privation point defeats the dark god and the Manichean, who are not your real rivals. It does not touch indifference.

*Patrick's side:* True. The privation point should be scoped to what it answers. Against indifference, the reply is the whole case: an indifferent source explains neither the debt that outlives every creditor on earth (W1), nor a worth that does not follow capacity (A4), nor a longing for a Person (W3). Your second and third points also stand only against an indifferent source if those witnesses hold. The evil paragraph should say that it rests on W1 to W3 and does not stand alone.

*Verdict: HELD WITH REPAIR (Minor).*

### Exchange 11. Brief: "guilt or shame."

*Expressivist:* A2 says every serious tradition knows that guilt points to a person, "whether it speaks of guilt or of shame." But shame is felt before *watchers*, and it concerns who I am, not whom I wronged. Shame cultures restore standing; they do not seek pardon. Bernard Williams made this point at length.

*Patrick's side:* Granted. But shame is also felt before someone, which is Smith's point about the judge within. Narrow the clause to say exactly that.

*Verdict: HELD WITH REPAIR (Minor).*

## 5. Remaining sound objections (against v2 as written)

1. **W1(1) contradicts A3.** If God forgives a dead victim's wrong, He forgives in her place, which A3 says no one may do. *Serious.* It is fully answered by *Spe Salvi* 44: grace does not make wrong into right, God forgives the offense against Himself, and the victim is raised so that justice is done to her.
2. **W1(1) uses the inference v2 calls only a clue in W3** (a felt need implies something that meets it). The double standard is visible to any careful reader. *Serious.* It is answered by labeling W1(1) a clue and resting it on convergence.
3. **W1(2)'s "received" begs the question.** Worth can be had in virtue of what one is, without being received. *Serious.* It is answered by stating openly that worth and being meet (ST I q.20 a.2), which makes the witness converge with the question of creation instead of smuggling it in.
4. **The "Feeling, refined" line straw-mans the rival at first order and drops his real gaps.** *Serious.* It is answered by the recast line in §6.
5. **A5's second half reopens the is/ought gap and cannot separate ordered from disordered desire.** *Serious.* It is answered by the repaired A5.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**A2, last sentence (replace):**
> Traditions that speak of shame rather than guilt know a form of it too: we feel shame before someone, not before a rule.

**A5 (replace):**
> **A5. What we are able to do does not settle what we ought to do.** We are capable of truth and lies, justice and injustice, mercy and cruelty, giving and grasping, and we carry good desires and disordered ones, none of which we gave ourselves. Listing what we can do, or what we want, never by itself tells us which to choose. The "ought" lives in what we are *for*: the good our nature is ordered to, which reason recognizes as good and to be pursued (ST I-II q.94 a.2). Whether we are for anything at all is what the witnesses below must show.

**W1(1) (replace):**
> (1) *Forgiveness.* A wronged person needs forgiving, and only a person can forgive. No one can forgive in the victim's place, not even God: what was done to her is hers to forgive. But every wrong against a person is also a wrong against the One who made that person precious (CCC 1850), and that wrong He can forgive. And when the one we wronged is dead, or never knew, the debt to her does not die with her (A3). Either it can never be settled and the bond stays broken forever, or she is not simply gone, and Someone can bring us face to face with her again. Christian hope says the second. Grace "does not make wrong into right," and the wrongdoer does not sit at the final banquet beside his victim as though nothing had happened (*Spe Salvi* 44). Reason cannot prove that hope. It can see that guilt is shaped for it: guilt seeks a person, and it keeps seeking after everyone on earth who could answer is gone. Like the longing in W3, this is a clue, and it weighs as one. A truth can be true; only a person can forgive.

**W1(2) (replace):**
> (2) *A standing that is ours but not ours to cancel.* The victim's claim is truly his own. Yet he did not make it, cannot sign it away, and it belongs as fully to the newborn who cannot speak as to the man who can. Every serious account can say this. The question is what makes it so. If her worth lies in what she is, then her worth is a gift exactly as far as her being is a gift. Here the moral question and the question of creation meet: our love answers to a worth already there, but God's love "infuses and creates goodness" (ST I q.20 a.2) by giving her being.

**W1(3) (replace):**
> (3) *Law or no law.* The Catholic philosopher Elizabeth Anscombe saw that our words "obligation," "guilt" and "morally wrong" came to us from faith in a divine law, and she asked whether they keep their sense without it. Some answer that we hold one another to account, and that this is law enough. It is, among the living. But a debt still owed when everyone on earth who could collect it is gone (A3), and a standing no one on earth conferred (A4), reach past every agreement among the living. If morality reaches that far, it is a law, and a law has a Lawgiver.

**W1 closing paragraph (add at the end):**
> The wrong is necessary, given what we are; what we are is His gift. He did not choose whether cruelty is evil, for goodness is His nature (Chapter 3). He chose to make creatures who can be wronged, and He made them for communion with Himself.

**W2 (add at the end):**
> This answers those who say moral facts simply exist. Those who say our moral words voice our feelings, refined, need no meeting of law and mind; for them the question is the one in C.

**C, "Feeling, refined" (replace):**
> - **Feeling, refined** (Hume, Adam Smith, and today's quasi-realists: moral words voice our attitudes, made informed, consistent and sympathetic) can say nearly everything in A in its own voice, even "her worth does not depend on us." Where it parts from us is in *why* that is true. On its account our love does not answer to her worth; in the end, the word "worth" answers to our love. Refinement can make feelings informed and consistent, but two refined hearts can still part over whether the newborn who will never think is worth as much as you, and then equal worth wins only by being ours. Its founders were honest about the cost. Hume admitted that creatures too weak to make us feel their resentment would be owed gentleness but not justice, and that he had no argument to satisfy the "sensible knave" who keeps the rules only when it pays. Smith pictured conscience as an impartial judge above everyone we know, and on this account that judge is no one.

**Evil paragraph, after "not evidence of a dark god" (insert):**
> It does not by itself rule out a source that is simply indifferent, as David Hume suggested. That is answered only by the whole case: an indifferent source explains neither a debt that outlives every creditor on earth, nor a worth that does not follow ability, nor a longing for Someone.

**Conclusion, after the last sentence (add):**
> What he feels then is not the source of her worth. It is his answer to it.

**W1(1) citation note:** remove Psalm 51:4 from this spot. CCC 1850 already quotes it, and without the context of *Spe Salvi* 44 it reads as though the victim drops out.

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **The Church's answer to sentimentalism is not "reason, not feeling." It is "feeling that answers to what is there."** Hildebrand's value-response, Wojtyła's personalistic norm (love is the only fitting response to a person) and Pascal's "reasons of the heart" let the book agree with Hume that the heart sees, and part from him only on whether there is anything to see. That turns this school's greatest strength into the book's.
- **Aquinas's ST I q.20 a.2 is the exact dividing line from quasi-realism,** and the chapter already quotes it in the right section. It should also be used in C: "Our love answers worth; God's love gives it."
- ***Spe Salvi* 42–44 is the magisterial home of the forgiveness witness** (verified on vatican.va). Benedict calls the question of justice "the essential argument, or in any case the strongest argument" for eternal life. He says grace "does not make wrong into right," and he endorses Dostoevsky's protest against a heaven of grace as a sponge. That sources W1(1) and the evil section from one encyclical, and it disarms Ivan Karamazov by agreeing with him.
- **Remorse versus reconciliation** is the honest difference on guilt toward the dead. The sentimentalist can end the guilt, but not heal the bond. That is a clean, non-polemical sentence for the chapter.
- **"Necessary given what we are; what we are is His gift"** reconciles Ch. 3's anti-voluntarism with the title "Lawgiver." Without it, W1's own honesty ("not even God makes cruelty right") hands the reader the conclusion that the binding needs no one.
- **Restore Smith and Hume's two admissions (weak creatures, sensible knave) to C.** v2 dropped three of the four explanatory advantages Round 1 found against this school.
- **For the chapter redline:** "Every time an atheist says 'That's wrong!'... they can't ground" and the Atheist's Dilemma options (1) and (3) still assume an atheist who cannot say "wrong." The quasi-realist says it, means it, and does not redefine it. Replace them with the honest form: everyone can say "wrong"; the question is what we are saying it about.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "Our love answers worth; God's love gives it."
- "No one can forgive in the victim's place, not even God."
- "Grace does not make wrong into right." (*Spe Salvi* 44)
- "Remorse can end the guilt. Only forgiveness can heal the bond."
- "We agree that the heart sees. The question is whether there is anything there to see."
- "What you feel when you look at her is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it."
- "The wrong is necessary, given what we are; what we are is His gift."
