# Argument Lab, Round 2: Nietzschean opponent vs. v2

## 1. School and voice

Friedrich Nietzsche in his mature works (*Beyond Good and Evil*, *On the Genealogy of Morality*, *The Gay Science*, *Twilight of the Idols*, *The Antichrist*), argued as his strongest recent readers argue him: Brian Leiter's naturalist reading, Bernard Williams's genealogy and his defense of shame and tragic conflict (*Shame and Necessity*; *Moral Luck*), and Philippa Foot's "Nietzsche's Immoralism". In Round 1 he pressed from outside. In Round 2 he presses v2 on its own repairs: where the new heading turns into a vote, where the new appeal to truth turns against the book, and where the closing appeal to the newborn turns into an appeal to pity. All Nietzsche material below is paraphrased.

---

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges (Round 2 angle)

**1. Starting point for justice.** As in Round 1: justice starts in exchange between rough equals, debtor and creditor (*GM* II §8). New in this round is its end. A community that grows strong enough "overcomes" justice in mercy and lets the offender go (*GM* II §10). Mercy is the luxury of power.
*Verdict: it holds as a consistent position, and it breaks at A4.* Mercy here reaches only offenders the strong can afford to ignore. It never reaches those who could never have been creditors at all, such as the newborn, the dying and the profoundly disabled. They are owed nothing. Nietzsche says so openly.

**2. A hard case (v2's own ashes case).** I promised my dying friend I would scatter her ashes at sea. I bury them in a field to save money. Nietzsche's verdict is that I should have kept the promise. His highest human type is the "sovereign individual", the one who has earned the right to make promises and keeps his word even against misfortune (*GM* II §2). Breaking the promise makes me less.
*Verdict: it decides the case, and it reaches the book's verdict without saying that anyone was wronged.* This is a real challenge to A2 (see Exchange 2). But it borrows. Keeping one's word is fidelity, and fidelity is truth lived out over time. That is the one value Nietzsche cannot ground (Exchange 5). His "sovereign individual" also gives his word to someone; the promise is made *to* her.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** He denies it, as in Round 1 (*Antichrist* §43; *BGE* §257). New this round: the denial is turned against the Church. Her own heaven is ranked. Star differs from star in glory (1 Cor 15:41). Some see God more perfectly than others (*ST* I q.12 a.6). The Council of Florence taught that the blessed see God more or less perfectly according to their merits. The angels have a hierarchy. Christianity, he says, preaches equality on earth and keeps an aristocracy in heaven.
*Verdict: the denial holds as a consistent position; the attack on the Church fails, and it gives the book a stronger A4.* The Church distinguishes ontological dignity, which is equal and given, from moral dignity, which is unequal and depends on how we live (*Dignitas Infinita* 7–8). Rank in holiness is real. Rank in what we are worth is not. See Exchange 4.

**4. Ultimate flourishing.** The goal is the higher type, self-overcoming and *amor fati*: saying yes to the whole of life and willing it again forever. New this round: Nietzsche can take W3's line "it is good that you exist" and say it to *everything*.
*Verdict: it breaks.* A yes said equally to the child and to her torturer's act is not love of the child. Love that affirms everything picks out no one. W3's line is said to *someone*. *Amor fati* is said to the whole, and no one is there to receive it.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1: The new heading is a headcount.**
*Nietzsche:* In Round 1 your side conceded that A must not be a show of hands. v2 now calls A "what almost everyone finds they cannot give up." That is a show of hands. Three problems follow. (a) Your own A3 says a nation's vote cannot make a wrong right; now a vote of the species makes your premises credible. (b) Your own A4 says the ancient world, which was almost everyone, gave equal dignity up without blinking. So the heading is false for A4 on your own telling. (c) Near-agreement is exactly what my genealogy predicts. Herd morality is the morality of the many (*BGE* §202). The more people hold A, the better my story fits. Agreement adds nothing to your case and may take something away. And the note under the heading lists only A2, A3, A4 and A6 as denied. Mackie denies A1, and your own C says so in its first line. I put the value of truth itself in question (*GM* III §24), which denies A7.
*Patrick's side:* Points (a) and (b) are fair. The appeal was always meant to be first-person: each reader's own considered seeing, which we trust as we trust perception. "Almost everyone" turns that into a poll. On (c): the herd story explains why many people *hold* A. It does not show that what they hold is false, and the same logic would debunk your rank order as the taste of a nineteenth-century classical philologist. The note should say every item has been denied.
*Rejoinder:* Even first-person, your reader's "seeing" was trained by two thousand years of Christianity.
*Reply:* And your seeing was trained by Homer and Theognis. That puts seeing against seeing. The way to settle it is the comparison in C, which is the honest place for it. It is not settled by counting.
**Failed as worded (the heading); Held with repair (the substance).** Serious.

**Exchange 2: Shame, and the ashes case.**
*Nietzsche:* A2 now says every serious tradition knows guilt, "whether it speaks of guilt or of shame." That claims too much. Shame does not point to a person who can pardon you. Ajax does not ask anyone's forgiveness; he falls on his sword. Williams argued that Greek shame is a richer moral emotion than guilt, not a primitive form of it. And your ashes case proves less than you think. I agree he must keep the promise. But the reason is that a man who breaks his word to save money is base (*GM* II §2). He has lowered himself. No one needs to be "wronged" for that verdict.
*Patrick's side:* The shame sentence goes; you are right that it folds shame into guilt. The honest line is that both are real and they differ. Shame says "I am less than I should be." Guilt says "I wronged her." Only guilt goes looking for the person. On the ashes case, ask the man what he actually says. Your noble type says "I lowered myself." The ordinary man, and most readers, also say "I'm sorry, Anna," and some say it at the grave. Your self-reverence explains why the promise should be kept. It does not explain whom the apology is spoken to. And your own sovereign individual makes his promise *to* someone. In *GM* II the promise begins as a debt owed to a creditor.
*Rejoinder (GM II §19):* Talking to the dead is the ancestor cult. The debt to the dead, growing as the tribe grew, is the seed from which gods grew. You are using the seed to prove the tree.
*Reply:* Then your genealogy describes our inference; it does not refute it. If a debt still felt toward the dead has led people, across history, to think of a Creditor beyond death, that is what W1 says is the natural reading. Showing that people have always made the inference is not showing that it is wrong.
**Held with repair (A2's last sentence replaced).**

**Exchange 3: A3 and W1(1) put the Creditor into the premises.**
*Nietzsche:* A3 ends: "when the one we wronged is gone, the debt is not." That is exactly the move that made the debt infinite: a debt with no creditor, so a creditor is invented (*GM* II §§20–21). You have put the conclusion's shape into the data. Then W1(1) states outright that "every wrong against a person is also a wrong against the One who made that person precious," citing a psalm and the Catechism. That assumes the Maker in order to prove Him.
*Patrick's side:* The W1(1) charge lands as written. In a comparison of explanations, the theistic account has to be stated in the voice of "on this account," showing what it would explain. It cannot be stated as a premise. A3's line should stay a datum, but in the first-person form the heading now uses: we do not find that the debt went with her. That is what the man at the grave shows.
*Rejoinder:* Your W1(1) also says only God can forgive "without wronging the victim again" because He can raise her. That is the slave's dream of compensation, the Last Judgment as revenge postponed (*GM* I §15). And who are you, or your priest, to forgive in her place?
*Reply:* The Church does not forgive in her place. Absolution forgives the offense against God, and it still requires the penitent to make restitution to the one he harmed wherever he can (CCC 1459). The point of W1(1) is that God forgives *with* the victim, not over her head: He raises her, makes her whole, and leaves her own forgiveness free. Nothing in that is revenge.
**Held with repair (W1(1) rephrased; A3's last line put in first person).** Serious as written, because the premise is circular.

**Exchange 4: Rank in heaven.**
*Nietzsche:* Your "equal dignity" is a slogan of resentment, and your own heaven is an aristocracy: degrees of glory, choirs of angels, saints above sinners. Christianity wants rank for itself and equality for everyone else.
*Patrick's side:* Yes, there is rank, and this makes A4 stronger. *Dignitas Infinita* distinguishes ontological dignity, which every human being has equally because of what he is, from moral dignity, which depends on how he lives and can be greater or less. Your pathos of distance has a Christian counterpart in reverence: the saints are higher than I am, and I know it. What the Church denies is that anyone can be worth less, so that the higher may use him up. And in her order, rank is reached by going down: whoever would be first must be servant of all (Mark 10:43–45). The highest creature in her heaven is a village girl who called herself a handmaid.
*Rejoinder:* Then equality is only a floor. Why should the floor be equal and without limit, rather than graded by capacity, as everything else is?
*Reply:* Because the floor is the call, not the capacity. Every human being is called to the same end, union with God (CCC 1700–1703). Capacities differ, and the call does not. A worth grounded in a call that no one earns explains why it reaches the newborn who will never walk. That is exactly the ground the non-theist does not have.
**Held, improved.** This is a new strength for A4.

**Exchange 5: Truth turns on the book (A7, W2, and C's Nietzsche line).**
*Nietzsche:* Four points. (a) C says I "cannot give up truthfulness." I did give up truth as unconditional. A false judgment is no objection to it if it serves life (*BGE* §4). I put the value of truth itself in question (*GM* III §24). I doubted that truth stays truth once its veils are pulled away (*GS*, Preface §4). (b) What I keep is honesty, as the virtue of my type. It is a taste, not a law binding you. (c) "Follow the evidence" is a hypothetical ought: you should, *if* you want to see. (d) And this is your worst error. In Round 1 you called my genealogy of your morality the genetic fallacy. Now C uses genealogy against me: my honesty is "a borrowed faith." If the Christian origin of my honesty discredits it, then the Christian origin of your A4, which your own A4 admits, discredits A4 in the same stroke. You cannot have it both ways. (e) Finally, Christian truthfulness, followed to the end, is what killed God (*GM* III §27). The borrowed capital spends itself down.
*Patrick's side:* Point (d) is a fair hit, and C must drop the jab. The honest form is a dilemma, not a genealogy. Either truthfulness has authority over you or it does not. If it does, enough authority to make you give up the comfort of God, then something binds every honest mind regardless of taste. That is A7, and it has to be explained. If it does not, your genealogy can be offered only as one taste among others, not as the truth about us. On (c): belief aims at truth by its nature; you cannot even *believe* without aiming at it. And A7's own case, a lie that comforts and no one would ever know, shows that we count lying to ourselves a fault, not just a poor strategy. On (e): "truthfulness requires atheism" is the very question this chapter weighs. It cannot be assumed as a premise. If God *is* Truth, the will to truth cannot finally lead away from Him.
*Rejoinder (the sharpest point of the round):* Then A7 destroys W3. The most comforting belief there is, is "a Person knows you wholly and will hold you forever." Your own A7 forbids believing it because it comforts.
*Reply:* W3 already says "a longing does not prove its answer"; it is a clue weighed with the others, not a comfort taken as evidence. And the duty to truth cuts both ways. You promised that atheism would bring a "second innocence," freedom from guilt (*GM* II §20). That is a comfort too, and A7 tests it just as hard. A7 should say so.
**Held with repair (C line replaced; A7 strengthened).** Serious as written, because the book uses against Nietzsche the argument it refuses to let him use.

**Exchange 6: W2's second headcount, and tragedy.**
*Nietzsche:* W2 says "no one really believes" that torturing a child for fun is wrong only for creatures shaped like us. That is your heading's poll again, and history says otherwise. There was no festival without cruelty; whole peoples enjoyed public torment (*GM* II §§6–7). Then W2 says the law "never requires us to do evil" and cites St. Paul. The Greeks knew better. Agamemnon at Aulis and Antigone at the tomb stand where every path is a wrong. Citing Scripture for the unity of the law, inside an argument from reason, is circular.
*Patrick's side:* On the first point, put it to the reader, not to a poll: do *you* believe it? That cruelty has been enjoyed shows what we are able to do (A5). It does not show that anyone held such cruelty to be rightly approved by every rational mind; the festivals were nearly always defended as punishment of the guilty or the enemy. On tragedy, give the argument, not only the verse. Aquinas holds that no one is trapped where every path is evil, except by an earlier wrong of his own (*ST* I-II q.19 a.6 ad 3). Real tragic conflicts are either clashes of goods, where either choice is innocent and grief remains, or traps that an earlier wrong has set. Grief without guilt is real. A2 already separates it from guilt. Scripture then confirms this; it is not the premise.
*Rejoinder:* "Grief without guilt" is Williams's agent-regret, and it is heavier than you allow.
*Reply:* Granted, and Chapter 11 should say so. But regret, however heavy, is not the law contradicting itself.
**Held with repair.** Minor.

**Exchange 7: "Look at the newborn."**
*Nietzsche:* Your last word to the man who gives up A4 is: look at the newborn and ask whether you can. (a) What the look gives you is the baby schema, an evolved trigger for caring for infants, and the strongest one we have. You picked the image that most moves the feeling. Try instead the forty-year-old who cannot speak and has to be changed, the senile man who bites, the enemy who killed your son. The look does not say "equal" there. (b) The ancients looked at newborns, and they left the deformed ones on the hillside. Your own A4 admits it. The look failed. (c) "Ask whether you can": I can. That is a question about the reader's nerves, not about truth. (d) You are asking pity to decide, and pity is what I indicted (*Antichrist* §7).
*Patrick's side:* (a) Fair, and the fix is to make the case harder, not softer. The appeal is to the newborn who will never walk or speak, the very one the ancient world exposed. It is not to the look of cuteness. It is to the look that love teaches: the look of St. Teresa of Calcutta at the dying in the gutter, which saw a person where others saw refuse. (b) Yes, the look failed for most of history. That is why the conclusion offers a witness and not a compulsion, and why CCC 37–38 says such truths are held firmly by all only with revelation's help. (c) Change the question. Not "can you?" but "which are you surer of: that she is worth as much as you, or any argument that says she is not?" That is a rational question, the one we ask whenever a clever argument ends somewhere we have better reason to reject. (d) It is not pity. It is justice: what she is owed.
*Rejoinder:* The same question answers for me. I am surer of rank.
*Reply:* Then you and the reader part, and the conclusion already admits it cannot compel you. It is parity at the limit. What the reader sees is that holding your answer means saying it about *her*, by name.
**Held with repair.** Serious as written, because the last line of the case is its weakest.

**Exchange 8: Evil and the meaning of suffering (*GM* III §28).**
*Nietzsche:* Man does not refuse suffering. He refuses suffering that means nothing. Your Cross and your wiped-away tears do what the ascetic ideal always did: they give pain a meaning so the sufferer can bear it. Hope is the worst of evils because it drags out the torment (*Human, All Too Human* §71).
*Patrick's side:* v2 already says the hope is "not evidence, but it is where that account stops." That honesty is right and should stay. Your own answer is also a meaning: to will the suffering again, forever, under *amor fati*. The question is not which account gives suffering a meaning, since both do. It is which meaning is true, and whether it is better to affirm the child's suffering forever or to hope it is overcome.
**Held.**

## 4. Ledger for v2

**Did v2 fix Round 1?** A3's ambiguity: fixed. A2 aimed at a person: fixed, but a new overreach about shame was added. A6 "costs the giver": fixed. W1 now names will versus Good: fixed. W2 moved onto truth: fixed. Nietzsche added to C: done, but badly (genetic jab, no credit given for what it explains). The heading: **not fixed.** Round 1 warned specifically that it "must not be a headcount," and v2 wrote "almost everyone."

- **Form line: Holds.** Converging, reason's witness, CCC 31/35. Right modesty.
- **A heading ("what almost everyone finds they cannot give up"): Fails as worded.** It is a poll (Exchange 1). It contradicts A3 (no vote makes right) and A4's own history, and the genealogy predicts the near-agreement. The note also wrongly limits the denials to A2, A3, A4 and A6.
- **A1: Holds with repair.** Denied by error theory and by Nietzsche. It survives as the reader's comparative judgment ("which are you surer of?"), and *amor fati*'s price (willing the torture again) makes A1 more credible than its denial. "Not merely wrong for creatures shaped like us" is good.
- **A2: Holds with repair.** The wronged/worsened distinction and the ashes case are strong. Nietzsche's self-reverence explains *keeping* the promise but not *whom the apology is spoken to*. The last sentence ("every serious moral tradition... guilt or shame") overclaims and must go.
- **A3: Holds with repair.** Forgiveness versus remaking the law is now clean. "When the one we wronged is gone, the debt is not" puts W1's conclusion into a datum; restate it in first person.
- **A4: Holds, and can be improved.** "Equal worth is not equal duty" is good, and so is the history with CCC 37–38. Nietzsche's charge of rank in heaven is answered by DI's ontological versus moral dignity; add one sentence. Nietzsche agrees with "not traded for better totals," since he despised the utilitarians too.
- **A5: Holds with repair.** "An inclination we did not give ourselves" does not pick out the Thomist end. The will to dominate and disordered desires are also inclinations we did not give ourselves. Say which inclinations count (*ST* I-II q.94 a.2), and that disordered pulls do not.
- **A6: Holds.** The surgeon case and CCC 1789 are fine. Nietzsche's own noble "never" (self-reverence, *BGE* §287) shows the disagreement is over *what* is never done, not *whether*.
- **A7: Holds with repair.** The core, that we are bound to truth even against comfort, survives Nietzsche's attack through the dilemma (Exchange 5). It should say that self-deception is a fault and not only a mistake, and that the duty cuts both ways (against comfortable belief *and* comfortable unbelief). That stops the A7-versus-W3 turn.
- **W1 (frame: both sides agree the wrong is necessary; Chapter 3): Holds.** Honest and correct by *ST* I-II q.100 a.8.
- **W1(1) Forgiveness: Holds with repair.** As written, Psalm 51:4 / CCC 1850 assert the Maker inside the argument for Him, which is circular. Restate it in the voice of "on this account." Add that God forgives *with* the victim, not over her head (answers *GM* I §15; restitution, CCC 1459).
- **W1(2) A standing ours but not ours to cancel: Holds** at parity. It stands or falls with A4. Nietzsche simply denies that such a worth exists.
- **W1(3) Anscombe's fork: Holds.** Nietzsche takes the second horn (keep only excellence) with open eyes, and the fork shows exactly what that costs: A2 and A3.
- **W1 Lawgiver paragraph ("A will that invents values can command, but it cannot bind"): Holds.** It answers *BGE* §211 directly.
- **W2: Holds with repair.** Resting on truth is now right. "No one really believes..." is a second headcount, and the festival of cruelty (*GM* II §6) falsifies it; restate it in first person. The unity claim leans on 1 Cor 10:13; give Aquinas's argument against perplexity first and treat tragedy honestly. The Darwin's-bees dilemma is strong. Nietzsche takes the first horn, and A1 shows the cost.
- **W3 (communion as clue; "it is good that you exist"): Holds** as a clue. *Amor fati* turns the line into a yes to everything, which picks out no one.
- **W3 hiddenness paragraph: Holds.** CCC 847 / LG 16 is properly modest. "A light without yet knowing its name" may read to an atheist as annexing him; a minor tone point.
- **C heading (accommodate versus explain; God is also a stopping point): Holds.**
- **C: Nothing really binds: Holds.**
- **C: Feeling, refined: Holds** (not pressed by this school).
- **C: Reason giving itself the law: Holds** (not pressed).
- **C: What we owe each other: Holds** (not pressed).
- **C: Well-being, counted impartially: Holds.** Nietzsche agrees that it trades persons.
- **C: Flourishing of our kind: Holds** (not pressed).
- **C: Necessary truths that belong to no one: Holds** (not pressed).
- **C: Impersonal sacred order: Holds** (not pressed).
- **C: Power and rank: Fails as worded.** (a) "Cannot give up truthfulness" is false; he gave up unconditional truth. (b) "By its own admission a borrowed faith" is the genetic argument the book refuses to let him use against A4. (c) Unlike the other rivals, it is given no credit for what it explains. Replace it with the truthfulness dilemma.
- **C: A God who is Love: Holds.**
- **Evil on the scale: Holds.** "That is not evidence" is the right honesty against *GM* III §28. Privation and skeptical-theism points are left to the evil school.
- **Conclusion: Holds with repair.** The verdict and its modesty are right. "Look at the newborn... ask whether you can" invites the baby-schema and pity objections, and history shows the look failed. Name the hardest newborn and ask the comparative question.

## 5. Remaining sound objections

Against **v2 as written**:

1. **The A heading is a headcount** ("almost everyone"), contradicting A3 and A4's own history, and fitting the herd genealogy better than the case. The note also undercounts the denials. **Serious.**
2. **C's Nietzsche line uses genealogy against him** ("a borrowed faith") after the book rejected genealogy as the genetic fallacy when he used it against A4. It also misstates him ("cannot give up truthfulness"). **Serious.**
3. **W1(1) is circular as written:** it asserts that every wrong is a wrong against the Maker in order to argue for the Maker. **Serious.**
4. **The conclusion's "look at the newborn ... whether you can"** appeals to an evolved affect in its easiest case, a look that history shows failed, and asks a psychological question rather than a rational one. **Serious** (it is the last line of the case).
5. **A2's "every serious moral tradition... guilt or shame"** folds shame into guilt. **Minor.**
6. **W2's "no one really believes"** is a second headcount, falsified by the history of public cruelty; the unity claim rests on a Scripture citation. **Minor.**
7. **A3's last sentence** states as a datum the shape of W1's conclusion. **Minor.**
8. **A5's "inclination we did not give ourselves"** does not tell the Thomist end apart from the will to dominate or from disordered desire. **Minor.**

**After the repairs in section 6: NONE.** What remains is the honest limit already confessed: Nietzsche gives up A2–A4 with open eyes and says he is surer of rank. That is not a sound objection to the case. Its key premise (the newborn who will never walk is worth less than you) is less credible to the reader than A4, and the argument does not claim to compel. Against every other Nietzschean move, the repaired case holds at parity or better. The genealogy describes our inferences without refuting them. The truthfulness dilemma has no comfortable horn for him. Rank in heaven turns into support for A4.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**A heading** (replace the heading and its note):
> **A. What we find, on reflection, we cannot give up.** This is not a vote: for most of history, most people gave up A4 without a second thought. None of these is beyond denial, and serious thinkers have openly denied every one of them, A1 and A7 included. The comparison (C) shows what each denial costs. Each reader is asked one thing: which are you surer of, the item itself or the argument that asks you to give it up?

**A2** (replace "Every serious moral tradition knows this, whether it speaks of guilt or of shame."):
> Shame is something different. Shame says, "I am less than I should be"; guilt says, "I wronged her." Many traditions speak mostly of shame, and shame is real. But only guilt goes looking for the one we wronged.

**A3** (replace "And when the one we wronged is gone, the debt is not."):
> And when the one we wronged is gone, we do not find that the debt went with her: people still stand at a grave to say they are sorry.

**A4** (add after "but no one is worth less."):
> Nor is equal worth equal holiness: the saints are higher than I am, and the Church has always said so. Rank in how well we love is real; rank in what we are worth is not (*Dignitas Infinita* 7–8).

**A5** (replace "an end written into our nature as an inclination we did not give ourselves (ST I-II q.91 a.2)."):
> an end written into our nature that we did not give ourselves. That end is not every pull we feel, since some of our desires are disordered. It is the goods our powers are made to reach: life, truth, friendship, the good of others (*ST* I-II q.91 a.2; q.94 a.2).

**A7** (replace the item):
> **A7. We are bound to the truth, even when a lie would comfort us and no one would ever know.** We count lying to ourselves a fault, not just a mistake. Following the evidence is itself something we *ought* to do, and it cuts both ways: against believing in God because that comforts, and against believing that no one will ever judge us, because that comforts too.

**W1(1)** (replace "Yet every wrong against a person is also a wrong against the One who made that person precious (Psalm 51:4; CCC 1850). Only He can forgive without wronging the victim again, because only He can raise the victim and make her whole."):
> But if the victim's worth was given by Someone, then every wrong against her is also a wrong against the One who made her precious, as the Church teaches (Psalm 51:4; CCC 1850), and the debt still felt at a grave has someone to whom it is owed. Only such a One could forgive without wronging the victim again: not over her head, but by raising her, making her whole, and leaving her own forgiveness free.

**W2** (replace "Yet no one really believes that torturing a child for fun is wrong only for creatures shaped like us (A1)."):
> Yet ask yourself whether you believe that torturing a child for fun is wrong only for creatures shaped like us (A1). Whole peoples have enjoyed cruelty; that shows what we are able to do (A5), not what any rational mind could rightly approve.

**W2** (replace "And this law is one: its demands can be very hard (Chapter 11), but they never require us to do evil (1 Corinthians 10:13)."):
> And this law is one. Its demands can be very hard, and some choices leave real grief behind (Chapter 11). But no one is ever trapped where every path is evil unless an earlier wrong set the trap (*ST* I-II q.19 a.6 ad 3). The tragic poets saw such traps, and Christian faith adds that God does not let us be tested beyond our strength (1 Corinthians 10:13).

**C, Power and rank** (replace the line):
> - **Power and rank** (Nietzsche) is the most honest rival. It explains a great deal: why guilt grew so large, why moral feeling has changed across history, and why equal dignity spread where Christianity spread (on that last point the Church agrees with its history, CCC 37–38). It stays consistent by giving up A1–A4 openly. Its hard point is truth. If truthfulness binds every honest mind, even against its comfort, then something binds us that no one chose, which is A7. If it does not, the genealogy can be offered only as one taste among others, not as the truth about us.

**Conclusion** (replace "To him it says only: look at the newborn in front of you, and ask whether you can."):
> To him it says only this: look at the newborn who will never walk or speak, the one the ancient world left on the hillside, and ask which you are surer of: that she is worth as much as you, or any argument that says she is not.

## 7. New insights for the book

1. **This is not a vote.** An argument that says "a nation's vote cannot make a wrong right" must not rest its own premises on a vote. The first-person comparative question ("which are you surer of?") is the honest form throughout: the heading, W2 and the conclusion.
2. **One rule of fairness:** never use a genealogy against an opponent that we refuse to let him use against us. Answer Nietzsche by dilemma, not by pointing out where his honesty came from.
3. **Rank by going down.** Nietzsche's strongest rhetoric, "Christianity levels," is false. The Church keeps rank, in holiness, glory and office. She denies only rank in worth, and she reaches the top of her order by going down (Mark 10:43–45; the Magnificat). Ontological versus moral dignity (DI 7–8) is the tool. This belongs in A4 and could serve Ch. 8 (mercy) and Ch. 13 (virtue).
4. **Nietzsche's highest man keeps his word.** The "sovereign individual" (*GM* II §2) is the one who can make and keep a promise. Without meaning to, even Nietzsche puts fidelity at the top, and fidelity is the root of covenant (Ch. 4). He keeps the promise for his own sake; the book shows why it is also for *hers*.
5. **The duty to truth cuts both ways.** Nietzsche's promised "second innocence" (*GM* II §20) is itself a comfort. A7 tests comfortable unbelief as hard as comfortable belief. That makes A7 fair, and it stops the "your God is wishful thinking" turn without special pleading.
6. **Shame and guilt are different, and the difference serves A2.** Shame says "I am less"; guilt says "I wronged her." Only guilt goes looking for a person, and the shape of guilt is what W1 reads.
7. **The hardest newborn, not the sweetest.** Every appeal to the reader's seeing should use the case where the feeling is weakest and the worth is still there. That is what the saints teach us to see (St. Teresa of Calcutta).
