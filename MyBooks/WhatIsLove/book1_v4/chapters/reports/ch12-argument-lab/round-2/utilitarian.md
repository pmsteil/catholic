# Argument Lab, Round 2: Utilitarianism / Consequentialism vs. v2

## 1. School and voice

The opponent again speaks for realist, impartialist utilitarianism at full strength: Sidgwick's *Methods of Ethics*, Hare's two-level theory (*Moral Thinking*, 1981), Parfit (*Reasons and Persons*; *On What Matters*), and de Lazari-Radek and Singer's *The Point of View of the Universe* (2014). This round it also brings in the school's neighbours: preference and desire-fulfillment theories of well-being (Hare, the earlier Singer, Parfit's "success theory"), rule consequentialism (Brad Hooker, *Ideal Code, Real World*), and the debunking of deontological intuitions from moral psychology (Joshua Greene, used by Singer in "Ethics and Intuitions," 2005). Round 1 lost on scope: wronging, guilt, untradeable dignity. So Round 2 does not argue that utilitarianism explains more. It argues that v2's sharpest weapons (the deathbed promise, the transplant surgeon, "not to be traded") are blunter than v2 thinks, and that some of them cut the book's own theology.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges (Round 2 restatement, new case)

**Challenge 1: Starting point for justice.**
*Answer:* Equal consideration of interests. Justice is the set of rules that protect the most vital interests (security, reliance, freedom from harm). Its sternness is explained by how much depends on those rules. Hooker's rule consequentialism adds that the best code, once internalized by nearly everyone, will contain *directed* duties: promises owed to this person, a ban on killing this innocent. These hold even when breaking them would add a little good.
*Verdict:* **Coherent, and it now borrows less than in Round 1.** The rule version reproduces the *shape* of what is owed. But the duty is owed to the person only because the code that names him does the most good. If the totals moved, the code would move, and with it what he is owed. "Owed" is derived, not original. That still breaks at the point A2 presses.

**Challenge 2: A hard case resolved by its own principles.** (New case this round, chosen by the opponent as the hardest for the book.)
*Answer:* A *vital conflict* in obstetrics. The mother will die without an intervention that ends the pregnancy. The child will die whether or not the intervention is done, because it cannot survive outside the womb and will not survive her death. The utilitarian verdict takes one line: save the one who can be saved. Any rule that lets both die when one could live is, in J. J. C. Smart's phrase, "rule worship."
*Verdict:* **Holds as a decision, and it is the strongest case this school has.** It is honest to say that a real case of this kind (Phoenix, 2009) divided Catholic moralists. Some held that the procedure was direct killing and forbidden. Others, notably Martin Rhonheimer in *Vital Conflicts in Medical Ethics*, argued that when the child cannot be saved, the act is not chosen as killing in the sense the absolute forbids. The book does not have to settle that dispute. But it must not present the absolute as if it never costs a life. On the paradigm reading it sometimes does, and the Church accepts that cost rather than intend an innocent's death (CCC 2271, 1789). The utilitarian handles this case cleanly. It does not borrow the book's facets. It wins the hard case and still has to pay at the limit case: the clean, secret transplant.

**Challenge 3: What grounds equal, unconditional dignity?**
*Answer:* There are two answers, and both are honest. The *critical* answer (Singer): nothing grounds it. Status follows capacities, and some humans are not persons. The *practical* answer (Hare): a firm, felt, nearly unbreakable rule of equal human rights is what a perfectly informed, impartial planner would teach a species like ours. So utilitarianism *recommends* that we feel A4 as inviolable.
*Verdict:* **It borrows the practice and breaks at the ground.** Hare's answer is new to this lab and important. It predicts the felt inviolability of A4, so the feeling alone does not count against utilitarianism. What A4 needs is the *truth* of inviolability, and at the critical level the theory denies it. Its practice of human rights rests, in Bernard Williams's phrase, on "Government House" foundations: a truth the planners hold and the governed are better off not believing.

**Challenge 4: The ultimate good.**
*Answer:* Well-being, counted impartially: pleasant consciousness (de Lazari-Radek and Singer), satisfied preferences (Hare), or an objective list (Parfit takes this view seriously).
*Verdict:* **Coherent but thin at the summit.** Benevolence says *it is good that there is happiness*. Love says *it is good that you exist*. The object differs: welfare, or the person. That difference is W3's whole point, and the utilitarian cannot draw it without leaving the theory.

## 3. Debate highlights (Round 2)

**First, did v2 fix what Round 1 found?**
- A3's symmetry ("not God either"): **fixed.** W1 now grants that the wrong is necessary and that not even God makes cruelty right. A3 now separates forgiving from making the wrong right.
- "Cannot honestly deny": **fixed.** The A heading now names the honest deniers.
- A4 "untradeable": **fixed in substance**, but over-fixed (Exchange 5).
- A6 restated as finitude, plus the transplant case and CCC 1789: **fixed.** But the transplant case is the deontologist's *easiest* case (Exchange 4).
- W1 "asserts owed-to-someone": **largely fixed** by worse vs. wronged and forgiveness. But (2) and (3) now carry new gaps (Exchange 8).
- W2 must answer the rival that reason is a general by-product of evolution: **not fixed.** v2 answers the third-factor reply and drops the by-product reply that Round 1 asked it to meet (Exchange 9).
- A separate C line for well-being: **added.** It overclaims (Exchange 10).
- A5 slips the thesis into a datum: **not fixed.** It is worse in one respect (Exchange 11).

**Exchange 1: A3 and W1(1) now contradict each other.**
*Utilitarian:* A3 says "No one else can forgive in his place." W1(1) says only God "can forgive without wronging the victim again." So God *does* forgive in the victim's place. Either A3 is false or W1(1) is. And Ivan Karamazov's protest stands behind the second reading: no one, not even God, has the right to forgive the torturer for the child's tears.
*Defense:* The tension is in the wording, not in the doctrine. Every sin wrongs two parties: the person harmed and God (CCC 1850; Psalm 51:4). God forgives the offense against *Himself*. He does not cancel the victim's claim behind her back. The Church still requires the wrongdoer to repair what he can (CCC 1459). What God alone can do is *keep* the one we wronged. The dead are not lost to Him (CCC 958). So the pardon we could not ask for on earth is not lost forever, and the victim's own voice is not overruled.
*Rejoinder:* Then W1(1) does not show that a debt with no living creditor *needs* God. It shows that theism, if true, can settle such debts. The secular side denies there is any debt still unpaid. There is the past fact that you did wrong, and there are present reasons for remorse and repair.
*Result:* **Held with repair.** W1(1) is fine as inference to the best explanation, but only if (a) it stops saying God forgives in the victim's place and (b) it admits that the opponent describes the leftover as "a past wrong," not "a debt." The theist's reply to (b) is good: remorse toward the dead does not feel like regret over a bad sum. It reaches for *her*. That is the datum, and it should be stated as one.

**Exchange 2: The deathbed promise is theory-laden.**
*Utilitarian:* A2 says "no one is ever worse off." That is true only on hedonism. On desire-fulfillment views of well-being, which Hare held, which the earlier Singer held, and which Parfit called the "success theory," a person's life goes worse when what she wanted for her own life does not happen, even after her death and even if she never learns of it. Parfit's own example is a stranger on a train whose wish is fulfilled or frustrated without his ever knowing. On these views your friend *was* made worse off. So your case does not separate wronging from worsening. It separates hedonism from every other theory of welfare. And the hedonist has Hare's answer: keep the promise, feel the wrong, and know that the feeling is a well-trained rule.
*Defense:* Then the preference theorist grants the point that matters. The wrong is a setback to *her*, not a bad state added to the world. Ask where that setback lives. On a godless view she no longer exists, so you have an interest with no one left to have it. Joel Feinberg and George Pitcher answer that the harm falls on the living woman she was, and reaches back. That is an ingenious answer. But it needs a subject who still counts after she can no longer be helped or hurt. That fits a world in which she is not gone far better than a world in which she is.
*Rejoinder:* Feinberg's view needs no afterlife. It is a claim about which events make a whole life go well or badly.
*Result:* **Held with repair.** The case still works, but its claim must be narrowed. Drop "no one is ever worse off," which is a contested theory of welfare. Say instead: *nothing in anyone's experience is ever different, and still she was wronged; whatever theory of welfare you hold, the wrong is done to her.* That version holds against all three welfare theories, and it sends the preference theorist toward the question of the subject. That is where the book wants him.

**Exchange 3: Two levels, and why the intuition proves too little.**
*Utilitarian:* Hare predicts your data. A wise planner would teach us to feel A4 and A6 as absolutes, to recoil from the transplant, and to feel guilt *toward* the dead friend. So the *feeling* of inviolability fits utilitarianism as well as it fits theism. Your evidence is phenomenology, and phenomenology does not discriminate.
*Defense:* It discriminates on one point: truth. On your view the absolute is false and useful. So the ordinary person who holds it is, by your lights, kept in a useful error. Sidgwick said this himself. He wrote that a utilitarian may have to keep the morality of exceptions comparatively secret, and keep even that opinion secret (paraphrase, *Methods* IV.5). The book's question is not "which theory predicts the feeling?" but "which theory lets the feeling be true?" Second, your own method rests on intuition. Sidgwick's axioms are self-evident truths seen by reason. His tests for self-evidence are clarity, consistency with other convictions, and the agreement of competent judges. On the third test the constraint against killing the innocent does at least as well as the axiom of impartial maximizing. Probably it does better.
*Rejoinder:* Consensus is just what evolution would produce for a constraint against violence at close quarters.
*Result:* **Held at parity.** This is a standoff. The book should say so in plain words: we trust A4 and A6 not because they are felt strongly but because, on reflection, we *see* them. A theory that keeps them only as useful errors has to ask everyone to believe what it thinks is false.

**Exchange 4: The debunking argument turns W2 against A6. The transplant is the easy case.**
*Utilitarian:* This is the new and sharper attack. W2 trusts conscience most where survival pulls against it. Run that test. Evolution would build a fast alarm against personal, up-close violence. Greene's experiments find that alarm: people approve of switching a runaway trolley onto one person and condemn pushing a man in front of it, and the difference seems to follow "personal force," which is not a morally relevant feature. Evolution would *not* build concern for distant strangers who can never repay. De Lazari-Radek and Singer argue that impartial benevolence therefore survives debunking and constraints do not. By W2's own test, our principle beats your A6. And the transplant case is chosen to set off the alarm: a knife, a body, a healthy patient. Sophisticated utilitarians forbid that surgeon in the real world anyway, because hospitals that harvest patients destroy the trust medicine runs on. The transplant tells your view apart from ours only in a clean, secret, one-off version that no one has ever faced.
*Defense:* Three answers. (1) The data have been seriously contested. Guy Kahane and colleagues (2015) found that "utilitarian" verdicts in sacrificial dilemmas track a readiness to accept harm, with links to antisocial traits, and not impartial concern for others. They also found that willingness to cause harm and impartial generosity are two separate things in people. Selim Berker (2009) argued that the neuroscience by itself has no normative force. (2) The constraint is not only an up-close alarm. People hold it as firmly in cases with no knife and no body: secretly framing an innocent man so that a riot does not happen; medical experiments on patients who did not consent, which the world condemned after Nuremberg on reflection, not by reflex. (3) Concern for distant strangers is not the same thing as a readiness to spend one of them. The part of the impartial view that survives debunking is beneficence, and beneficence is fully compatible with constraints. The Church commands both (CCC 2443–2449 and 1789).
*Rejoinder:* Fine, but then admit that the transplant case is your weakest illustration, not your strongest.
*Result:* **Held with repair.** A6 should keep the surgeon, because readers grasp him at once, but add a case with no knife: the secret scapegoat, or the unconsented experiment. The book should also say plainly that the absolute matters in the clean case, the one where breaking it would never be found out. That is where the two views actually part.

**Exchange 5: Double effect breaks "not to be traded for better totals."**
*Utilitarian:* A4 says dignity is "not to be traded away for better totals." Your own tradition trades lives every day. Just-war teaching allows strikes that will foreseeably kill civilians if the harm is proportionate (CCC 2309). The principle of double effect permits acts with a deadly side effect when the good outweighs it. The ectopic-pregnancy surgery Catholic hospitals perform is one example. "Proportionate" is a weighing of totals. So A4 as worded is false of Catholic teaching. What you actually forbid is narrower: *intending* an innocent's death, as an end or as a means. Then press the vital conflict. The intention/foresight line lets two die where one could be saved, and the dead are just as dead whichever way you intended.
*Defense:* Agreed on the wording. The repair makes the claim more precise and makes it stronger. No one may be *killed or used as a means* to buy a better outcome. Side effects may be weighed. Persons may not be spent. This is the line between the surgeon and the trolley switch, and between the soldier who strikes a military target knowing civilians may die and the one who targets civilians to break morale. Elizabeth Anscombe drew this line in her protest against Truman's honorary degree and in "War and Murder." She argued that the intention/foresight line is what keeps *any* limit on war from dissolving. On the vital conflict, honesty first: on the paradigm reading the absolute can cost a life, and the book should say so in one sentence. Then say what the alternative costs. If one may intend an innocent's death whenever the numbers favour it, then every innocent person's safety depends on the numbers. The absolute's price is sometimes paid in a life. The calculator's price is paid by everyone: no one is safe from being spent.
*Rejoinder:* The "everyone" is rhetoric. In practice we would keep the rule.
*Defense:* Then, by your own account, you keep a rule you believe is false. That is Exchange 3 again.
*Result:* **Held with repair, at parity on the vital conflict.** A4's "not to be traded" must be narrowed to "not to be killed or used as a means." As written it contradicts CCC 2309 and double effect, and a Catholic reader trained in moral theology will notice at once.

**Exchange 6: The paradox of constraints.**
*Utilitarian:* (Samuel Scheffler, Robert Nozick.) If killing an innocent is so terrible, then surely a world with fewer such killings is better. So you should kill one innocent to stop five such killings by others. Refusing seems to care more about your own hands than about the five victims.
*Defense:* That assumes the constraint protects a *bad state of affairs* (killings) to be minimized. It protects a *status* that each person has: no one may be used. If I may kill one to prevent five killings, then no one has that status. All six are merely protected, and only while protection pays. A world where each person is inviolable is a better world for each person than one where everyone may be spent for the rest. (Frances Kamm makes this argument in secular terms.) Catholic teaching names it from the agent's side: some acts are wrong in their object whatever the intention or circumstances (*Veritatis Splendor* 80). Choosing them makes the chooser's will the will of one who kills the innocent (VS 71). Philip Pettit, himself a consequentialist, put the difference as "honouring" a value versus "promoting" it. The constraint honours persons.
*Rejoinder:* "Honouring" is a name, not a reason.
*Result:* **Held.** This is the standard answer, and it works. It should be the reply the book gives if it ever addresses the paradox. It does not need to go into the argument itself.

**Exchange 7: The theodicy is a greater-good trade.**
*Utilitarian:* The new objection that cuts deepest. Your evil paragraph says God permits evil "only because He can bring a greater good out of it" (CCC 311–312). Aquinas says God permits some evils for the good of the whole universe, as a provident ruler tolerates some defects for the sake of the whole (ST I q.22 a.2 ad 2; I q.48 a.2). That is aggregative reasoning: the part is sacrificed to the whole. Your A6 forbids a human surgeon to let one die for five, and yet your God lets the fawn burn and the child suffer cancer for a greater total good. Either God does what A6 forbids, or A6 is not as absolute as you claim.
*Defense:* Two distinctions do the work, and both are already Catholic teaching. (1) *Doing vs. permitting, and intending vs. foreseeing.* God never intends evil or does it, directly or indirectly (CCC 311). He permits it within a world of real causes and free creatures. The surgeon in A6 *intends* a death as a means, so he is not a parallel case. (2) *The victim is not spent.* This is decisive against the aggregative reading. In the Christian account, the good God brings out of evil is not bought at the victim's expense and paid out to others. It is offered *to her*: the raised child, every tear wiped away (Revelation 21:4; Romans 8:18, 28). A theodicy that sacrifices the part to the whole would contradict A4. One that promises good to each sufferer does not. Marilyn McCord Adams, an Anglican thinker building on the medieval Catholic tradition and on Julian of Norwich, argued this at length: God must defeat horrors *within the life of each participant*.
*Rejoinder:* Aquinas's "good of the universe" language is exactly the aggregative reading.
*Defense:* Aquinas speaks of the order of the whole, but he never says any rational creature is merely a means to it. Rational creatures are ordered to God for their own sakes (ST I q.22 a.2 ad 5; SCG III.112). The book should take its stand on that, not on the "whole and parts" image.
*Result:* **Held with repair. Serious until repaired.** As v2 now reads, a utilitarian can quote the evil paragraph against A6 in one sentence. One added sentence closes the gap (see §6).

**Exchange 8: W1(2) and W1(3): "received" and Anscombe's fork.**
*Utilitarian:* (2) "A worth that is ours, that we did not earn and no one conferred, is best explained as received." That does not follow. Sentience is unearned and unconferred, and the badness of pain is unearned and unconferred, yet no one calls them "received." "Had" will do. And Mill explains why the standing cannot be signed away without God: a freedom used to sell yourself into slavery defeats the purpose of freedom (*On Liberty*, ch. 5). (3) Anscombe's fork is "law, and so a Lawgiver, or only the excellences of our kind." We are on neither tine. Our "ought" is a *reason*, an agent-neutral truth (Sidgwick, Parfit). The fork leaves out its own most famous target. Anscombe coined "consequentialism" in that same paper. And "a debt still owed when the one we wronged is gone" does not make morality a law. It shows only that wrongs are permanent facts about the past.
*Defense:* On (3), granted. The fork has a third tine. But reasons do not forgive, cannot be wronged, and are not what remorse reaches toward, so the third tine sends us straight back to W1(1). On (2), the argument is stronger than v2's wording of it. The point is not that the worth is unearned. It is that the worth follows the *kind of being* one is, not one's powers: it belongs as fully to the newborn as to the man. A worth fixed by kind means the kind is *for* something, and that is A5's "what we are for." Then the question is the one Patrick put: who established what we are for?
*Result:* **Held with repair.** (2) must argue from kind to "for" to the One who made us for it, not from "unearned" to "received." (3) must name the third tine and answer it, or be dropped to a single clause.

**Exchange 9: W2: the by-product rival, and a law that is one.**
*Utilitarian:* W2's dilemma is "a passing product of evolution" or "an eternal order of ends." We take a third horn. Two necessary truths (suffering is bad; equal interests count equally), grasped by reason, which evolution built for other jobs and which sees necessary truths as a by-product, as it sees mathematics. That is not "an order of ends," and it is nowhere near "God's wisdom ordering all things." Round 1 asked v2 to meet this, and v2 met only the third-factor reply. As for "this law is one; it never requires us to do evil": we agree. Act-utilitarianism also denies genuine moral dilemmas, because there is always a best act. Unity is not evidence for your side over ours.
*Defense:* On the by-product: it moves the coincidence and does not remove it. A mind assembled for survival turns out to be fitted to truths that survival never needed, in mathematics and in morals alike. "By-product" names the fit; it does not explain it. On unity: fair. Unity alone does not tell the two views apart. The claim that does is *unity without reduction*. Utilitarianism gets one principle by making truth, justice and mercy into tools. Its famous crises (the useful lie, the scapegoat, the esoteric morality of Exchange 3) come exactly where a tool is put down. The book holds the facets together without any of them becoming a tool of another.
*Result:* **Held with repair.** Restore the by-product sentence, and make "one law" claim unity without reduction. Also, 1 Corinthians 10:13 concerns temptation. Its use for "the commandments can always be kept" has magisterial warrant (*Veritatis Splendor* 102–103; Trent, Session 6), and v2 should cite VS 102 with it so that it does not look like proof-texting.

**Exchange 10: The C line overclaims.**
*Utilitarian:* "It cannot tell wronging from worsening." Preference utilitarians draw that line by locating the wrong in the frustrated interest of the one wronged. Rule consequentialists build directed duties into the code. What is true is narrower: we *reduce* wronging to worsening, and at the critical level we have no place for a wrong that worsens no one. Also, credit where it is due: we explain A1 fully and A3's first half ("no vote can make it right"), and Sidgwick is the classic defender of A5's first half.
*Defense:* Accepted. The book gains by being exact. And add the cost v2 leaves out. Hedonistic utilitarianism must allow, in principle, that a comforting lie no one ever discovers is better than the truth (A7), and its founder thought even the theory might be better kept from the many.
*Result:* **Held with repair.** See the new C line in §6.

**Exchange 11 (brief): A5 and disordered inclination.**
*Utilitarian:* A5 says the "ought" lives in "an end written into our nature as an inclination we did not give ourselves." But by Patrick's own account our nature includes *disordered* desires, and evolution gave us those too, unasked. Inclination alone cannot pick out what we are *for*. You need a standard to sort good inclinations from bad ones, and that standard is the "ought" you were trying to locate. The is/ought gap opens again.
*Defense:* Aquinas does not say every inclination is a precept. Practical reason grasps as *goods* the objects of the inclinations proper to our rational nature: life, handing on life and raising children, truth (including about God), and living in society (ST I-II q.94 a.2). Disordered desire is a wound in that nature, not part of what it is for. Even so, the sorting is done by reason seeing goods, which is the book's thesis, not a datum the opponent grants.
*Result:* **Holds with repair** (as in Round 1, still not fixed). Mark the second half of A5 as the book's claim. It should not appear in a list headed "what almost everyone finds they cannot give up."

**Exchange 12 (brief): "Signed away when life grows hard."**
*Utilitarian:* A4's clause "or signed away when life grows hard" is the Church's teaching on euthanasia (CCC 2277). It is not something "almost everyone finds they cannot give up." In many Western countries most people support assisted dying for competent, suffering patients, and several countries allow it. Putting it under "almost everyone" is the same kind of overclaim Round 1 removed from the heading.
*Defense:* The book holds it, and A3 already gives the reason (consent cannot make a wrong right). But it is a conclusion, not a shared datum.
*Result:* **Fails as worded (in A4); holds if moved.** Take it out of A4. Let the book draw it later, from A3 and A4 together.

## 4. Ledger for v2

- **Form** (converging case, CCC 31/35): **Holds.** It is properly modest.
- **A heading** (what almost everyone finds they cannot give up; deniers named): **Holds with repair.** Hedonistic utilitarians also deny A7 in practice (Sidgwick's esoteric morality; biting the bullet on the experience machine). Add A7 to the list of items honestly denied, or soften "some serious thinkers have denied A2, A3, A4 or A6" to "several of these."
- **A1** (some things really wrong, not merely for creatures like us): **Holds.** Realist utilitarians grant it fully. It does not discriminate, and does not need to.
- **A2** (guilty, not merely mistaken or flawed; the deathbed promise): **Holds with repair.** "No one is ever worse off" holds only on hedonism. Desire-fulfillment views (Hare, Parfit's success theory) say she was worse off. Restate the case as "nothing in anyone's experience is different; whatever theory of welfare you hold, the wrong is done to *her*." (Exchange 2.)
- **A3** (no one on earth can make the wrong right; forgiveness heals the bond, does not remake the law): **Holds with repair.** It fixes Round 1's symmetry. But "No one else can forgive in his place" now clashes with W1(1). And "the debt is not [gone]" is the contested description. Call it a datum about remorse, which reaches for the person. (Exchange 1.)
- **A4** (equal dignity; not traded for totals; not signed away; equal worth vs. equal duty): **Holds with repair.** "Not to be traded" contradicts double effect and CCC 2309. Narrow it to "not killed or used as a means." "Signed away when life grows hard" is a conclusion, not a shared datum; move it. The ordo amoris clause ("equal worth is not equal duty") is a strength. Utilitarianism's impartiality loses to it with most readers (Williams's "one thought too many"). (Exchanges 5, 12.)
- **A5** (ability does not settle ought; the ought lives in what we are for): **Holds with repair.** The first half is granted (Sidgwick). The second half is the book's thesis and must be marked as such. It also needs "not every inclination," per ST I-II q.94 a.2. (Exchange 11.)
- **A6** (good asks something of the giver; some things never done; the transplant; CCC 1789): **Holds with repair.** The cost clause is now right. The constraint is at parity. Add a case with no knife (secret scapegoat or unconsented experiment), state the clean case as where the views part, and admit in one sentence that the absolute can cost a life. (Exchanges 4, 5, 6.)
- **A7** (bound to the truth when a lie would comfort and no one would know): **Holds.** This is the school's weak point. Hedonism must allow the undiscovered comforting lie, and Sidgwick allowed esoteric morality. Use it in C.
- **W1 opening** (two accounts; both agree the wrong is necessary; not even God makes cruelty right): **Holds.** This is the decisive fix of Round 1's failure.
- **W1(1) Forgiveness** (only a person can forgive; every wrong is also against God; only He can forgive without wronging the victim): **Holds with repair.** Remove "forgive in the victim's place." God forgives the offense against Himself and keeps the victim (CCC 958, 1459, 1850). (Exchange 1.)
- **W1(2) A standing ours but not ours to cancel; "best explained as received"**: **Holds with repair.** The step from "unearned and unconferred" to "received" is a non sequitur, since sentience is unearned too, and Mill offers a godless explanation of inalienability. The step that works is from "worth follows kind, not powers" to "the kind is for something" to "who made it for that?" (Exchange 8.)
- **W1(3) Anscombe's fork**: **Holds with repair.** It is a false dilemma as written. The third tine (agent-neutral reasons) is the utilitarian's own home. Name it and answer it: reasons do not forgive and cannot be wronged. (Exchange 8.)
- **W1 closing** (not a commander; a will that invents values can command but not bind; the Creator's authority): **Holds.** Utilitarians agree that voluntarism fails, so there is nothing to contest here.
- **W2** (bound to truth; Darwin's hive-bees; passing product vs. eternal order; third-factor reply; one law): **Holds with repair.** The by-product rival from Round 1 is still unanswered. "Fixed for any rational mind → an eternal order of ends, very near God's wisdom" overreaches against a two-axiom realism. "One law" does not tell the views apart unless it says "without reduction." Cite VS 102 with 1 Cor 10:13. Also note that the debunking argument can be turned against A6 (Exchange 4), so W2 should not say conscience reads truest wherever survival pulls against it without a qualifier. (Exchanges 4, 9.)
- **W3 paragraph 1** (only a person loves back; "it is good that you exist"; if the highest good is no one…): **Holds.** Benevolence says it is good that there is happiness. Love says it is good that *you* exist. The utilitarian cannot draw that distinction from inside his theory. This supports W3 as a clue.
- **W3 paragraph 2** (hiddenness; CCC 37, 847; Isaiah 45:15; LG 16): **Holds.** It is not this school's ground. The utilitarian presses evil, not hiddenness.
- **C intro** (every account stops somewhere; accommodate vs. explain): **Holds.**
- **C, error theory:** **Holds.**
- **C, sentiment / quasi-realism:** **Holds** (not this school's ground).
- **C, Kant:** **Holds** (not this school's ground). "A law I give myself I can untie" is a fair line.
- **C, contractualism:** **Holds** (not this school's ground).
- **C, utilitarianism:** **Holds with repair.** "Cannot tell wronging from worsening" overclaims against preference and rule versions; say "reduces wronging to worsening." Credit A1, A3's first half and A5's first half, and add the A7 cost. (Exchange 10.)
- **C, secular Aristotelian:** **Holds** (not this school's ground).
- **C, godless realism:** **Holds with repair.** The utilitarian is a godless realist with only one or two axioms. "Nothing explains why they hold together" is weaker against a monist. The answer that works is the one about scope (guilt toward a person, worth that follows kind, communion), not the one about unity.
- **C, impersonal sacred order:** **Holds** (not this school's ground).
- **C, Nietzsche:** **Holds** (not this school's ground).
- **C, A God who is Love** ("one stopping point instead of many"): **Holds with repair.** Against the utilitarian, "instead of many" is false: he stops at one axiom. Claim scope, not number of stopping points: "it explains what the others must deny or leave unexplained."
- **C, the other side of the scale (evil):** **Holds with repair. Serious until repaired.** "God permits evil only because He can bring a greater good out of it," together with Aquinas's "good of the whole," reads as the aggregative trade A6 forbids. "Not seeing a reason is weaker evidence than seeing a love" survives the utilitarian, who also uses the asymmetry of seeing and not seeing elsewhere.
- **Conclusion** (evil weighs against but does not overturn; cannot compel the one who gives up A4; "look at the newborn"): **Holds.** "Look at the newborn" is exactly the right last appeal against Singer. It asks for a *seeing*, which matches the result of Exchange 3.

## 5. Remaining sound objections (against v2 as written)

1. **A4's "not to be traded away for better totals" contradicts double effect and just-war proportionality (CCC 2309).** The Church does weigh foreseen deaths. What she forbids is *intending* an innocent's death. As worded, A4 is false of the book's own tradition. **Serious.**
2. **The evil paragraph's "greater good" reads as the trade A6 forbids.** Without the doing/permitting distinction and "the victim is not spent," a utilitarian can turn the theodicy against A6 in one sentence. **Serious.**
3. **A3 and W1(1) contradict each other on who may forgive in the victim's place.** This is an internal inconsistency, and Ivan Karamazov stands behind the objection. **Serious.**
4. **W2's debunking test can be turned against A6, and the by-product rival is still unanswered.** De Lazari-Radek and Singer argue that impartial benevolence survives debunking and constraints do not. W2 as worded gives them the premise. **Serious.**
5. **A4's "signed away when life grows hard" is not something almost everyone holds.** It is a Catholic conclusion placed in a list of shared data. **Serious** (it is the same honesty error Round 1 removed from the heading).
6. **The deathbed case's "no one is ever worse off" holds only on hedonism.** **Minor-to-Serious.** The case survives once narrowed.
7. **W1(3) Anscombe's fork omits the third tine (agent-neutral reasons); W1(2) steps from "unearned" to "received."** **Minor-to-Serious.**
8. **The C utilitarian line overclaims ("cannot tell"), and the God line's "one stopping point instead of many" is false against a monist.** **Minor.**
9. **A5's second half is still the thesis presented as a datum, and "inclination" needs ST I-II q.94 a.2's qualifier.** **Minor.**

**After the repairs in §6: NONE that our side cannot answer at least to parity.** What remains is the same honest standoff as in Round 1, now drawn more exactly. A utilitarian who will deny untradeable dignity, absolute constraints and wronging that worsens no one holds a coherent position. Hare even lets him keep all three as useful rules. The book cannot force him out. It can show three things. First, his practice requires believing what his theory says is false (Government House; Sidgwick's esoteric morality). Second, his strongest case, the vital conflict, costs the absolutist a life on the paradigm reading, while his own view puts every innocent within reach of the numbers. Third, his benevolence loves welfare, not *this* person. None of the objections is Fatal.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**A2: replace the case sentence:**
> Making the world worse and wronging someone are not the same. If I promise my dying friend to scatter her ashes at sea, and quietly bury them in a field to save money, nothing in anyone's experience is ever different, and still she was wronged. Whatever we think makes a life go well, the wrong was done to *her*.

**A3: replace "No one else can forgive in his place … the debt is not." with:**
> No one else can forgive in his place: not his friends, not a nation by vote, not the whole human race. And when the one we wronged is gone, our remorse does not settle into regret over a bad outcome; it still reaches for *her*.

**A4: replace the first two sentences:**
> **A4. Every human being has the same dignity, whatever he can or cannot do.** The newborn, the dying, the severely disabled, the enemy: equal, prior to anyone's recognition (*Dignitas Infinita* 1, 15). Not only equal in having their pain counted, but equal in this: no one may be killed or used as a means to make the totals come out better.

**A5: replace the last sentence:**

**A6: replace from "A surgeon may not…" to the end:**
> A surgeon may not kill one healthy patient to give his organs to five, even if no one would ever find out; a court may not hang an innocent man to calm a riot; doctors may not experiment on patients who never agreed. ## 7. New insights for the book

- **"Not to be traded" must become "not to be killed or used as a means."** The narrower line is the one the Church actually draws. It is also the stronger line, because it is exactly what separates the surgeon from the trolley switch and the soldier from the terror-bomber.
- **The absolute has a price, and honesty pays it.** One sentence admitting that the constraint can cost a life the numbers would have saved will disarm a philosophical reader more than any argument. Then name the other side's price: no innocent is safe from the numbers.
- **Every theodicy must keep A4 too.** God's greater good is not a trade of the one for the many. The good is offered to the sufferer. This sentence makes the evil paragraph and A6 consistent with each other.
- **Two-level utilitarianism predicts our feelings, so the book should rest on seeing, not feeling.** The question is not which theory explains the shudder at the transplant, but which lets the shudder be true.
- **Sidgwick's esoteric morality** is the fair, non-polemical way to show what utilitarian practice costs. The founder admitted that the theory might be better kept from the many. A morality that must hide itself is in tension with A7.
- **Benevolence vs. love:** benevolence says it is good that there is happiness; love says it is good that *you* exist. That is the cleanest statement of why W3 is beyond this school's reach.
- **The deathbed promise is strongest when it is stated without a theory of welfare.** Whatever makes a life go well, the wrong was done to *her*. That version sends every reply back to the question of who "her" still is.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "Side effects may be weighed. Persons may not be spent."
- "If an innocent may be killed whenever the numbers favour it, no innocent is safe from the numbers."
- "The good God brings out of evil is offered to the one who suffered, not bought with her."
- "Benevolence says it is good that there is happiness. Love says it is good that you exist."
- "A reason cannot be wronged, and it cannot forgive."
- "We trust it not because we feel it strongly, but because we see it."
