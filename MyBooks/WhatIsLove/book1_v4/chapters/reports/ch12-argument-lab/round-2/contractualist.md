# Round 2: Contractualism and the Second-Personal Standpoint vs. v2

## 1. School and voice

The voice is again a composite of T. M. Scanlon (an act is wrong if any principle allowing it could reasonably be rejected), Stephen Darwall (obligation as the authority of free and equal persons to make claims and demands of one another; second-personal reasons cannot be built out of impersonal ones), and John Rawls (justice as fairness among free and equal persons), with Eva Kittay brought in where she strengthens the school on dependency. In Round 1 this school broke W1 and forced the "received standing" rebuild. In Round 2 it takes v2's own new materials, the ashes example, the "no one else can forgive in his place" rule, and the forgiveness witness, and turns them against W1(1) and W1(2).

**Did v2 fix Round 1?** Mostly, yes. A2 now names the directed wrong ("worse vs. wronged"). A3 is rebuilt as Round 1 asked. A4 reaches those who can never make a claim and adds "equal worth is not equal duty," which this school welcomes. A5's last clause was moved. W1 no longer says "not laid by any human, so laid by Someone." C now names contractualism. Two Round 1 points were only half fixed: W2 still does not name the constructivist's price, and the clause added to A5 ("an inclination we did not give ourselves") brings the book's own reading back into a datum. The new attacks below are aimed at what v2 *added*.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges (Round 2 form)

**1. Starting point for justice.**
*Their answer:* We start inside the second-person standpoint. Darwall's point is that any claim or demand already presupposes that the one addressed has equal standing to ask for a justification back. You cannot step outside this "second-personal circle" to derive it from anything impersonal, and you do not need to: anyone who makes a demand has already granted it. What anyone is owed is what follows from principles no one could reasonably reject.
*Verdict: Holds, and its own admission marks its limit.* The circle explains why equality cannot be derived from impersonal facts. That is a candid admission that the school assumes equal standing rather than grounds it. It binds those who can address and be addressed. It says nothing yet about those who cannot.

**2. A hard case, resolved by their own principles.**
*Case (Scanlon's own):* A technician, Jones, is pinned by fallen equipment in a television transmitter room during a World Cup match. Freeing him means cutting the broadcast to millions for fifteen minutes; leaving him means an hour of agonizing shocks.
*Their answer:* Free Jones. Each viewer's complaint (a lost quarter-hour) is trivial beside Jones's, and complaints are compared one person at a time, not added up. No sum of small pleasures outweighs one person's agony.
*A second case, on mercy:* A drunk driver kills a teenage boy and later asks the boy's mother for forgiveness, both for her loss and for her son's death.
*Their answer:* She may forgive what was done *to her*. No one, not even she, may forgive what was done *to him*. That wrong stays unforgiven.
*Verdict: Holds on justice, borrows nothing.* It reaches the book's verdicts by its own route, and its refusal to add up harms is exactly A6's surgeon case. On mercy it is honest and bleak: it has a clear rule about who may forgive, and it accepts that some wrongs will never be forgiven. That rule is the weapon this school uses against W1(1) below.

**3. Ground of equal, unconditional dignity.**
*Their answer, strengthened since Round 1:* Not capacity alone. Scanlon allows that being born of human parents, and the relations that come with it, gives strong reason to count a being as one of us even when it lacks the capacities that make it a participant. Kittay adds that every human being is "some mother's child," a relation of dependency and care that does not depend on capacity. Equal standing can thus rest on a relation, not on a power.
*Verdict: Closer, but it borrows or breaks at one case.* A relational ground reaches the wanted newborn and the cherished disabled son. It protects the *abandoned* child, the one no mother claims, only by borrowing a relation she does not actually have. The ground becomes truly universal only if every human being is someone's child even when no human wants her. That is the theist's claim, not the contractualist's (see Exchange 5).

**4. Ultimate good for humans.**
*Their answer:* There is none to impose. Rawls offers the image of a "social union of social unions," in which each person's good is completed by the shared life of others. Scanlon's answer to "why be moral?" is the value of standing in mutual recognition with everyone.
*Verdict: Coherent and bounded.* It is a picture of a finite communion. It stops at death, and at those whom no one recognizes. It names no good that holds the whole of anyone (W3).

## 3. Debate highlights (escalating)

**Exchange 1. W1(1) contradicts A3.**
*Contractualist:* A3 says, rightly, "No one else can forgive in his place." That is the standing-to-forgive principle, and I accept it. W1(1) then says that when the victim is dead, God can forgive. Either God forgives *the wrong done to her*, and then He forgives in her place, which A3 forbids; or God forgives only *the wrong done to Himself* (Psalm 51:4), and then her wrong is still unforgiven, and W1(1) has not solved the problem it named. You have added a second creditor, not a forgiver for the first debt. Your own phrase concedes the point: "forgive without wronging the victim again." Forgiving her wrong in her absence *would* wrong her again.
*Defense:* There are two wrongs, and the Church keeps them apart. A murder is a wrong against the child and an offense against God (CCC 1849–1850). Even in the sacrament, God's forgiveness of the sin does not cancel what is owed to the neighbor; the penitent must still repair what harm he can (CCC 1459). God forgives what is His to forgive. He does not forgive in her place.
*Contractualist:* Then the absent victim's wrong is still unforgiven on your view, exactly as on mine.
*Verdict: Failed as written; Held with repair.* W1(1) as worded either violates A3 or leaves the victim's wrong untouched. The repair (Exchange 3) moves the weight from "God forgives her wrong" to "God keeps her, so her forgiveness remains possible."

**Exchange 2. "Raising her" is the wrong answer by A2's own test.**
*Contractualist:* W1(1) says only God can forgive without wronging the victim again, "because only He can raise the victim and make her whole." But A2 taught us that worsening and wronging are different: the friend whose ashes were buried in a field was wronged though no one was worse off. By the same token, making a victim whole repairs the *harm*; it does not address the *wrong*. Compensation is not pardon. If I steal your car and a stranger buys you a better one, I am still unforgiven. Resurrection undoes the worsening and leaves the wronging exactly where it was.
*Defense:* Granted, and the point is a good one. The resurrection matters for a different reason: it means the one we wronged is not gone. A2 says guilt sends us back to the person for pardon. If she is raised, there is someone to go back to. The resurrection does not forgive the murderer; it keeps the only one who can.
*Verdict: Failed as written; Held with repair.* "Make her whole" must not be offered as the ground of forgiveness. Its true role is to keep the victim, and with her the possibility of her own forgiveness.

**Exchange 3. Then immortality, not God, does the work, and the dead are already creditors.**
*Contractualist:* Two rejoinders. First, if what solves the absent-victim problem is that she survives, then what you need is survival, not a Lawgiver. Second, your own ashes example shows the dead can be wronged now. So the debt is owed to her, here and now, with no living creditor needed. C says of godless realism that nothing explains "to whom guilt is owed when no one on earth is left." It is owed to her. The real question is not *to whom* but *who can release*, and on that point your view, like mine, says: only she can.
*Defense:* Both points land partly. On the second: yes, the dead can be wronged; A2 shows it. But the dead cannot forgive. On every account without God, the murdered child is a creditor who can never release, forever. That is coherent, and it is the bleak picture W1 asks the reader to weigh. On the first: no serious account without God offers the victim's survival; survival "with no one to keep her" is not a live option. What fits the shape of the guilt is a world in which no one we wronged is lost, and only Someone who never loses anyone could make that so. That is a clue, not a proof.
*Contractualist:* So the forgiveness witness is a hope dressed as an explanation.
*Defense:* It is a clue about what would fit. W3 already says a longing does not prove its answer. W1(1) should say the same.
*Verdict: Held with repair, at reduced weight.* W1(1) survives as a clue. C must stop saying the rivals have no one "to whom guilt is owed" and say instead that they have no one who can ever release it.

**Exchange 4. W1(2): "unearned and unconferred" does not mean "received."**
*Contractualist:* W1(2) says a worth that is ours, that we did not earn and no one conferred, is best explained as received. This is Round 1's non sequitur moved one step back. Many properties are neither earned nor conferred and are not received either: they are *constitutive*. Seven is prime; no one made it so and it was not given to seven. Our standing comes with being the kind of creature that can give and answer reasons. "Received" quietly assumes a giver. That is the conclusion, put into the premise.
*Defense:* Grant the constitutive point. Our worth comes with *what we are*, not with anything we do. But seven is necessary; we are not. None of us gave himself his being. A worth that comes with what we are is no less a gift than the being it comes with. So the moral question becomes the old question of existence: who gave us to ourselves?
*Contractualist:* Then W1(2) is the argument from contingency in moral clothes. It adds weight only if contingency already points to God.
*Defense:* Partly true, and the chapter should say so plainly. But it adds two things contingency alone does not. First, it explains why the worth is *equal*: it follows the gift of being, not the unequal gifts of ability. Second, it explains why the right response to our worth is gratitude and not pride, which is how good people actually hold it.
*Verdict: Failed as written; Held with repair.* The inference "unearned, so received" is invalid. Rebuilt as "worth comes with being, and being is given," it holds, but it openly borrows the question of existence. That is honest, and it links Chapter 12 to the book's account of creation.

**Exchange 5. The relational ground: "some mother's child."**
*Contractualist:* You claimed in Round 1 that we can reach the newborn only by borrowing a worth based on nature. We need not. Scanlon allows that being born of human parents, with the relations that come with it, gives reason to count a being as one of us. Kittay grounds equality in the plain fact that every human being is some mother's child. That is a relation, not a teleology and not a God.
*Defense:* It is the best answer this school has, and it is close to the truth. Every human being is someone's child. The test is the abandoned infant no one wants, the one left in a ditch as the ancient world left weak infants to die (A4; CCC 37–38). Is she still someone's child? On a relational ground, only by courtesy: we treat her *as if* she belonged to someone. If there is a God who is Love, she belongs to Someone in fact, and was wanted before anyone saw her.
*Contractualist rejoinder:* We extend the relation to her by solidarity. That is enough to oblige us.
*Defense:* It is enough to oblige you if you choose solidarity. It does not make her worth something that is *there* before anyone extends anything (DI 15). That is the line between "a commitment we make" and "a worth that is there," which C already draws for the expressivist.
*Verdict: Held.* This is still the strongest point for our side against this school, and v2 does not yet use it. It belongs in W1(2).

**Exchange 6. Anscombe's fork is a false choice.**
*Contractualist:* W1(3) says: either morality is a law, and then it has a Lawgiver, or we drop "law" and keep only the excellences of our kind. That leaves out the third road, which is my whole school. Kant's kingdom of ends, and Darwall's moral community, keep morality as law: real obligation, real demands, real blame. The lawgivers are the persons themselves, each holding every other to account. Anscombe's fork omits the one rival C now admits is the nearest neighbor.
*Defense:* Fair. The honest form is a three-way choice. Keep morality as law with a Lawgiver; make persons themselves its joint lawgivers; or drop "law" and keep excellence. The second road explains a great deal about the wrongs competent adults do to one another. It stops in two places: it has no lawgiver who speaks for those who can never give law (A4, Exchange 5), and it has no one who can release a debt when the one we wronged is gone (Exchange 3).
*Verdict: Failed as written; Held with repair.* W1(3) reproduced, inside W1, the omission Round 1 found in C.

**Exchange 7. A Lawgiver who holds us to account must be answerable.**
*Contractualist:* Darwall's deepest point: second-personal authority is reciprocal. Whoever addresses a demand to me grants that I may ask him for a justification back. If God holds us to account, we may hold God to account. And here is the account we demand. C says God permits evil "because He can bring a greater good out of it" (CCC 311–312). Read plainly, that is the surgeon's principle you rejected in A6: one child's suffering is allowed so that good may come to others. Scanlon's restriction holds against God too: a burden on one person cannot be justified by benefits to others. A principle that let a bystander with infinite power and no cost watch a child suffer for the sake of a better whole is one the child could reasonably reject.
*Defense:* Two answers. First, the reciprocity is real, and Scripture keeps it. Abraham asks, "Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?" (Genesis 18:25); Job demands an answer; the psalms of lament argue with God. The God of the Bible lets Himself be questioned, and the Cross is the place where He answers by entering the suffering Himself. Between Creator and creature the authority is not equal, as between parent and child, but the child may still ask "why?", and the Father does not forbid it. Second, the greater-good teaching must be read the way the best Catholic thinkers read it. God does not *do* evil; He permits it (CCC 311). And the Catholic philosopher Eleonore Stump argues that whatever good God brings from a person's suffering must be good *for that person*, not merely for others. (Paraphrase; verify against *Wandering in Darkness* before it enters the book.) On that reading, no child is spent to buy good for anyone else, and the individualist restriction is kept, not broken. Whether it is kept for the fawn in the fire, the chapter already admits we cannot see.
*Contractualist rejoinder:* "Good for her, at the end" is a promise, not a justification I can inspect.
*Defense:* Agreed. The chapter says exactly that: not seeing a reason is weaker evidence than seeing a love, and the account without God does not remove evil but removes hope for its victims. That is parity, not victory.
*Verdict: Held with repair, at parity.* As written, the "greater good" line can be read as the aggregation A6 condemns. It must say that God does not trade one person's suffering for others' gain.

**Exchange 8. A4 smuggles in a contested conclusion.**
*Contractualist:* A4 says dignity is not to be "signed away when life grows hard." That is a ruling against assisted suicide, placed inside a datum that "almost everyone finds they cannot give up." The leading figures of my school, Rawls and Scanlon among them, joined Dworkin, Nagel, Nozick and Thomson in the 1997 "Philosophers' Brief" supporting a right to assisted dying. (Verify signatories before use.) They hold equal dignity as firmly as you do and give up that clause. Many readers will too. Packing it into A4 makes your strongest datum look loaded.
*Defense:* Granted on placement. The Church's teaching stands, but it is a conclusion the chapter can argue for, not a datum the reader must bring. A4 is strongest when it says only what nearly everyone grants: equal worth, prior to recognition, not to be traded for better totals.
*Verdict: Held with repair.* Move the clause out of A4.

**Exchange 9. A7: truth's "ought" is built into belief.**
*Contractualist:* The "ought" of A7 is not a debt to anyone. Belief simply aims at truth; you cannot believe what you take to be false. That norm is part of what belief is, so it needs no source.
*Defense:* That explains why I cannot believe what I see is false. It does not explain why I *ought to keep looking* when not looking would be easier: why I may not avoid the test result, the evidence against my cause, or the friend's honest rebuke. That is a practical ought, and it binds where no one would ever know.
*Verdict: Held with repair (Minor).* A7 should name the duty to seek, not only the duty to believe what one sees.

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**Against v2 as written:**

1. **W1(1) is inconsistent with A3.** If no one may forgive in the victim's place, God may not either; if He forgives only the offense against Himself, the victim's wrong is untouched. **Serious.**
2. **W1(1) grounds forgiveness in repair.** "Raise her and make her whole" answers worsening, not wronging, which A2 itself distinguishes. **Serious** (it is the same sentence as item 1; one repair fixes both).
3. **W1(2) is a non sequitur.** "Unearned and unconferred" does not imply "received"; constitutive worth fits equally. **Serious.**
4. **W1(3) is a false dichotomy.** It omits joint legislation by persons, the nearest rival. **Serious.**
5. **The theodicy line conflicts with A6 and the individualist restriction.** A greater good *for others* is the surgeon's principle. **Serious.**
6. **A4 carries a contested conclusion on assisted dying.** **Minor** for validity, but it costs the strongest datum its near-universal standing with this school's readers.
7. **C's "to whom guilt is owed" and "no one to answer to."** False by A2's own ashes case: the dead are still owed. The point is release, not creditor. **Minor.**
8. **A7 readable as only the norm built into belief.** **Minor.**

**After the repairs in section 6: NONE at Fatal or Serious.** The forgiveness witness survives as a clue at moderate weight: it shows that every account without God must say some wrongs are forgiven never, by anyone, and that what fits the shape of guilt is a Keeper of the victim. Received worth survives by openly borrowing the question of existence, and wins at the abandoned child. The trilemma form of Anscombe's point is fair and still favors our side at the edges. The theodicy reaches parity, not victory, and the chapter already says so. The contractualist's best residual line is that "God keeps her" is a hope, not an explanation one can inspect. That is answered at parity by saying so, which W3 already does for longing.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**A4** (drop the contested clause):
> **A4. Every human being has the same dignity, whatever he can or cannot do.** The newborn, the dying, the severely disabled, the enemy: equal, prior to anyone's recognition, and never to be traded away for better totals (Dignitas Infinita 1, 15). Equal worth is not equal duty: we owe more to our own parents and children than to strangers, but no one is worth less. Reason can see this (DI 1), yet history shows how easily it is lost: the same ancient world that taught that slaves are men also left weak and deformed infants to die (CCC 37–38).

*(The Church's teaching on euthanasia belongs in the chapter's argued text, where it can be shown to follow from A4, not in the datum.)*

**A5** (end after "what we are *for*"; move the Thomist clause to W1(2)):
> **A5. What we are able to do does not settle what we ought to do.** We are capable of truth and lies, justice and injustice, mercy and cruelty, giving and grasping. Listing what we can do never by itself tells us which to choose. The "ought" lives in what we are *for*.

**A7:**
> **A7. We are bound to the truth, even when a lie would comfort us and no one would ever know.** Not only to believe what we see, but to keep looking when looking away would be easier. Following the evidence is itself something we *ought* to do.

**W1(1):**
> (1) *Forgiveness.* A wronged person needs forgiving, and only a person can forgive. Only the one we wronged can forgive what was done to her; no one may do it in her place. Yet every wrong against a person is also a wrong against the One who made her and loves her (Psalm 51:4; CCC 1850), and that wrong He can forgive. Even then, what we owe her is still owed (CCC 1459). When she is dead, or cannot be found, no one on earth can forgive what was done to her. If there is no one beyond the earth, that wrong is forgiven never, by anyone: the murdered child is owed forever and can never release. If there is a God who is Love, He does not forgive in her place. He keeps her. The one we wronged is not lost to Him, so the way back to her is still open. A truth can be true; only a person can forgive, and only Someone who never loses anyone can keep forgiveness possible.

**W1(2):**
> (2) *A worth we did not give ourselves.* The victim's claim is truly his own. Yet he did not make it, cannot sign it away, and it belongs as fully to the newborn who cannot speak as to the man who can. It comes with what he is, not with what he can do: an end written into our nature that we did not give ourselves (ST I-II q.91 a.2). A worth that comes with what we are is no less a gift than the being it comes with, and none of us gave himself his being. So the question of our worth becomes the question of our existence: who gave us to ourselves, and to each other? Every human being is someone's child. The test is the abandoned infant no one wants. Is she still someone's child? If there is a God who is Love, she is. She was wanted before anyone saw her.

**W1(3):**
> (3) *Law, agreement, or excellence.* The Catholic philosopher Elizabeth Anscombe saw that to speak of a moral *law* while denying any lawgiver is to keep a word after giving up what made sense of it. Three roads are open. Keep morality as law, and it has a Lawgiver. Make persons themselves its joint lawgivers, each holding every other to account (Kant's kingdom of ends; today's contractualists): this explains much about the wrongs grown persons do to one another, but no one in it speaks with authority for those who can never give law, and no one in it can release a debt when the one we wronged is gone. Or drop "law" and keep only the excellences of our kind. If A2–A4 are real, the second road stops short and the third gives too much up.

**W1 closing paragraph** (add two sentences at the end):
> … because He is Himself the Good He asks of us. The law binds because He is good; it can be forgiven because He is Someone. And a Lawgiver who holds us to account lets Himself be questioned: "Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?" (Genesis 18:25).

**W2** (one sentence):
> If instead it holds for any rational mind, then there are eternal truths about what is good and what we owe, which minds can know and no one made. That is very near what the Church means by God's wisdom ordering all things (ST I-II q.91 a.1). And if the law is only what reasoners like us would agree to, a differently made reasoner would have a different law, and A1 is lost one level up.

**C, Feeling refined** (last clause):
> … and a wrong no living person can forgive can never be forgiven at all.

**C, What we owe each other:**
> - **What we owe each other** (contractualism) explains why a wrong is done to someone and why we answer to the one we wronged. It assumes persons' standing rather than explaining it. It reaches the newborn and the profoundly disabled through trustees, or through the bond of being born one of us, which protects the wanted child better than the abandoned one. And when the one we wronged is gone, it can say she is still wronged, but not that she can ever forgive.

**C, Godless realism** (replace the third item in the list):
> … why minds should read them truly, who can ever release a debt when the one we wronged is gone, or why worth follows kind rather than capacity.

**C, evil** (replace the CCC 311–312 clause):
> … God permits evil only because He can bring a greater good out of it (CCC 311–312). He does not do the evil, and a God who is Love does not spend one child to buy good for others: whatever good He brings from her suffering must be good for her too, and she will see it.

## 7. New insights for the book

- **God does not forgive in the victim's place. He keeps the victim.** This is truer to Catholic teaching than v2's wording and stronger against this school. The sacrament itself keeps the two debts apart: God forgives the sin, and what is owed to the neighbor is still owed (CCC 1459). The resurrection is not the means of forgiving the murderer; it is the victim's justice, because it keeps the only one who can forgive him.
- **The dead can be wronged; they cannot forgive.** v2's ashes example proves the first half, and the first half is shared by every school. The second half is where the accounts part. Without God, some victims are owed forever and can never release.
- **"Someone's child."** The best secular ground of equal dignity is relational: every human being is some mother's child. The book can honor it and ask the one question it cannot answer: is the abandoned infant still someone's child? This is A4, W1(2) and W3 in a single picture, and it fits a book about love.
- **Worth comes with being, and being is given.** The honest form of "received standing" admits that it borrows the question of existence. That is not a weakness to hide; it ties Chapter 12 to the book's account of creation.
- **The law binds because He is good; it is forgiven because He is Someone.** This answers Round 1's "necessity, not personhood" cleanly: personhood does its work in forgiving and in giving, not in making the law hold.
- **God lets Himself be asked.** Darwall's reciprocity, turned into Scripture: Abraham at Sodom (Genesis 18:25), Job, the lament psalms. The Cross is God answering, not explaining. This links W1 to the evil section.
- **No child is spent for someone else's good.** The individualist restriction, which this school and A6 share, is the right shape for Catholic theodicy. Eleonore Stump (Catholic, Thomist) is the witness; verify before use.

**Lines worth keeping:**
- "God does not forgive in her place. He keeps her."
- "The dead can still be wronged. They cannot forgive."
- "Every human being is someone's child. The question is the child no one wanted."
- "She was wanted before anyone saw her."
- "The law binds because He is good. It can be forgiven because He is Someone."
- "No child is spent to buy someone else's good."
- "The Judge of all the earth lets Himself be asked."
