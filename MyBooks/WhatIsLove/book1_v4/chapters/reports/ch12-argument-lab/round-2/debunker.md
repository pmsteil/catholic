# Round 2: Moral Error Theory + Evolutionary Debunking + Cognitive Science of Religion vs. v2

## 1. School and voice

This is the same voice as Round 1, pressed harder. It draws on the error theorists (J. L. Mackie, Richard Joyce, Jonas Olson, Bart Streumer, Richard Garner), the debunkers (Sharon Street, Michael Ruse, Joshua Greene, Wilkins and Griffiths, Buchanan and Powell), and the cognitive science of religion (Justin Barrett, Pascal Boyer, Ara Norenzayan, Robert Frank on commitment, attachment and terror-management research). Its stance has not changed. Morality *claims* binding, uncancelable, person-directed authority. A Lawgiver would be the natural home for such authority. There is no such authority and no such Lawgiver, and evolved minds in evolved cultures explain why we believe in both. Round 1 conceded the conditional and left a Moorean standoff. This round tests whether v2's new material (truth in A7 and W2, the hive-bee dilemma, the forgiveness argument, the evil paragraph) adds weight or opens new gaps.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

This round uses new cases and pushes harder on each answer, so it does not simply repeat Round 1.

**1. Starting point for justice.** No one is owed anything categorically. Justice is a set of conventions that let creatures with limited sympathy cooperate (Hume, Mackie). "Owed" is a working word inside that practice. A fictionalist (Joyce) keeps it because it steadies our commitments. An abolitionist (Garner) drops it and keeps the compassion.
→ **Coherent; it borrows words openly and no substance.** It breaks where the book says it breaks: anyone who cannot take part in the practice gets standing only through other people's sympathy. New point this round: a fiction kept *because it is useful* can be dropped when it stops being useful. And its usefulness ends exactly at the costly cases: the disabled newborn, the dying who need expensive care, the enemy who has surrendered. The fiction protects people where protection is cheap and lets them go where it is dear.

**2. A hard case (new): a dying patient's family asks the doctor to tell him he is recovering, so that his last weeks are peaceful.** The error theorist settles it by what the people involved want, plus "if" reasons: if we want a medicine people can trust, doctors should not lie, and if the patient has said he wants the truth, his wishes govern. With no such wish on record, a kind lie may be best.
→ **Coherent, and it borrows none of the book's definitions.** It does lean on truth, though only as something useful. The cost: it cannot say the patient is *owed* the truth, only that lying is usually bad policy. Watch what happens when the same school argues for its own view (Exchange 2). It does not treat truth as merely useful there.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** It denies that equal dignity is true. The belief is valuable, historically late, and spread mainly by Christianity. It is worth keeping as a fiction or as a commitment.
→ **An honest denial.** New pressure from the book's side: the debunker's own science shows that our instinctive regard for infants is *unequal*. Adults respond more warmly to healthy, attractive infants than to disfigured ones (the baby-schema research; Glocker and colleagues 2009; Langlois and colleagues 1995 on maternal behavior). So the equal-dignity judgment, where people hold it, runs *against* the evolved response. That makes it harder to debunk than the instincts it overrides (Exchange 10).

**4. Ultimate good.** There is none. A good life is satisfied considered desires and a working body and society. Its guiding ideal, in practice, is lucid truthfulness: seeing the world without comforting illusions.
→ **Coherent within a life. It breaks at "ultimately."** It also shows a telling preference. This school's highest value is not pleasure but *truthfulness kept even when a lie would comfort*. That is A7. The school lives by the one "ought" it has the hardest time explaining (Exchange 2).

**Fairness note:** this school still answers the four challenges more cleanly than any rival that borrows. Its weakness is not that it contradicts itself. Its weakness is what it costs, and in A7 it now faces a cost to its own way of life.

### 3b. Exchanges

**Exchange 1: The new heading changes what is being explained.**
*Opponent:* v2 calls A "what almost everyone finds they cannot give up." That describes our minds, not the world. Granted: almost everyone finds they cannot give up A1–A7. My account explains that fact with one cause. Selection and enculturation built moral convictions to be sticky, because convictions that could be argued away would be useless as commitments (Frank, Joyce). The same cognitive equipment, agency detection and a moralized full-access watcher, explains why people believe in God (Barrett, Boyer, Norenzayan). On your own heading, I explain A1–A7 *and* belief in God from one stopping point. C's claim that God is "one stopping point instead of many" is then matched, and I add nothing that cannot be observed.
*Reply:* The heading was softened for honesty, but the case never meant A as a report about psychology. It means A as true. Your single cause explains why we *believe* A. It explains A as truths only by declaring them false. The comparison in C asks which account explains A1–A7 *if they are true*. An account that explains a belief by saying it is mistaken cannot also count as explaining what the belief is about.
*Rejoinder:* Then say so. As written, a careful reader takes the heading at its word, and on that reading I win the comparison.
*Reply:* Granted. The heading must state A as claims of truth. It should also give the Moorean reason for trusting them: what we cannot give up after honest reflection, we have reason to trust until something stronger shows it false.
**Result: Held with repair.** A **Serious** gap as written, because it hands this school the comparison. The heading wording needs fixing.

**Exchange 2: A7, the obligation to truth.**
*Opponent (Olson):* "We are bound to the truth" names no categorical authority. "You ought to believe what the evidence supports" means two things. First, the evidence supports p, which is a plain fact. Second, if you aim at truth, believe p. Or it is a standard *internal* to belief, like a rule of chess: a belief is correct if true, the way a bishop's move is legal if diagonal. That is no queerer than chess. And if it is queer, I go global with Streumer: all normativity is in error, including this one.
*Reply:* Test it on the case in A7 itself: a lie that would comfort and that no one would ever discover. Suppose the epistemic ought is only "if you want truth." Then you cannot fault a man who prefers the comfort of believing in God. He simply has other aims. Yet your whole school's way of life (Challenge 4) is a refusal of comfortable belief, and you fault the believer for exactly that. The chess analogy fails for the same reason. No one is blamed for not playing chess, but you blame the man who believes what he wishes. And Streumer's global view admits we *cannot believe* it, so it takes itself out of the contest of reasons.
*Rejoinder:* I don't blame him morally. I think less of him, the way I think less of a bad driver.
*Reply:* You think less of a bad driver *as a driver*, and he can answer that he does not care to drive well. Ask yourself whether the believer can answer that he does not care to believe truly, and leave it at that. If he can, your critique of religion is a matter of taste. If he cannot, there is a categorical "ought" of truth, and it is as hard to explain without a Source as any moral one.
*Rejoinder (new):* Then A7 wounds you as well. Catholic faith asks assent beyond what the evidence compels, and Clifford's rule condemns it.
*Reply:* The Church holds that faith is not belief against the evidence. Its assent rests on reasons for credibility. It is not a blind movement of the mind, and reason can examine it (Vatican I, *Dei Filius* ch. 3; CCC 156). A7 binds both sides equally, and the book should say so. The Church itself teaches A7: all are bound to seek the truth and hold to it once known (CCC 2104, quoting *Dignitatis Humanae* 1). One narrowing is needed. A7 should forbid believing *against* the evidence for comfort. It should not demand that a dying man hear every fact, because declining to look is not the same as believing a lie.
**Result: Held, with a wording repair.** A7 is a real gain for v2. It forces the error theorist either to cheapen his own critique of religion into taste or to go global. This is the companions-in-guilt argument (Terence Cuneo's *The Normative Web* develops it at length; Cuneo is a Christian philosopher), and it is the strongest thing v2 added against this school.

**Exchange 3: W2 uses A7 for two different things.**
*Opponent:* W2 opens with "We are bound to truth (A7), though survival often runs on useful errors," and never says what follows. The sentence runs two different claims together. One is the *norm* of truth: we ought to believe truly (Exchange 2). The other is the *reliability* of our minds: do they in fact track truth? The norm is an argument against error theory. Reliability is Street's problem for the godless realist. Then consider the empirical half, "survival often runs on useful errors." It is either true or false, and either way it hurts W2. If it is true, then wherever errors pay, our faculties are untrustworthy, and moral beliefs, whose usefulness is my whole story, are the prime example of a useful error. In that case A7 strengthens the debunking of A1. If it is false, and McKay and Dennett's review of the evidence (2009) found adaptive false belief to be rare, mostly limited to flattering self-assessment, then evolution explains why our minds track truth without any Source. In that case the premise does no work.
*Reply:* The objection is right about the wording. The defensible use of A7 in W2 is the norm, not reliability. Evolution explains well why our senses and inferences mostly get the world right. It does not explain why we *ought* to follow the evidence when a false belief would serve us better. The norm of truth binds every mind that can reason, and a Source who is Truth (ST I q.16 a.5) explains that norm and the moral law together. The "useful errors" clause should go.
**Result: Failed as written; Held with repair.** **Serious.** W2's first sentence must be rewritten to make the norm of truth its premise and drop the claim about useful errors.

**Exchange 4: The hive-bee dilemma.**
*Opponent:* The dilemma has two horns. On the first, the human form is a passing product, so A1 is "wrong only for creatures shaped like us." On the second, the law is "fixed for any rational mind," so there is an eternal order of ends. Both horns misfire. (a) Darwin's bees do not torture anyone for fun. They kill brothers for the hive, out of duty. The thought experiment shows that *mid-level* norms (who may be killed, when, and for whom) depend on a species' life. It says nothing about gratuitous cruelty, which is A1's example. So the first horn's "could have been otherwise" does not reach A1. (b) Your own tradition rejects the second horn as stated. For Aquinas the natural law is the rational creature's share in the eternal law *according to its nature*. Much of its content, such as the precepts tied to nourishment, family and sex, is fitted to the human kind. It is not "fixed for any rational mind." Secondary precepts can even vary (ST I-II q.94 a.4–5). v2's A5 itself puts the "for" inside our nature. So the theist also holds that much of the law is "for creatures shaped like us." The dilemma tells you to reject a position the Thomist himself holds.
*Reply:* Granted, and the repair makes the argument better. The real fork is not "species-relative or valid for every mind." It is "is our kind an accident, or is it intended?" Much of the law is fitted to our kind, and the Church has never denied it. The *core* is not: gratuitous cruelty, betrayal, treating a person as a thing would be wrong for any creature that can reason and suffer. Where the content is species-relative, the theist says the species is an idea in the mind that made it, not an accident of selection. In that case the law fitted to our kind is still an order set by wisdom (ST I-II q.91 a.1–2). The bee case then shows exactly what accident would mean: fratricide could have been duty.
*Rejoinder:* "Intended" is the point at issue.
*Reply:* Yes. But now the dilemma is fair: accident or intention, with its cost on each side named.
**Result: Failed as written; Held with repair.** **Serious.** v2 also says "no one really believes that torturing a child for fun is wrong only for creatures shaped like us." That is false: Ruse, Joyce and Street believe something close to it. It also brings back the "cannot honestly deny" overclaim the heading removed. Say "few of us can believe."

**Exchange 5 (new): Moral error follows self-interest.**
*Opponent:* Theism predicts a mind made for the law. Look at where the mind fails. v2's own A4 tells the story: the ancient world that called slaves men also left deformed infants to die. Conscience is reliable where cooperation pays: among in-group members, kin and reciprocators. It fails exactly where it doesn't: the slave, the stranger, the infant who cannot repay. That is the error pattern debunking predicts. Theism has to explain it by adding a further story.
*Reply:* The Church predicts the same pattern from her own principles, not as an afterthought. Disordered self-love is the root of every sin (ST I-II q.77 a.4). Passion, bad custom and self-interest cloud the application of first principles while leaving the principles themselves known (q.94 a.4, a.6). So both accounts predict errors that follow self-interest. The datum that tells them apart is the *correction*. Again and again, conscience has moved against interest toward those who cannot repay.
*Rejoinder (Singer; Buchanan and Powell):* The correction is predicted too. Reason works like an escalator: once you reason impartially within your group, consistency pushes you to extend the circle. Buchanan and Powell add that inclusion grows under security and shrinks under threat, so it depends on conditions rather than pointing at truth.
*Reply:* Your escalator is driven by *consistency*, which is a norm of reasoning, an "ought" (A7). The debunking story explains moral progress only by relying on the one "ought" it cannot reduce. Fragility under threat is just what q.94 predicts: passion clouds. The pattern fits both accounts; the engine of correction fits one better.
**Result: Held at parity, with a slight edge after repair.** It is **Serious** if v2 leaves it unanswered, and right now v2 does: A4 states the historical fact, and W2 never draws on it. The repair puts q.77 a.4 and the escalator point into W2.

**Exchange 6: W1(1), the forgiveness argument.**
*Opponent:* W1(1) runs as follows. A wronged person needs forgiving. When the victim is dead, no one on earth can forgive. Every wrong is also against God. Only He can forgive without wronging the victim again, because only He can raise her. Four problems.
(a) *It assumes its conclusion.* "Every wrong against a person is also a wrong against the One who made that person precious (Psalm 51:4; CCC 1850)" is a premise only a theist grants. It is the conclusion, cited from authority.
(b) *Its hidden premise is a hope.* Take away (a), and the argument needs this premise: every real debt has someone who can forgive it. Why believe that? Secular thinkers hold that some guilt is never forgiven and some deeds cannot be. Hannah Arendt and Vladimir Jankélévitch each wrote that certain evils lie beyond forgiveness (paraphrase only). That is tragedy, not evidence of God. Inferring a forgiver from the need for one is believing what would comfort. A7 forbids exactly that.
(c) *v2's own example cuts against it.* The ashes case shows that a dead woman can be wronged, so wrongs do not need a living victim. The secular answer to "to whom is the debt owed when she is gone?" is: to her, as she was. Philosophers who defend wrongs to the dead (Joel Feinberg, George Pitcher) need no present claimant. "I wronged her and cannot repair it" is a complete, timeless fact.
(d) *The persistence of guilt is predicted.* Think of a guilt system that switched off when the victim died. It would reward getting rid of the people you had wronged. Selection for reliable commitment would build guilt that outlives the victim. A3's "the debt is not gone" is exactly what a well-built commitment device would feel like.
*Reply:* (a) Conceded. Psalm 51 and CCC 1850 must come *after* the inference, as the Church's answer, not as its premise. (b) Conceded in part. The argument may not infer a forgiver from the wish for one. (c) This is the real point at issue, and it can be restated fairly. A debt still owed *now* is a present claim, and a present claim needs someone who holds it now. Either the claim died with her, so that "I still owe her" is strictly false and remorse is only grief for a past fact, or someone still holds it. The secular realist may answer "to her, as she was." Then it is a debt no one can ever receive, release or forgive. That is consistent, and the book should say plainly that it is consistent. (d) Your device explains the *feeling*. Whether the debt is real is A2–A3 again: Moorean parity.
*Rejoinder:* So W1(1) does not show that God exists. It shows that theism *offers* something naturalism doesn't: forgiveness for the unforgivable. That is a benefit, like the evil paragraph's "hope for evil's victims," which v2 honestly calls "not evidence."
*Reply:* Half right. The datum W1(1) can claim is not that forgiveness is offered. It is that "still owed" is a present-tense debt, which the rival can keep only by turning it into "once owed, now unpayable." The reader can test which description fits the guilt he actually knows. The forgiveness offered is a cost on the other side, and it should be counted as a cost, not as evidence.
**Result: Failed as written; Held at parity after repair.** **Serious.** As written it begs the question with (a), rests on a hope with (b), and ignores the rival reading of its own example with (c). The repaired form, built on the present claim and stating the rival honestly, holds at parity.

**Exchange 7 (new): Ivan's objection, in this school's voice.**
*Opponent:* "Only He can forgive without wronging the victim again, because only He can raise the victim and make her whole." Dostoevsky's Ivan Karamazov already answered this (*The Brothers Karamazov*, "Rebellion"): not even the mother may forgive the torturer for her child's suffering. Only the child may. Making a victim whole is compensation, and compensation does not give anyone standing to forgive on her behalf. A God who forgives the torturer *for the child* overrides the child, whatever He later gives her. And "raise the victim" is a Christian-only premise put into an argument meant for non-Christians.
*Reply:* The objection hits v2's wording, not Catholic teaching. God does not forgive *in place of* the victim. He forgives what was done against Him, since every sin is also against Him (CCC 1850). Justice to the victim is His to restore, and the victim herself, raised and made whole, can forgive in her own name. That keeps Ivan's principle (only the victim forgives her own wrong) and answers his question of who could ever do it. The resurrection belongs in the Church's answer, after the argument, not among the premises.
**Result: Held with repair.** **Serious** as written, because the wording implies forgiveness on the victim's behalf. Also "the one we wronged is dead, *or never knew*" is careless. A victim who never knew is still alive and can be told. Cut "or never knew."

**Exchange 8 (new): Anscombe's own genealogy, and Big Gods.**
*Opponent:* W1(3) cites Anscombe's fork: law with a Lawgiver, or no law. But Anscombe also argued that the modern moral "ought" survives from the divine-law conception after belief in the Lawgiver faded. She compared it to the word "criminal" lingering after criminal law and courts are gone (paraphrase). That is a debunking genealogy, from a Catholic. The sense that "a debt is still owed when the victim is gone," felt by a secular reader, may simply be inherited Christian culture, not independent evidence. Cognitive science of religion adds the wider story. Moralizing high gods who watch and judge spread with large, anonymous societies (Norenzayan's *Big Gods*). In smaller societies the gods are often indifferent to human morality (Peoples and Marlowe 2012). Law-plus-Lawgiver is a cultural package invented to hold strangers together. It is not something found.
*Reply:* Two points. First, the genetic fallacy: how a belief spread does not settle whether it is true, and a true belief can be passed on by culture, as arithmetic is. Second, and better, the Church predicts your data. The natural law is "written on the hearts" of those who do not know its Author (Romans 2:14–15), and moral knowledge comes before knowledge of the Lawgiver (CCC 1960). If peoples everywhere had a moral law with no moralizing God attached, that is exactly what the Church would expect. The fork concerns what the law *requires to be explained*, not what anyone believed. Antigone's "unwritten laws" and Cicero's "true law" show the law-conception outside Israel and the Church. They also show it reaching for the divine.
*Rejoinder:* Antigone's laws are the gods' laws. You have just shown that the law-conception travels with gods. That is my point.
*Reply:* And ours. Where people meet the law as a law, they reach for a Lawgiver. Whether that is a cognitive bias or what reason sees is the standoff again.
**Result: Held at parity.** **Minor** once W1(3) states that the fork concerns explanation, not anyone's belief. It is worth one sentence.

**Exchange 9 (new): A5 sets "inclination" against Patrick's own rule.**
*Opponent:* A5 says the "ought" lives in "an end written into our nature as an inclination we did not give ourselves." I agree we did not give ourselves our inclinations: selection did. We also did not give ourselves our disordered ones: aggression, jealousy, wandering lust. An inclination is part of what we *are*. The book's own rule is that describing what we are never yields an ought. So either A5 derives the ought from a fact about our nature, which breaks the book's rule, or it needs a criterion to sort good inclinations from disordered ones, and that criterion is the law itself, which A5 has not supplied.
*Reply:* Correct about the wording. Aquinas does not read the ought off inclinations as biological facts. The first precept (good is to be done and pursued) is known through itself by practical reason, and the inclinations count as pointing to goods *as reason grasps them* (ST I-II q.94 a.2). A5 should say that the end is one reason can see but did not set, and that our unchosen desires include both good and disordered ones.
**Result: Held with repair.** **Serious** as an internal inconsistency with Patrick's own distinction. Easy to fix.

**Exchange 10 (new): "Look at the newborn," and the surgeon case.**
*Opponent:* The conclusion's last appeal is "look at the newborn in front of you." That is an appeal to the baby-schema response, the best-documented caregiving trigger in the human repertoire. It is plainly selected and plainly unequal: it is weaker for infants who are ill or disfigured. In the hard case, the severely disabled newborn the ancient world left to die, your appeal points to a feeling that *fails* exactly there. A6 has the same problem. The surgeon who may not kill one patient for five is the textbook "personal force" alarm that Greene argues is an emotional heuristic and not a moral insight.
*Reply:* On A6: Berker (2009) showed that neuroscience does no normative work in Greene's argument. The argument needs an independent premise that responses to "personal force" do not matter morally, and that premise is the very point in dispute. And the "utilitarian" answers in sacrificial dilemmas have been found to go with antisocial traits, not with impartial concern for the greater good (Kahane and colleagues 2015). On the newborn: the objection is a gift. The dignity judgment holds *most clearly where the instinct fails*. Instinct favors the healthy, the beautiful and our own. The worth A4 means holds for the infant no instinct favors. That judgment runs against the evolved response, so the evolved response cannot be what produces it. The conclusion should point there.
**Result: Held, and the case is stronger after repair.** **Serious** as written only because the last line points to the instinct rather than past it.

**Exchange 11 (new): A7 against W3.**
*Opponent:* A7 says we must not believe what comforts. W3 ends: "If the highest good is no one, then love ... is answered in the end by no one." Terror-management research predicts that reminders of death strengthen belief in an afterlife and in caring gods. Attachment theory predicts a longing for an all-attuned caregiver. Boyer predicts the full-access mind. So the likelihood that the longing exists is about the same with God or without. A "clue" whose likelihood is equal under both hypotheses is no clue at all. And the closing line appeals to comfort, which is exactly what A7 forbids.
*Reply:* Granted on the closing line. It must say openly what the evil paragraph already says: this is a cost, not evidence. On the likelihood point: the three mechanisms each predict *part* of the longing. Attachment predicts longing for caregivers. The watcher template predicts a mind that *sees* us, not one that loves us. Death anxiety predicts a wish to survive, not a wish to be wholly known. Explaining the longing takes all three, and even together they predict a longing that a lesser object could satisfy. What we find is a longing that no finite object answers (W3). That gives a modest likelihood edge, which is all "clue" claims. And A7 binds the other side too: a man may not disbelieve because there being no Judge would suit him.
**Result: Held with repair.** **Minor** once the closing line is restated as a cost and W3 adds one sentence about the science.

**Exchange 12 (new): Skeptical theism undercuts W2.**
*Opponent:* The evil paragraph's second point says God's reasons for permitting evil are "something we would not expect to see." Fine. But then you cannot predict what God would make either. W2's "a law and a mind made for each other are best explained by one Source" infers from what we see to what a good God would do. If our grasp of His purposes is too weak to judge evils, it is too weak to support that inference. Skeptical theism disarms natural theology along with the argument from evil.
*Reply:* The skepticism has a limit. It does not say we know nothing of what a good God values. We know enough of goodness to expect that a Source who is Truth would make minds able to know truth and law. That is a general expectation, and no defender of skeptical theism denies it. What we would not expect to see is how *each particular* evil serves a good. The asymmetry is between general goods, which we can see, and the reasons behind particular permissions, which we would not expect to see.
*Rejoinder:* A good God would also, generally, value children not suffering. By your own standard, we know that much.
*Reply:* Yes, and that is why the paragraph rightly says the weight is "real" and "heavy." The second point only claims that not seeing a reason weighs less than seeing a love. The paragraph should make this limit explicit, so that it does not seem to buy safety from evil at the price of W2.
**Result: Held with repair.** **Serious** if it is left unstated, because a sharp reader will see the tension. One sentence fixes it.

**Exchange 13 (minor): "Its great teachers reach for personal words."**
*Opponent:* The impersonal-order line in C counts it as evidence that Confucian and Stoic teachers "reach for personal words" at the points of forgiveness and love. Barrett and Keil (1996) showed that people who hold abstract, non-personal ideas of God still slip into treating God as a person when they reason quickly. Reaching for personal words is what minds do. It is no clue.
*Reply:* Barrett's data concern quick, unreflective reasoning. The sayings in question (for example, "Heaven knows me," *Analects* 14.35) are considered statements from a lifetime of reflection. Barrett's finding also applies to Christians, who slip into picturing God as bounded in place and time. It is a fact about how minds process ideas, not about which way the evidence points. Still, the line should present this as where impersonal accounts strain, not as evidence.
**Result: Held at parity.** **Minor.**

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**A heading:**
> **A. What we take to be true.** These are offered as truths, not as feelings people happen to share. Almost everyone finds he cannot give them up, and what we cannot give up after honest reflection we have reason to trust until something stronger shows it false. None is beyond denial: serious thinkers have denied each of them, and a few deny them all. The comparison (C) shows what each denial costs; the reader can ask whether he can pay it.

**A2, last sentence:**
> Most moral traditions know this debt to the one we harmed, even those that speak more often of shame before those who see us.

**A4, add after "no one is worth less":**
> Our instincts favor the healthy, the beautiful and our own. The worth we mean holds most plainly where instinct fails: for the infant no one is drawn to hold.

**A5:**
> **A5. What we are does not settle what we ought to do.** We are capable of truth and lies, justice and injustice, mercy and cruelty, giving and grasping, and we carry good desires and disordered ones, none of which we gave ourselves. Listing them never tells us which to follow. The "ought" lives in what we are *for*: an end that reason can see but did not set (Romans 2:14–15; ST I-II q.91 a.2, q.94 a.2).

**A7:**
> **A7. We are bound to the truth.** We may not believe against the evidence because a falsehood would comfort us, even if no one would ever know. "Follow the evidence" is not mere advice for those who happen to want truth; it is something every reasoning mind *ought* to do (CCC 2104). This binds believer and unbeliever alike.

**W1(1):**
> (1) *A debt that is still owed.* A wronged person needs forgiving, and only a person can forgive. When the one we wronged has died, no one on earth can forgive in her place, and yet we do not think the debt died with her (A3). A debt still owed now is owed to someone now. One can say it is owed to her as she was; but then it is a debt no one can ever receive, release or forgive, and some guilt is simply never lifted. That view is consistent, and its price is real. The Church gives another answer: every wrong against a person is also a wrong against the One who made that person precious (Psalm 51:4; CCC 1850). He does not forgive in her place. He forgives what was done against Him, and He can raise her and make her whole, so that she herself can forgive. A truth can be true; only a person can forgive.

**W1(2), last sentence:**
> A worth that is ours, that we did not earn, that no one conferred, and that comes before anyone's recognition (A4), is best explained as received, and received from beyond the whole human community.

**W1(3), add at the end:**
> Some of the most rigorous atheist philosophers agree with the first half of this: J. L. Mackie granted that real moral values would make God more likely, which is why he denied the values. The question is not whether people have believed in a Lawgiver, but what a law that truly binds requires.

*(Verify the Mackie paraphrase against* The Miracle of Theism *(1982), ch. 6, and the Anscombe paraphrase against "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958) before either enters the book. Keep both paraphrased.)*

**W2 (full replacement):**
> **W2. The law and the mind that reads it point to one Source.** Begin with truth (A7). Evolution explains well why our senses and reasoning mostly get the world right: a creature that misreads cliffs and lions does not last. It does not explain why we *ought* to believe what is true when a falsehood would serve us better. Whoever denies that anything binds must deny this too, and then he cannot fault the one who prefers a comfortable belief to a true one. Then ask whether our kind is an accident. If the pattern of a good human life is only a passing product of evolution, its law could have been otherwise: Darwin imagined rational creatures raised like hive bees who would think it a sacred duty to kill their brothers. Much of the natural law is indeed fitted to our kind, and the Church has never denied it. But its core is not: gratuitous cruelty, betrayal, treating a person as a thing would be wrong for any creature that can reason and suffer, and few of us can believe that torturing a child for fun is wrong only for creatures shaped like us (A1). If our kind is intended and not an accident, its law is an order set by wisdom, which is what the Church calls the eternal law (ST I-II q.91 a.1–2). The best answer without God says survival and well-being are themselves good, so a mind shaped by survival was roughly aimed at the good; its best defenders admit that some of the fit is still good fortune. Where conscience fails, it fails where self-interest pulls: the slave, the stranger, the infant who cannot repay (A4). The Church expects exactly this, because disordered self-love is the root of every sin (ST I-II q.77 a.4). But conscience also corrects itself, against interest and toward those who can never repay; and what drives that correction is reason holding itself to consistency, which is the law of truth again. And this law holds together: its demands can be very hard (Chapter 11), but they never require us to do evil. A law and a mind made for each other, with truth binding both, are best explained by one Source who made both. W1 and W3 show why that Source is Someone and not something.

*(Verify Darwin, Descent of Man, ch. 3 of the 1871 edition (ch. 4 in 1874), and keep it a paraphrase.)*

**W3, insert after "not even ourselves":**
> Science can trace the paths this longing travels in us, as it traces the paths of sight; that tells us how we long, not whether there is Someone to long for.

**W3, replace the last two sentences of the first paragraph:**
> A longing does not prove its answer, but it shows what answer would fit: a Person who knows us wholly, can hold us forever, and loved us first (1 John 4:19). If the highest good is no one, then love, the best thing in us, is answered in the end by no one. That is not evidence, for we may not believe a thing because it comforts (A7); but it is where that account stops.

**C, Nothing really binds:**
> **Nothing really binds** (error theory, often joined to an evolutionary account of conscience and of religion) is consistent and borrows nothing. With one cause it explains why we feel bound, guilty and watched. Its price: torturing a child for fun is not really wrong (A1); the newborn has no worth except what we choose to give him (A4); and even "follow the evidence" becomes advice for those who happen to want truth (A7), so it cannot say why anyone *ought* to accept it.

**C, Impersonal sacred order, last sentence:**
> At exactly those points the account strains, and its own great teachers reach for personal words.

**C, A God who is Love, add:**
> It does not remove all mystery; but it is one mystery, not many, and a mystery that explains.

**Evil paragraph, point 2:**
> Second, the love in A is something we see, while God's reasons for permitting each particular evil are something we would not expect to see. We know enough of goodness to know what a good God would value, and that is why this weight is real; what we cannot expect is to see how each evil serves a good. Not seeing a reason is weaker evidence than seeing a love.

**Conclusion, last sentence:**
> To him it says only: look at the newborn no instinct favors, the one the ancient world left on the hillside, and ask whether his worth depends on how he makes you feel.

## 7. New insights and lines worth keeping

**Insights:**
- **A7 is the case's best weapon against this school, once it is aimed correctly.** It makes the error theorist pay in his own coin. His critique of religion relies on a categorical "ought" of truth. If he drops it, the critique becomes a matter of taste. If he keeps it, he has a companion in guilt. Aim A7 at the *norm* of truth, never at whether our faculties are reliable.
- **The hive-bee dilemma is really "accident or intended."** Thomism already holds that much of the law is fitted to our kind. What it denies is that our kind is an accident. Framed this way, the dilemma is fair and Thomist.
- **The pattern of moral failure is a datum both sides predict, and q.77 a.4 is the Church's prediction.** What tells them apart is the engine of correction, and that engine is consistency, which is A7 again. That makes W2 a single argument rather than a list.
- **The forgiveness argument's honest form is about a debt in the present tense, not about wanting forgiveness.** The rival can say "owed to her, as she was, and never forgivable." The book should state that answer and its price, and let the reader test which description fits the guilt he actually knows.
- **"He does not forgive in her place"** keeps Ivan Karamazov's principle and answers his question. It is good Catholic theology and good apologetics.
- **Where judgment runs against instinct, debunking is weakest.** The infant no instinct favors is a better witness to A4 than the newborn everyone wants to hold.
- **Skeptical theism has to be limited, or it costs natural theology.** Say "general goods we see; the reasons behind particular permissions we would not expect to see."

**Lines worth keeping:**
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive." (v2)
- "Forgiveness heals the bond; it does not remake the law." (v2)
- "He does not forgive in her place. He forgives what was done against Him, and He can raise her, so that she herself can forgive."
- "Look at the newborn no instinct favors, and ask whether his worth depends on how he makes you feel."
- "The question is not whether people have believed in a Lawgiver, but what a law that truly binds requires."
- "A mind shaped by survival can explain why we see cliffs truly; it cannot explain why we ought to believe the truth when a lie would serve us better."
- "That is not evidence, for we may not believe a thing because it comforts; but it is where that account stops."

## 8. Score

**v2 as written: 7/10. With the repairs above: 8.5/10.** v2 fixes nearly everything Round 1 found, and A7 gives the case a real advantage over this school for the first time. But W2 misuses that advantage, the hive-bee dilemma misdescribes the Thomist side, and W1's forgiveness argument still assumes its conclusion and rests on a hope. Once repaired, all that remains is the honest standoff a converging case is supposed to leave, now tilted slightly our way by the norm of truth.
