# Round 2 — Kantian Constructivism vs. v2

## 1. School and voice

The opponent speaks for the Kantian tradition at full strength: Kant himself (*Groundwork*, second *Critique*, *Metaphysics of Morals*, *Religion within the Bounds of Mere Reason*, the Collins *Lectures on Ethics*, the 1791 theodicy essay) and his constructivist heirs (Rawls, Korsgaard, O'Neill, Herman, Wood), plus the critics who sharpened the debate inside the school (G. A. Cohen's reply to Korsgaard in *The Sources of Normativity*; Kleingeld and Willaschek, "Autonomy without Paradox," 2019). Core claim: the moral law is the law of practical reason itself, humanity is an end in itself with dignity and not price, and God is at most a postulate of practical hope, never the ground of duty.

This round the Kantian finds v2 much better than v1 on W1, A3 and A4. But v2's new row for Kant in C attacks a view Kant did not hold, and two of v2's new lines (W1(2) "received," and God forgiving in the victim's place) open new holes. All are repairable, and in repairing them the book gains a sharper answer to Kant than Round 1 found.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges (pressed harder this round)

**1. Starting point for justice.**
*Kantian answer:* The one innate right, freedom from being forced by another's choice, so far as it fits with everyone's freedom under a universal law. Kant extends what is owed beyond the living competent adult: parents owe care to a child because they brought a *person* into the world without his consent (*Doctrine of Right* §28), and a person's good name is still owed to him after death (*Doctrine of Right* §35).
*Verdict: Holds.* The two extensions are real and fair, and they mean A2's ashes example and A3's "the debt is not gone" do not separate the Kantian from the theist. Kant already says we can wrong the dead. What the Kantian lacks is not a claimant but someone who can *answer* the dead: the debt stays, and on his view no one remains who can receive it (see Exchange 3).

**2. A hard case, resolved by their own principles.**
*The case:* A woman, fully competent at 60, writes an advance directive: when dementia has taken her memory and she no longer knows her children, she is to be given a lethal injection. At 75 she has severe dementia, seems content, enjoys her meals and her garden, and cannot now consent or refuse. Should the doctor carry out the directive?
*Kantian answer:* No. Kant forbids disposing of the humanity in one's own person, even with one's own consent (*Doctrine of Virtue*, on self-murder). The earlier self's choice does not give anyone the right to kill the later woman, because she is not a thing her earlier self owned. Some recent Kantians permit assisted dying for the competent; very few would carry out *this* directive, because the person to be killed is not the one consenting.
*Verdict: Holds, but by borrowing.* The answer matches the book's. But look at what carries it: the woman at 75 still has dignity *without exercising rational agency*. Her humanity "in her person" is present though she cannot legislate anything. That is dignity grounded in the kind of being she is, not in what she can do. The Kantian resolves the case by standing on natural-law ground (A4: "whatever he can or cannot do"). The constructivist who keeps strictly to "value is conferred by rational choosing" has no clean answer here; the earlier chooser looks like the only one whose will counts.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.**
*Kantian answer, two forms.* (a) Kant: every member of the rational kind has dignity from conception (§28 treats the newborn as a person); dignity is not a reward for using reason. (b) Late Korsgaard (*Fellow Creatures*, 2018): we must value ourselves as creatures for whom things can be good or bad, and consistency requires valuing every such creature as an end in itself, newborns, the demented and animals included.
*Verdict:* (a) *Holds, by borrowing.* Kant reaches the newborn by appealing to a rational *nature* present where no reasoning happens, which is a claim about what a being is *for*. He also places that personhood in a realm he says theoretical reason can never know. (b) *Holds for equality, breaks for "prior to anyone's recognition."* Korsgaard does reach the newborn, and she keeps humans equal. But the worth exists only because valuers, by the structure of their valuing, must confer it. On her view, in a world with no rational valuers, nothing would be an end in itself. That denies A4's "prior to anyone's recognition," and it extends end-in-itself status to all sentient life in a way *Dignitas Infinita* would not accept. Either way, v2's C line, which says Kant's ground "leaves out the newborn and the dying," is false of both forms (see Exchange 6).

**4. The ultimate end of human flourishing.**
*Kantian answer:* The highest good: virtue joined to happiness in proportion to it, in an ethical commonwealth. Secular constructivists: a unified agent in a just society.
*Verdict: Kant borrows openly, and more than Round 1 recorded.* In *Religion* Book III, Kant argues that an ethical commonwealth (a community bound by inner virtue, not outer coercion) cannot have the people themselves as its lawgiver, because laws of virtue concern inner dispositions that no people can see or enforce. So it can be thought of only as a people under a moral Ruler of the world who knows hearts, in whom all true duties are represented as His commands. (Paraphrase; verify, *Religion* 6:98–99.) The philosopher of autonomy, asking what could gather human beings into one moral community, reached for a Lawgiver. The secular constructivist drops this and keeps a just society, which is coherent but has no word for the victims who died unrighted.

### Exchange 1: "A law I give myself I can untie" (the main hit)

- *Kantian:* This line misstates Kant. Kant never held that the moral law is a rule I choose. In the Collins lectures he says plainly that no one, not even God, is the *author* of the moral law, because it does not arise from choice but is practically necessary. In the *Metaphysics of Morals* he separates the *lawgiver* from the *author* of a law. "Self-legislation" means I am bound only by a law that is my own reason's, not by a law laid on me from outside. A will that could untie the moral law would be a will ruled by inclination, which is exactly what Kant calls heteronomy. And your own W1 says not even God can make cruelty to a child right. If "untie-ability" counted against a lawgiver, it would count against yours too, since neither of us can untie this law. The line does not discriminate between us at all.
- *Patrick's side:* Conceded as written. The line has a real source, though: G. A. Cohen's objection to Korsgaard, that if the self truly *legislates* the law, the self can repeal it, as Hobbes's sovereign can release himself from his own civil laws. So the line hits one reading of Kant (the strong constructivist one) and misses Kant himself. The honest line is a dilemma. Either reason *makes* the law, and then Cohen is right: what reason makes, reason could unmake, and worth becomes something valuers confer (Exchange 6). Or reason *finds* the law, as Kant and Kleingeld–Willaschek say, and then the law is older than every reasoner. In that case "reason gives itself the law" names where the law is read, not where it comes from, and the Kantian has joined the realist: there is a necessary law that belongs to no one.
- *Kantian rejoinder (Korsgaard):* False dilemma. The law is *constitutive* of rational agency, the way the rules of logic make thinking what it is. Neither made at will nor found outside: it is the form of willing itself.
- *Reply:* Round 1 answered the authority side (inescapability is not authority). The new point is this. Constitutivism tells me why I cannot act without the law. It does not tell me why the law's verdict on me stands when I do act against it, or to whom I answer when the one I wronged is dead. And Kant himself, when he asked what could make human beings one moral community, said the people cannot be its lawgiver; only a Ruler who knows hearts can (*Religion* 6:98–99, verify). The constitutive form of reason was not enough even for its founder.
- **Verdict: C's Kant row, first clause, Failed as written. Held with repair** (the made-or-found dilemma, §6).

### Exchange 2: W1(2), "best explained as received"

- *Kantian:* "A worth that is ours, that we did not earn and no one conferred, is best explained as received." That begs the question. Kant's whole point about dignity is that it is *unconditioned*: it is the condition of every other value and depends on nothing. Received worth is conditioned worth, because it depends on a giver. You have described a worth that needs no source and then said it must have one. A necessary truth (that seven is prime) was not earned, conferred or chosen either, and no one thinks it was "received." And "not ours to cancel" is fully explained by my view: I cannot sign away the humanity in my own person, because it is not my property to dispose of. Kant forbids self-enslavement for exactly that reason.
- *Patrick's side:* Two parts of that are right. "Not ours to cancel" is explained by the Kantian about as well as by the theist; v2 should not lean on it. And "received" is not proved by "unearned and unconferred"; the argument needs another step. The step is this. There are only three places an unearned, uncancelable, equal worth can come from. (a) It is conferred by valuers, by the structure of their valuing (Korsgaard). Then it is not "prior to anyone's recognition" (A4), and a world without valuers has no ends in it. (b) It is self-standing and necessary, like the primeness of seven (Kant read as a realist; the godless realist). But seven exists in no particular time; the newborn is a contingent animal who did not exist a year ago. Unconditioned worth in a being whose very existence is conditioned is not like a truth of number. Something must explain why *this* creature, who began and could have not begun, bears a worth that depends on nothing. (c) It is given with its existence by One who is Himself the Good and who wills each creature for its own sake. That is what *Dignitas Infinita* means when it grounds dignity in each person's being willed, created and loved by God. A gift truly given is truly the receiver's own (Chapter 4), so "received" does not make it less ours.
- *Kantian rejoinder:* "Received" still makes it hang on God's will. He could withdraw it.
- *Reply:* Only a voluntarist God could, and the book has no such God (Chapter 3). God's love does not respond to a goodness already there; it *causes* the goodness it loves (ST I q.20 a.2). It is as faithful as His being. The dignity lasts as long as the person does, and the Church holds the person lasts forever.
- **Verdict: Failed as written** (asserted, and it trades on "not ours to cancel," which the Kantian explains). **Held with repair**, to parity against (b) and better than parity against (a).

### Exchange 3: W1(1), God forgiving in the victim's place

- *Kantian:* v2 says only God "can forgive without wronging the victim again, because only He can raise the victim and make her whole." That is a confusion of forgiveness with compensation. Making someone whole repays a loss; it does not give anyone else standing to forgive a wrong done to *her*. Your own A3 says "no one else can forgive in his place." If God forgives the murderer for what he did to the dead girl, God is doing exactly what A3 forbids. Literature knows this: Ivan Karamazov says the mother may forgive her own suffering, but she has no right to forgive the torturer for her child's. A God who forgives for the victim is the God Ivan hands his ticket back to.
- *Patrick's side:* A good hit, and v2 should change. Catholic teaching does not say God forgives in the victim's place. Sin wrongs the neighbor *and* offends God (CCC 1849–1850; Psalm 51:4). God forgives the offense against *Himself*. What only He can do for the victim is not to forgive for her but to *keep her*: to raise her, make her whole, and give her back the standing to forgive, or to be answered, herself. On the theist's view, the dead are not gone; the debt is owed to someone who still exists and will be met. That is precisely what the Kantian cannot offer. On his account the debt to the dead stands (Kant says we can wrong the dead) and no one exists to receive it, ever.
- *Kantian rejoinder:* Then you need resurrection and judgment, which are revealed doctrines, not reason.
- *Reply:* Kant himself postulated immortality and a moral Judge for the same reason: the highest good must be possible. The book claims only that the hypothesis of a living God who keeps the dead explains this feature of guilt and that its rivals do not. It is a best-explanation step, not a proof.
- **Verdict: Failed as written. Held with repair.** The repaired form is stronger than v2: it turns the Karamazov objection into the argument.

### Exchange 4: A3 and consent

- *Kantian:* A3 says the victim "could never have consented it into being right beforehand." For most acts that is false. Consent is what makes surgery not assault, a gift not theft, a marriage not a seizure. Kant's own Doctrine of Right takes the old principle that no wrong is done to one who consents. Consent fails only where the act degrades the humanity in a person, such as self-enslavement or being tortured for fun, and *my* theory says exactly why: no one owns his own humanity. So A3 overstates, and the true part is explained without God.
- *Patrick's side:* Conceded as to wording. A3 should say "for the gravest wrongs." The Kantian explanation of why consent fails there is good and close to the Church's (one may not dispose of oneself as a thing). But A3's other clauses (the wrong is not undone by forgiveness; the debt outlives the victim) are untouched, and W1 rests on those.
- **Verdict: Held with repair** (one clause).

### Exchange 5: A5's "inclination"

- *Kantian:* A5 says listing what we *can* do never tells us what we *ought* to do, and then grounds the ought in "an inclination we did not give ourselves." But an inclination is just one more fact about what we are. If facts about capacities give no ought, facts about inclinations give none either. And an ought that rests on an inclination is hypothetical at best: *if* you want what you are inclined to, do this. A5 breaks its own rule.
- *Patrick's side:* The wording invites that; Aquinas does not. For him the ought is not inferred from the inclination. Reason grasps the goods we are inclined to *as good*, and grasps "good is to be done" as first and self-evident (ST I-II q.94 a.2). The inclination shows *which* goods; reason's grasp of them as good is where the ought lives. Here Catholic and Kantian stand closer than either stands to Hume. The difference is that for Aquinas the goods reason grasps are written into a nature that is *for* something, and that raises the question who made it for it.
- *Kantian rejoinder:* Self-evidence is a stopping point.
- *Reply:* So is the fact of reason. Round 1 settled this at parity.
- **Verdict: Held with repair** (A5 last sentence).

### Exchange 6: C's Kant row on dignity

- *Kantian:* "Dignity grounded in reasoning leaves out the newborn and the dying." Kant calls the newborn a person (§28). Wood and Kain read him as giving status to the rational *kind*. Korsgaard (2018) gives end-in-itself status to every creature with a good of its own. None of us leaves out the newborn.
- *Patrick's side:* Granted; the line must change. But see what each road costs. Kant reaches the newborn through a rational *nature* that is present where no reasoning happens, which means a nature that is *for* reason. That is a teleological claim about what a being is, which the constructivist cannot make on his own ground and which Kant places beyond anything theoretical reason can know. Korsgaard reaches the newborn by making worth something valuers must confer. That keeps equality but gives up "prior to anyone's recognition," and it extends the same status to all sentient life. The honest line: Kant can include the newborn only by borrowing a nature with an end; his heirs who refuse to borrow make her worth depend on valuers.
- **Verdict: Failed as written. Held with repair.**

### Exchange 7: Heteronomy returns at W1's last paragraph

- *Kantian:* W1 ends: God has authority "to define what love must be submitted to." Either we can see by reason that His definition is right, and then reason is the authority and God is idle; or we cannot, and then we obey an outside will, which is heteronomy. Round 1 gave you a way out, participated theonomy (VS 41), and v2 left it out.
- *Patrick's side:* Correct, and it should go back in, next to Patrick's wording, not in place of it. The law is ours because it is in our reason, and His because our reason, and the nature it reads, are His gift. "What love must be submitted to" is not a rule laid on reason from outside. It is the order of the Good that reason itself recognizes as good and as binding, now known to come from the One who made reason.
- **Verdict: Held with repair** (one sentence).

### Exchange 8: Anscombe's fork has a third horn

- *Kantian:* W1(3) says either morality is a law with a Lawgiver, or we drop "law" and keep the excellences of our kind. Kant is the third option: a law with no lawgiver but reason. Anscombe dismissed it in a single line (legislating for oneself is absurd), and most Kant scholars think she misread him.
- *Patrick's side:* The third horn must be named, not ignored. But it splits as in Exchange 1: either reason makes the law (and could unmake it) or finds it (and the law belongs to no one, which is the realist's position, with the realist's gaps: forgiveness, why worth follows kind, why it all holds together). And Kant's own *Religion* shows the third horn did not satisfy him: for a moral community he reached for a public Lawgiver who knows hearts.
- *Kantian rejoinder:* That was Kant's piety, not his argument.
- *Reply:* It *was* his argument: that laws of inner virtue cannot be given by a people to itself. The book may cite it as an opposing witness, never as an authority.
- **Verdict: Held with repair.**

### Exchange 9: Korsgaard's normative question, turned on the theist

- *Kantian:* Your God-who-is-the-Good is substantive realism with a Person attached. Korsgaard's challenge to realism stands: the reflective agent asks "why must I?" and "because it is true that you must" is no answer for him.
- *Patrick's side:* Every account answers "why must I?" by pointing to something it does not further explain; Korsgaard points to our practical identity as human, which we must value. The Catholic answer is not only "it is true." It is: because this is the Good you are made for, it is *your* good, and the One who made you for it loves you first. Here the Catholic view does what Kant could not. Kant split duty from happiness and needed God at the end to join them again (the highest good). Aquinas never split them: the law that binds us and the happiness we long for are the same Good.
- **Verdict: Held (parity), and an insight** (§7).

### Exchange 10: Evil, and Kant's theodicy essay

- *Kantian:* Kant's 1791 essay on the failure of every philosophical theodicy sides with Job against his friends: honest uncertainty over excuses made for God. v2 mostly agrees. But the second point is badly formed: it sets "the love we see" against "God's reasons, which we would not expect to see." The fair comparison is datum against datum. We see love, and we see evil. What we do not see is either God or His reasons.
- *Patrick's side:* Fair. The point should read: both the love and the evil are seen; what is not seen is God's reasons for permitting evil, and if there is a God whose wisdom exceeds ours, that is what we would expect. So not seeing a reason is weak evidence that there is none.
- *Kantian, offering something:* For hiddenness, Kant gave the book a reason in the second *Critique*: if God's majesty stood always before our eyes, we would obey from fear, and the moral worth of our actions would vanish.
- *Reply:* That is Chapter 4's own point: a gift forced is no gift, and love presupposes freedom. The book should make it from Pascal (enough light for those who want to see, enough shadow for those who do not), with Kant as an opposing witness at most.
- **Verdict: Held with repair** (minor wording); one insight gained.

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**As written, four are Serious:**
1. **C's Kant row misstates Kant** (Exchanges 1, 6). Two of its three clauses are false of Kant, and the first would count against the book's own God. **Serious.**
2. **W1(2) begs the question** with "received" and leans on non-cancelability, which Kant explains without God (Exchange 2). **Serious.**
3. **W1(1) has God forgive in the victim's place**, which A3 forbids (Exchange 3). **Serious.**
4. **A3 overstates consent** (Exchange 4). **Minor to Serious**: a skeptical reader will spot it at once.

**After the repairs in §6, none is Fatal or Serious. Three Minor ones remain, all at parity:**
1. **Self-standing worth (Minor).** The Kantian-realist can keep holding that unconditioned worth in a contingent creature is simply necessary and needs no source. Our side answers to parity, and does better against the constructivist form. A converging case can live with this.
2. **Need is not supply, for forgiveness (Minor, carried from Round 1).** The Kantian can say the debt to the dead is simply never paid. The repaired W1(1) shows this is the price of his view, not a refutation of ours.
3. **The critical limit on reasoning to God (Minor, carried from Round 1).** A standing disagreement; *Dei Filius* against Kant; v2's claim is modest enough.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**A3 (replace the second and third sentences):**
> The one we wronged can forgive us, and should. But he cannot make the wrong not wrong. And for the gravest wrongs he could not have consented them into being right beforehand: no one can agree to be enslaved, or to be tortured for someone's amusement.

**A4 (add the citation):**
> … (*Dignitas Infinita* 1, 15, 24).

**A5 (replace the last sentence):**
> The "ought" lives in what we are *for*: goods written into our nature, which we did not give ourselves, and which reason grasps as good and to be done (ST I-II q.94 a.2).

**W1(1) (replace from "Only He can forgive…" to the end of the paragraph):**
> He does not forgive in the victim's place; that would wrong her a second time. He forgives the wrong done against Himself. And He alone can keep the one we wronged: raise her, make her whole, and give her back the chance to forgive us herself. Without Him, the debt to the dead is real and no one is left to receive it, ever. A truth can be true; only a person can forgive.

**W1(2) (replace):**
> (2) *A worth no one gave themselves.* The newborn's worth is truly her own. She did not earn it, and no one can sign it away. Where can such a worth come from? If valuers confer it, then it is not there before anyone recognizes it, and a world with no one to value would have no one worth anything. If it simply holds, like the truths of number, we must ask why a creature who did not exist a year ago, and might never have existed, bears a worth that depends on nothing. The answer that fits is that it was given with her existence, by One who is Himself the Good and who wills each person for her own sake (*Dignitas Infinita* 1). A gift truly given is truly the receiver's own (Chapter 4).

**W1(3) (add after the first sentence):**
> Kant offered a third way: the law is reason's own. But either reason makes that law, and then could unmake it, or reason finds it, and then the law is older than every reasoner and the question returns: whose is it? Kant himself held that no one, not even God, is the author of the moral law. Yet when he asked what could gather people into one moral community, he concluded that they could not be their own lawgiver; only a Ruler who sees the heart could be.

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **Kant is closer to Chapter 3 than the chapter knows.** He held that no one, not even God, authors the moral law, yet allowed that all duties may be recognized as divine commands. That is nearly Chapter 3's answer to Euthyphro. The real disagreement is narrow and can be named: is the law's home *reason as such*, or the *reason of Someone*? The book wins it on forgiveness, on the newborn, and on community, not on authority.
- **The "made or found" dilemma is the right answer to self-legislation.** It is fair to Kant, it uses the debate inside his own school (Cohen vs Korsgaard; Kleingeld–Willaschek), and it sends the Kantian either to conferral (losing A4's "prior to recognition") or to realism (where the realist row already does the work).
- **The Karamazov objection becomes the argument.** God does not forgive for the victim; He keeps the victim so she can forgive. No secular view can say that the dead will be answered.
- **Use the contingent newborn against "self-standing worth."** Unconditioned worth in a creature who began, and might never have begun, is the strongest form of the "received" argument.
- **Kant split duty from happiness and needed God to join them again; Aquinas never split them.** This is the Catholic answer to "why must I?": the law that binds you is the Good you long for.
- **Kant's own *Religion* Book III** (an ethical community cannot be its own lawgiver) is the best opposing-witness admission for W1. Use it beside, not instead of, the forgiveness argument.
- **Chapter-text flag:** "laws require lawgivers" (lines 23, 133, 137, 140 of the current chapter) is still stated bare. Against Kant it needs the made-or-found dilemma at least once.
