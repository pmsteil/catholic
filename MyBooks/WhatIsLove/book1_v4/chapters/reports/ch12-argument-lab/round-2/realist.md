# Argument Lab, Round 2: Non-Naturalist Secular Moral Realism vs. v2

## 1. School and voice

This is the "godless normative realism" of Erik Wielenberg (*Robust Ethics*, 2014; "Sceptical Theism and Divine Lies," *Religious Studies* 2010), David Enoch (*Taking Morality Seriously*, 2011), Derek Parfit (*On What Matters*, 2011), Russ Shafer-Landau and Michael Huemer, with Stephen Darwall's second-personal account of obligation (*The Second-Person Standpoint*, 2006) and the "third-factor" replies to Sharon Street's Darwinian dilemma. Moral facts are objective, necessary, grounded in non-moral features (a child's pain is bad because of what pain is), known by intuition and reflection, and need no God. In Round 1 this school was the book's ally against relativism and materialism and its sharpest critic on W1. In Round 2 it grants most of what v2 repaired and presses on what v2 now says about forgiveness, worth, Anscombe, necessity and evil.

---

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**Challenge 1: starting point for justice.**
*Their answer:* Justice is giving each person what he is owed. What he is owed is fixed by necessary truths about persons: his suffering matters, he may not be used merely as a tool, and (Darwall) he has the standing to make demands of us and to hold us accountable as one free and rational person among others. These truths are basic, not derived.
*Verdict:* **Holds as a working principle; borrows the form and posits the ground.** It is the classical *suum cuique* without its metaphysics. Darwall's version adds a real insight (obligation is accountable to persons), but it ties standing to the competence to address claims, which the newborn and the profoundly disabled lack. There the school either posits equal standing as brute or falls back to "trustees," as contractualism does.

**Challenge 2: a hard case.**
*Their answer:* The conjoined twins case (Re A, English Court of Appeal, 2000): "Mary" could live only through "Jodie's" heart and lungs; without surgery both would die within months; separation would save Jodie and end Mary's life. The realist resolves it by weighing two necessary truths: Mary may not be killed as a means, but Jodie is not obliged to keep sustaining Mary with her own body, and the surgery removes a burden rather than using Mary as a tool. Most of the school permits separation. Some deontologists hesitate, and they would also weigh the parents' authority (the parents, Catholics from Gozo, refused; the court overrode them).
*Verdict:* **Coherent, and it borrows justice and mercy in all but name.** The reasoning is the reasoning of the book: what each is owed, the refusal to treat a person as a resource, care for the one who cannot speak. Catholic moralists themselves divided on this case. That is a point *for* the book, not against it: the law is knowable without knowing its Author (Romans 2:14–15), and the realist proves it. The case also shows that the realist and the Catholic disagree at the limit, not in the ordinary run.

**Challenge 3: equal, unconditional dignity.**
*Their answer:* Two camps. Capacity views (McMahan) rank humans by psychological capacities above a threshold and admit unequal worth at the margins. Kind-based views (S. Matthew Liao's secular account: every human with the genetic basis for moral agency has equal high status, even where that basis is blocked) keep equality.
*Verdict:* **Breaks or borrows. Still the strongest ground for our side.** Capacities come in degrees, so they cannot ground equal worth. Liao's "genetic basis *for* moral agency" is a nature directed toward something it may never reach: that is "what we are for" under a secular name. And why should a basis that varies in how well it works confer *exactly equal* worth? The realist can only posit it. The theist can explain it (see Exchange 9): each human being is willed for his own sake and called to the same end, and an equal call explains an equal worth (Gaudium et Spes 24).

**Challenge 4: the ultimate good.**
*Their answer:* An objective list (knowledge, friendship, achievement, pleasure, beauty, autonomy) lived with integrity. Wielenberg: a life can have real meaning from within without any final end; death ends it.
*Verdict:* **Coherent as a plan for a life; breaks on unity and on the victims.** The list does not say why these goods belong together, and it has nothing to say to the child who died before any good on the list reached her. That is a gap in explanation, not a contradiction.

### New exchanges

**Exchange 1: A3 contradicts A2's own example.**
*Realist:* A3 says the one we wronged "could never have consented it into being right beforehand." Your best example of a wrong is in A2: burying the dying friend's ashes in a field. Had she said, "Do whatever is cheapest," burying them would have wronged no one. Most directed duties (promises, property, privacy) can be released by consent. A3 is true only of A1's paradigm wrongs. And "the debt is not" gone builds the creditor into a premise W1 is meant to argue for.
*Defense:* Conceded on both counts. A3 must be restricted to wrongs like A1's, and the restriction makes a better point: a friend can release a promise as a gift, but no one can make torturing a child right. "The guilt remains" says what the reader can grant; "debt" belongs in W1.
*Result:* **Held with repair.** (A3 as written fails.)

**Exchange 2: A7 is an ought owed to no one.**
*Realist:* You added A7: we are bound to follow the evidence. Whom do I wrong when I believe against the evidence in private? No one. So here is an ought that is real, categorical and impersonal. Cuneo's companions-in-guilt argument (epistemic norms are as normative as moral ones) is usually aimed against error theorists; it helps me too. If an epistemic ought binds with no one to whom it is owed, why must a moral one?
*Defense:* W1 never needed every ought to be owed to someone. It rests on the oughts whose breaking *wrongs a person*: A2's data. Those have a creditor, and the question is who holds the claim when no human is left. A7 shows that not all normativity has that shape; it does not show that the shape of guilt is an illusion. (The Catholic can add that failing the truth offends the One who is Truth, but that is not needed and would beg the question here.)
*Result:* **Held with repair.** W1 must say plainly that it rests on wrongs done to persons, not on "ought" as such. Otherwise A7 becomes a counterexample inside the version's own list.

**Exchange 3: A5 breaks its own rule.**
*Realist:* A5's first half says that listing what we can do never settles what we ought to do. Its second half says the ought "lives in" an inclination written into our nature. An inclination is a fact about us. If facts about what we are cannot yield an ought, facts about what we lean toward cannot either. Either A5 contradicts itself, or (with Grisez and Finnis) the first principle of practical reason ("good is to be done") is self-evident, known without God, and you have conceded my view at the base.
*Defense:* Aquinas's answer is that the inclination is not itself the ought: reason *sees* the goods we lean toward as good and as to-be-done, and the first principle is known per se (ST I-II q.94 a.2). That is not a deduction of ought from is. But the realist is right that this belongs to the argument, not to the list of things almost everyone cannot give up. Patrick's distinction (what we are vs. what we are for) is sound; it is the Catholic thesis being argued, and the text should say so.
*Result:* **Held with repair.** Keep Patrick's wording; present the second half as the claim the witnesses defend.

**Exchange 4: Is God a redundant reason?**
*Realist:* Torturing a child for fun is wrong because of what it does to the child. That is a complete explanation. Adding "and because it is contrary to God's nature" is a second reason competing with the first, and the first does all the work. Your God is explanatorily idle at the level where the wrongness lives.
*Defense:* The two answers are not rivals; they answer different questions, like "Why did the house burn?" (a dropped candle) and "Why does anything burn at all?" The wrong is wrong because of what it does to the child, first and plainly, and a person who never heard of God sees it (Romans 2:14–15). The theist's claim is about the child and the order she lives in: what she is, that she exists, and why the truth about her binds. The book must never suggest that torture is wrong *because God forbids it*. That would be voluntarism, which Chapter 3 rejects.
*Result:* **Held with repair.** W1 should say this in one sentence; it disarms the commonest reader's objection.

**Exchange 5: The theist has brute ethical facts too, and the realist can unify.**
*Realist (Wielenberg):* C says that on my view "each item of A can be added as one more necessary truth. But nothing then explains why they hold together." Two replies. First, my best colleagues do unify: Ross reduces morality to a handful of basic duties; Parfit argues that the best versions of Kantian, contractualist and consequentialist theories converge on one principle. Second, your view has its own brute moral fact: that God's nature is the standard of good, and that creatures ought to love what is good. You never derive that; you assert it. One brute normative fact plus bridge principles against a few brute normative facts plus bridge principles: that is not "one stopping point instead of many."
*Defense:* Partly granted. The fair count is not one against many; it is one against a few, and both sides need a first principle. Aquinas's is "good is to be done" (ST I-II q.94 a.2), and it is no more derived than the realist's. What remains uneven is not the *number* of normative axioms but what else the stopping point does. The realist's axioms explain nothing outside ethics: not why there are persons for them to apply to, not why minds exist that can read them, not why the equal worth they posit is equal. The theist's stopping point is also the source of the beings the law is about and of the minds that know it.
*Realist rejoinder:* That is the cosmological argument doing the work, not the moral one.
*Defense:* Yes, and the case says so. It is a converging case (CCC 31): the moral witness and the order of the world point to the same place. That is a strength of the method, as long as the text does not pretend the moral data alone tip it.
*Result:* **Held with repair, at parity.** C's realist line overstates the gap and must be rewritten; the "one stopping point" line should claim breadth, not a lower count of axioms.

**Exchange 6: Necessary truths cannot be explained by a free act.**
*Realist:* You and I agree the wrong is necessary. But God's creating is free, so contingent. Then the conditional truth "if there is a child, torturing her for fun is wrong" holds in a world where God creates nothing. God explains which natures are *instantiated*; He does not explain the necessary truths about them. On your own view, the moral law belongs to no one.
*Defense:* The Catholic tradition does not ground the moral law in God's free act of creating. It grounds the natures of things, as possibilities, in God's knowledge of His own being: the divine ideas (ST I q.15 a.1–2; Augustine). What a child is and what she is for are first known in God's own mind, whether or not He makes her. So necessary moral truths are not free-floating; they are rooted in the One who is Truth. This also covers the necessity of mathematics, which is why W2's mathematics parallel should be restored.
*Realist rejoinder:* That is a heavy metaphysics of modality, and it is as brute as mine.
*Defense:* It is a stopping point, as conceded. But it answers the question "why are there necessary truths at all, and why can minds know them?" with one answer instead of two.
*Result:* **Held with repair, at parity.** v2 never says where necessity lives on the theist view; without it this objection lands. One sentence in C fixes it.

**Exchange 7: Anscombe's fork is a false dichotomy.**
*Realist:* W1(3): either morality is a law with a Lawgiver, or we keep only the excellences of our kind. There is a third option, and it is mine: irreducible normative truths, reasons that hold necessarily and belong to no one. Parfit wrote a thousand pages defending exactly that third option. Then v2 adds: "If A2 and A3 are real (a debt still owed...), morality is a law." That does not follow. A debt that persists shows directed duties persist; it does not show law in Aquinas's sense (an ordinance of reason, from one who has care of the community, promulgated: ST I-II q.90 a.4).
*Defense:* The fork as written fails against this school. What survives is Anscombe's challenge, recast: the realist keeps everything law-like about morality (it binds, it is universal, its breaking leaves guilt) while denying that anyone gives it. A truth gives reasons. It does not hold a claim, receive an apology, or forgive one. If A2 is real, morality behaves less like a theorem and more like a claim held by Someone.
*Result:* **Failed as a fork; held with repair as a challenge.** Present it as Anscombe's question, not as an exhaustive choice.

**Exchange 8: Does God forgive in the victim's place? (Ivan Karamazov)**
*Realist:* A3 says no one can forgive in the victim's place. W1(1) then says God forgives the wrong when the victim is dead, "without wronging the victim again." That is forgiving in her place. Dostoevsky's Ivan put the moral objection at full strength: no one, not even the mother, has the right to forgive the torturer of a child on the child's behalf. Griswold's account of forgiveness makes the same point philosophically: only the one wronged can forgive. Your God, forgiving the torturer over the dead child's head, looks like an injustice, not its remedy.
*Defense:* The objection is right about the wording and wrong about the teaching. God does not forgive the child's wrong *for* her. Every wrong against a person is also an offense against the One who made her and loves her (Psalm 51:4; CCC 1850). He forgives what was done against Him. Forgiveness does not cancel justice: sin leaves consequences that must still be healed (CCC 1472), and the Church promises a judgment in which every hidden wrong is brought to light and God's justice triumphs over every injustice (CCC 1038–1040). Because He can raise the victim and make her whole, He is the only one who can forgive without the victim being forgotten. Ivan's protest is against a forgiveness that skips the child. The Catholic hope is a forgiveness that includes her.
*Result:* **Held with repair.** W1(1) must say "He does not forgive in her place" and name the judgment. As written it contradicts A3.

**Exchange 9: "Received" does not follow, and Aquinas says God loves some more.**
*Realist:* W1(2): "A worth that is ours, that we did not earn and no one conferred, is best explained as received." A triangle's angles sum to two right angles: unearned, not conferred, not received. Necessary worth follows from what a person is. That is Round 1's gap ("not laid by any human, so laid by Someone") in new clothes. Worse, your own Aquinas teaches that God loves better things more (ST I q.20 a.3–4). So "each person is loved by God" does not yield *equal* worth even on your view.
*Defense:* On the first point: granted that "no one conferred, so received" is not an inference. The honest claim is that the realist's answer moves the question back one step: why is there anyone who is like that, and why does the same worth belong to the one who cannot use his gifts as to the one who can? On the second: Aquinas's "loves more" concerns the greater goods God wills to some (grace, glory), not the worth of the person as such. The Church distinguishes the dignity that belongs to every person by the bare fact of existing, which is equal and cannot be lost, from the dignity of how one lives (*Dignitas Infinita* 7–8). And the theist has something the realist lacks: an explanation of the *equality*. Each of us is the one creature God willed for its own sake, and all of us are called to the same end, God Himself (Gaudium et Spes 24). Worth follows the call, not the capacity. An equal call explains an equal worth.
*Realist rejoinder:* The call is posited too.
*Defense:* Yes; every account posits something. The question is which posit explains the datum. "Equal worth is a brute necessary truth" restates A4. "Each is called to the same end" explains it.
*Result:* **Held with repair, and our side leads here.** W1(2) must drop "best explained as received" as a bare inference and carry the equal-call explanation.

**Exchange 10: The pattern of moral error fits evolution better than design.**
*Realist:* If one Maker fitted conscience to the law (W2), why did conscience miss slavery and the abandoned infant for thousands of years, among Christians too? The third-factor account predicts exactly what we see: reliable where survival tested us, weak at the margins, improving slowly by reflection. Design predicts better than we got.
*Defense:* The Church predicts the same pattern for its own reasons: our minds are wounded (CCC 405), and moral truths reason could reach are in practice not reached by all, easily, firmly and without error, which is why revelation helps (CCC 37–38). That teaching is not invented for this objection; it is the doctrine of the Fall. What the third factor still does not explain is the reach itself: once conscience sees that the slave and the infant are equal, it sees it as true, against every survival interest of those who see it.
*Realist rejoinder:* Your auxiliary hypothesis fits the data because it was built to.
*Defense:* It was built centuries before the evolutionary debate, to explain the pull of disordered desire. Both accounts fit the pattern of error; only one has a Source for the reach.
*Result:* **Held with repair, at parity.** W2 should admit the fit is partial and say why the theist expects that.

**Exchange 11: A4 smuggles in a disputed conclusion.**
*Realist:* I hold A4 as firmly as you: equal worth, whatever one can do, prior to recognition, never traded for better totals. But A4 adds "or signed away when life grows hard." That is the case against voluntary euthanasia, which most of my school rejects while holding equal dignity. You have put a contested Catholic conclusion inside a premise you need me to grant, and you lose my assent at your strongest point.
*Defense:* Conceded. The brief forbids premises only a Catholic can grant. Equal worth is the datum; whether a dying person may consent to being killed is a further question the book can argue elsewhere.
*Realist (also):* And "history shows how easily it is lost" cuts both ways: Christians kept slaves for centuries.
*Defense:* Also conceded. The point is human weakness, not pagan weakness. The Church has asked pardon for the sins of her children against human dignity (the Day of Pardon, 12 March 2000). The text should say so in a few words; it makes the point more credible, not less.
*Result:* **Held with repair.**

**Exchange 12: The evil paragraph's second point undercuts the whole case.**
*Realist (Wielenberg 2010):* The evil paragraph says God's reasons for permitting evil are "something we would not expect to see." That is skeptical theism. But if we cannot judge what a perfectly good God would have reason to do, we cannot judge that He would give us a conscience fitted to the law (W2), forgive (W1), or answer our longing (W3). We could not even trust His word: He might have reasons beyond us for deceiving us. You use our insight into God's purposes when it helps and disclaim it when it hurts.
*Defense:* The asymmetry is real but principled. The witnesses do not predict hidden reasons; they read what is given to us and for us: a conscience we use, a guilt we feel, a longing we have. What the text says we would not expect to see is narrower: how each permitted evil is woven into the whole of history, from inside one short life. That is not a claim that God's goodness is unknowable, and it does not touch His truthfulness, which is His nature, not a policy He might suspend for reasons (Hebrews 6:18). But the line "not seeing a reason is weaker evidence than seeing a love" is too broad: the love we see is human love, which the atheist sees too.
*Result:* **Held with repair.** Narrow the second point. Also note that the first point (evil has nothing of its own) answers the idea of a dark god, not the idea of no god; the text should not lean on it for more.

**Exchange 13: Counterpossibles.**
*Realist:* If there were no God, would torturing a child be wrong? You and I both say yes, intuitively. Then its wrongness does not depend on God.
*Defense:* On the theist view, "there is no God" is impossible, and our intuitions about impossible worlds are not reliable guides to dependence (if there were no number 2, would 2+2=4?). What the intuition shows is what the book already grants: we know the wrong without knowing its Source.
*Result:* **Held.**

**Exchange 14: Realism sharpens the problem of evil.**
*Realist:* My view gives an independent standard by which to judge any God. If pain is necessarily bad, hundreds of millions of years of animal suffering before any sin are necessarily bad, and a perfectly good and powerful being would need a reason. You admit you do not know it.
*Defense:* The text already admits it, and that honesty is right. The weight is real; the case says it does not overturn the converging evidence, and it gives three reasons, not an explanation.
*Result:* **Held at parity, by honest concession.** This is a weighing, not a refutation on either side.

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**Against v2 as written (below parity until repaired):**
1. **A3 contradicts A2 and pre-loads "debt."** *Serious.* Repaired below.
2. **W1(1) has God forgive in the victim's place, against A3 (Ivan's objection).** *Serious.* Repaired below.
3. **W1(3) is a false fork against this school.** *Serious.* Repaired as a challenge.
4. **C's realist line overstates the gap (unification), and C never says where necessity lives on the theist view.** *Serious.* Repaired below.
5. **A4 contains a disputed conclusion (euthanasia).** *Serious*, because it loses the realist's assent at the version's strongest point. Repaired.
6. **A5's second half is a thesis listed as a datum.** *Minor.* Repaired.
7. **W1(2) "best explained as received" is a non sequitur.** *Minor* (the repair makes it the strongest line).
8. **The evil paragraph's second point invites the skeptical-theism reversal.** *Minor.* Repaired.

**After the repairs: NONE below parity.** What remains are honest contests of fit in which our side holds at least parity:
- *Both stopping points are brute* (God's nature with the divine ideas, or a few necessary truths). Parity, openly granted.
- *The pattern of moral error* fits the third-factor account and the doctrine of wounded nature alike. Parity; our side keeps the reach.
- *Evil, sharpened by realism* (animal suffering judged by an independent standard). *Serious in weight*, held at parity only because the text concedes it, weighs it, and does not claim to explain it. This is the right posture; no wording can do better without overclaiming.
Our side **leads** on equal dignity (the equal call explains what the realist must posit) and on forgiveness (a truth cannot forgive; the repaired W1(1) includes the victim instead of skipping her).

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**A2, last sentence (replace):**
> Every serious moral tradition knows this; even those that speak more of shame than of guilt know the need to be restored to the one we failed.

**A3 (replace):**
> **A3. No one on earth can make the wrong right.** The one we wronged can forgive us, and should. But for wrongs like those in A1, he cannot make the wrong not wrong, and he could never have consented it into being right beforehand. (Lesser wrongs are different: my friend could have released me from my promise about her ashes. That is a gift she could give, not a law she could remake.) No one else can forgive in his place: not his friends, not a nation by vote, not the whole human race. And when the one we wronged is gone, the guilt remains. Forgiveness heals the bond; it does not remake the law.

**A4 (replace):**
> **A4. Every human being has the same dignity, whatever he can or cannot do.** The newborn, the dying, the severely disabled, the enemy: equal, prior to anyone's recognition, and never to be traded away for better totals (*Dignitas Infinita* 1, 15). Equal worth is not equal duty: we owe more to our own parents and children than to strangers, but no one is worth less. Reason can see this (DI 1), yet history shows how easily it is lost: the same ancient world that taught that slaves are men also left weak and deformed infants to die, and Christians too were slow to see all that slavery denied (CCC 37–38).

**A5 (replace):**
> **A5. What we are able to do does not settle what we ought to do.** We are capable of truth and lies, justice and injustice, mercy and cruelty, giving and grasping. Listing what we can do never by itself tells us which to choose. The "ought" lives in what we are *for*: the good our nature leans toward, which reason sees must be done (ST I-II q.91 a.2; q.94 a.2). Many readers will grant the first half of this before the second; the witnesses below are the case for it.

**W1, insert after "On one point both agree: the wrong is necessary.":**
> And the wrong is wrong first because of what it does to the child; no one needs to know God's name to see it (Romans 2:14–15). The question is what the child is, and what kind of law this is, that it should be so. Not every "ought" wrongs someone when it is broken (A7); this witness rests on the ones that do.

**W1(1) (replace):**
> (1) *Forgiveness.* A wronged person needs forgiving, and only a person can forgive. When the one we wronged is dead, or never knew, no one on earth is left who can, and no one may forgive in her place (A3). Yet every wrong against a person is also a wrong against the One who made her and loves her (Psalm 51:4; CCC 1850). He does not forgive in her place, as if her wrong did not matter. He forgives what was done against Him; and because He can raise her and make her whole, He promises a judgment in which every hidden wrong is brought to light and set right (CCC 1038–1040). A truth can be true; only a person can forgive.

**W1(2) (replace):**
> (2) *A standing that is ours but not ours to cancel.* The victim's claim is truly his own. Yet he did not make it, cannot sign it away, and it belongs as fully to the newborn who cannot speak as to the man who can. One can say this worth simply follows from what a person is. Then the question moves one step back: why should exactly the same worth belong to the one who cannot use his gifts as to the one who can? Capacities come in degrees; this worth does not. The Church's answer is that worth follows the call, not the capacity: each of us is willed for his own sake, and all of us are called to the same end, God Himself (Gaudium et Spes 24). An equal call explains an equal worth.

**W1(3) (replace):**
> (3) *Law or no law.* The Catholic philosopher Elizabeth Anscombe pressed a question: if morality binds like a law, whose law is it? One serious answer is "no one's": it binds as a necessary truth binds. But a truth gives reasons; it does not hold a claim, receive an apology, or forgive one. If A2 and A3 are real (guilt that still needs pardon when the one we wronged is gone), morality behaves less like a theorem and more like a claim held by Someone.

**W1 closing, one gloss (replace "and to define what love must be submitted to"):**
> and to define, in the very natures He creates, what love must be submitted to

**W2 (replace from "If instead the pattern is fixed" through "W1 and W3 show why that Source is Someone and not something."):**
> If instead the pattern is fixed for any rational mind, there is an eternal order of ends that minds can know. The godless realist accepts exactly this and says it belongs to no one; the Church calls it God's wisdom ordering all things (ST I-II q.91 a.1). Whether that order is a set of truths or the mind of Someone is what W1 and W3 weigh. What this witness adds is the fit. The best answer without God says survival and well-being are themselves good, so a mind shaped by survival was roughly aimed at the good; its best defenders admit that some of the fit is still good fortune. The same question hangs over the mind that reads mathematics, and one Maker of law and mind answers both. The fit is not perfect: for thousands of years conscience failed to see the slave and the unwanted child. The Church expects that too, because our minds are wounded (CCC 37, 405). Yet once conscience reaches that far, it knows what it sees as true, against the interests of the one who sees it. And this law is one: its demands can be very hard (Chapter 11), but they never require us to do evil; when a choice seems to force a wrong, some wrong was done before it (ST I-II q.19 a.6 ad 3; 1 Corinthians 10:13). A law and a mind made for each other are best explained by one Source who made both.

**C, godless realism (replace):**
> - **Necessary truths that belong to no one** (godless realism) stand with us against relativism and matter alone, and their best defenders gather them under a few deep principles, as we do. What they leave unexplained is narrower, but real: why there are persons for these truths to be about, why minds able to read them exist at all, who can forgive when no one on earth is left, and why the newborn's worth is exactly equal to ours. Its stopping point is a set of truths; ours is Someone.

**C, a God who is Love (replace):**
> - **A God who is Love** is also a stopping point, and it carries its own mystery. But it is one stopping point where the others need several. It holds the necessary and the actual together: on the Catholic view, the natures of things and what they are for are first known in God's own mind (ST I q.15), so the law, the beings it governs and the minds that read it come from one Source. It makes sense of A1–A7 together. And it is Someone, which is what the shape of guilt, forgiveness and communion suggests.

**Evil paragraph, first point (append after "not evidence of a dark god."):**
> That does not yet answer the thought that there is no God at all; the next two points speak to that.

**Evil paragraph, second point (replace):**
> Second, we would expect a God who is Love to show us love, and we find it in A. We would not expect to see, from inside one short life, how every evil He permits is woven into the whole of history. Not seeing that is real weight, but it is the kind of not-seeing we should expect. It does not mean we cannot tell good from evil, or that God might deceive us; it concerns only His reasons for permitting, not what love is.

**Conclusion (append):**
> To the one who keeps A4 without God, it asks only: what is it about that newborn that makes her worth exactly what you are worth?

## 7. New insights; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
1. **Worth follows the call, not the capacity.** This is the theist's best answer to the equal-dignity question, and v2 never states it. The realist must posit equal worth; the Catholic can explain it from one equal end (GS 24). It also fits Patrick's "what we are for": equal dignity because the same end.
2. **The wrong is wrong first because of what it does to the victim.** Saying this plainly defuses the "God is a redundant reason" objection and keeps the chapter clear of voluntarism. God enters as the Source of the child and of the order, not as an extra prohibition.
3. **Forgiveness must include the victim, not skip her.** Ivan Karamazov's protest is the strongest moral objection to W1(1), and the Catholic answer (God forgives the offense against Himself; judgment brings every wrong to light; the victim is raised) is better than v2's wording.
4. **Where necessity lives.** Without the divine ideas (ST I q.15) the realist's "conditional necessity" objection lands: God's free creation cannot explain necessary truths. With them, one Source covers moral and mathematical necessity and the minds that know both.
5. **A7 is a gift and a warning.** It shows the realist that truthfulness binds; it also shows that not every ought is owed to someone, so W1 must stand on wrongs against persons.
6. **Chapter-text flag (new):** chapter line 77, "Every clause of it is something only a person can do, and only for a person — and a gift can only come from a Giver," repeats the Round 1 gap (only a person *does* the law, so a Person *gave* it). The realist grants the first half and denies the second. Rework it along the lines of repaired W1(1)–(2).

**Lines worth keeping**
- "Worth follows the call, not the capacity."
- "An equal call explains an equal worth."
- "A friend can release a promise as a gift; no one can make cruelty right."
- "A truth gives reasons; it does not receive an apology, or forgive one."
- "He does not forgive in her place, as if her wrong did not matter."
- "The wrong is wrong first because of what it does to the child."
- From v2, keep: "Forgiveness heals the bond; it does not remake the law." "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive." "Equal worth is not equal duty."

## 8. Score

**v2 as written: 7/10.** It fixed this school's two Round 1 complaints that mattered most (W1's invalid step and the release test), but its new wording opened fresh gaps (A3 against A2, God forgiving in the victim's place, a false Anscombe fork, an overstated C line, a disputed conclusion inside A4). **With the repairs above: 8/10.** Every remaining disagreement is then a fair contest of fit at parity, with our side leading on equal dignity and forgiveness; it is not higher because both stopping points remain brute and the weight of evil is honestly carried rather than answered.
