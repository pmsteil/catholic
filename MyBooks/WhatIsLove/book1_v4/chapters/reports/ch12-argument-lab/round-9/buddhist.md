# Blind review: the BUDDHIST school (Theravāda and Madhyamaka)

## 1. School and voice

I speak for the Buddha's Dhamma, a non-theist account: no creator, a beginningless round of dependently arisen minds and worlds (SN 15.1), a moral order of intention and its fruit that holds "whether Tathāgatas arise or not" (AN 3.134 PTS / 3.136 Bodhi), and an end that is the unconditioned, nibbāna. I draw on the Pāli Canon and Buddhaghosa for the Theravāda, and on Nāgārjuna, Candrakīrti and Śāntideva for the Madhyamaka. Among modern scholars I draw on Harvey and Keown (the virtue-ethics and "sanctity of life" reading), Goodman and Siderits (the reductionist and consequentialist reading), and Garfield (ethics as a change in how we see). Where these voices disagree I say so, because the text's strongest claims about Buddhism land differently on each.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** Non-harm, grounded in reciprocity: every being holds its life dear and shrinks from pain, so "taking oneself as the measure" one does not do to another what one would not have done to oneself (Dhp 129–130; SN 55.7). Beings are owed what follows from that: not to be killed, robbed, deceived or betrayed, and, from rulers, protection and the means to live, since poverty breeds theft and violence (DN 26). Equal treatment in law and punishment is owed too (Aśoka's edicts, PE IV, *verify*). **Holds, but thin on "owed to her."** Buddhism speaks mainly of duties, intentions and fitting responses (DN 31), not of claims that the victim holds. Keown argues that rights are implicit in the Dhamma; that is a reconstruction (*verify*). No borrowing.

**2. A hard dilemma.** Case: a dying man in pain asks his physician, a lay Buddhist, for a lethal dose. Principles: (a) the moral quality of the act is its intention (AN 6.63), and the intention to end a human life is always unwholesome, rooted in aversion (Abhidhamma commentary, *verify*). His request does not change that: the third pārājika arose exactly from monks who asked to be killed. (b) Compassion (karuṇā) requires full relief of pain. Pain relief aimed at relief, not death, is permitted (Keown, *verify*). (c) The rule does not require clinging to life by every means. The Vinaya commentary allows a gravely ill monk to refuse food and medicine in some cases (Samantapāsādikā, *verify*). (d) He should be accompanied so that he dies with a clear and calm mind. Result: refuse the dose, give full palliation, allow him to refuse burdensome treatment, stay with him. **Holds, and borrows nothing.** It converges strikingly with CCC 2277–2279, but the Vinaya is older and its reasons are its own: compassion as the wish that beings be free of suffering, and giving (dāna) as letting go, not as covenant or self-gift to a Person. **Where it strains:** our deepest reason is the act's root and the patient's chance to awaken, which is exactly what the text says it is.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** We ground equal *regard*, not a metaphysical "dignity," on four things: every being equally holds its life dear; every being is a continuum capable of awakening that is not defined by this life's faculties; every being has at some time been one's own mother (SN 15.14–19); and no crime cancels the capacity for awakening (Aṅgulimāla, MN 86). The enemy is included: even if bandits saw you limb from limb, hatred is not our teaching (MN 21). The human line holds from conception, by kind and not by capacity (Vin III 73, *verify*). **Holds** for equal regard and equal protection. **Strains or breaks** in three places:
- We grade the gravity of killing by the victim's virtue and size (commentarial, *verify*: Harvey). At parity, St. Thomas also grades sins by the excellence of the person sinned against (ST I-II q.73 a.9).
- Karma has been used to explain disability and low birth as earned (MN 135), and AN 8.29 counts a birth "dull and unable to understand" as an inopportune moment for the path. So our rationale for the special weight of a human life ("her chance") does not reach the severely disabled in this life; it reaches her only in future lives.
- The bearer of regard is a conventional person, not an ultimate self.

**4. The end of human flourishing.** Nibbāna, the ending of greed, hatred and delusion, "the highest happiness" (Dhp 204). The Mahāyāna adds buddhahood for the sake of all beings, with wisdom and compassion inseparable. Guiding principles: the Eightfold Path (conduct, concentration, wisdom), the four boundless states, and the middle way. **Holds and is coherent.** The end is not annihilation; the Buddha refused all four alternatives about the Tathāgata after death (MN 72). That is our own refusal of a name, at parity with CCC 206. **Parts** from the text exactly where W3 says: at the end, no one says to me that it is good that I exist.

## 3. Debate highlights

**E1. Equal worth needs no Giver.**
- *Attack:* The Buddhist meets nearly all of A4 from his own resources: shared love of life, a capacity for awakening that does not wait on present ability, Aṅgulimāla, every being as one's mother. W1(2) says that "an equal call, which does not wait on what anyone can do, explains an equal worth," and the final C row claims a ground for what the best rivals "must take as given ... equal, measured against one end." But we too measure every being against one end, awakening, which does not wait on what the newborn or the dying can do.
- *Defense:* Your end is reached by each one's own effort across lives; on AN 8.29 the severely disabled cannot even begin in this life. It is not given to her by anyone, and it does not keep her as herself. Ours is a call from Someone. It can be given as pure gift to the infant (CCC 1250, 1261), and its gifts are "irrevocable" (Rom 11:29). You also grade the gravity of killing by the victim's virtue, so you have equal regard but unequal gravity.
- *Rejoinder:* St. Thomas grades gravity by the excellence of the person sinned against too (ST I-II q.73 a.9), so that point is at parity. The rest of the defense is fair: the difference is not *whether* one end measures all, but whether it is *given by Someone* and *keeps her*. The text's own Buddhist row half-says this ("not a call to this one") but never grants us the one end.
- **Held with repair.** The advantage claimed in the final C row should be located in "given and kept," not in "equal, measured against one end" (Repair 4).

**E2. Persons are empty: W1(1)'s "her, herself."**
- *Attack:* W1(1)'s clue is that guilt asks to be forgiven by *her, herself*, which requires a persisting subject. The Madhyamaka gives arguments, not moods, that there is none: the chariot (Milindapañha), MMK 18 and 27. Conventional persons are enough for amends, forgiveness and dedicating merit. So the clue rests on the very point in dispute.
- *Defense:* The text already grants that this is a clue, not a proof. It weighs only "as much as the reader finds that a debt truly owed to her is one she must be able, one day, to receive." It states your view fairly. And your own practice asks the dead's pardon at funerals and dedicates merit to departed spirits who rejoice in it, so your practice presupposes someone who receives.
- *Rejoinder:* "Conventional" does not mean fictional. For Candrakīrti, a person designated in dependence on the aggregates is as real as anything conventional is, and the funeral pardon is fully coherent at that level. But one sentence misstates us: "the wish to be forgiven by her, herself, is on his path a form of the clinging the path dissolves." Asking pardon (khamāpana) is wholesome; the Buddha required it (Cullavagga I). What the path dissolves is the *view* of her as an abiding self, not the wish to be reconciled.
- **Held with repair** (Repair 1). The substantive standoff (is there a self who can receive?) is at parity, and the text weighs it honestly.

**E3. An unchanging cause cannot act; one Source is one svabhāva too many.**
- *Attack:* Whatever arises dependently is empty (MMK 24.18–19). A cause with its own unchanging being cannot cause: if it produces effects at different times it changes, and if it does not change, all its effects arise at once (Dharmakīrti, Pramāṇavārttika II; Vasubandhu on Īśvara, *verify*). And if, as St. Thomas holds, God bears no real relation to creatures (ST I q.13 a.7), then "Someone who wants *her*" is said only by analogy, and the text's contrast with Zhu Xi's Heaven, which "has a mind and has none," narrows.
- *Defense:* God's causing is not an event in time. To will a change is not to change one's will (ST I q.19 a.7), and creation is the creature's real dependence on God. The relation is "of reason" in God because His love is not caused by ours: it *creates* the goodness it loves (ST I q.20 a.2). The text says this exactly: "Our love answers to a worth already there; God's love gives it." That is not less than personal but more, "more truly than in us" (W2). And your own lawful order, which stands whether Tathāgatas arise or not, is just as much a stopping point. The text's final C row admits that every account stops somewhere.
- *Rejoinder:* Parity on stopping points. One small tension remains: W3 says "only a person can love us back," yet on this defense God's love is never a response. The text also says, rightly, that He "loved us first" (1 John 4:19).
- **Held.** The text's "not a brute stopping point" is contestable but explicitly argued as reach, not count. This is an honest standoff, not a defect.

**E4. The Theravāda ground is not "the killer's heart."**
- *Attack:* W1(2) says our reason for the absolute line "speaks of the killer's heart and of her chance, not of a worth she was given." But our ground looks first to *her*: she holds her life dear as I hold mine (Dhp 129–130, which the text cites; SN 55.7). The act is wrong because it is aimed against her. Numbers never enter, because the moral quality of the deed lies in the intention and its object, not in a sum. That gives a wrong done to *her* without any Giver.
- *Defense:* The text grants "a real reason, and not a count." But others love their lives too. If her love of life is the ground, why may five loved lives not outweigh one? You answer: because the *intention* to kill is unwholesome whatever the sum. That is the killer's heart. At her own asking she does not, just then, hold her life dear, and you appeal to her chance. So the summary is fair.
- *Rejoinder:* Granted in substance. We draw the line from the structure of the act (an intention aimed at a being who is a place of suffering and of possible freedom), not from a worth she holds that would outrank totals. But the text's summary leaves out her love of her own life, which is the first thing the Canon says.
- **Held with repair** (Repair 2). The main contrast stands: we do not ground the line in a worth she was *given*.

**E5. Ownerless suffering against A6.**
- *Attack:* A6 says accounts that count totals "cannot say why crossing it would be a wrong done to *him*." Śāntideva argues that sufferings have no owner and are to be removed simply because they are suffering (BCA 8.90–103, *verify*). He also allows the compassionate to do even what is forbidden when they see the benefit (BCA 5.84, *verify*), and Mahāyāna texts let a bodhisattva kill one who is about to kill many (Upāyakauśalya Sūtra; Bodhisattvabhūmi, *verify*). Goodman and Siderits read this as consequentialism grounded in the absence of a self. So "the Buddha's Dhamma" does not, as a whole, explain why the line "holds at every number." Part of it denies the rationale A6 rests on: the separateness of persons.
- *Defense:* The Mahāyāna cases concern a guilty would-be killer, not an innocent, so the line on the innocent still stands there. The monastic rule binds every school's monks. And the A heading lets any reader deny an A item and asks what the denial costs. A theory under which the surgeon may spend one for five pays the cost A6 names.
- *Rejoinder:* Fair. But the C row should not generalize the Theravāda line to "the Buddha's Dhamma." Note also that the correction *helps* the text: where our no-self teaching is pressed hardest, the one who is spent becomes hardest to see. That is evidence for the book's claim that "her" needs a ground.
- **Held with repair** (Repair 3).

**E6. The method trusts seemings, and the self is the most universal seeming of all.**
- *Attack:* The A heading trusts "what still seems true to us after honest reflection ... until something more certain shows it false." But the four perversions (AN 4.49) are seemings, and the most persistent of them, that there is a self, is false. Insight and analysis defeat it. The case's personal-ground arguments lean on that seeming.
- *Defense:* The principle has defeaters built in ("until something more certain shows it false, or shows that what produced the belief had nothing to do with its truth"), and your no-self arguments are offered as exactly such defeaters. A1–A7 are moral seemings, and you grant almost all of them. A persisting self enters only W1(1), as a clue and as doctrine (CCC 366), never as an A premise. And you trust the same method when you hold that cruelty is wrong and suffering is to be ended.
- *Rejoinder:* Granted. The method is shared; we differ over which seemings are defeated.
- **Held.**

**E7. Evil, providence and hell.**
- *Attack:* "If Brahmā is lord of the world, why did he not make it happy?" (Bhūridatta Jātaka, J 543, paraphrase). Ascribing all experience to a Lord's making leaves no reason for effort (AN 3.61). And an eternal hell sits ill with a God whose first word to each is "it is good that you exist." No Buddhist hell is eternal, and even Devadatta will one day be freed (Milindapañha; Lotus Sutra ch. 12, *verify*).
- *Defense:* Providence works through real secondary causes (CCC 306, 308), so effort is not idle. God gives the act its being, never its defect (CCC 311). Hell is a free refusal held to the end (CCC 1033, 1037). And the text itself calls hell "the heaviest thing there" and grants that the Buddhist bears this weight more easily.
- *Rejoinder:* The concession is honest. But it gives the wrong reason why we bear the weight more easily: not only that we "never said that being born is good," but that no one made the round, so no one must answer for the child, and that no being, not even the one who wronged her, is lost forever. The book's evil section should name that universal hope, because there the comparison is sharpest.
- **Held with repair** (Repair 5).

**E8. Karma as built-in justice: the design inference.**
- *Attack:* The Buddhist C row says "justice built into a world looks like the work of a mind." That is Udayana's old argument against us. Vasubandhu and Dharmakīrti answered it: intention seeds its own fruit in the mind's own continuum, and a supervising Mind adds nothing except the problem of evil.
- *Defense:* The text records the Buddhist answer and immediately weighs the other side (pain does not follow justice within one life). It rests nothing on the inference.
- *Rejoinder:* Then the sentence does little work. It is harmless, but it should either cite the classical debate or be cut.
- **Held** (Minor).

**E9. Mettā and "it is good that you exist."**
- *Attack:* Loving-kindness wishes every being well, and that is all love needs. To need "it is good that you exist," said forever, is the craving for existence (bhava-taṇhā) dressed as love.
- *Defense:* W3 already separates the longing that grasps from the longing to be given. It grants that grasping must die, and it grants that the Buddhist keeps wholesome desire for the path. It locates the parting exactly: "what is left," and "whether it is good that we exist at all."
- **Held.** This is a standoff of first principles, stated at parity.

## 4. Ledger

- **A heading:** Holds. The epistemic principle carries its own defeaters (E6). The list of deniers is fair and does not misstate Buddhism.
- **A1:** Holds. Buddhism agrees: unwholesome deeds are such for every intending being in every world-system.
- **A2:** Holds. Buddhist intention-ethics explains the secret-versus-blown-letter asymmetry at parity; hiri and ottappa are not the self-diminishing shame the text sets aside.
- **A3:** Holds. Consent cannot make killing wholesome (third pārājika). Forgiveness heals hatred, but the fruit still ripens (DN 2; MN 86).
- **A4:** Holds. Buddhism grants it nearly whole as equal regard. Graded gravity is at parity with ST I-II q.73 a.9.
- **A5:** Holds with repair. Aquinas's list (procreation, truth about God) is his own, not what every reflecting mind finds; offer it as his and add the Buddhist witness (Repair 6).
- **A6:** Holds with repair at C. A6 itself is fair, citing the Vinaya correctly as a witness against killing at one's request. The over-general Buddhist claim sits in the C row (Repair 3).
- **A7:** Holds. Buddhism agrees: seeing things as they really are; the Kālāma Sutta (AN 3.65) is a witness, but do not overread it as empiricism.
- **W1 frame:** Holds. Correctly lists the Buddhist among those who say pardon is asked of the victim, and that "an order of cause and fruit" is no creditor.
- **W1(1), the three roads:** Holds with repair. The Buddhist description is accurate (rebirth, departed spirits, the Dhp 5 commentary story, "neither the same nor another"), except the "clinging" sentence (Repair 1).
- **W1(1), the Catholic answer:** Holds. It is honestly marked as a clue and a hope; the doctrine is accurate.
- **W1(2):** Holds with repair. The Theravāda ground is summarized fairly but incompletely (Repair 2); the "one end" claim needs scoping (Repair 4).
- **W1 closing ("This Lawgiver is not a commander"):** Holds. Non-voluntarist and accurate; no Buddhist objection beyond E3's parity.
- **W2:** Holds with repair. Its "minds built for survival" premise does not touch Buddhism, for which mind is beginningless; the Buddhist belongs with those for whom law and mind are one (Repair 8).
- **W3, longing:** Holds. The Buddhist parting is stated exactly (what remains after grasping dies); the four boundless states are accurately named.
- **W3, hiddenness:** Holds. Fair to Buddhist contemplatives, and symmetrical ("each tradition can read the other's summit").
- **C: Nothing really binds:** Holds. Buddhism agrees with the text against error theory.
- **C: Feeling, refined:** Holds. Outside my school; no objection.
- **C: Our best words, widening:** Holds. Buddhism also denies that the slave is wronged only once "we" widens.
- **C: Reason's own law:** Holds. The Kant parallel ("his ground must stretch") applies to our "her chance" too (see §2, challenge 3).
- **C: What we owe each other:** Holds. Outside my school.
- **C: Care between persons:** Holds. Buddhism answers "the infant no one holds" with "every being has been your mother" (SN 15.14–19), a past bond kept by no one. The book could note it as an insight; it is not a defect here.
- **C: Well-being, counted impartially:** Holds. It applies also to the Buddhist consequentialist reading (Goodman), and fairly so.
- **C: Flourishing of our kind:** Holds. Outside my school.
- **C: Necessary truths:** Holds. The Buddhist lawful order is a cousin; the parity is acknowledged.
- **C: Impersonal sacred order:** Holds. Its "moon in ten thousand rivers" image fits buddha-nature too, and the text uses it so.
- **C: The Way that gives and does not claim:** Holds.
- **C: God, or Nature:** Holds.
- **C: The Buddha's Dhamma:** Holds with repair. It is generous and largely accurate (every citation checked is apt: Cullavagga I, DN 2, Sn 1.8, SN 36.21, Dhp 165, AN 8.29, NA 2). It needs Repairs 3, 4 and 7, and the karma design sentence does no work (E8).
- **C: History and class:** Holds.
- **C: Power and rank:** Holds. The Buddha as one free of resentment is correctly cited as a counterexample to Nietzsche.
- **C: Freedom that makes its values:** Holds.
- **C: A God who is Love:** Holds with repair. "Measured against one end" must be scoped (Repair 4). "Against the Buddhist, for whom love is not the ground of things" is accurate for the Theravāda and Madhyamaka; some Mahāyāna currents (Pure Land, buddha-nature) come nearer, but none makes love the *maker* of beings.
- **Evil ¶1 ("On the other side of the scale"):** Holds with repair. The weight is honestly heavy, and hell is named as the heaviest. Give the fuller reason the Buddhist bears it more easily (Repair 5).
- **Evil ¶2, First (privation):** Holds. Buddhism has no supreme evil either; delusion is a lack, not a power.
- **Evil ¶2, Second (permission is not a trade):** Holds. The Thomist doctrine is accurate; "a hope, not a proof" is honest.
- **Evil ¶2, Third (God suffers with):** Holds with repair. The Buddhist hope is correctly given but incompletely: it includes the wrongdoer and admits no eternal loss (Repair 5).
- **Conclusion:** Holds. The Buddhist grants that the abandoned newborn was wronged no less. "However gifted" (gifts, not virtue) keeps the comparison clear of the graded gravity both traditions teach. "It follows what she is" is the book's answer, not a forced inference.

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**As written: NONE.**

Every point at which my school presses hardest (no self who receives, a beginningless order needing no Source, an end that does not say "it is good that you exist," evil borne more easily without a Creator) the text either states fairly as the place where the accounts part, or hedges explicitly as "a clue, not a proof." I could not find a premise a Buddhist must grant that contains a Christian-only demand, nor a comparative claim against Buddhism that fails at parity once the wording repairs below are made.

**Minor:**
1. W1(1): the wish to be forgiven is called "clinging"; it is the self-view, not the wish, that the path dissolves (Repair 1).
2. W1(2): the Theravāda ground is reduced to "the killer's heart and her chance," omitting her love of her own life (Repair 2).
3. C, Buddha's Dhamma: "the line ... holds at every number" is generalized from the Vinaya and the Theravāda to the Dhamma as a whole. Mahāyāna upāya ethics and Śāntideva's ownerless suffering differ (Repair 3).
4. C, Buddha's Dhamma / A God who is Love: the claimed advantage "equal, measured against one end" overlooks the Buddhist one end; the real difference is "given and kept" (Repair 4).
5. C, Buddha's Dhamma: "No one judges" overlooks Yama, who questions the dead (MN 130), though he does not decide the fruit (Repair 7).
6. Evil section: the Buddhist's easier bearing of evil is explained only by "never said that being born is good"; the deeper reasons are no maker and no eternal loss (Repair 5).
7. C, Buddha's Dhamma: the karma design inference ("looks like the work of a mind") is raised and never cashed (E8).
8. W3: "love us back" sits a little awkwardly with a God whose love is never a response; the text's own "loved us first" is the better phrase.

**Misstatements of my school:** items 1, 2, 3 and 5 above. All are slight; none drives the argument. The text is, overall, the fairest presentation of Buddhism I have seen in a theistic apologetic.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**
- Is there a self who can one day receive the debt (W1(1))?
- Must worth be *given* and *wanted* to be one's own, or is a beginningless, unconditioned capacity for awakening enough (W1(2))?
- Does an unchanging God cause, and is His "wanting" more than analogy (E3)?
- Is it good that we exist, or only that we be freed (W3)?
- Which account bears evil better: one with a loving Maker who must answer for the child but can raise her, or one with no maker, no answer owed, and no eternal loss?

## 6. Proposed repairs

**Repair 1 (W1(1)):**
- Old: "and the wish to be forgiven by her, herself, is on his path a form of the clinging the path dissolves."
- New: "and while asking her pardon is, on his path, a wholesome act, the wish to be forgiven by her as an abiding self rests on the view of self that the path dissolves."

**Repair 2 (W1(2)):**
- Old: "That is a real reason, and not a count; but it speaks of the killer's heart and of her chance, not of a worth she was given. That she can suffer explains why harming her matters; it does not explain the line, for others can always suffer more."
- New: "That is a real reason, and not a count: it looks to her, who holds her life dear as I hold mine (Samyutta Nikaya 55.7), and to the act aimed against her, which no sum can make wholesome. But it draws the line from the act, the killer's heart and her chance, not from a worth she was given. That she can suffer, or holds her life dear, explains why harming her matters; by itself it does not explain the line, for others can always suffer more and hold their lives as dear."

**Repair 3 (C, Buddha's Dhamma):**
- Old: "It explains why intention matters most, why real good costs, why the line against killing a human holds at every number and even at his asking (A6, W1(2)), and why we must follow the truth (A7)."
- New: "It explains why intention matters most, why real good costs, why, in its monastic rule and in the Theravāda, the line against killing a human holds at every number and even at his asking (A6, W1(2)), and why we must follow the truth (A7). Not every Buddhist holds the line so: some Mahāyāna teachers allow a bodhisattva, out of compassion, to kill one who is about to kill many, and Śāntideva's teaching that suffering has no owner leads some modern readers to count totals (verify); where the teaching that no one owns suffering is pressed hardest, the one spent is hardest to see."

**Repair 4a (C, Buddha's Dhamma):**
- Old: "Some of its teachers say every being has the nature of a buddha, so that worth follows nature, not capacity; but a nature held whole by all, like the moon in ten thousand rivers, is not a call to this one."
- New: "Some of its teachers say every being has the nature of a buddha, so that worth follows nature, not capacity; and every being, the newborn and the one who will never think in this life included, is measured against one end, awakening, which does not wait on what she can do now. Its teachers add that every being has at some time been one's mother (Samyutta Nikaya 15.14–19), so that regard reaches this one, not only the kind. But a nature held whole by all, like the moon in ten thousand rivers, is not a call to this one, and a mother in a past life does not keep her now."

**Repair 4b (C, A God who is Love):**
- Old: "why worth follows kind and not ability, and is equal, measured against one end (W1(2));"
- New: "why worth follows kind and not ability, and is equal, measured against one end that Someone gives to each and that keeps her as herself (W1(2); the Buddhist too measures every being against one end, but no one gives it, and it does not keep her as a self);"

**Repair 5a (Evil ¶1):**
- Old: "The Buddhist never said that being born is good; he prizes a human birth as rare, but as the chance to be free of birth, so a world full of suffering is what he expects."
- New: "The Buddhist never said that being born is good; he prizes a human birth as rare, but as the chance to be free of birth, so a world full of suffering is what he expects; and since no one made the round, and no being is lost in it forever, he has no one who must answer for the child and no eternal loss to weigh."

**Repair 5b (Evil ¶2, Third):**
- Old: "the Buddhist hopes that every being, this child too, will be freed from suffering;"
- New: "the Buddhist hopes that every being, this child too, will be freed from suffering, and holds that no being, not even the one who wronged her, is lost forever;"

**Repair 6 (A5):**
- Old: "On reflection we find certain goods good and to be pursued, and find that good is to be done and evil avoided. Aquinas, who called this the first principle of practical reason, names them:"
- New: "On reflection we find certain goods good and to be pursued, and find that good is to be done and evil avoided (so too the Buddha's summary of the teaching of all the buddhas: not to do evil, to take up the good, to purify one's mind, Dhammapada 183). Aquinas, who called this the first principle of practical reason, names the goods he found:"

**Repair 7 (C, Buddha's Dhamma):**
- Old: "No one judges, the Buddhist says; the fruit simply ripens."
- New: "No one decides the fruit, the Buddhist says (even Yama, king of the dead, only asks the wrongdoer what he did, Majjhima Nikaya 130); the fruit simply ripens."

**Repair 8 (W2):**
- Old: "Those who say, with Kant, that the law is the very form of a reasoning will need none either, for in them law and mind are one; to them it puts the question why there are such wills at all."
- New: "Those who say, with Kant, that the law is the very form of a reasoning will need none either, for in them law and mind are one; nor does the Buddhist, for whom the deed is the intention and the law of deeds a law of minds. To these it puts the question why there are such wills at all (the Buddhist answers that the round of minds has no beginning that can be found, Samyutta Nikaya 15.1)."

**Optional (C, Buddha's Dhamma):** after "looks like the work of a mind," add "(so the Nyāya philosopher Udayana argued against the Buddhists, verify)", or cut the inference.

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights:**
- **Dhp 183 is a Buddhist statement of the first principle of practical reason** ("not to do evil, to take up the good, to purify one's mind"). It is a strong witness for A5, from outside theism.
- **The third pārājika's scope runs from the first moment in the womb** (Vin III 73, *verify*). A non-theist tradition holds the human line by kind, not capacity, from conception. This is a powerful witness for A4 and for the conclusion's newborn.
- **Aṅgulimāla (MN 86)** is a perfect non-Christian witness that the worst crimes do not cancel what a person can become (A4), and that the deed's fruit still stands after reconciliation (A3).
- **"Every being has been your mother" (SN 15.14–19)** is the Buddhist answer to "the infant no one holds." Naming it makes the book fairer. The book's reply is ready to hand: it is a past bond, and no one keeps her now.
- **AN 8.29 cuts against my own school.** Our rationale for the special weight of a human life (the chance to awaken) does not reach the severely disabled in this life. It is the exact analogue of the book's line on Kant ("his ground must stretch to reach her"). Use it gently, and only with the Vinaya's kind-based rule beside it.
- **Where no-self is pressed hardest, the person spent disappears.** Śāntideva's ownerless suffering leads the Goodman/Siderits readers toward counting totals. That is quiet evidence for the book's thesis that "her" needs a ground. The Theravāda keeps the line by the structure of the act, not by her worth.
- **Graded gravity is shared.** Both traditions weigh a wrong more heavily as the victim is more virtuous (ST I-II q.73 a.9). The conclusion is right to compare the newborn with the "gifted," not the holy.
- **Universal hope.** The sharpest Buddhist contrast in the evil section is not "birth is not good" but "no one is lost forever, the wrongdoer included." The book is stronger for naming it beside CCC 1033–1037.
- **Apophatic parity.** MN 72 (the Tathāgata after death is beyond all four alternatives) and CCC 206 are matching refusals of a name, and could be set side by side.

## 8. Score

**8/10.** The text states the Buddha's Dhamma accurately and generously, places every real divergence (self, Source, the goodness of existence, evil) where it truly lies, and hedges each witness at parity, so that what remains are a few wording-level misstatements, not unanswered objections.

**Honest ceiling for any theistic case against my school: 8.5/10.** The deepest disagreements are between first principles: a beginningless, dependently arisen order versus a Creator, emptiness versus a persisting self, freedom from becoming versus "it is good that you exist." Reason can show fit on both sides but cannot compel either. This text is close to that ceiling.
