# Blind review: the non-naturalist secular moral realist

## 1. School and voice

I speak for robust non-naturalist realism: Parfit, Enoch, Shafer-Landau, Wielenberg and Huemer; Fine on essence and grounding; Darwall, Wallace and Thompson on directed duties; Kamm and Nagel on constraints; McMahan on the moral status of infants. My view: there are objective moral truths. They are necessary. They are grounded in the natures (the essences) of persons, pain, promises and agency, and we know them by careful reflection on what seems true, which is Huemer's phenomenal conservatism. They need no God, and they need no dwelling, as Parfit says the truths of logic need none. I am the text's nearest secular ally. I stand with it against error theory, relativism, expressivism and naturalist reduction, so the only fights that matter here are the ones over what *God* adds. I hold the text to its own standard. Where it says theism explains something my school "must take as given," I ask whether it has explained that thing or just moved it somewhere else.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** Each person has a standing that is hers: she is a being whose life can go well or badly for her, who has a point of view, and who has authority over herself. That standing creates *directed* claims, which are bipolar relations of right and duty (Thompson, Darwall, Wallace). Kamm calls it inviolability: a status that expresses her worth, not a quantity to be maximized. Justice is honoring what each person is owed under those claims. → **Holds.** It borrows nothing from theism. It ends in a necessary first principle (persons have this standing), and the text also ends in a first principle.

**2. A hard dilemma, resolved.** Take conjoined twins: Jodie and Mary in the English *Re A* case (2000). Mary's life depends on Jodie's heart. Separation kills Mary. Without it, both die within months. My principles are these. Doing harm is worse than allowing it. Intending harm is worse than foreseeing it. Kamm's Principle of Permissible Harm (verify) allows harm that flows from the good being done, not harm used as a means to it. Mary has no claim on the continued use of Jodie's body (Thomson's violinist structure). And the most extreme kind of beneficence (kindness) has no priority over another person's claims. Verdict: separation is permissible. Mary is not used, and her death is not aimed at. She is no longer kept alive by a body she has no claim to. Palliative care is owed to her, and the truth is owed to the parents. → **Holds.** It uses justice as directed claims, honesty and beneficence. It does not use the book's covenantal "self-gift." Here I part concretely from the English Catholic bishops, who opposed the separation, and that is a real difference, not a borrowing. My distinctions echo the double-effect tradition, but by the fairness rule that genealogy proves nothing.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** My school does not speak with one voice here, and I will not pretend it does. The essentialist wing (Fine-style; it overlaps with the radical-capacity view held without God) says: worth supervenes on the essence of the kind, not on how far its powers are exercised. Every human, including the newborn, the dying, the severely disabled and the enemy, has the rational nature by kind, so each has the same basic standing, necessarily. The McMahan wing denies this. On its time-relative-interest account, killing a newborn is a lesser wrong than killing an adult, and some realists accept that conclusion. → **The essentialist answer holds.** It ends in a given: "essence by kind grounds worth." **The McMahan answer breaks against A4**, and it pays that cost openly.

**4. Ending point for flourishing.** An objective list (Parfit, Huemer, verify): knowledge, deep personal relationships and love, achievement, enjoyment, aesthetic experience, and moral virtue, lived in right relation to others. The guiding principles are a plurality of goods, impartial beneficence, and constraints and directed claims that block trading persons for totals. There is no single last end beyond a finite life. → **Coherent, with a real cost.** The list resembles Aquinas's basic goods (A5), but it does not borrow them. It has nothing to offer the infant who never flourished or the dead person who can no longer be repaid. That is tragedy, not incoherence. The text has seen this cost accurately.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1: Is the Mind idle once the wrong is "because of what it does to her"?**
- *Attack.* W1 says the wrong is wrong "because of what it does to her," and not merely because it is forbidden. That is my grounding: the wrong-making features belong to the victim and the act. Then what does "it holds first in a Mind" add to the *wrongness*? Either God's nature is good because it has good-making features, in which case the features come first and I have everything I need. Or the features are good-making because God's nature is as it is, which is an arbitrariness at the level of God's nature (Wielenberg's dilemma, verify).
- *Best defense.* There are two levels of grounding. The nearest ground is the victim's nature and the act's features, and the text gives that to the realist in so many words: "the fact that no one can release us does not decide between the accounts. Nor does the fact that guilt is owed." The ultimate ground is that God's wisdom is the exemplar (the original pattern) of every nature, so the natures are what they are because each images the Good in its way (ST I q.15). The Euthyphro dilemma is answered by identity, not by priority: God *is* the Good (Ch. 3).
- *Rejoinder.* Granted, and it is coherent. But it relocates the brute necessity ("the Good is good" in place of "cruelty is wrong") rather than removing it. The text admits this ("Every account has a first principle, ours included"). What remains is a claim of unification, not of a deeper explanation.
- **Held.** The text already concedes what it has to concede. This is a standoff at parity on grounding.

**Exchange 2: The "reach" argument in W2. This is the main witness aimed at my school.**
- *Attack.* W2 grants that "the features that make a deed wrong (pain, trust, betrayal) are the very ones survival taught us to notice" and that "Both are right as far as they go." That grants local reliability, which is the substance of Enoch's third-factor explanation (verify): survival and well-being really are good, and selection tuned us toward them. W2 then says that "neither explains the reach" and that Darwin's bees "might have fixed on the wrong ones." But a few lines later the text credits the very thing that supplies the reach: "the engine of that correction is reason holding itself to consistency with goods it already sees," which it says is how conscience extended itself to the slave and the infant. If consistency-reasoning carries truth when it corrects conscience, it carries truth when it extends a judgment from "wrong here" to "wrong wherever these features are." The step is guaranteed by supervenience: what makes a deed wrong makes it wrong in every world. Local reliability plus reasoning that preserves truth yields the reach. The text grants the first and credits the second.
- *Best defense.* The defense has three parts. (a) The general-reasoning faculty still needs explaining: why do minds built for survival grasp *any* necessary truths, which have no causal effects? (b) A7: the realist relies on the "ought" of evidence, which survival did not select. (c) Darwin's bees show that the starting points could have been wrong. One Source of law and mind explains why they were not.
- *Rejoinder.* (b) misses me: I affirm A7 as a necessary normative truth that I grasp the same way I grasp the others. That is the companions-in-guilt argument (Cuneo), not a borrowing. (c) cuts both ways. God could also have made bee-minds. And our moral starting points *were* badly wrong for millennia, as the text concedes about slavery, Seneca and the infant, which it explains by sin. Third-factor theory predicts that same pattern, reliable near survival and unreliable where interest pulls, with no extra hypothesis. That leaves (a), and (a) is the general problem of a priori knowledge. It arises just as much for logic and number. It is not a specifically *moral* witness, and the realist's answer there (Parfit: evolution selected reasoning abilities whose use gives access to non-causal truths, verify) is the same answer. Theism offers one explanation of that fit. The realist says no further explanation is needed beyond the necessity of the truths and the general reliability of reason. That is a disagreement about where explanation may stop.
- **Failed as written; Held with repair.** As written, "What neither explains is the reach" and "One Source of law and mind would answer that" claim more than the text's own concessions allow. The defensible claim, that a single source unifies the fit of reason to necessary truth across logic, number and right, is already in the text ("Unity without reduction…") and survives. See Section 6, R1.

**Exchange 3: Equal worth. "Must take as given," or grounded in essence?**
- *Attack.* The "Necessary truths" row says that realism must take as given, or deny, "why worth follows kind and not ability," and the "God who is Love" row says theism "gives a ground for what the best rivals hold but must take as given." My essentialist wing does not take it as brute. Worth is grounded in the essence of the kind, and the essence belongs to the kind, not to the exercise of its powers. That is the same structure as the text's: "Her worth rests on what she is." The text's own admission makes this sharper: "If her worth lies in what she is, then it is a gift exactly as far as her being is a gift, and here the moral question and the question of creation meet." So God's love explains why *she exists*. It does not explain a worth that my account lacks. Worse, the text's authority for "God's love gives it" is ST I q.20 a.2, and St. Thomas goes straight on, in a.3–4, to say that God loves some things more, because He wills them a greater good, and that one thing would be no better than another unless God willed it a greater good (paraphrase, verify). So a theory on which love gives worth, taken alone, predicts *unequal* worth.
- *Best defense.* (a) The good that grounds dignity is the one God gives every human being alike: a rational nature in His image (ST I q.93) and one call to one end (GS 19, 29). Further goods, grace and holiness, are unequal, which is why the text says "the saints are higher than I am in how well they love, never in what they are worth." That is *Dignitas Infinita*'s distinction between the dignity of what she is and the dignity of a good life (DI 7–8). (b) Measured against an infinite end, the genius and the newborn are "for the same thing." That explains incommensurability with price, the "worth beyond price," in a way a bare essence does not. (c) Unification: one Source grounds the worth, the law and the minds.
- *Rejoinder.* (a) is accurate Catholic doctrine and it rescues equality. But it rescues it by going back to *nature*, which is where I ground it. (b) is a real resource. But Kamm explains the no-trading constraint as a *status*, not an infinite magnitude: inviolability is a deontic structure, so it needs no infinite end. The newborn who will never think and the genius share one essence and are for the same thing *by essence*. Whether that end is ever reached concerns hope, not worth. There is one further Catholic-internal point. The supernatural call is a free gift, not owed to any nature (*Humani Generis* 26). If equal dignity rested *on the call* rather than on the nature, dignity would depend on a grace reason cannot see, contrary to DI 1. So the text has to lead with the nature, and that puts the two accounts at parity.
- **Held with repair.** Theism keeps an honest claim: it can say why there is such a kind and what it is for. It loses the claim that the realist "must take as given" what it grounds. See R2, R3, R4.

**Exchange 4: The debt to the dead (W1(1)).**
- *Attack.* The weight-bearing premise is that "a debt truly owed to her is one she must be able, one day, to receive." Why should that be true? Ought implies can for the *debtor*. Nothing shows that being owed implies being able to receive for the *creditor*. That a wrong can never be undone is part of why wronging is grave. A view on which every wrong is eventually received risks making the wrong lighter.
- *Best defense.* The text makes no stronger claim than this. It calls the debt "a clue, not a proof." It says the clue "weighs as much as the reader finds" in that premise, and that "what he owes her is the same on the second road and the third." It calls the realist's road "noble" and says "the Church walks much of it." It also blocks the cheap version itself: grace "does not make wrong into right," and the wrongdoer does not sit beside his victim "as though nothing had happened" (*Spe Salvi* 44).
- *Rejoinder.* None needed. The clue is honestly weighted. I give it little weight, and the text lets me.
- **Held.** A standoff at parity.

**Exchange 5: Constraints, and whether the line is hers to waive (A6, W1(2)).**
- *Attack.* A6 says accounts "that count only totals" cannot say why crossing the line is a wrong done *to him*. I agree. But Kamm and Nagel are not totalists. Inviolability as a status, and the agent-relative force of constraints, explain the wrong to him without God. And on waiver, much of my school holds that a competent person's request *can* make assistance in dying permissible, because her authority over herself is part of what she is owed. The "trust" ground (life "held in trust from the One who alone is Lord of life") also seems to make the wrong of killing depend on the Owner's prerogative, which is the shape W1 disavowed ("not a second injury beside hers").
- *Best defense.* W1(2) already names the inviolability answer ("a person has the standing of one who may not be violated") as true "as far as it goes." Unwaivability is marked repeatedly as "this book's claims, … not shared ones," and it is offered as a reason for those who already hold the line at every number. As for the trust ground: killing her at her own request is also against "the just love of herself" (CCC 2281), so the wrong to her is primary, and the trust explains why no one, she included, has authority to aim at her death. The asymmetry "He who … can give it back" explains why God's calling her home does not wrong her.
- *Rejoinder.* That is coherent, and the labeling is scrupulous. We disagree on the substance, and the text does not claim to have proved its side.
- **Held.** A standoff on waiver, and it is correctly labeled.

**Exchange 6: Evil, judged by the text's own moral principles.**
- *Attack.* By the text's own rules (A6: "Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent"; ask of each harm whether it could be justified *to her*), God's position is worse than a human bystander's. God sustains every cause (CCC 308) and wills the order in which the parasite feeds on the child (ST I q.19 a.9). The predation-and-parasite order existed "hundreds of millions of years" before any sin, so the Fall cannot explain it. Double effect lets a side effect be weighed only if it is proportionate. Proportionate to what, for the child?
- *Best defense.* The text agrees with this structure and does not dodge it. God gives the act its being, never its defect. The harms of natural causes come with an order He wills, not for their own sake. No person is given away to another creature's life (SCG III.112). Only "what is offered to her" can answer whether it could be justified to her, "which is a hope, not a proof." The parasite "stays on this side of the scale." For the fawn "that hope is thinner … and this book does not pretend otherwise."
- *Rejoinder.* Then the verdict that evil "does not overturn" the case is a judgment, not something shown. The text says exactly that ("in this book's judgment"; "An honest reader may weigh it differently").
- **Held.** The weight is real, and the text weighs it honestly. I weigh it more heavily, at parity in method.

**Exchange 7: The epistemic clause in the A heading.**
- *Attack.* The A heading adopts my epistemology: what still seems true after honest reflection is trusted unless something "shows that what produced the belief had nothing to do with its truth." Street's Darwinian dilemma presses exactly that defeater against non-causal moral facts. The theist faces the same defeater for religious and moral seemings formed by the same evolved faculties.
- *Best defense.* Each side has a story connecting the producing process to the truth: the realist has the third factor, the theist has design. The text's rule about origins is symmetric ("A history settles neither"; "No rival's origins are used against it that we would not let it use against ours").
- *Rejoinder.* Agreed. The clause is fair to both sides.
- **Held.** Parity.

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**S1. Serious: W2 claims more about "reach" than its own concessions allow.** W2 grants local reliability ("the features that make a deed wrong … are the very ones survival taught us to notice"; "Both are right as far as they go"). It later credits "reason holding itself to consistency" as what corrects conscience toward the truth. Those two together are the realist's explanation of the reach, because supervenience makes the extension from "here" to "anywhere" preserve truth. So "What neither explains is the reach … One Source of law and mind would answer that" is not supported against my school. What remains is the general fit between survival-built reason and necessary truth, which arises equally for logic and number. On that, theism offers a unifying explanation and realism holds that none is needed. That is parity, not an advantage. The text's own hedge ("it weighs, it does not settle") saves the section, but the flat sentences do not survive. *Repair: R1, R2 (last clause).*

**S2. Serious: The claim that theism grounds equal worth by kind where the realist "must take [it] as given" is not sustained at parity.** The essentialist realist grounds worth in the essence of the kind. The text grounds it in "what she is." The text admits that what God's love adds is her *being*: "the moral question and the question of creation meet." Read alone, the "love gives worth" rationale (ST I q.20 a.2) predicts unequal worth, given St. Thomas's next article (q.20 a.3). Equality is rescued only by going back to the nature and the one call, and the call is a free gift (*Humani Generis* 26), so dignity cannot rest on it alone without clashing with DI 1. Theism keeps a real but narrower claim: it can say why there is such a kind and what it is for. *Repair: R2, R3, R4.*

Neither objection is Fatal. Both are repaired by rewording, without giving up the argument.

**Minor:**
- M1. The "God who is Love" row says the realist must take law and mind "separately." The third-factor account connects them through survival's tracking of real goods plus reason. Say "connects through survival and reason rather than through a common source" (in R3).
- M2. Darwin's bees mostly illustrate goods that are relative to a nature (killing fertile brothers within a hive economy), which the text itself grants vary by kind. It is a weaker example for the *core* universal law (gratuitous cruelty, betrayal) than the text implies. R1 keeps the example and narrows its role.
- M3. The conclusion's closing appeal answers the person who denies A4, not the realist who keeps A4. One sentence should say where the case parts from that reader (R5).
- M4. A6 could name the non-consequentialist secular account of constraints (Kamm, Nagel) as agreeing with the line at five, so that its contrast with "accounts that count only totals" is not misread as a contrast with all accounts without God. Optional.

**Misstatements of my school:**
- "which it must take as given, or … deny" (the "Necessary truths" row): the essentialist wing grounds it in essence. Only some of us deny it (McMahan, and arguably de Lazari-Radek and Singer).
- "The realist answers that the reach comes with the concept": this understates the realist's position. The real answer is the third factor plus generalization guaranteed by supervenience, and Parfit's point that evolution selected abilities to reason (verify).
- "which he must take separately" (the "God who is Love" row): see M1.
- Everything else about my school is accurate and fair, especially "Every account has a first principle, ours included," Parfit's "no dwelling," and the concession in W1 that release and owed guilt do not decide between the accounts.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**
- First principles: "the Good is good" against "persons have inviolable standing." Each side ends in a given, and the disagreement is whether a single source counts as an explanatory gain.
- The debt to the dead: whether being owed implies being able to receive.
- The longing for communion: a clue that I weigh at nearly nothing.
- Why there are persons at all: this is a question about creation, not a gap in the moral explanation. The realist's moral truths are conditional on whatever persons there are.
- Waiver and the line at every number: correctly labeled as the book's own claims.
- Evil: weighed honestly. I weigh it more heavily.

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1 (W2, the reach paragraph).**
Old: "Both are right as far as they go. What neither explains is the reach. Survival can teach us that pain is bad for us; it does not explain how we can see that cruelty would be wrong for any such mind in any world. The realist answers that the reach comes with the concept, for whatever makes a deed wrong would make it wrong anywhere. That shows that some features make a deed wrong of necessity, not how minds built for survival came to know which; Darwin's bees are the reminder that such minds might have fixed on the wrong ones. One Source of law and mind would answer that."
New: "Both are right as far as they go, and the realist goes further. Survival can teach us that pain is bad for us; it does not by itself show us that cruelty would be wrong for any such mind in any world. The realist answers that it need not: if survival tuned us to features that really do make deeds wrong, reason, holding itself to consistency, carries the judgment wherever those features are, as it carries a proof, for whatever makes a deed wrong would make it wrong anywhere. That is the same engine this book credits below with correcting conscience, and it is a real answer. What it leaves is the fact it rests on: that minds built for survival grasp necessary truths at all, of logic and number as well as of right, and that the starting points survival rewarded were ones that are truly good. Darwin's bees are the reminder that minds could have been tuned otherwise. The realist holds that this fit needs no further explanation; this book holds that one Source of law and mind explains it, in logic and number as in right. That is a judgment about where explanation may stop, and the realist does not share it."
Also, in the closing parenthesis of W2, old: "its question of reach, to those who say moral facts simply exist." → New: "its question of fit, to those who say moral facts simply exist."

**R2 (C, "Necessary truths that belong to no one").**
Old: "why worth follows kind and not ability, which it must take as given, or, as some of its ablest defenders do, deny (W1); and whether the reach of conscience to truths that hold for every mind can be explained without a Mind, that is, how minds built for survival came to track truths that owe nothing to survival (W2)."
New: "why worth follows kind and not ability, which its best defenders ground in the essence of the kind, much as we ground it in what she is, and some of its ablest defenders deny; we add that what she is, and the end she is for, were given her by One who wanted her (W1); and whether the fit between minds built for survival and truths that hold for every mind, in logic and number as in right, needs explaining beyond the truths' necessity and reason's general reach (W2)."

**R3 (C, "A God who is Love").**
Old: "the difference is not fewer first principles but one source for the law and the minds that read it, which he must take separately (W2), and for the beings the law is about"
New: "the difference is not fewer first principles but one source for the law and the minds that read it, which he connects through survival and reason rather than through a common source (W2), and for the beings the law is about"
Old: "and gives a ground for what the best rivals hold but must take as given: why worth follows kind and not ability, and is equal, measured against one end (W1(2));"
New: "and gives one source for what the best rivals ground in several places or take as given: why worth follows kind and not ability, and is equal, measured against one end (W1(2)); the realist grounds the same in the essence of the kind, and the difference is that this account can say why there is such a kind at all, and what it is for;"

**R4 (W1(2), after "God's love gives it, by giving her being (ST I q.20 a.2).").**
Insert: "God wills greater goods to some than to others, and in that, St. Thomas says, some are better than others (ST I q.20 a.3); but the good that grounds her dignity is the one He gives every human being alike: a nature made in His image, and one call to one end. That call is His free gift, owed to no nature (*Humani Generis* 26), and so the dignity reason alone can see (DI 1) rests on what she is, while the Church finds its root reason in what she is called to (GS 19)."

**R5 (Conclusion, after "and it cannot compel the one who gives up A4.").**
Insert: "Nor does what follows part this book from the reader who keeps A4 without God; he too will say that her worth follows what she is. With him the case parts where W1 and W2 part: over who keeps her, and why there is a *she* at all."

## 7. New insights; lines worth keeping

**New insights for the book:**
1. Against my school, the honest hinge is creation, not morality. The text already half-says this ("here the moral question and the question of creation meet"). Saying it outright makes the case *stronger* against the realist. The realist has no explanation of why there are beings of a kind that carries such worth, or why their reason fits necessary truth, and the moral argument at its best hands off to that question.
2. The text's credit to reason's self-correction (Singer's expanding circle) is the realist's answer to the reach question. Use it honestly, and W2's general claim about fit, covering a priori reason and necessary truth across logic, number and morality, becomes the strongest version of the witness.
