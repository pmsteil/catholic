# Contractualist / second-personal review of "A moral argument for God"

## 1. School and voice

I speak for contractualism and the second-personal account of obligation: Scanlon (*What We Owe to Each Other*, 1998; *Moral Dimensions*, 2008; *Being Realistic About Reasons*, 2014), Darwall (*The Second-Person Standpoint*, 2006), Rawls (*A Theory of Justice*, *Political Liberalism*) and R. Jay Wallace (*Responsibility and the Moral Sentiments*; *The Moral Nexus*, 2019). On our view, wronging is relational. An act is wrong when any principle that allowed it could reasonably be rejected by someone it affects. Obligation is what people with equal standing to make claims on one another can rightly demand of each other. Underneath that, we are realists about reasons and do not need any metaphysics behind them. I am a non-theist, and I am arguing against the text. The text has absorbed more of my school than any theistic argument I know, so a good part of what follows is me admitting where it already stands on our ground.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**(1) Starting point for justice.** What anyone is owed is what principles no one could reasonably reject would require (Scanlon). The authority to demand it comes from the equal second-personal standing that any demand already presupposes in the person it addresses (Darwall). For basic institutions, justice means fair terms of cooperation among free and equal persons (Rawls). **Holds**, as far as it goes. It stops at a starting point it does not derive: equal standing is presupposed, not proved. That is a stopping point, not a borrowing.

**(2) A hard case.** Take the conjoined twins in *Re A* (England, 2000; verify details). Separating them will kill the weaker twin, who depends on her sister's heart. Not separating them will kill both within months. We compare individual complaints and never add them up. The weaker twin, through her trustee, objects to a principle that allows separation: it shortens her life by a few months. The stronger twin objects to a principle that forbids it: she loses a whole life. Her complaint is far greater, and the weaker twin is not being used as a resource. Separation is permitted. **Holds without borrowing**: the reasoning uses justice as justifiability and nothing the book means by "self-gift." I note that Catholic moralists were divided on this case (the English bishops opposed the surgery; verify), so it does not show a gap between us and settled Church teaching.

**(3) Equal, unconditional dignity.** For the competent, Darwall's answer is second-personal competence. Rawls's is a "range property": moral personality, held equally by everyone above a threshold (*TJ* §77, verify). The enemy keeps his standing whatever he has done, though he may become liable to some things. The dying keep the standing of the person they were. For the newborn and the person who will never be competent, Scanlon appeals to the relation of being born of human beings, and to trustees who speak for them (*WWO* ch. 4, verify). **Borrows / strains.** Why that relation gives full and *equal* standing is something we assert rather than explain. The text says exactly this about us, and it is right.

**(4) Ending point for flourishing.** We decline to name a single ultimate good. Because judgment is hard, reasonable people will hold different conceptions of the good (Rawls). The good of standing with others in mutual recognition (Scanlon) is the one we come closest to calling supreme. **Coherent, but it stops short.** It cannot answer the longing in W3 to be received whole by someone who will not die. We do not borrow anything to answer it. We have no answer.

## 3. Debate highlights

**E1. Anscombe and "Pufendorf's point."**
*Attack.* Darwall shows that the legal shape of obligation (a demand plus accountability) does not need a divine legislator. It needs a community of persons with equal standing to make claims on one another. Even a divine command could bind only an addressee who is competent to hold himself responsible, so the authority of persons comes first. The Anscombe opening of W1 therefore points no further than the moral community.
*Defense (text).* W1 does not argue from Anscombe to God. It grants that "the best accounts without God keep" these words. It also rejects command-based obligation outright: "A will that invents values can command, but it cannot bind; even a promise binds only under a law no promiser invented."
*Rejoinder.* None needed. The text does not draw the inference I was attacking. **Held.**

**E2. Whose wrong comes first?**
*Attack.* A2 is second-personal through and through: "Guilt faces a person: I did this to *her*." Then W1 says every such wrong is "before all else, an offense against Him," and W1(1) adds that "only God forgives sins." That reads like Ivan Karamazov's complaint: who gave God the right to forgive what was done to the child? Read that way, the victim's claim becomes secondary to God's honor.
*Defense.* "Before all else" is the wording of CCC 1440 and is accurate. The text blocks the hijacking reading three times. The offense is "not a second injury beside hers ... but His love refused in her." The wrong "is wrong because of what it does to her, and so it offends Him." And God "does not take her place in giving the pardon that is hers to give," while the wrongdoer must still repair what he can (CCC 1459; *Spe Salvi* 44).
*Rejoinder.* That is a sound answer, but it is spread across three passages. The sentence that says "before all else" never says *in what respect*. A second-personal reader meets the priority claim before he meets the explanatory order, and concludes the account has two ground floors. One clause would settle it (Repair R1). **Held with repair.**

**E3. Does "owed" imply "receivable"?**
*Attack.* Standing to forgive is a normative power, and normative powers can end with the person who holds them. When they end, the wrong is not cancelled. Debts that can never be released are a familiar and coherent moral structure. The second road (the debt outlives her and the living pay what they can) is complete. The third road needs an extra premise, that a debt truly owed must one day be receivable by her, and that premise assumes what it has to show. A further point: on Catholic teaching itself, some debts are never received. A victim who is damned does not forgive. A wrongdoer who is damned is never forgiven by her.
*Defense.* The text concedes nearly all of this. W1(1) is "a clue, not a proof." It "weighs as much as the reader finds that a debt truly owed to her is one she must be able, one day, to receive." It grants that a mind made for living bonds "would ask whether or not she hears." On the damnation point, the third road keeps the possibility open for every victim, and a refusal belongs to the one who refuses. The second road closes that possibility for everyone.
*Rejoinder.* Accepted. One adjustment: this is a gap in naturalism, not in contractualism. A contractualist who believed in survival could hold the third road. The C row puts the cost on the moral theory when it belongs to the metaphysics joined to it (Repair R6). **Held** (an honest standoff at parity, labeled as one).

**E4. The reach argument and where contractualism sits.**
*Attack.* W2 asks how minds built for survival can see what would be wrong for any mind in any world. Contractualism has its own answer: the form of justification. Any reasoner who takes up the standpoint of each person affected and asks what could be justified to each will reach the same core: gratuitous cruelty, betrayal, treating a person as a thing. Darwin's bees are wrong about killing their brothers for the same reason any of us would be: the brothers could reasonably reject that principle. The reach comes from the form, not from what survival happened to select.
*Defense.* The form only works if it has reasons to weigh: that pain counts, that trust matters. On Scanlon's own view those reasons are realist facts (*Being Realistic About Reasons*). So the question of how minds built for survival came to track them comes back at that level. The Kantian version of the question also applies: why are there reasoners at all, for whom this form is their own?
*Rejoinder.* True. The form explains convergence, not why the reasons are there to be weighed. Still, the C row should give the form its due rather than treating us as plain realists (Repair R7). **Held with repair** (minor placement). The question itself is a standoff, and W2 calls it one: "it weighs, it does not settle."

**E5. Equal standing for someone who will never be competent.**
*Attack (the text on us).* Why does being born of us, and not having certain powers, give full and equal standing? We take it as given. *Counterattack.* Theism asserts something too. "An equal call ... explains an equal worth" does not follow, because things ordered to the same end can still differ in worth, the way a better and a worse instrument do. And the scope of the call comes from revelation.
*Defense.* The text separates worth from attainment ("the saints are higher than I am in how well they love, never in what they are worth"). The end is attained by gift, not by capacity (CCC 1250, 1261), so capacity is beside the point. The call goes with the rational soul each human being receives (CCC 366), so the boundary of the call is principled, not stipulated. The relation is being willed for her own sake by the One who made her (GS 24). Unlike "born of us," that relation explains on its face why it gives worth: it *is* a valuing.
*Rejoinder.* I cannot match this. Both of us appeal to a relation. Theirs explains why the relation gives worth; ours ("of human born," as Scanlon put it, verify) does not. Darwall's route is worse for us here, because second-personal competence does not reach the newborn at all. The text's charge stands, and my counterattack fails. **Held.**

**E6. Grounding the line in God's dominion.**
*Attack.* At the point where the text claims to beat its rivals (the line holds at every number), W1(2) grounds it this way: "what protects her is ... that no number of others has any right to take her life. No one may take what is not his to take," because her life is "held in trust from the One who alone is Lord of life." That moves the source of the wrong from her standing to a third party's title. Then W1(2) reports, without ruling on it, that "some great theologians held that God, as Lord of life, could so command" the killing of children (Aquinas did, ST I-II q.100 a.8 ad 3; II-II q.64 a.6 ad 1, verify). If title is the ground, a holder of the title could in principle license the killing. In that case the killing would be justified to God, not to her, which contradicts A1 and W1's closing.
*Defense.* There are two grounds, and each does its own work. Her worth, willed for her own sake (GS 24), makes the wrong a wrong *to her*. The trust explains why no human, herself included, has authority to override that worth at any number. Then the text's principle that God "never spends her as a mere means against her own good" applies: God's will is His goodness, so a command to spend an innocent for others would be against her good, and the text's own principles rule it out. The Church reads those passages as "incomplete and temporary" (DV 15; VD 42), and the text measures them by Abraham's question.
*Rejoinder.* That defense is available to the text, but the text as written does not give it. It leaves Aquinas's view on the table with no ruling, and the sentence about what protects her mentions only the right of others, not her worth. A reader trained in second-personal ethics will take the worst reading. Both fixes are in R2 and R3. **Held with repair.**

**E7. Intention, consent, and "argued in W1(2)."**
*Attack.* The book's distinctive claims (no one may kill her even at her request; dying may be accepted but not aimed at) rest on the difference between aiming at death and accepting it. Scanlon argues at length in *Moral Dimensions* that whether an act is permissible does not depend on the agent's intention; intention bears on what the act means and on blame. Rawls and Scanlon both signed the 1997 "Philosophers' Brief" supporting assisted suicide (verify). And W1(2)'s argument for the line against killing on request starts from theism ("held in trust from the One who alone is Lord of life"). To a non-theist that is an explanation that holds only if theism is true, not an argument. Yet A6 says these claims are "argued in W1(2)."
*Defense.* The text labels these claims as "this book's claims ... not shared ones," and the C row for theism grants that "not every reader grants it." The case does not need the reader to accept them. Catholic teaching here also does not rest on psychological intention alone. It rests on the object of the act as chosen (VS 78), which Scanlon's criticism does not directly address.
*Rejoinder.* Fair. The flagging is honest, and the point about the object of the act blunts Scanlon. But "argued" promises more than an argument that assumes theism can give a non-theist (R4). **Held with repair** (minor).

**E8. Evil, judged by whether it could be justified to her.**
*Attack.* This is our strongest weight. Could God justify, *to the child with cancer*, the principle under which He made an order in which, by Aquinas's account, He "wills some things to be naturally corrupted" (ST I q.19 a.9)? A good given afterward does not make a harm permissible: you may not break my arm because you will later give me something wonderful. And the text itself says God "can give the highest good as pure gift" (CCC 1250), which He does for the baptized infant. So her suffering is not needed for her own good. It is needed for the order of the whole and for other creatures' freedom. That is the Stoic structure the text criticizes ("cares for the whole and spends the person"). The point is sharper for the fawn, and for anyone who in the end does not accept the good offered.
*Defense.* The text has already taken this on, in my school's own terms: "The best rivals rightly ask of Him what they ask of us, whether it could be justified to her; an order of the whole cannot answer that by itself, and only what is offered to her can, which is a hope, not a proof." It grants that "whether it is worth this world's cost is part of the weighing," and that hope for the fawn is "thinner." Its strongest available answer is a justification *before the fact*, not compensation after: a principle that creates a world of real causes and free creatures and offers every one of them an infinite good may be one the child could not reasonably reject, judged from her own standpoint. That is different from paying her afterward. And God permits; He does not aim at the loss.
*Rejoinder.* Justification before the fact fails for anyone who never receives the offered good, and for the fawn. Aquinas's "wills ... to be corrupted" moves God closer to the one who does the harm than "permits" suggests. Even so, the text claims no more than a hope. Its conclusion ("in this book's judgment it does not overturn it. An honest reader may weigh it differently") is labeled as a judgment, and the text puts the reader's question to itself in the right form. **Held** as a weight the text concedes. This is where my school and the text honestly part, not a defect.

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**As written: NONE.**

No objection from my school is both valid and unanswerable by the text at least to parity. The strongest candidate (E6) can be answered from the text's own principles. The heaviest weight (E8) the text already concedes, in my school's own terms.

**Minor items**
1. "Before all else" (W1 frame and W1(1)) is never tied to a respect, which invites the reading that the victim's claim is derivative (E2; R1).
2. The at-every-number sentence in W1(2) grounds the line in others' lack of right alone, and the passage on the theologians who held God could command the killing of children is left without a ruling (E6; R2, R3).
3. "Argued in W1(2)" claims more than an argument that assumes theism can deliver to a non-theist (E7; R4).
4. A7's "may not stop looking because we fear what we will find" is stronger than any norm of inquiry needs to be. Nothing depends on it.

**Misstatements of my school (all minor)**
- "That worth too it takes as given." Scanlon does treat the value of mutual recognition as basic. Darwall argues instead that equal standing is presupposed by any demand at all, including guilt's demand of oneself. The fuller statement actually helps the text, because that argument reaches only those who can be addressed, so it does not reach the newborn (R5).
- "It cannot say that she can ever forgive." That is true of contractualism joined to naturalism, not of contractualism as such (R6).
- "With Stephen Darwall, that no trustee can forgive in her place." That the victim alone has the individual authority to forgive is securely Darwall's. The specific claim about trustees should be cited by work and page or reworded (verify).
- The C row could say what the form of justification adds to realism about reasons (R7).

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Whether a debt that can never be released is a complete moral structure (second road) or points to a receiver (third road) (E3).
- Whether the reach of conscience is explained by the form of justification plus reasons that simply hold, or by one Source (E4).
- Whether permitting evil could be justified to the child, through a good offered before the fact (E8). This is the deepest point where we part.
- Whether intention, or the object chosen, bears on permissibility (E7). This applies only to the book's flagged claims.

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1 (W1 frame).**
Old: "every such wrong is also, and before all else, an offense against Him (CCC 1440, 1850): not a second injury beside hers,"
New: "every such wrong is also, and before all else, an offense against Him (CCC 1440, 1850): before all else in what it puts at stake, not in what makes it wrong, for it is wrong because of what it does to her. It is not a second injury beside hers,"

**R2 (W1(2)).**
Old: "That is why, on this account, the line has no exceptions for any of us: what protects her is not a weight that enough lives could outweigh; it is that no number of others has any right to take her life."
New: "That is why, on this account, the line has no exceptions for any of us: what protects her is not a weight that enough lives could outweigh; it is her own worth, willed for her own sake, and that no number of others has any right to take her life. His lordship over life does not stand in for her claim; it is why no one, not even she, holds the authority to set it aside."

**R3 (W1(2)).**
Old: "This book reads them as the record of a people God was still teaching, not as His approval of killing children, and measures them by Christ,"
New: "This book reads them as the record of a people God was still teaching, not as His approval of killing children; His lordship over life is not a right to spend anyone against her own good, and this book does not hold that He would command it. It measures them by Christ,"

**R4 (A6).**
Old: "Those are this book's claims, argued in W1(2), not shared ones:"
New: "Those are this book's claims, explained in W1(2) on this book's own premises, not shared ones:"

**R5 (C, What we owe each other).**
Old: "And why answering to each one is what we owe each other, it explains by the worth of standing with each person in mutual recognition, and that worth too it takes as given."
New: "And why answering to each one is what we owe each other, it explains by the worth of standing with each person in mutual recognition, or, with Darwall, by the standing that every demand already presupposes in the one addressed; the first it takes as given, and the second reaches only those who can be addressed."

**R6 (C, What we owe each other).**
Old: "When the one we wronged is gone, it can say she is still owed, and, with Stephen Darwall, that no trustee can forgive in her place; it cannot say that she can ever forgive."
New: "When the one we wronged is gone, it can say she is still owed, and, with Stephen Darwall, that the standing to forgive is hers alone; joined, as it usually is, to a view on which nothing of her remains, it cannot say that she can ever forgive."

**R7 (C, What we owe each other).**
Old: "Its best defenders hold these as truths that simply hold, and there it meets the realist's question (W2)."
New: "Its best defenders hold these as truths that simply hold, and there it meets the realist's question (W2): the form of justification it adds explains why any reasoner who weighs each person's reasons reaches the same core, not why those reasons are there to be weighed."

## 7. New insights and lines worth keeping

**New insights for the book**
- **The Philosophers' Brief (1997).** Rawls and Scanlon signed it, along with Dworkin, Nagel, Nozick and Thomson (verify). It is a concrete, named witness for A6's "many who hold the rest of A would let a dying man ask for death," drawn from the strongest non-theist school on the non-aggregation core.
- **Contractualism's individualist restriction** is the strongest secular reason for holding the line at vast numbers: no sum of others' complaints answers hers. Crediting that by name would make the comparison fairer. It also sharpens the book's real distinctive claim, which is not the line at every number but the line against killing even at her request.
- **Darwall's argument cuts for the book at the newborn.** If standing is what any demand presupposes in the one addressed, it reaches only those who can be addressed. The best second-personal ground for dignity is therefore the one least able to reach the infant no one holds. The conclusion could use this.
- **Ivan Karamazov's question.** The text's answer (God forgives the offense against Himself whole, does not pardon in her place, and leaves repair owed, per CCC 1459 and *Spe Salvi* 44) is the best Catholic reply to the second-personal worry about divine forgiveness that I know. Naming the worry would show the text has already met it.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent."
- "Guilt faces a person: I did this to *her*."
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."
- "The best rivals rightly ask of Him what they ask of us, whether it could be justified to her."
- "Heaven and Earth give life to things; God wanted *you*."
- "The rebel can take her side; he cannot keep her."
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it."

## 8. Score

**8 / 10.** The text has already absorbed my school's two central contributions: that wronging is directed at a person, and that her justifiability claim applies even to God. It wins the one point where we really strain (equal standing for the never-competent). It leaves only honest standoffs, plus a few clarity repairs at W1's "before all else" and the dominion sentence.

**Honest ceiling for any theistic case against my school: 9.** No theistic argument from reason alone can show that permitting a child's suffering could be justified *to her*. The text says this itself.
