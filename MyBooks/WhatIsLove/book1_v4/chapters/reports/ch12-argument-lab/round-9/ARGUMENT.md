# A moral argument for God (for Chapter 12 of a Catholic book)

**Form:** a converging case (CCC 31). Things we take to be true (A), witnesses that point beyond them (W), and an honest comparison of where each account stops (C). Reason's witness: the Church teaches that arguments of this kind can bring a person to certainty (CCC 31, 36), and it can predispose one to faith (CCC 35), but it does not replace faith.

**A. What we take to be true.** These are offered as truths: not as feelings people happen to share, nor as the best words one community has come to so far. What still seems true to us after honest reflection, including honest reflection on how we came to believe it, we have reason to trust until something more certain shows it false, or shows that what produced the belief had nothing to do with its truth. (For the one who says these truths are real but need no Mind, W2 takes up the claim that evolution has shown this; for the one who says nothing binds, A4 and the conclusion take it up, and there the case ends in a choice, not a proof.) None is beyond denial. Mackie denies that any of them is objectively true; Engels, that any moral truth is eternal, since each morality has answered to the economic life of its age; Rorty asserts most of them but denies that any is true of anything beyond our widening "we," and Harman and Wong hold them true only relative to a morality; Sartre denies that any is found rather than made by freedom; Spinoza, who keeps God's name, denies that anything is a wrong before a people's laws, and that worth is equal where power is not; Nietzsche denies A1 through A4, the second half of A6, and that A7 binds everyone; Singer denies parts of A2 through A7, and McMahan, no utilitarian, denies that killing a newborn wrongs her as gravely as killing a grown man; Zhuangzi would deny that we still owe the one who is gone; and many who hold all the rest deny the Church's teaching that the line against killing the innocent holds at every number and against a man's own request or consent, which A6 names as this book's claims. This is not a vote. The comparison (C) shows what each denial costs; the reader can ask whether he can pay it.
- **A1. Some things are really wrong, for everyone.** Torturing a child for fun, betraying a friend for gain, enslaving the weak. Not merely disliked: wrong even if every culture approved.
- **A2. Doing them leaves us guilty: not merely mistaken, and not merely flawed.** A wrong sum needs correcting; a flawed character needs reforming; a wrong done to a person needs forgiving. Making the world worse and wronging someone are not the same. If I promise my dying friend to keep a secret she trusted to me, and after her death tell it for a laugh to a stranger in a far city who never knew her and will never repeat it, nothing in anyone's experience is worse, and still she was wronged. Had the same stranger found it in a letter blown into the street, her wish that it stay hidden would be just as frustrated, and no one would have wronged her. Guilt faces a person: I did this to *her*. Shame that looks only at itself says I am less; guilt says I wronged her; only guilt asks her pardon. Traditions differ on what heals guilt, but almost none treats it as a mere mistake. And many wrongs are done by many hands, through customs and systems no one of us chose. They are still done to *her*, and whoever takes part in them willingly, approves them, or fails to hinder them when he is bound to, shares in the wrong (CCC 1868); the Church calls such systems structures of sin, the expression and effect of personal sins (CCC 1869).
- **A3. No one on earth can make the gravest wrongs right.** A friend can release a promise as a gift. But no one could consent his own enslavement, or a child's torture, into being right, beforehand or after. The one we wronged can forgive us; that heals the bond, but it does not make the wrong not wrong. No one else on earth can give that pardon in her place: not her friends, not a nation by vote, not the whole human race. And when she is gone, the wrong does not go with her: we still owe her something we can no longer fully pay her.
- **A4. Every human being has the same dignity, whatever he can or cannot do.** The newborn, the dying, the severely disabled, the enemy: equal, prior to anyone's recognition, never to be used as a mere means, not for the many and not for the few; never, if innocent, to be deliberately killed for others' sake, nor "for his own good" without his asking (nor, the Church adds, even at it); and not lost when life grows hard, nor even by the worst of crimes (*Dignitas Infinita* 1, 15; CCC 2261, 2267). (That his asking can never make it right to kill him, and that these lines hold however vast the numbers, the Church teaches: CCC 2277; *Evangelium Vitae* 65–66. A6 names both as this book's claims, not shared ones.) Equal worth is not equal duty: we owe more to our own parents and children than to strangers, but no one is worth less. Nor is it equal holiness: the saints are higher than I am in how well they love, never in what they are worth. Our instincts favor the healthy, the beautiful and our own; where instinct fails, for the infant no one is drawn to hold, we learn whether what we meant was worth or only attraction. So this is the belief that an account of us as built only for survival least predicts. Reason can see it (DI 1), yet history shows how easily it is lost (CCC 37–38; *Dignitas Infinita* 10–13): Seneca, who wrote that slaves are men, wrote as calmly that weak and deformed newborns are drowned, and Christians too were slow to see what slavery denied.
- **A5. What we can do, and what we want, do not by themselves settle what we ought to do.** We are capable of truth and lies, justice and injustice, mercy and cruelty, giving and grasping, and we carry good desires and disordered ones. We also begin, and many of us end, wholly in others' hands, and those who hold us need holding too (CCC 2208–2209). Listing what we can do, or what we want, never by itself tells us which to choose. On reflection we find certain goods good and to be pursued, and find that good is to be done and evil avoided. Aquinas, who called this the first principle of practical reason, names them: life; the union of man and woman and the raising of children; knowing the truth, the truth about God included; and living in society with others (ST I-II q.94 a.2). Whether that means our nature carries its own ends, as Aristotle held, and whether Someone gave it those ends, is what W must weigh.
- **A6. Real good for another always asks something of the giver** (time, attention, comfort, sometimes far more). That is how reality works for creatures like us. And some things must never be done: not to save ourselves, and not to buy a better outcome for many. A surgeon may not kill one healthy patient to give his organs to five, even if no one would ever know; a city may not secretly hang an innocent man to calm a riot; no prisoner may be experimented on to death, though many would be saved. Almost everyone sees these at once. Some say this certainty is only the flinch of a mind built to recoil from violence up close; but no one is pushed in the riot or in the hidden experiment, and the line holds there too; and the regard for the many that would spend the one has a history of its own, in kin and in favors returned. A history settles neither. Fewer hold the line when the numbers grow vast (Mencius did: none of the sages, he said, would kill one innocent man even to win the whole realm, *Mencius* 2A2), and many who would never hang the innocent man would bomb a city to end a war. Fewer still hold that a man's own request or consent could never make it right to kill him (the Buddhist monastic rule is one witness, Vinaya, third pārājika): if he may lay down his life for others, as a firefighter does (John 15:13), why may he not ask for death? The Church holds the line at every number (*Veritatis Splendor* 52, 80; CCC 2314), and even against a man's own request or consent (CCC 2277, 2280–2282, 2295; *Evangelium Vitae* 65–66; *Samaritanus Bonus* III): a man may lay down his life for another, or for the truth, and accept the death that comes, even at the hands of those who wrongly kill him: so Christ, who laid down His life of His own accord (John 10:18; CCC 609), and so St. Maximilian Kolbe, who offered himself in a condemned man's place; but a man may not aim at his own death, as an end or as a means, by his own hand or another's, and his asking never makes it right for anyone to kill him. Those are this book's claims, argued in W1(2), not shared ones: many who hold the rest of A would let a dying man ask for death, or a volunteer give his life to an experiment. Yet many who would cross the line for a city will not cross it for five. The best accounts that count only totals can say why it is good that we hold the line firmly, and why a rule against crossing it does more good than a rule that allows it; they cannot say why crossing it would be a wrong done to *him*, the one spent, and not only the breaking of a useful rule. Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent. Weighing is not a license: those whose usurious and avaricious dealings lead others to hunger and death indirectly commit homicide, and a society that accepts murderous famines without trying to remedy them commits a grave injustice (CCC 2269). The absolute can cost lives, even many. Without it, no innocent is safe, in principle, from being spent. The Church's rule is that one may never do evil so that good may come of it (CCC 1789).
- **A7. We are bound to the truth.** We may not believe against the evidence because a falsehood would comfort us, even if no one would ever know; and we may not stop looking because we fear what we will find. "Follow the evidence" is not advice for those who happen to want truth. It is something every reasoning mind ought to do (CCC 2104). It binds believer and unbeliever alike.

**W. Witnesses**
- **W1. Wronging points to her, and beyond her to Someone.** The Catholic philosopher Elizabeth Anscombe saw that our words "obligation," "duty" and "morally wrong" came to us from faith in a divine law, and she asked whether they keep their sense without it. She did not answer that question with God; she proposed setting the words aside. The best accounts without God keep them, and keep a great deal: justice is owed to another, a wrong must be set right, and no vote can make cruelty good. On one point every account that keeps these words in the sense Anscombe meant agrees: the wrong is wrong of necessity, not up to anyone. (Some keep the words and let that sense go: for the pragmatist the wrong is not up to any one of us, but it is ours; for the relativist it is wrong relative to a morality. C weighs what that costs.) We see it first in what it does to the child, and those who have never heard of a revealed law can see it (Romans 2:14–15): it is not wrong merely because it is forbidden. Not even God makes cruelty right; He cannot, because He is the Good (Chapter 3). And the Church adds that, because His love made her precious, every such wrong is also, and before all else, an offense against Him (CCC 1440, 1850): not a second injury beside hers, for nothing harms God (Job 35:6–8), but His love refused in her, and so a turning away from Him. He is offended by us, St. Thomas says, only because we act against our own good, the good He wills for us (*Summa contra Gentiles* III.122). So the fact that no one can release us does not decide between the accounts. Nor does the fact that guilt is owed. An order of cause and fruit can say the deed's fruit will come, and a necessary truth can say the deed was wrong; neither is anyone the guilt could be owed to. But the nearest rivals, the realist, the Kantian, the contractualist, the Aristotelian and the Buddhist among them, answer that pardon must be asked of the one we wronged and amends made to her, and this book agrees: guilt is owed to her, truly and in her own right, and not to God alone (A2). We agree she is owed. We part at the grave, and over what gives her the claim at all. Two witnesses take up those two questions. (Not every *ought* wrongs someone when it is broken, A7; these two rest on the wrongs that do.)
  (1) *The debt still owed.* A wrong done to a person needs forgiving, and the pardon that heals the bond between us is hers to give. When she has died, no one on earth can give it in her place, and still we owe her (A3). There are three ways to take that. Some deny it: the debt died with her, and what is left is remorse, a wound that understanding may quiet, as Spinoza taught, or forgetting may heal, as Zhuangzi taught, but that will never be healed by the one it was done to. Or the debt outlives her, owed to her as she was, and the living repay what they can: they keep her memory, honor her wishes, care for those she loved. That is the road of many philosophers without God, who hold that the dead can be wronged as they were and that the wrong stays fixed forever; and it is the road of Xunzi, who taught that the cultivated man keeps the rites for the dead as the human way of completing a bond, without holding that the dead hear. Confucius himself sacrificed to them as though they were present, and would say no more. Or she is still someone, and the debt can one day be received by her. The second road is noble, and the Church walks much of it. But on that road, what the living pay, she is not there to take up or answer; what can never be repaid, no one ever releases; and many of the dead have no one left to remember them by name. The ancient critic Mozi said that to deny the spirits and still study the rites is like learning the ceremonies for guests when there are no guests. Xunzi's answer, that the rites were never for guests but give grief its due form and finish well what death broke off, is true of the living; it does not reach her. Guilt does not only mourn her. It asks to be forgiven by her, and only someone can forgive. Science can show why minds like ours would speak to their dead whether or not anyone hears, and Nietzsche traced how a people's debt to its founders grew into a debt to its gods; so the speaking alone settles nothing. Neither can show that no one hears. Most who have kept the ancestral rites, in China and among many peoples, walk a form of the third road too: the named dead come to the offering, and are told, thanked and asked. But on that road the dead are kept by the living; each is fed as long as her own line remembers her, and those whom no one remembers become the wandering dead, whom a whole people feeds without a name. What keeps her there is the memory of her kin, and memory fails; where rites of universal deliverance reach the forgotten, they feed them and send them on, but do not keep each as herself. The Buddhist walks another form of the third road without a Keeper: the dead are born again, those born among the departed spirits can receive what the living dedicate to them, and in some lands the living ask the dead person's pardon at the funeral. His tradition even tells of two women who had wronged each other through many lives, brought face to face by the Buddha so that the hatred between them could end (Dhammapada Commentary on verse 5). But on his own account the one born again is neither the same as she was nor another, and is not, at the ground of things, a self at all; whether the two ever meet again depends on the currents of their deeds, and no one keeps her for the meeting; and the wish to be forgiven by her, herself, is on his path a form of the clinging the path dissolves.
  The Catholic answer begins with God. Every wrong against a person is also, and before all else, an offense against the One whose love made her precious (CCC 1440, 1850), and only God forgives sins (CCC 1441). He does so through His Church: first in Baptism, and for sins after Baptism ordinarily in the Sacrament of Reconciliation (CCC 977, 1263, 1442, 1484); and His forgiveness is whole. Yet it does not cancel what we owe her: absolution "does not remedy all the disorders sin has caused" (CCC 1459), so we must still repair what we can, and God does not take her place in giving the pardon that is hers to give. Nor has she ceased to be. The soul does not perish at death (CCC 366); she lives to God (Luke 20:38), who forgets no one (Isaiah 49:15); and on the last day He will raise her body too (CCC 998). Death does not cut the bond: the living can still reach the dead in love, with prayer and even with a request for pardon, and it is never in vain (*Spe Salvi* 48; CCC 958). And no one dies with no one left to speak for her: at every Mass the Church prays for the dead, including, in one of her prayers, those whose faith God alone has known. He did not keep her from what was done to her; that weight is on the other side of the scale, and this book weighs it there. What He keeps is her claim, and her, so that what was done to her is not the last word over her. How the last reconciliation between a victim and the one who wronged her is completed, the Church does not spell out. She says that grace "does not make wrong into right," and that the wrongdoer does not sit at the eternal banquet beside his victim as though nothing had happened (*Spe Salvi* 44); and Benedict XVI, taking up the view of some recent theologians, proposes that the fire which both burns and saves is Christ Himself: before His gaze "all falsehood melts away," and His touch heals whoever is open to Him, the guilty too (*Spe Salvi* 47). Reason cannot prove that hope. It can see that guilt asks for what it could have only if she is still someone, herself, and that this hope holds it open: to be forgiven by her. That guilt asks proves nothing, for a mind made for living bonds would ask whether or not she hears, as grief asks for what may not come back. And what he owes her is the same on the second road and the third; the roads part over whether she is there to receive it. So, like the longing in W3, this is a clue, not a proof: it weighs as much as the reader finds that a debt truly owed to her is one she must be able, one day, to receive. (For a reader who holds that our word "owed" voices refined feeling, it weighs little, since on his account no judgment of what is owed can tell him who is still there.) A truth can be true; only a person can forgive.
  (2) *A worth no deed confers and no order wants.* The victim's claim is truly her own. She did not make it, cannot sign it away (A3), and it belongs as fully to the newborn who has done nothing to earn it as to the man who has done much. Being human explains why the newborn belongs. The best accounts without God also explain much of why she may not be killed for others: the wrong would be done to *her*; each person must be answered one by one, and no total answers her; the five who need her organs have no claim on her life; and doing harm is not the same as failing to help, which is Philippa Foot's own answer to the magistrate and the mob. Asked why justice has that shape, some answer that this is simply what justice is; others, that a person has the standing of one who may not be violated. Many let the line bend when the numbers grow vast (A6). What they say is true as far as it goes, and each is a stopping point; and few say why the line is not even hers to waive. Kant did. He grounded it in the humanity in her own person, a worth beyond price that admits no equivalent and so cannot be traded even for many; so that she cannot release herself, he regarded her in two ways, her free reason binding her as a natural being; and he counted the newborn a person, though his ground must stretch to reach her. The Theravāda Buddhist does too. He grounds regard in what every being shares, that each fears pain and holds life dear (Dhammapada 129–130), and holds the line against killing a human at every number and even at his asking, because the will to end a life is rooted in aversion and takes from him the rare birth in which he could be freed. That is a real reason, and not a count; but it speaks of the killer's heart and of her chance, not of a worth she was given. That she can suffer explains why harming her matters; it does not explain the line, for others can always suffer more. A law or an order gives a nature to every member of a kind; it does not want any one of them. Even a Way that gives each thing its own self-so, and not only its kind, as Zhuangzi's does, gives without wanting: it lets each one be, and lets each one go. If her worth lies in what she is, then it is a gift exactly as far as her being is a gift, and here the moral question and the question of creation meet. Our love answers to a worth already there; God's love gives it, by giving her being (ST I q.20 a.2). Her worth rests on what she is, a person made in God's image, and on the call He gives with her being, to communion with Him, which the Church calls the root reason for human dignity (GS 19; CCC 1700). It is not added later, and this dignity, the dignity of what she is, cannot be lost, not even by her own refusal, though the dignity of a good life can be (DI 7, 15, 22). The human person is the only creature on earth God willed for itself (GS 24): not for the species, not for the total, not for us (so too Aquinas, *Summa contra Gentiles* III.112). So her life is not ours to spend, not even for many, and, as the Church holds, not hers to dispose of, even when it has grown hard: it is held in trust from the One who alone is Lord of life and remains its sovereign Master (CCC 2258, 2280), who willed her for her own sake (GS 24), gave her life to her keeping and not to her disposal (CCC 2280), and never spends her as a mere means against her own good. She may refuse treatment that is burdensome or out of proportion to what it can do, and be given relief from pain even at the risk of shortening her days; but the ordinary care owed to the sick may not be withdrawn, and food and water are part of it for as long as they nourish her without causing harm or intolerable suffering (CCC 2278–2279; *Samaritanus Bonus* V.3). What the trust forbids is that her death be the thing aimed at, for that turns against the good of her own life even when she asks it, against the just love of herself as well as against the love of God, as the Church says of taking one's own life (CCC 2281). That is why, on this account, the line has no exceptions for any of us: what protects her is not a weight that enough lives could outweigh; it is that no number of others has any right to take her life. No one may take what is not his to take, even to keep others from taking more. He who gives life its being, and can give it back, does her no wrong when He calls it home; we, who can do neither, could only take it. That right is never ours: no one can under any circumstance claim for himself the right directly to destroy an innocent human being (CCC 2258). (Defending the innocent against an unjust aggressor is another matter: CCC 2263–2265.) Nor may anyone claim that right in God's name, whatever voice he says he heard, for no private revelation can surpass or correct the Revelation of which Christ is the fulfillment (CCC 67). Scripture tells of wars in which Israel was told to destroy whole peoples, children too (1 Samuel 15:3), and some great theologians held that God, as Lord of life, could so command without injustice. The Church reads those pages in the light of Christ, as part of a revelation suited to the moral level of distant times, which contains "things which are incomplete and temporary" and recounts violence and massacre without always condemning them (*Dei Verbum* 15; *Verbum Domini* 42). This book reads them as the record of a people God was still teaching, not as His approval of killing children, and measures them by Christ, and by Abraham's question, asked so that the innocent would not die with the guilty: "Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?" (Genesis 18:25). A reader who holds the line only at five still faces the question why justice has that shape; a reader who holds it at every number has here a reason for it. All share one origin and one calling (GS 29); an equal call, which does not wait on what anyone can do, explains an equal worth, and measured against one end that is God Himself, the genius and the newborn are for the same thing. A worth measured by that end is not measured by strength or mind. Not every child is wanted by those who made her; every child was wanted by the One who did, before anyone saw her (Jeremiah 1:5).
  This Lawgiver is not a commander who picks rules. A will that invents values can command, but it cannot bind; even a promise binds only under a law no promiser invented. The wrong is wrong because of what it does to her, and so it offends Him who loves her. That there is a *her* at all, made for an end, is His gift; and what we are, as He made us, is good, our desires included. Their disorder, their pull against reason and against the good of others, is not His work but the wound of sin: the loss of a harmony that was His gift (CCC 405; ST I q.95 a.1). He did not choose whether cruelty is evil, for goodness is His nature. He is the author of the law (*Veritatis Splendor* 36, 40), not as a will that invents it, but because it is His own wisdom: He chose to make creatures who can be wronged, established what they are for in the very natures He created, and defined what love must answer to, because He is Himself the Good He asks of us. Our share in that law is reason's own light, not a foreign rule (*Veritatis Splendor* 41). The law binds because He is good; the offense against Him can be forgiven because He is Someone; and the one we wronged is kept, because He forgets no one. And the Judge lets Himself be asked (Genesis 18:25).
- **W2. The law and the mind that reads it point to one Source.** Begin with truth (A7). Evolution explains well why our senses and reasoning mostly get the world right: a creature that misreads cliffs and lions does not last. (The Church has no quarrel with that, so long as it is not taken to explain the mind itself: the spiritual soul is not produced by matter or by the parents but created immediately by God, CCC 366; *Humani Generis* 36. The argument here does not assume it.) It does not explain why we *ought* to believe what is true when a falsehood would serve us better; whoever denies that anything binds must say that no one ought to care whether a belief is true, including his own. Then ask about our nature. Both the Church and her best rivals agree that what is good for a creature depends on what it is, and that much of the natural law is fitted to our kind. So the question is not whether our nature could have been otherwise, but whether it is an accident or a wisdom. Darwin imagined rational creatures raised like hive bees who would think it a sacred duty to kill their brothers. If our nature is only an accident, it still aims at its own good, as every living kind does; but that good is only the good of the kind that happened to arise, and another kind could have found its good in what we call cruelty. Yet the core of the law is not fitted to our kind only: gratuitous cruelty, betrayal, treating a person as a thing would be wrong for any creature that can reason and suffer. If some of the law holds for any mind that can reason and suffer, in any world where such minds could be, there are eternal truths about what is good and what we owe, and minds can know them. The Church places such truths in God's own wisdom, where every possible nature is first known (ST I q.15; q.16 a.7; I-II q.91 a.1–2; *Veritatis Splendor* 43). The best rivals give two answers. One says the order is a fact that needs no mind, and that evolution and the truth meet without luck: the features that make a deed wrong (pain, trust, betrayal) are the very ones survival taught us to notice, and the same reasoning that serves us everywhere does the rest. The other says mind and law belong to one nature, so that a rational animal simply reads its own form. Both are right as far as they go. What neither explains is the reach. Survival can teach us that pain is bad for us; it does not explain how we can see that cruelty would be wrong for any such mind in any world. The realist answers that the reach comes with the concept, for whatever makes a deed wrong would make it wrong anywhere. That shows that some features make a deed wrong of necessity, not how minds built for survival came to know which; Darwin's bees are the reminder that such minds might have fixed on the wrong ones. One Source of law and mind would answer that. The debunker presses from the other side: logic paid its way in survival by being true, a belief that kin must be cared for paid its way either way, and the step from "wrong for us" to "wrong for any mind" is made by the same consistency that corrects conscience, carrying only our attitude. If he is right, the reach is no witness against him. Against him the case rests on A7, for he relies on the ought of evidence in the very argument he makes, and survival did not select for that either; and on A4, the belief survival least predicts (see the conclusion). This is a question of fit, and the rivals contest it; it weighs, it does not settle. Our conscience fails where self-interest pulls (the slave, the stranger, the infant who cannot repay), which is what the Church expects, because disordered self-love is the root of sin (ST I-II q.77 a.4; CCC 1850, 405); a world with no one behind it predicts that failure too, and the section on evil weighs it. Yet conscience also corrects itself, against interest and toward those who can never repay, and the engine of that correction is reason holding itself to consistency with goods it already sees: the law of truth again. (Peter Singer calls this the expanding circle, and he too credits reason.) Nor does one Source require that every people agree: Aquinas held that the first principles of the law are the same for all and known to all, while its further conclusions can be darkened by passion, bad custom or bad habit (ST I-II q.94 a.4, a.6), and the Church teaches that its precepts are not perceived by everyone clearly and immediately (CCC 1960). And this law holds together: its demands can be very hard (Chapter 11), but one can always refuse to do evil (*Veritatis Splendor* 52, 102); that much follows from the form of its prohibitions. What does not follow from form is that its goods fit: truth needs mercy to be told well, justice needs mercy to stay just, and mercy needs truth to be mercy (Chapters 5–9). Christians hold the rest on God's promise (1 Corinthians 10:13). The law of truth belongs to that unity too: if truth and life have one Source (John 14:6), the will to truth cannot finally lead away from life. Unity without reduction: a law and a mind fitted to each other, with truth binding both, are, this book judges, best explained by one Source of both. (Its first point, that truth binds, speaks most strongly to those who say conscience is only what survival built, and so binds no one; its question of reach, to those who say moral facts simply exist. Those who say our moral words voice refined feeling or prescription, and the pragmatists, need no meeting of law and mind; for them the question is the one in C. Those who say, with Kant, that the law is the very form of a reasoning will need none either, for in them law and mind are one; to them it puts the question why there are such wills at all. And those who find the one Source in the heart itself, as Wang Yangming and Mou Zongsan did, or in God or Nature, as Spinoza did, agree with this witness and part from this book in W1 and W3.) W1 and W3 give the reasons to think that Source is Someone and not something, and not less than personal: knowing and loving are in Him more truly than in us.
- **W3. Communion is a clue that points to One who is Love.** Only a person can love us back; a law, a force, an order or an idea cannot. We long to be known and loved wholly, and no one on earth can do it, because no one knows the whole of us, not even ourselves; even if we are made of our relationships, the longing remains, to be received whole, across every role and every year, by someone who will not die. Science can trace the paths this longing travels in us, as it traces the paths of sight: attachment, the wish not to die, a mind that pictures being seen. That tells us how we long, not whether there is Someone to long for, and the longing alone settles nothing. Adam Smith observed that when the world misjudges us and the judge within us is shaken, we appeal to the all-seeing Judge of the world (*Theory of Moral Sentiments* III.2.33); a longing to be judged truly could be explained that way. Smith's account does not as easily explain a longing to be told, before we have earned anything, that it is good that we exist; the science of attachment does, for a child seeks that word long before he can earn it, though that too tells us how we long, not whether anyone answers. Sartre went further: we are a lack that longs to be its own ground, which is to long to be God, and so the longing must fail. That is true of the longing that grasps, the wish to be like God without God (Genesis 3:5; CCC 398); it is not true of the longing to be given, to be told by another that it is good that we exist. Part of our longing grasps, and both the Buddhist and the Catholic say grasping love must die; the Buddhist keeps a wholesome desire for the path until it reaches its end, and the Catholic adds that desire itself, eros too, is not to be killed but healed (*Deus Caritas Est* 5). We part over what is left. The Buddhist says what is left is wisdom that no longer clings to a self, and with it boundless kindness, compassion, joy in another's good, and an even mind that holds every being alike. The Catholic says all of that is real, and that beneath it is love's first word to another, in Josef Pieper's phrase: "it is good that you exist." Love does not say that for a while only. Nietzsche heard that wish for always too, and answered it without a Person: say yes to your life so wholly that you would will it all again forever; but that yes is said by me, not to me, and it must be said to what was done to her as well. Some wise friends of nature answer that a human life must end to be human, and that to want more is to want to stop being human; Zhuangzi answers more gaily that it is good to be human now and good to be changed into whatever comes next, and that to demand to stay oneself is to be metal leaping up in the forge. But the Christian hope is not more of the same years, nor a refusal of change, for "we shall all be changed" (1 Corinthians 15:51). It is that the one we love will go on being herself, body and all, made whole (CCC 1025, 999). The risen Christ kept His wounds (John 20:27): what a finite love lived through is not erased but kept, and healed. A longing does not prove its answer, but it shows what answer would fit: a Person who said that word to each of us first, who knows us wholly, and who loved us first (1 John 4:19). That knowing is promised in full only at the end, when we shall understand fully, even as we have been fully understood (1 Corinthians 13:12); for now it is begun. Spinoza objected that whoever truly loves God cannot want God to love him back, for that would be to want God changed. But to love is to will another's good (ST I q.20 a.2), and God wills ours without gaining anything from us; to long for His love is to want to be wanted by One who lacks nothing. If the highest good knows and loves no one, or knows each of us only as a truth it holds and wants none of us, then love, the best thing in us, is answered in the end by no one. That is not evidence, for we may not believe a thing because it comforts (A7); but it is where that account stops.
  This witness raises the hardest question: if such a Person loves us, why do many honest seekers not find Him? Some of the most disciplined seekers in history, the Buddhist contemplatives, looked inward for a lifetime and report a peace beyond the self rather than a Person; and the Neo-Confucians, who for centuries sat in stillness and searched things and their own hearts, found a principle, some of them in the heart itself, rather than a Person. The Church rejects nothing that is true and holy in their way, even as she proclaims Christ, in whom all may find the fullness of religious life (*Nostra Aetate* 2). Her own mystics speak of a darkness on the way to God too, though theirs is the night of faith before Someone. Each tradition can read the other's summit as a stage on its own road; neither reading is evidence, and this book does not count it as such. The Church does not presume that such seekers are hiding from Him (CCC 847), though she knows that any of us can (CCC 29). She teaches that God can be known with certainty by the light of reason (CCC 36), yet that knowing Him by reason alone is hard for us as we are (CCC 37). He makes Himself known through the world He made and the conscience He gave (CCC 32–33), fully in Christ (*Dei Verbum* 4), and, for most of us first, through persons: a mother's love before a creed, a neighbor's mercy before a name (Matthew 25:37–40). He has entrusted us with making Him known to one another (CCC 848), as He gives His creatures the dignity of being causes for one another in everything else (CCC 306). And He does not withhold what is needed for salvation from anyone who, through no fault of his own, has not yet come to an explicit knowledge of Him and, by grace, tries to live a good life (*Lumen Gentium* 16). She hopes that such a person is already following a light without yet knowing its name, and that when he turns from a wrong out of love for the good, he is already turning toward the One who forgives. A child is loved long before he can say who loves him. That is part of an answer to why many do not find Him, though not a full answer to why Love would choose this way; and this book's own definition makes the question harder, for a love that binds into communion wants to be known back, not only to be at work unseen. It has a real cost: for many honest seekers the conscious knowing comes late, and for many it does not come in this life at all. Yet this life is where the choice is made (CCC 1021), and it can be made before the name is known: the Spirit, in a manner known only to God, offers every person the possibility of sharing in Christ's Paschal Mystery (GS 22), and God can lead him, in ways known to Himself, to the faith without which it is impossible to please Him (CCC 848). For the infant no one holds, there is no mother's love to carry His love to her; yet she is held by Him, and if she dies unbaptized, the Church, which holds that God is not bound by His sacraments, entrusts her to His mercy and hopes that there is a way of salvation for her (CCC 1257, 1261). The Church does not hide that cost; she calls it a summons to mission. Revelation gives the Lover's name: God is Love (1 John 4:8).

**C. An honest comparison.** Every account stops somewhere, God's included. Each rival below *accommodates* much of A, often in its own words; the question is what each *explains* and what it must give up. Each is stated as its best defenders state it. No rival's origins are used against it that we would not let it use against ours.
- **Nothing really binds** (error theory, often joined to an evolutionary account of conscience and of religion; others use the same evolutionary argument to show that our values bind us only from within, a view weighed under the next heading) is consistent and borrows nothing. With one cause it explains why we feel bound, guilty and watched. Its price: torturing a child for fun is not really wrong (A1); the newborn has no worth except what we are built and taught to give her, and we are least built to give it to the infant no one is drawn to hold (A4); and "follow the evidence" binds only as the aim built into believing, or as advice for those who happen to want truth, so it cannot say why anyone ought to keep looking when he fears what he will find, or ought to care whether its own case is true (A7).
- **Feeling, refined** (Hume, Adam Smith, and today's quasi-realists: moral words voice our attitudes, made informed, consistent and sympathetic) can say nearly everything in A in its own voice, even "her worth does not depend on us" and "my guilt is owed to her." Where it parts from us is in *why* that is true. On its account, in the end, "worth" is the name our refined love gives to what it answers to; and it holds that once we have said what cruelty does to the child, no further why is needed. (Some near neighbors, who refuse the word "projection," say worth and feeling arise together, like color and sight: then worth is real, but it is worth to a human heart.) Two refined hearts can still part over whether the newborn who will never think is worth as much as you. Its defenders will say, in their own voice, that the heart which holds she is sees truly, and would see truly if no heart held it; and each of us judges from where he stands. So the difference is not who must judge, but what the truth of the judgment comes to: on its account, nothing beyond the judgment and the hearts that reach it; on ours, a truth held first in the Mind that made her. Its founders were honest about their costs. Hume granted that creatures too weak to make us feel their resentment would be owed gentleness under the laws of humanity, but not justice; that cost belongs more to his theory of justice than to sentiment, for Smith rested justice on the impartial spectator's resentment on behalf of the injured, which reaches the infant who cannot resent. Hume told the "sensible knave" who cheats when it pays that he is a dupe, trading peace of mind for gain; but he admitted that if the knave's heart does not rebel, no answer will satisfy him. No argument compels such a man, ours included; the difference is what he is missing. On Hume's own answer he has lost a feeling and the peace it brings, and if he never misses it, he will never know what he lost. Today's quasi-realists will go further and say that he is turning from a good that is there whether he knows it or not; we part at the last step: he is turning also from Someone. Smith pictured conscience as an impartial judge above everyone we know, and wrote that when that judge within is shaken, we appeal to a higher tribunal, the all-seeing Judge of the world (*Theory of Moral Sentiments* III.2.33). On the account that keeps only feeling, and no one beyond nature, both judges are no one. Remorse can be lived through; only forgiveness can heal the bond, and at the grave, on this account, no one is left to give it.
- **Our best words, widening** (pragmatism, above all Richard Rorty; and the relativism of Gilbert Harman and David Wong, for whom moral truth is relative to a morality) does not deny A; it declines to ask what makes A true. It says torturing a child is wrong, from within the words we have come to. Moral progress, it holds, is the widening of "we," won more by stories than by proofs, and the Church grants much of that history (W3). Its price is the infant on the hillside. If worth is what our widest "we" extends, then before anyone extended it the slave was not yet wronged in a way any truth could record, and the infant no one holds is safe exactly as far as our stories reach her. Why "we" should go on widening rather than shrink, it cannot say.
- **Reason's own law** (Kant) explains universality, and its "never merely as a means" stands with A4 and A6: humanity has a worth beyond price, which admits no equivalent and so cannot be traded even for many. Kant held that no one, not even God, is the author of the moral law in the sense of choosing it; each rational will must regard itself as giving the law to itself, because the law is the very form of a free will (*Groundwork* 4:431). It holds for every possible reasoner: no reasoner set it by choice, and each finds it his own. Then the question of W2 returns in Kant's form: not where such a law is kept, but why there are beings at all whose own law it is, and how they arose in a world of nature. Kant answered that how freedom is possible we cannot understand. He counted the newborn a person from birth (*Metaphysics of Morals* 6:280); but his best readers, who ground her dignity in the rational nature she has by kind, must say why that kind carries such worth before reason is ever used, and Kant, who denied that we can know what the soul is, can hold it only as a demand of practical reason. Reason can condemn us; it is persons who forgive. Kant made forgiving a duty between persons, and held that the dead can still be wronged (*Metaphysics of Morals* 6:460–461, 6:295–296); but when the one we wronged is gone, his account has no one left who can forgive. He also held that a people cannot be its own lawgiver in matters of the heart, which no human court can see, so that an ethical community can be thought of only as a people under a moral Ruler who knows the heart (*Religion* 6:98–99). He did not make that Ruler the author of the law. This book does, though not as a will that invents it: the law is God's own wisdom, which He did not choose, because He is the Good, and whose light He gave to man at creation (Chapter 3; *Veritatis Splendor* 36, 40). Where guilt and goodness are inward, the philosopher of autonomy found that he had to think of Someone, though he held that thought as rational faith, not as evidence; this book counts it as a clue. And his rule for clemency, that a ruler may remit a crime against himself but never what one subject did to another, God keeps in both halves: the sin, first an offense against Himself, He forgives whole (CCC 1440–1441); but not as impunity, for the one forgiven must still repair what he can and make satisfaction for his sins (CCC 1459), and what was done to her He does not pardon in her place.
- **What we owe each other** (contractualism: a wrong is what principles no one could reasonably reject would forbid, and so what could not be justified to the one it is done to) explains why a wrong is done to someone, why we answer to the one we wronged, and why no total can justify spending one person for others, since each must be answered one by one. It reaches the abandoned child as surely as the wanted one, for it asks what could be justified to *her*, not whether anyone chooses to speak for her. But it stops at two things. Why being born one of us puts her among those to whom we must answer, its best defenders explain by a relation: she is born of us and of our kind, and others may speak for her as trustees; why that relation, and not her powers, gives her full and equal standing, it takes as given. And why answering to each one is what we owe each other, it explains by the worth of standing with each person in mutual recognition, and that worth too it takes as given. Its best defenders hold these as truths that simply hold, and there it meets the realist's question (W2). When the one we wronged is gone, it can say she is still owed, and, with Stephen Darwall, that no trustee can forgive in her place; it cannot say that she can ever forgive.
- **Care between persons** (the ethics of care: Nel Noddings, Virginia Held, Joan Tronto, Eva Feder Kittay) begins where this book's hardest cases begin, with the newborn and the one who holds her. It sees what accounts built for equals who bargain miss: that each of us began, and many will end, wholly in others' hands; that a wrong is a betrayal of trust (A2); and that carers need care too (CCC 2208–2209). With Kittay it says why worth does not follow ability: each of us is some mother's child (A4). But it stops at the infant no one holds. If she is owed only within a relation of care, hers was refused; if she is owed care by all, that is a worth already there, which care answers and does not give. This book's answer has care's shape: the One who made her wanted her before anyone saw her (W1(2)).
- **Well-being, counted impartially** (utilitarianism) counts every pain equally, refuses to let the stranger count for less, and is right that consequences matter; the Church weighs them too (CCC 1754). It has often seen first whom others passed by, as Mill did the slave and women, and Singer the animals and the distant poor. Its best defenders can say why we should hold firm rules against killing the innocent (rule consequentialism), and even live by them day to day as if they had no exceptions (Hare's two levels). But it reduces wronging to worsening. Counting experiences, it finds nothing wrong in the secret told to a stranger who will never repeat it, for no one feels it (A2); counting wishes, it can blame the teller, but only because he could have done better overall, so that, had the telling brought a little more good to someone else, no one, she included, would have been wronged at all. It makes every person, in principle, someone who may be deliberately killed for better totals (A4, A6). Where it counts lives as goods, it can say that it is good that a happy life exists, not that it is good that *you* do, for one such life may in principle replace another (W3). And it makes truthfulness a servant of the total: where a falsehood, believed or left in place, would do more good, it must in principle favor the falsehood (A7). Some of its masters have allowed that its own truth might be better kept from the public (Sidgwick; de Lazari-Radek and Singer defend that secrecy still). Sidgwick himself gave up his fellowship rather than profess what he could not believe, and held that a teacher's honesty was worth that price; the question is whether, on his theory, it always is.
- **The flourishing of our kind** (secular Aristotelian) sees the most: what we need, why virtue is good for us, why justice is owed to another and a wrong must be repaid, why one may not be killed for others, and, in the hands of its best modern defenders, why the weak still belong (though not in Aristotle's own, who would have had deformed infants left unreared). Its modern heirs have much to say about forgiveness between persons, some of it wary of forgiveness that waits on apology. But it must say whether our nature, and the good it carries, is an accident or an order it cannot explain. Its best defenders give two answers: that a living thing's good is set by its own form, which acts for an end without deliberating, so that no mind is needed behind it; and that the question asks for a place to stand outside the life of reason, and there is none. Each is a stopping point. And the end it measures us by is a life of reason well lived, which the newborn who will never think can never reach: it can say she belongs and is owed our care, not that she is for the same thing as you (W1(2)). When the one we wronged is gone, it has no one left to receive what we owe. And some of its best defenders hold that when a person's life has stopped being a good to him, he may ask to have it ended and be helped, since justice guards a life for the sake of the one who lives it; the Church answers that his life does not stop being a good to him, and that he holds it in trust rather than owning it (CCC 2258, 2280; this book's claim, A6).
- **Necessary truths that belong to no one** (godless realism) stand with us against relativism and matter alone. Its best defenders gather those truths under a few deep principles, and say the truths need nothing behind them: their necessity lives in the natures of pain, persons and promises themselves, or, as Parfit held, needs no dwelling at all, as the truths of logic need none. Every account has a first principle, ours included. Where we part is where persons are: the debt to the dead, which on this account can be honored but never released (W1); why worth follows kind and not ability, which it must take as given, or, as some of its ablest defenders do, deny (W1); and whether the reach of conscience to truths that hold for every mind can be explained without a Mind, that is, how minds built for survival came to track truths that owe nothing to survival (W2). It says the law holds of itself. We say it holds first in a Mind, the same Mind that made the natures it is about and the minds that read it.
- **An impersonal sacred order** (Heaven's principle as the Neo-Confucians, above all Zhu Xi, understood it; the Stoic Logos) is among the strongest rivals a book about love can face. It explains a great deal (A1, A3, A5, A6, W2). It holds that every person, however clouded in body or mind, carries the whole of Heaven's principle, as the one moon shines whole in ten thousand rivers, so that worth follows nature, not capacity (A4). Its sages say the heart of Heaven and Earth is to give life, and Zhu Xi called that heart humaneness. But its principle, he said, has no intention, and Heaven both has a mind and has none: it keeps each kind true to itself and lets each one go. It can say that each one holds the whole nature, not that *this* one is wanted for her own sake; when one river is drained, the moon never knew which river it was. Heaven and Earth give life to things; God wanted *you*. Its teachers know well that a wrong is done to a person, a father, a friend, a stranger (A2); but the order itself cannot forgive, and it cannot know or love anyone (W3). Confucius, in danger, in grief, and when no one understood him, spoke of Heaven as the one who gave him his virtue and would not let the culture he carried perish, who bereaved him when his best student died, and who alone knew him (*Analects* 7.23, 9.5, 11.9, 14.35). Minds under threat reach for a knowing Heaven, ours as well; this shows where the account strains for its own teachers, not that it fails. The modern Confucian Mou Zongsan would refuse the word "impersonal": for him the ground is a living moral heart, the same in all of us, that is itself the infinite. Then the law is mine at my deepest, and others love me back; but at the ground of things there is no one besides me who knows me as myself, and no one at all who can release what I did to another or keep the one I lost. Such an order is free of the problem of evil only if it intends no one in particular. Zhu Xi's answer, that principle is wholly good and the unevenness lies in a material force it does not steer, is close to the Church's in one respect: the source is wholly good, and the world's unevenness belongs to a creation still on its way to its perfection (CCC 310). It parts from hers in another, for she holds that God's providence governs all things, from the least to the great events of the world (CCC 303); and a principle that does not steer can promise the seeker his own completion, not that the one he lost will be kept. The more such an order cares, the more it faces our question, as the historian Sima Qian did when he set the good men who starved beside the robber who lived long, against the old saying that the Way of Heaven has no favorites and is always with the good (*Daodejing* 79), and asked whether the Way of Heaven is just. (Xunzi's Heaven, the bare constant course of nature, is free of that question because it cares for no one; but for him the moral Way was made by the sages, and he belongs nearer the flourishing of our kind. The Stoic Logos sits nearer a Person: it was prayed to as Providence, and the Stoics had to answer for evil; but their Providence cares for the whole and spends the person, as a part, for it, and does not keep the dead for good.)
- **The Way that gives and does not claim** (Laozi and Zhuangzi) holds that Heaven and Earth are not humane: the Way gives life to each thing and does not possess it, and benevolence and righteousness appear when the Way is lost. It sees much of A: the cruelty of rulers, the newborn as the image of whole virtue, a sage who abandons no one (A1, A4), and that to kill in the Executioner's place is to cut in the master carpenter's place (A6). But it heals guilt by forgetting, not by pardon (A2, A3), and lets the dead be transformed (W3). Where we say God wanted *you*, it says the highest giving does not want. Nor does God possess: He willed her for her own sake and gave her life to her keeping (GS 24; CCC 2280). We part over whether the Giver knows her, and whether she is kept or let go.
- **God, or Nature** (Spinoza, and those who, like Einstein, believe in Spinoza's God) keeps God's name and denies Him a will: all things follow from one infinite, necessary being as a triangle's properties follow from the triangle. It sees much: law and mind are one order of one God (W2); the free man will not break faith even to save his life (A7); the world's pain needs no excuse, for no one intended it. But it parts from us where persons are. Nothing is a wrong except under a people's laws (A1); repentance is cured by understanding that the deed had to happen, not by her pardon (A2); what is eternal in us remembers nothing (W1); worth is power, so the newborn who will never think has less (A4); and its God loves men only in loving Himself, and wants no one (W3).
- **The Buddha's Dhamma** (karma, and beyond it the unconditioned; for the Madhyamaka, emptiness) is the most searching account of the moral life without a Creator. It explains why intention matters most, why real good costs, why the line against killing a human holds at every number and even at his asking (A6, W1(2)), and why we must follow the truth (A7). It holds with A2 that we must ask pardon of the one we wronged, though it counts remorse that lingers past amends as a hindrance to be let go: the Buddha sent a monk who had insulted a layman to ask his pardon (Vinaya, Cullavagga I). And it knows a receiver of confession who does not take the victim's place: the Buddha accepted King Ajātasattu's confession that he had killed his father, and the deed's fruit still stood (Dīgha Nikāya 2). Its ground for compassion is among the most beautiful anyone has offered: as a mother would guard her only child with her life, so one should hold every being in a boundless heart (Sn 1.8). It also bears the weight of suffering more easily than we do, because it never said that being born is good. Birth itself, the Buddha taught, is suffering, as is all that is conditioned, and a human birth is prized as rare because it is the chance to be free of birth; craving keeps the round turning; and it can end. Karma explains some of how suffering falls, though the Buddha would not say that all of it comes from past deeds (SN 36.21). No one judges, the Buddhist says; the fruit simply ripens. But so far as bodies and fortunes follow the moral quality of each heart, across lives, the world has justice built into it, and justice built into a world looks like the work of a mind (by the same measure, a world in which pain does not follow justice weighs against a just Mind; so far as one life lets us see, ours is such a world, and this book weighs that in the section on evil below). The Buddhist answers that it is the work of minds, the doers' own, since the deed is the intention, and that no one Mind set the order. When the one we wronged is gone, the Buddhist says she is born again, neither the same as she was nor another, and she may one day meet the one who wronged her and let the old hatred go (W1). But no one keeps her for that meeting, and the deed ripens in the doer whether or not it comes; a ripening is owed to no one and cannot forgive. No one, the Buddha taught, can purify another (Dhammapada 165); the Catholic says that One can, and does (1 John 1:7). Its highest good is an insight each must reach, which some will never reach in this life (AN 8.29); where Buddhists look for higher help, as in the Pure Land, it comes from a buddha who, in the sutra's telling, was once bound as we are (the Council itself speaks of Buddhists who seek to attain, "by their own efforts or through higher help, supreme illumination," *Nostra Aetate* 2). The Catholic's highest good is also a seeing, but a seeing given, which God can give to the infant and to the one who will never think (CCC 1250, 1261). Some of its teachers say every being has the nature of a buddha, so that worth follows nature, not capacity; but a nature held whole by all, like the moon in ten thousand rivers, is not a call to this one. And where its buddhas know and love, they love beings already there; they gave no one being. For the Buddhist, compassion is the seed and the fruit of the path; for the Catholic, love is also the root of the world. Here the two part honestly: both say grasping must die, and they part over what is left, and over whether it is good that we exist at all.
- **History and class** (Marx and Engels, and their heirs) holds that moral codes answer to how a society produces its life and often serve those who rule, and that the gravest wrongs are done by systems as well as by persons. Much of this the Church grants (A2, W2). But where it says what anyone is owed, it divides. If justice is only what fits each age, the slave of antiquity was stunted but not wronged (A1, A2); if, with its analytic heirs, the deepest principles of justice hold in every age, it meets the realist's question (W2). Need reaches the newborn but does not make her worth as much as you (A4). Where it spent the innocent for history's goal, it gave up A6, and its humane heirs restored the line. Its hope is for those who come after (see the section on evil).
- **Power and rank** (Nietzsche) is the most honest rival, and it explains a great deal: why guilt grew so large, how moral feeling has changed across history, and why equal worth spread where Christianity spread (the Church agrees that equal worth was slow to be seen, and came to be seen more fully in the light of the Gospel: *Dignitas Infinita* 10–13). It stays consistent by giving up A1 through A4, and the second half of A6, openly, and asks the hardest question of all: why should truth matter more than life? Nietzsche kept his own honesty, which he called the virtue his kind could not get away from, but he denied that it binds every mind. So the case puts a choice. Either truthfulness binds every reasoning mind, and then something binds every mind by what a mind is, which is the question of W2 (A7); or it binds only those who hold it, and then his unmasking can turn our own honesty against us, as he meant it to, but it cannot show that anyone ought to keep that honesty, his own included. He held that Christian truthfulness had already turned on Christian belief in God and must next turn on Christian morality; whether honest inquiry ends there or at the Lawgiver is the question of the whole case, which this dilemma does not settle. His own account gives the will to truth the same Christian ancestry he gives equal worth, so neither ancestry settles anything. Nor does resentment explain regard for every human being: it grew too where he found no resentment, for he praised the Buddha as free of it; and it reaches the infant and the enemy, who can repay no one (W2). Nor need he praise cruelty: he can despise the man who tortures a child as base. What he cannot say is that the child was wronged. To his hardest question this book answers that truth and life have one Source, so that honesty may cost a life but can never finally be the enemy of life; on his account it might be, which is why he said we need art to bear the truth. He also denies the free will with which Chapter 1 answers the evil we do; Chapter 1, not this case, must meet him there.
- **Freedom that makes its values** (Sartre and Simone de Beauvoir, with Camus near them) shares more of this case than it seems: Sartre and Beauvoir condemned the torture of prisoners in Algeria whatever their country approved (A1). It even grants the hinge: only a Creator, Sartre held, could give us a nature we are *for*, so without God values are made, not found (A5). Its best answer to A4 is that freedom has no degrees. Its price: one who never will be, or never again will be, conscious has only the worth our freedom gives her (A4); its masters let the line bend for liberation (A6); and what binds one freedom to will another's is a law no freedom chose (W2). Camus, who held that revolt finds a nature common to all, stood nearer to us. The rebel can take her side; he cannot keep her.
- **A God who is Love** is also a stopping point, but not, the Church holds, a brute one: He is the One whose very being is to be (Exodus 3:14; ST I q.3 a.4), who needs nothing outside Himself to explain Him. The realist says the same of his necessary truths, and the difference is not fewer first principles but one source for the law and the minds that read it, which he must take separately (W2), and for the beings the law is about, which we say were made for what it asks. Against the flourishing view, the sacred order, the nameless Way and the God of Spinoza, which also stop at one, it is the one that wills each of us for her own sake, not from any need, for He lacks nothing, but to share His goodness (ST I q.44 a.4; CCC 293, 295), and is Someone besides ourselves; and the Church too says that His name is "at once a name revealed and something like the refusal of a name" (CCC 206). Against the ethics of care, which also begins with this one, it is the one relation no one can refuse her. Against the Buddhist, for whom love is not the ground of things, it is the account in which love is the first word and the last (1 John 4:8; 1 Corinthians 13:8). Its claim is reach, not count: it fits A1–A7 together, and gives a ground for what the best rivals hold but must take as given: why worth follows kind and not ability, and is equal, measured against one end (W1(2)); and why one person may not be spent for others' totals, a rule many of the best rivals share (Mencius held it at every number) and rest on a standing or a humaneness they take as given, or, with Kant, on rational nature held by kind. Why that line has no exceptions and is not even her own to cross, the Church teaches and this book argues, though not every reader grants it; Kant and the Theravāda hold that line too, Kant from a worth beyond price, the Theravāda from the killer's heart and her chance to be freed, but neither from a worth she was given. Two things it answers but does not explain better than its rivals, since science can say why we would feel them whether or not anyone hears: guilt's request to be forgiven by the one who is gone, and a longing no one on earth can answer. These are clues of fit, not evidence (W1, W3). It does not remove all mystery; it is one mystery, and a mystery that explains. And it is Someone, not less than personal, which is what forgiveness itself and communion suggest.

**On the other side of the scale is real weight, and it is heavy.** Children suffer horribly. Animals suffered for hundreds of millions of years before any human chose anything, and pain in nature falls roughly where survival would put it, not where justice would. So, roughly, does conscience: it is warmest toward kin, friends and those who can repay, and it reached the slave and the stranger late. The Church explains that by sin (W2), but a world with no one behind it predicts it without help. In the world God made, one creature's good often comes through another's loss: the wolf lives by the fawn, and the parasite lives by the child; if our nature is a wisdom and not an accident (W2), so is theirs, and that too is on this side of the scale. Many who do not resist Him never come to know Him by name in this life, whether they sought Him or not, and whether one does depends far more on where and to whom one was born than on how honestly one sought. And the Catholic faith adds a weight of its own. It teaches that hell is real and eternal (CCC 1035): a person can refuse God forever by his own free choice (CCC 1033). God predestines no one to that refusal (CCC 1037) and desires all to be saved (1 Timothy 2:4), and He holds no one to account beyond what he knew, or could and should have known, and freely chose: fear, wounds and what others did to him can lessen or even remove his responsibility (CCC 1735, 1859–1860). The Church sees in this the seriousness of freedom: God does not force His love on anyone able to refuse it (Chapter 4), and a refusal made with full knowledge and consent and held to the end becomes final, as the angels' choice became final, through no defect in His mercy (CCC 393, 1033, 1857). But freedom does not finish the answer. God kept Mary from every sin by grace without forcing her yes (CCC 491–493), so He can bring a free creature to say yes without overriding her; why one will is won by His grace and another refuses it to the end, the Church does not explain, and she lets her theologians differ over it. That death fixes the will is her teaching too (CCC 393, 1021), not something this case shows. So hell stays on this side of the scale, and for a book that says God's first word to each of us is that it is good that we exist, it is the heaviest thing there. And some rivals bear this whole weight more easily than we do. A world with no one behind it would put pain where survival puts it, which is where we find it. The Buddhist never said that being born is good; he prizes a human birth as rare, but as the chance to be free of birth, so a world full of suffering is what he expects. Nietzsche did not merely expect great suffering but prized it as the discipline behind every human greatness; what he pays for that is the yes answered below. A Way that is not humane gives life and lets each thing go, and owes no one an answer. We say that it is good that she exists, and that Someone willed her for her own sake, and so we must answer for the child, as anyone who loved her and could have spared her would have to. Free will (Chapter 1) answers much of the evil we do to one another: it is ours, not His. Why He did not keep the first man from sinning, as by grace He kept Mary free of every sin her whole life long, the Church answers, with St. Leo the Great, only with what He brought from it, and gave to the very ones who fell: Christ's grace gave us more than the devil's envy took away (CCC 412). Free will also bears on why God did not simply begin with heaven, where the saints are free and cannot sin. He can give the highest good as pure gift (CCC 1250), and the Church teaches that He gives it to the baptized child who dies before she can choose (Benedict XII, *Benedictus Deus*; CCC 1023). But He also willed creatures who can choose to give their love back of their own accord, in a life where it could be refused, before it is confirmed forever (Chapter 1; Chapter 4). That is a good, not a necessity; whether it is worth this world's cost is part of the weighing, not settled by it. It does not answer the fawn in the forest fire or the child with cancer. For those the Church gives a setting, not an explanation: God made a world still on its way to its perfection, and gave His creatures the dignity of acting on their own and being causes for each other, so they can fail and harm (CCC 306, 310). He wills the order of the whole, never the loss for its own sake (ST I q.19 a.9), and within that order He governs each person for her own sake (*Summa contra Gentiles* III.112). Beyond that she claims no explanation; only Christian faith as a whole answers it (CCC 309). Faith gives the certainty that God would not permit an evil unless He brought a good from that very evil (CCC 324), though the evil never thereby becomes good (CCC 312); and we will know His ways fully only at the end (CCC 314).
Three things bear on that weight, though none makes it light, and only the second lessens it as evidence. *First*, evil has nothing of its own: it is always a good thing harmed (Chapter 1). That does not make pain less real; it says that pain is the felt loss of some good. There can be a Supreme Good but not a supreme Evil (ST I q.49 a.3): every choice of evil reaches for a lesser good the chooser lacks, and a will that lacks nothing has nothing to grasp at. So the world's darkness is not evidence of a dark god. That rests on what evil is; a reader who holds that malice could be as full and real as love must weigh a dark god against the whole case. Nor does it by itself rule out a source that is simply indifferent; or a God, as Spinoza held, who wills nothing and from whose fullness everything follows, the ruin with the rest; or a god beyond good and evil who makes and breaks out of sheer fullness. Those are answered only by the whole case, and none of them fits as well a debt that outlives every creditor on earth, a worth that is each one's own, wanted for her own sake and not to be spent for others (an impersonal source can make all things equal, as Zhuangzi's Way does, but it wants none of them), or a longing to hear from Someone that it is good that *you* exist. A yes said to whatever happens, or a calm that understands it had to happen, is said to no one; it must say yes to what was done to her as well as to her, and so it cannot take her side. (Zhuangzi's yes is narrower: it is said to the changes of life and death, not to the tyrant's cruelty, which his Way condemns; but when she dies, it lets her go.) The rebel who refuses such a world, as Camus's doctor refused it at a child's deathbed, takes her side; he cannot keep her. God says no to what was done to her, and yes to her (Wisdom 1:13; CCC 413). *Second*, God's permission is not a trade, and two answers must be kept apart here. *Why* He permits is, so far as reason can see, the setting above: a world of real causes, and we are among them; faith adds that He permits no evil from which He will not draw a good, though the evil never earns that good and never becomes good itself (CCC 311, 312, 324, 412). God is at work in every cause (CCC 308), but the evil in what a free creature does is his own: God gives the act its being, never its defect, and is in no way, directly or indirectly, the cause of moral evil; He permits it (CCC 311; ST I-II q.79 a.2; Chapter 11). The harms that come in a world of real causes He does not will for their own sake, but only as they come with the order He wills; in willing that order, Aquinas says, He wills some things to be naturally corrupted (ST I q.19 a.9; q.49 a.2). Among animals that order does give one creature's loss to another's life: the lion lives by the slaying of other animals (ST I q.22 a.2 ad 2). A person it never gives away (*Summa contra Gentiles* III.112): her body is as corruptible as any, but she is not made for another creature's life, and she is governed for her own sake. The parasite takes from the child; God does not give her to it. What we see is the same as with the fawn; the difference is in what He wills. The Church adds that in the first harmony man was not to suffer or die, and that when it was lost, visible creation became alien and hostile to him (CCC 376, 400, 1008); the hold of a creature that can live only on us belongs, so far as the child is concerned, to that loss, not to what she was made for. That is a teaching, not something we see, and the parasite stays on this side of the scale. The hands that pull a child from the fire are part of what He made real, so His permission never excuses ours (CCC 2269). *What* He brings from the evil is a further thing: for a person who suffers, the good the Church hopes for is a good offered to her; God never spends anyone as a mere means against her own good. The best rivals rightly ask of Him what they ask of us, whether it could be justified to her; an order of the whole cannot answer that by itself, and only what is offered to her can, which is a hope, not a proof. That is not to deny that suffering can bear fruit for others. Christ's did: He laid down His life of His own accord (John 10:18), as a ransom for many (Mark 10:45), and the Father raised Him; and whoever joins her suffering to His shares in that fruitfulness (Colossians 1:24; *Salvifici Doloris* 19, 24). But what Christ gave was given, not taken, and it was not lost. We know enough of goodness to know what a good God would value, which is why this weight is real; what we would not expect to see, if the good is finished only in eternal life and in a creation set free (Romans 8:21; CCC 314, 324, 1046–1047), is how each particular evil is answered. So for one weighing by reason alone, not seeing God's reasons is weak evidence that He has none. It is not no evidence: the pattern of pain in nature still weighs. The Church does not say whether the fawn will live again; she says creation itself waits to be set free (Romans 8:19–22). For the fawn that hope is thinner than it is for the child, and this book does not pretend otherwise. *Third*, the God weighed here is not a designer watching from a distance. He entered suffering Himself: the eternal Son became man and suffered in His own flesh on the Cross. God, St. Bernard said, cannot suffer, but He can suffer with (*Spe Salvi* 39). Christian faith professes that He will raise the dead (CCC 998), and hopes in Him to wipe away every tear (Revelation 21:4; *Salvifici Doloris* 15, 25). This is not a further proof; it is what the God being weighed actually is. The question is not whether a distant designer fits this world, but whether a God who suffers with it and will not leave it in the ground does. That does not explain why. It means that, if He is, the last word over the suffering child is not the suffering. Not every account without a Creator is without hope: the Buddhist hopes that every being, this child too, will be freed from suffering; the Confucian hopes for a world in which the widow, the orphan and the disabled are all cared for; the Marxist works for a world in which no one is spent for another's gain; and the pragmatist hopes, with William James, that the world can be made better by our effort, though nothing promises that it will. The last two are hopes for those who come after; to the children already spent they can offer memory, not repair. Some of the Marxists' own heirs saw it: true justice, Adorno held, would require that even what is irrevocably past be undone, which, Benedict XVI observes, would mean a resurrection of the dead, and the question of justice, he adds, is the strongest argument for eternal life (*Spe Salvi* 42–43). The Christian hope is more particular: that this child, herself, body and all, will be raised and made whole, and will know the One who never forgot her. That is not evidence, but it is where the accounts part.

**Conclusion.** Weighing both sides, this book judges that the best explanation of love as we actually find it is a Lawgiver who is Love. Evil weighs against that conclusion, heavily; in this book's judgment it does not overturn it. An honest reader may weigh it differently, and may turn the question on us: which is he surer of, that a child left to die was wronged, or that a good God could have reasons to let her die? The section on evil says how far this book can answer that. He should still weigh it; the Church does not presume that he is at fault, and leaves the judgment of every heart to God (CCC 847, 1861). The Church teaches that arguments of this kind can bring a person to certainty, and that God can be known with certainty from the things He has made by the natural light of reason (CCC 31, 36; First Vatican Council, *Dei Filius* ch. 2). This book offers the case for weighing, not because certainty is out of reach, but because each reader must reach it with his own reason, and many of us find the way hard (CCC 37). Certainty of this kind comes, as St. John Henry Newman saw, from many signs converging in one mind, not from any one of them compelling every mind. It can predispose to faith (CCC 35) but does not replace it, and it cannot compel the one who gives up A4. To him it says only: look at the newborn whom instinct passed by, the one the ancient world left on the hillside, and ask which you are surer of: that she, weak and not yet able to know herself, was wronged when she was left there, before anyone said so, and wronged no less than a strong newborn left beside her, or a grown man, however gifted, put out there helpless to die, however differently one counts what each of them lost; or any argument that says she was not, or that it was a lesser wrong, or that you cannot know it. If she was wronged no less than either, her worth never followed her strength, or her mind, or how much she can feel or want, nor how much anyone cared for her, for no one did; and then, this book answers, it follows what she is, and she is that as fully as you are. What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it.
