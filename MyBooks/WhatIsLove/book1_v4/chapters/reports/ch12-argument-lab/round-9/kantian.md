# Blind review: the Kantian school

## 1. School and voice

I speak for Kant read through his strongest modern interpreters: Korsgaard (constructivism, the normative question, the regress argument for the value of humanity), Wood (the Formula of Humanity as basic, and the "personification principle" for beings with undeveloped or impaired rational nature; verify the label), Herman (rules of moral salience, the practice of judgment), O'Neill (principles that a plurality of agents can share; obligation before rights) and Hill (dignity and respect, and Kantian latitude on hard cases). The secular heirs have dropped Kant's postulates of God and immortality, and on that point I argue as they do. Where Kant himself differs from them, especially on rational faith, I say so. I grant everything in Kant that helps the text. My main tools against it are Kant's Critical limits on theoretical knowledge of the supersensible (the *Critique of Pure Reason*, Transcendental Dialectic), the autonomy of the moral law (G 4:431–436, 4:443), and the claim that dignity, as a worth beyond price, needs no further ground outside rational nature.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**(1) Starting point for justice.** The Universal Principle of Right: an action is right if it can coexist with everyone's freedom under a universal law (MM 6:230). There is one innate right, freedom independent of another's choice, and innate equality is contained in it (MM 6:237–238). O'Neill restates this as refusing principles that cannot be shared, above all deception and coercion. What anyone is owed follows from being a person under that law, not from deeds or need. **Holds.** This borrows nothing theistic, and it is not the book's covenantal "justice." It is thinner than the book's justice: it secures non-domination, not mercy.

**(2) A hard dilemma.** A murderer at the door asks where my friend is hiding. Kant's own answer, that I may not lie even here (*On a Supposed Right to Lie*, 8:425–430), is widely judged to **break**. The school's repair is Korsgaard's ("The Right to Lie," 1986). The murderer's maxim works only if I am ignorant of his aim, so a maxim of deceiving deceivers to protect their victims passes the universality test, while the Formula of Humanity remains the ideal to which I return as soon as I can. Herman's rules of moral salience explain why this case shows up as one that needs deliberation in the first place. The repair resolves the case from universality and respect, without borrowing mercy or self-gift. **Holds with repair.** One honest note: Catholic teaching sits closer to Kant's hard line than to Korsgaard's repair. It forbids the lie, permits silence, and holds that no one is bound to reveal the truth to someone without the right to know it (CCC 2483–2489).

**(3) Equal, unconditional dignity.** Humanity, meaning rational nature as an end in itself, has dignity: a worth beyond price that admits no equivalent (G 4:434–435). Autonomy is the ground of that dignity (G 4:436). Equality follows directly, because what cannot be priced cannot be ranked. The enemy is covered: even the vicious may not be denied respect (MM 6:463), and in war no means may destroy the possibility of future trust (MM 6:346–347). The dying are covered, because a waning rational nature is still rational nature. The newborn is covered: Kant counts the child a person from the act that brought her into the world (MM 6:280). The severely disabled infant who will never reason is reached only by kind, through Wood's personification principle or Kain's reading that Kant's teleology attaches the predisposition to personality to the species (Kain, *JHP* 2009; verify). **Holds for the newborn, the dying and the enemy, strains for the never-to-reason, and breaks in Kant himself at MM 6:336.** There he lets the law ignore a mother's killing of an infant born out of wedlock, a lapse his own MM 6:280 condemns. Korsgaard (*Fellow Creatures*, 2018) secures that every creature with a final good is an end in itself in a "passive" sense. That covers the infant, though not as a distinctively human dignity.

**(4) Ultimate good.** The highest good: virtue as the supreme condition, joined with happiness in proportion to it, in a realm of ends (CPrR 5:110–111). Kant's guiding principles are the three formulas of the categorical imperative. **Here Kant himself borrows, and says so.** The highest good is possible only if we postulate God and immortality (CPrR 5:122–134; CJ §87). The secular heirs (on Rawls's reading, and Reath's) reinterpret the highest good as an ideal for a just society within history. That is coherent but thinner: it offers nothing to those already spent, which is exactly what the text says of such hopes in the section on evil.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1: heteronomy.**
*Attack.* A morality grounded in God is either circular, because we judge God good by our own moral ideal (G 4:408–409), or heteronomous, because it binds through power and fear (G 4:443). A "Lawgiver" argument cannot escape this dilemma.
*Defense.* The text takes neither branch. The law is God's own wisdom, which He did not choose, "because He is the Good" (W1 closing; Chapter 3). We know it first by reason, without revelation (Romans 2:14–15). Our share in it is "reason's own light, not a foreign rule" (VS 41, which itself speaks of participated theonomy). No fear is involved, and no reader is asked to know God before knowing the law.
*Rejoinder.* Then God does no work in making the law binding *for us*: the unbeliever is bound to exactly the same degree. God can serve only as the law's ground of being, and Kant denies that this is theoretically knowable.
*Reply.* That is all the text claims. W2 says the question "weighs, it does not settle," and W1 says the law is not wrong because it is forbidden. **Held.**

**Exchange 2: the paradox of method against A5.**
*Attack.* The concept of good must be determined by the moral law, not before it (CPrR 5:62–65). A5 runs the order backwards: it starts from Aquinas's goods (life, offspring, truth, society) and from "good is to be done," and so makes duty answer to the objects of inclination.
*Defense.* A5 asserts only two things: that what we can do and what we want do not settle what we ought to do, which Kant affirms more strongly than anyone, and that on reflection certain goods present themselves as goods. Kant's own predispositions to good, to animality (self-preservation, propagation, community), to humanity and to personality (Religion 6:26–28), track Aquinas's list closely. A5 then leaves the question of teleology to W.
*Rejoinder.* Granted. The paradox of method remains a standoff at the level of first principles, not a defect in A5 as written. **Held.**

**Exchange 3: does theism ground kind-based, equal worth better than Kant? (the central exchange)**
*Attack.* The text twice claims to "give a ground" for something Kant cannot ground: why worth follows kind and not ability, and why it is equal. But its own sources ground being willed "for itself" in intellectual nature. Aquinas, whom the text cites, says rational creatures are governed for their own sake because they master their own acts through free will (SCG III.112). The Church places the image of God in intellect and free will (CCC 1705). So the theist's account ends where Kant's does, in rational nature held by kind, with a divine willing added on top that tracks that worth instead of explaining it. Meanwhile W2 brackets the spiritual soul ("The argument here does not assume it"), and the soul is exactly what a Thomist would need to show that the never-to-reason infant has a rational nature.
*Defense.* The text's added ground is the *end*, not the nature. "Measured against one end that is God Himself, the genius and the newborn are for the same thing." Kant's end is achieved by the agent's own will, while God's is given, and He can give the vision of Himself even to one who never thinks (CCC 1250, 1261). So capacity is irrelevant to equality, by construction. Second, Kant's kind-based reading rests on teleological judgment, which for Kant is only regulative (CJ §§65–68), while theism makes the teleology constitutive. Third, Kant's own MM 6:336 shows how easily a dignity grounded in rational standing lets the unwanted newborn slip.
*Rejoinder.* (a) Kant's dignity is not measured by any end. It is incomparable, and equality follows from that without the need for a common destination. (b) Practical cognition is not "as if" in a demeaning sense. Kant's noumenal person is not an empirical capacity at all, so brain damage cannot touch it, and that is at least as strong a reason as an "impeded rational soul" for making ability irrelevant. (c) 6:336 is Kant's Seneca, and the text already concedes Christian slowness on slavery. By the fairness rule, the lapse counts symmetrically and decides nothing.
*Verdict.* The theist's real advantage is *unity*: one source explains membership, equality and destination together. That advantage is one of fit, not a ground that Kant lacks. **Held with repair** (R4, R6, R9): the text should state the advantage as one ground in place of several, and admit that it faces the same "by kind" question.

**Exchange 4: the debt to the dead, and a misstatement of Kant.**
*Attack.* The C row says that when the one we wronged is gone, Kant's "account has no one left who can forgive." Kant postulated that the soul endures (CPrR 5:122–124) and grounded posthumous wrong in regarding the dead person as *homo noumenon*, apart from bodily existence (MM 6:295–296; verify). His own account does leave someone there, as an object of rational faith.
*Defense.* Kant's immortality postulate serves endless progress toward holiness, not reconciliation. He held it as faith, not evidence, which is the very status the text gives W1(1) ("a clue, not a proof"). His secular heirs drop it, and of them the sentence is true.
*Rejoinder.* Then the sentence should say so. Corrected, it strengthens the text: the philosopher of autonomy, at the grave, also had to hope that she is still someone. **Held with repair** (R1).

**Exchange 5: are the clues wishful thinking?**
*Attack.* W1(1) and W3 rest on asking and longing. A lover's longing does not prove the beloved exists (Wizenmann's objection to Kant). The text calls them "not evidence," yet its conclusion speaks of a "best explanation."
*Defense.* The text keeps the two apart. The clues weigh only "as much as the reader finds," and the explanatory weight rests on W1(2), W2 and the comparisons. It invokes A7 against itself ("we may not believe a thing because it comforts").
*Rejoinder, offered to the text.* Kant's own answer to Wizenmann gives the text a better footing: a need of *reason* is not a need of *inclination* (CPrR 5:143n; verify). Guilt's demand to be forgiven by her is not a wish for comfort. It is part of what being wronged involves. A secular Kantian replies that ought implies can, so when she is gone the demand lapses. That is a standoff. **Held (standoff at parity).** See insight 3.

**Exchange 6: "not hers to waive," and whether the trust moves the wrong onto God.**
*Attack.* If no one may kill the innocent because her life is "held in trust from the One who alone is Lord of life," then the wrong of killing becomes a matter of God's jurisdiction, and the wrong to *her* becomes derivative. That is the very defect A6 charges against those who count totals. And "He who gives life its being, and can give it back, does her no wrong when He calls it home" suggests that her death as such is not the wrong; the wrong is a non-owner taking.
*Defense.* The text gives two separate grounds. For not spending her for the many, she is willed for her own sake (GS 24): her worth is the ground, and it is centered on her. For not killing her even at her request, the ground is her life as a good *to her* that she did not give herself, "against the just love of herself" (CCC 2281). The trust explains only who may dispose of her life, not why taking it harms her. The offense against God is "not a second injury beside hers" (W1). God's "calling home" takes nothing from her, because He keeps her.
*Rejoinder.* Kant's *homo noumenon*, the humanity in her own person binding her as a natural being (MM 6:417–418, 6:422–423), already grounds non-waivability without any trust. The trust is therefore idle against Kant, though harmless. **Held.** No repair is needed; the text's two grounds are coherent.

**Exchange 7: evil, judged by the Kantian test.**
*Attack.* The text adopts the right test: whether an act "could be justified to her." By its own standard, those who could remedy a famine and do not commit grave injustice (CCC 2269). God could spare the child with cancer at no cost to any good we can see. Permission that falls foreseeably on her treats her as a means to the order of the whole.
*Defense.* The text concedes the weight in full: "we must answer for the child, as anyone who loved her and could have spared her would have to." It does not offer a trade: "only what is offered to her can" justify it, and that "is a hope, not a proof." It also keeps intended evil apart from permitted side effects (ST I q.19 a.9). Kant's own essay on the failure of all philosophical theodicies (1791) finds every doctrinal theodicy wanting, and finds the case for atheism from evil wanting too. What Kant called authentic theodicy is Job's sincerity (cf. Job 42:7: "you have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has"), and the text is sincere here.
*Rejoinder.* The weight stays on the scale, which is where the text puts it. **Held.** This is acknowledged weight, not a defect of the text.

**Exchange 8: Critical limits against the text's epistemic claims.**
*Attack.* Inference to the best explanation cannot reach past possible experience (CPR A620ff on the physico-theological proof). At most one may say "I am morally certain," never "it is morally certain" (CPR A829/B857). The conclusion's "not because certainty is out of reach" asserts something no moral argument can deliver.
*Defense.* The text separates what the Church teaches is possible (CCC 36; *Dei Filius* ch. 2) from what this case does: it "offers the case for weighing," and its certainty comes from "many signs converging in one mind" (Newman), "not from any one of them compelling every mind." Kant's own moral argument also reaches the supersensible from moral data. The two disagree only over the status of the result.
*Rejoinder.* Agreed. This is the deepest disagreement between the Critical philosophy and Thomist realism, and neither side can win it from moral data alone. **Held (standoff at parity).**

## 4. Ledger

- **A heading (frame, epistemic rule, list of deniers):** Holds. The rule of trusting what survives reflection, unless its cause is shown to be unrelated to its truth, is compatible with Kant's a priori validity, which is independent of genesis. Kant is not misplaced among the deniers.
- **A1:** Holds. It is categorical and independent of what any culture approves; Kant affirms it.
- **A2:** Holds. The secret told to a stranger is Kant's case par excellence: a wrong to her as a person, independent of any experienced effect (cf. MM 6:295–296).
- **A3:** Holds. No one can contract himself into slavery (MM 6:283, 6:330; verify the exact pages). Forgiveness does not undo the wrong.
- **A4:** Holds with repair. Kant shares the core, including dignity kept even by the vicious (MM 6:463). But the citation of CCC 2267 inside a shared premise carries a conclusion Kant denies (MM 6:333). See R8.
- **A5:** Holds. "Can and want do not settle ought" is common ground. The paradox of method is a standoff, and A5 leaves teleology to W.
- **A6:** Holds. This is the Formula of Humanity. The book's own claims (every number; even at his request) are correctly flagged as not shared. Kant himself held both; Hill and Velleman soften the second (verify).
- **A7:** Holds. Kant's duties against lying to oneself (MM 6:429–431) and the maxims of enlightened thinking.
- **W1 frame:** Holds. Kant is correctly listed among those who say pardon is owed to the one wronged. The reply that the offense against God is "His love refused in her," not a second injury (SCG III.122), answers Kant's worry that we have no duties *to* God (MM 6:443–444) as well as it can be answered.
- **W1(1):** Holds as a clue. It is correctly limited ("a clue, not a proof"; "that guilt asks proves nothing"). The Catholic paragraph is accurate (CCC 1459, 958; *Spe Salvi* 44, 47–48).
- **W1(2):** Holds with repair. Its account of Kant is accurate: humanity in one's own person, worth beyond price, the two-aspect solution, the newborn as a person. "His ground must stretch to reach her" needs the parity clause (R9). "Not from a worth she was given" is a contrast, not a shortfall, and should not be read as one.
- **W1 closing:** Holds. Kant agrees completely against voluntarism. Note that MM 6:227 lets Kant call God "Lawgiver" while refusing "author." The text's own gloss ("not as a will that invents it") is exactly what Kant meant by that refusal.
- **W2:** Holds with repair. Its handling of Kant ("need none either... why there are such wills at all") is right, but "Both the Church and her best rivals agree that what is good for a creature depends on what it is" misstates Kant for the moral good (G 4:389, 4:410–412). See R7.
- **W3 (longing):** Holds. Reason respects you and does not love you back; Kant concedes this. Kant does say that rational nature "exists as an end in itself" and that such existence has absolute worth (G 4:428). That is an impersonal form of "it is good that you exist," and it leaves the text's point standing: it is said by no one.
- **W3 (hiddenness):** Holds. Kant's universal moral religion and LG 16 converge, and the text names the cost honestly.
- **C intro:** Holds.
- **C, Nothing binds:** Holds. Kant would argue it the same way.
- **C, Feeling refined:** Holds.
- **C, Our best words:** Holds.
- **C, Reason's own law (Kant):** Holds with repair. Most of it is accurate: G 4:431; the limit at 4:459–463; MM 6:280, 6:460–461, 6:295–296; Religion 6:98–99; the rule of pardon, which is MM 6:337. Repairs needed: the forgiveness sentence misstates Kant himself (R1); Kant's own moral argument (CJ §§84–87) is left out (R3); "from birth" narrows 6:280 (R4); citations (R2, R5).
- **C, What we owe each other:** Holds. Darwall's second-personal view is the Kantian neighbor here, and it is stated fairly.
- **C, Care between persons:** Holds.
- **C, Well-being counted impartially:** Holds. The Kantian critique of totals is correctly put.
- **C, Flourishing of our kind:** Holds.
- **C, Necessary truths that belong to no one:** Holds.
- **C, Impersonal sacred order:** Holds. Outside my school; no Kantian objection.
- **C, The Way:** Holds.
- **C, God, or Nature:** Holds.
- **C, The Buddha's Dhamma:** Holds. The sentence "justice built into a world looks like the work of a mind" is Kant's highest-good argument in miniature; see insight 2.
- **C, History and class:** Holds.
- **C, Power and rank:** Holds.
- **C, Freedom that makes its values:** Holds. Correctly kept separate from Kantian autonomy, which is not invention.
- **C, A God who is Love:** Holds with repair. "Must take as given" and "rational nature held by kind" understate Kant's own ground, autonomy (G 4:436), and his admitted stopping point, the fact of reason (CPrR 5:31). See R6.
- **Evil, para. 1 (the weight):** Holds. Honest, and close to Kant's "authentic theodicy." Hell is correctly given as Church teaching and placed on this side of the scale.
- **Evil, First (privation):** Holds. Kant treats vice as a real opposition, not a mere lack (*Negative Magnitudes*, 1763), and that is a standoff. But he also denies a diabolical will (Religion 6:35), so the conclusion "no dark god" stands on his terms too.
- **Evil, Second (permission, not a trade):** Holds. It adopts the justified-to-her test and is candid that only a hope meets it.
- **Evil, Third (God who suffers; hope):** Holds. Kant's highest good is the philosophical counterpart of the Adorno and *Spe Salvi* point.
- **Conclusion:** Holds. Its final question is aimed at anyone who gives up A4, and the Kantian keeps A4, so the question does not divide Kant from theism. Against Kant the case rests on weighing, as the text says. "Not because certainty is out of reach" is a standoff with Critical epistemology, not a defect.

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**As written: NONE.**

Every major Kantian line of attack (heteronomy, the paradox of method, Critical limits, the sufficiency of rational nature for equal dignity, non-waivability without trust, evil judged by the justified-to-her test) is either met by the text's own wording or ends in a standoff at parity, which the text already concedes as weighing rather than proof.

**Minor items**
1. **Misstatement of Kant (C, Reason's own law):** "when the one we wronged is gone, his account has no one left who can forgive." This is true of the secular Kantians and false of Kant, who postulated that the soul endures (CPrR 5:122–124). R1.
2. **Understatement of Kant's ground (C, A God who is Love):** "must take as given ... or, with Kant, on rational nature held by kind." Kant grounds dignity in autonomy (G 4:436) and stops, admittedly, at the fact of reason; "by kind" is his readers' extension. R6.
3. **Misstatement (W2):** "Both the Church and her best rivals agree that what is good for a creature depends on what it is." For the moral law, Kant denies this (G 4:389, 4:410–412), though he allows that applying the law needs knowledge of our kind (MM 6:217). R7.
4. **Shared premise carrying a non-shared conclusion (A4):** CCC 2267 is cited for "not lost ... by the worst of crimes," but its conclusion against the death penalty is not shared (Kant: MM 6:333). R8.
5. **Narrowing (C, Reason's own law):** "a person from birth." MM 6:280 speaks of the act of procreation bringing a person into the world. R4.
6. **Omission (C, Reason's own law):** Kant's own moral argument for God (CPrR 5:122–134; CJ §§84–87) is absent. The row mentions only the moral Ruler of the ethical community. A reader would not learn that Kant answered W2's question ("why there are such wills at all") with a moral Author, as rational faith. R3.
7. **Citations:** "not even God is the author" is better supported by MM 6:227 than by G 4:431 alone (R2); the rule of pardon is MM 6:337 (R5).
8. **W1(2) parity:** "his ground must stretch to reach her" should admit that any ground in powers of mind stretches equally, including the image of God located in intellect and will. R9.

**Misstatements of my school:** items 1, 2, 3 and 5 above. All are minor and repairable. The text's account of Kant is otherwise unusually accurate.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Critical epistemology against *Dei Filius*: whether theoretical reason can know God, or only practical reason can believe.
- Constructivism against realism: whether "she must be regarded as a person in a practical respect" falls short of "she is a person" (Korsgaard says there is no further fact; the text says there is a truth held first in a Mind).
- Membership by kind: Kant's noumenal person against the theist's rational soul and call. Each is a metaphysical posit that makes empirical ability irrelevant.
- The debt to the dead: secular Kantians accept the cost (ought implies can; the demand lapses). The text calls its alternative a clue.
- Why rational beings exist at all: for the secular Kantian this is a cosmological question, not a moral one, and it ends in brute contingency or in Kant's practical faith.
- Evil as privation against evil as real opposition.
- Non-waivability: *homo noumenon* against life held in trust. Both are coherent.

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1 (C, Reason's own law; misstatement)**
Old: "Reason can condemn us; it is persons who forgive. Kant made forgiving a duty between persons, and held that the dead can still be wronged (*Metaphysics of Morals* 6:460–461, 6:295–296); but when the one we wronged is gone, his account has no one left who can forgive."
New: "Reason can condemn us; it is persons who forgive. Kant made forgiving a duty between persons, and held that the dead can still be wronged (*Metaphysics of Morals* 6:460–461, 6:295–296). When the one we wronged is gone, his account, taken without his hope, has no one left who can forgive. Kant himself added a hope, postulating that the soul endures (*Critique of Practical Reason* 5:122–124), though he held it as rational faith and for another need, and his secular heirs set it aside."

**R2 (C, Reason's own law; citation)**
Old: "because the law is the very form of a free will (*Groundwork* 4:431)."
New: "because the law is the very form of a free will (*Groundwork* 4:431); he allowed that it may be thought of as the command of a supreme Lawgiver, so long as that Lawgiver is not taken for its author (*Metaphysics of Morals* 6:227)."

**R3 (C, Reason's own law; omission)**
Old: "Kant answered that how freedom is possible we cannot understand."
New: "Kant answered that how freedom is possible we cannot understand; yet to why there are such beings at all he gave, as rational faith and not as knowledge, this book's answer: a being under moral law is the final end of creation, and that requires a moral Author of the world (*Critique of Judgment* §§84, 86–87)."

**R4 (C, Reason's own law; narrowing and parity)**
Old: "He counted the newborn a person from birth (*Metaphysics of Morals* 6:280); but his best readers, who ground her dignity in the rational nature she has by kind, must say why that kind carries such worth before reason is ever used, and Kant, who denied that we can know what the soul is, can hold it only as a demand of practical reason."
New: "He counted the child a person from the act that brought her into the world (*Metaphysics of Morals* 6:280); but his best readers, who ground her dignity in the rational nature she has by kind, must say why that kind carries such worth before reason is ever used, and Kant, who denied that we can know what the soul is, can hold it only as a demand of practical reason. This book faces the same question, for the image of God in her lies in an intellect and will she may never use (CCC 1705); its answer is that the call to communion comes with her being and does not wait on her powers (W1(2))."

**R5 (C, Reason's own law; citation)**
Old: "And his rule for clemency, that a ruler may remit a crime against himself but never what one subject did to another,"
New: "And his rule for clemency (*Metaphysics of Morals* 6:337), that a ruler may remit a crime against himself but never what one subject did to another,"

**R6 (C, A God who is Love; understatement of Kant's ground)**
Old: "and gives a ground for what the best rivals hold but must take as given: why worth follows kind and not ability"
New: "and gives one ground for what the best rivals hold on separate grounds or take as given: why worth follows kind and not ability"
Old: "or, with Kant, on rational nature held by kind."
New: "or, with Kant, on the autonomy of rational nature (*Groundwork* 4:436), which his best readers extend to every member of the kind."

**R7 (W2; misstatement)**
Old: "Both the Church and her best rivals agree that what is good for a creature depends on what it is, and that much of the natural law is fitted to our kind."
New: "The Church and most of her best rivals agree that what is good for a creature depends on what it is, and that much of the natural law is fitted to our kind; even Kant, who held that the moral law must not be drawn from human nature, held that applying it needs knowledge of our kind (*Groundwork* 4:389; *Metaphysics of Morals* 6:217)."

**R8 (A4; shared premise)**
Old: "nor even by the worst of crimes (*Dignitas Infinita* 1, 15; CCC 2261, 2267)."
New: "nor even by the worst of crimes (*Dignitas Infinita* 1, 15; CCC 2261). (What the Church concludes from this about the death penalty, CCC 2267, is her teaching, not a shared premise: Kant held both that even the vicious keep a dignity no one may deny them and that murder must be punished by death, *Metaphysics of Morals* 6:463, 6:333.)"

**R9 (W1(2); parity)**
Old: "and he counted the newborn a person, though his ground must stretch to reach her."
New: "and he counted the newborn a person, though his ground, like any ground laid in her powers of mind, must stretch to reach her."

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
1. **Kant supports the chapter title.** MM 6:227 says that a law binding us a priori through our own reason may also be represented as proceeding from the will of a supreme Lawgiver, provided He is not thought of as its author (paraphrase; verify). "The Lawgiver Is Love" is therefore a title Kant could accept. The one word he resists is "author," and the text's own gloss on VS 36 and 40 ("not as a will that invents it") is precisely his reason for resisting it. The chapter could say this in one sentence and take Kant's strongest objection off the table.
2. **Kant had his own moral argument, and the text already uses it.** The Dhamma row's line, that justice built into a world looks like the work of a mind, together with the section on evil's admission that our world does not show it, is Kant's highest-good argument (CPrR 5:110–134; CJ §87). Crediting Kant there makes the book's point that the philosopher of autonomy needed Someone twice over, not once.
3. **A better defense for the clues.** Kant's distinction between a need of reason and a need of inclination, made in answer to Wizenmann (CPrR 5:143n; verify), can protect W1(1) against the charge of wishful thinking. Guilt's request to be forgiven by her is not a wish for comfort. It belongs to the relation of having wronged someone. This still does not prove the answer, and the text is right to say so, but it lets W1(1) say *why* the request is not a mere longing.
4. **A further witness for A4's "how easily it is lost."** Beside Seneca, MM 6:336: the philosopher of dignity, who counted the child a person from her coming into being (6:280), still let the law ignore the killing of an infant born out of wedlock. This is fair to use because the text already admits Christian slowness on slavery.
5. **Convergence for A5.** Kant's predisposition to animality (self-preservation, propagation and the care of offspring, community with others; Religion 6:26–28) closely parallels Aquinas's goods in ST I-II q.94 a.2.
6. **Conscience as a court before Another.** Kant held that conscience must be thought of as a judge other than oneself, and finally as accountability to God (MM 6:438–439; verify). This is a stronger witness than Adam Smith for W3's and the C row's point, because it comes from the philosopher of autonomy.
7. **W2's "one can always refuse to do evil."** Kant: a collision of duties is inconceivable (MM 6:224). He is a non-theist-friendly ally for that claim.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent."
- "A will that invents values can command, but it cannot bind; even a promise binds only under a law no promiser invented." (Kant would sign this.)
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."
- "Equal worth is not equal duty."
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it." (This is Kant's own point that respect is the effect of the law and not its source, G 4:401n.)
- "The rebel can take her side; he cannot keep her."

## 8. Score

**8 / 10.** The text states Kant accurately, almost everywhere at his best. It frames every claim against him as weighing rather than refutation, and it leaves no Fatal or Serious objection standing. Only four minor misstatements remain (the immortality postulate, autonomy as the ground of dignity, W2's "best rivals agree," "from birth"), and all are repairable.

**Honest ceiling for any theistic case against my school: about 8.5.** Against Kant himself, a moral argument for God can at best converge with his own rational faith. Against his secular heirs, it hits a wall that moral data cannot breach: whether practical regard is the whole truth of worth (constructivism) or answers to a truth held in a Mind (realism), and whether theoretical reason may reach the supersensible at all. With R1–R9 applied, this text reaches that ceiling.
