# Blind review: Secular Neo-Aristotelian / Virtue Ethics

## 1. School and voice

I speak for the secular revival of Aristotelian naturalism: Philippa Foot (*Natural Goodness*, 2001; "Utilitarianism and the Virtues," 1985; "Euthanasia," 1977; the 1967 double-effect paper), Michael Thompson (*Life and Action*), Rosalind Hursthouse (*On Virtue Ethics*), John McDowell ("Two Sorts of Naturalism," *Mind and World*), and Martha Nussbaum (*The Fragility of Goodness*, "Transcending Humanity," *Frontiers of Justice*, *Anger and Forgiveness*). Cora Diamond and Raimond Gaita are close neighbours. Our thesis: moral goodness is the natural goodness of a rational animal. Its standards come from the human life-form, which is represented in "natural-historical" judgments that are neither statistics nor design. We reach those standards from inside a second nature formed by upbringing, and they need no foundation from outside the life of reason. I agree with most of A, so this is a quarrel between near neighbours, and I have tried to fight it where the real disagreement lies.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** Justice is a virtue: the settled will to give each person what is due to them (NE V). What is due comes from what human beings are and need. We depend on binding one another's wills, on non-violation, and on care in infancy and decline (Foot, *NG* ch. 3; Nussbaum's threshold of central capabilities). *Holds* for what is owed. *Borrows nothing from the book*. But it reaches a stopping point when asked why everyone who bears the human form is owed it, cooperator or not. "That is what justice is" is the answer, and it is given, not derived.

**2. A hard dilemma.** Case: a daughter cares for her father, who has advanced dementia. When competent he said he would rather die than live like this. Now he seems content. She is exhausted and her children are neglected. Resolution: charity aims at his good, and his present life is plainly a good to him, so the earlier wish does not license ending it (against "precedent autonomy," verify Dworkin/Dresser). Justice to her children and to herself means she owes care, not unlimited self-immolation, so she may and should share the burden. Honesty forbids pretending to him or to herself. Practical wisdom weighs the details, and some remainder of regret is fitting (Hursthouse on residue). *Holds*: justice, charity, honesty and practical wisdom are natural virtues, older than the Christian synthesis, and I use none of them in the book's covenantal sense. *Weakness*: there is no algorithm. Critics say "what the virtuous person would do" gives too little guidance. Hursthouse's v-rules answer that only partly.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** Ground: membership of the human life-form. The newborn, the dying and the severely disabled are bearers of the form. Its standards are theirs, and justice and care toward bearers of the form are themselves part of the form (MacIntyre's "virtues of acknowledged dependence" make the point best, though he is a theist). Diamond: "human being" is a moral concept, not merely a biological one. *Holds* for "equal" and "prior to recognition." *Breaks* in three places. First, our central evaluative notion, natural goodness, grades individuals, so equality must come from justice, taken as given. Second, some of us draw the line of a human life at capability: Nussbaum, verify, on the anencephalic or permanently vegetative. Third, we deny "unconditional" in the Church's sense that no request can waive it (Foot, "Euthanasia").

**4. Ending point for flourishing.** Eudaimonia: a complete life of activity in accordance with the virtues, among friends and fellow citizens. Nussbaum's capability list fills in its content. The guiding principles are the virtues and practical wisdom. *Coherent*. *Breaks* honestly at its edges. It is an achievement of activity, so luck can destroy it (Priam). Those who can never act can never reach it. Nothing in it answers to the dead. Aristotle's own highest good, contemplation as "divine" and "immortalizing so far as we can" (NE X.7, verify wording), points past the human. The secular heirs cut that off and do not replace it.

## 3. Debate highlights

**E1. Accident or wisdom.**
*Attack:* W2 frames our nature as "an accident or a wisdom." Aristotle's whole point in *Physics* II.8 is a third option: nature acts for an end without chance and without a deliberating craftsman. Thompson makes the life-form a logical category that Darwinism neither creates nor threatens.
*Defense:* The C row already states this answer and calls it a stopping point. Aquinas agreed that nature acts for an end without deliberating. He argued separately (the Fifth Way) that what lacks knowledge acts for an end only as directed by a knower.
*Rejoinder:* The Fifth Way is a different argument, and W2 rightly does not lean on it. The binary sentence in W2 still leaves us out.
**Held with repair** (R1).

**E2. The reach.**
*Attack:* We can grant that gratuitous cruelty would be wrong for any rational sufferer. That follows from the form of practical reason as such (Thompson; Foot on practical rationality), not from our species alone. As for how selection-built minds came to know it: through Bildung. We are initiated into the space of reasons, and from inside it reasons are seen for what they are. To demand a further fit between law and mind is to ask for a view from sideways-on (McDowell).
*Defense:* Then you have granted necessary truths across every possible rational nature, and the question "why are there such natures at all?" stands. The text also says only that this "weighs, it does not settle."
*Rejoinder:* "Why is there rational life at all?" is a cosmological question, not a moral one. The moral argument gains nothing distinctive from it. The text half-concedes this in its parenthesis (Kant-type views "need none"). Its main sentence, though, says neither the realist answer nor ours "explains the reach," without saying what the reach asks of us.
**Held with repair** (R6).

**E3. The newborn who will never think.**
*Attack:* The C row says we can only say she "belongs and is owed our care, not that she is for the same thing as you." That is false of Thompson and Foot. She bears the same form, the form is her measure, and what she lacks is privation, not a different end. This is the very structure of the Church's own philosophical ground (rational nature by kind; Boethius' definition of a person, verify whether *Dignitas Infinita* cites it).
*Defense:* Grant the same form. On your account the end is an activity, which each person must achieve, and she never can. Your measure, natural goodness, therefore grades her a defective specimen. Her equality has to come from justice, taken as given. On the Church's account the final end "surpasses the understanding and powers of man" and is a free gift (CCC 1722). Ability sets no one's end, and God can give that end to the one who never thinks (CCC 1250, 1261).
*Rejoinder:* That is the strongest structural point the text has against us, and I concede it. It is not what the text says, though. As written, the sentence misstates us. As repaired, it lands.
**Held with repair** (R2).

**E4. "God wanted *you*."**
*Attack:* Either worth follows what she is, in which case God's wanting is idle for grounding it, or worth follows being wanted, which is voluntarism about worth.
*Defense:* Neither. God's love gives being, and worth follows being (ST I q.20 a.2). The wanting does other work: it makes the person "willed for itself" (GS 24), not for the species or the total. Natural normativity strains exactly here. Foot derives an individual's good from the life-cycle of its kind, and her critics charge that this makes the individual a bearer of the species' ends.
*Rejoinder:* Foot and Hursthouse reply that in a rational animal reason transforms the natural ends: the human good is happiness, not reproduction. That reply is serviceable, but it is a reply under pressure. Here the text has a better argument than it uses.
**Held** (insight 2).

**E5. The line at every number.**
*Attack:* W1(2) gives Foot credit only for doing versus allowing at five. Foot also argued ("Utilitarianism and the Virtues") that there is no agent-neutral "best state of affairs," apart from what the virtues ask, that could make spending the innocent "better." That gives virtue ethics a reason to hold the line at any number without God.
*Defense:* Foot let the right to life be waived. Hursthouse allows tragic dilemmas in which even the virtuous agent's life is marred. And "that is what justice is" is a stopping point.
*Rejoinder:* All true, and at parity with "goodness is God's nature." The text does not say Foot bends at vast numbers, so this is a matter of giving credit, not an error.
**Held with repair** (R5, optional).

**E6. The waiver and the trust.**
*Attack:* "Held in trust from the Lord of life" is a Christian premise. On Aristotelian grounds the good of life is the good of *a life* (bios), and Aristotle himself excludes "a life spent in pains" from life that is good by nature (NE IX.9, around 1170a22–24, verify).
*Defense:* A6 and the C row both label this as the book's own claim, not a shared premise, as the brief permits. The best Aristotelian card for the Church is "for living things, to live is to be" (*De Anima* II.4, 415b13). To aim at his death is to aim at *him*, not at a bad condition of his.
*Rejoinder:* That is a good card, and the NE IX.9 qualification answers it. It is a genuine standoff at parity, and the text labels it honestly.
**Held** (as a labelled claim).

**E7. The debt to the dead.**
*Attack:* W1(1) needs the premise that "a debt truly owed to her is one she must be able, one day, to receive." We deny it. The tragic is real: some debts are truly owed and can never be paid (Nussbaum, *Fragility*; Williams on agent-regret is a near neighbour).
*Defense:* The text says exactly that it is "a clue, not a proof," weighing only as much as the reader grants that premise, and that "what he owes her is the same on the second road and the third."
*Rejoinder:* Agreed. The second road is ours, and it is stated fairly.
**Held.**

**E8. Transcending humanity.**
*Attack:* Nussbaum's point is not about duration. Courage, generosity and love as we know them are constituted by vulnerability and loss. A life that cannot lose has no human excellence.
*Defense:* The risen Christ kept His wounds, so what finite love lived through is kept. Aquinas holds that the moral virtues remain in heaven as to their formal element, though not as to the passions and dangers they govern (ST I-II q.67 a.1, verify). The text could add this.
*Rejoinder:* Nussbaum would say a virtue with no danger to meet is no longer the human virtue. The text claims only fit, not evidence.
**Held** (parity).

**E9. Evil.**
*Attack:* We bear it lightly; we never said the cosmos cares. The sharpest version for the theist, who accepts natural normativity for every kind, is this: God's wisdom contains as good the form of a human-obligate parasite, whose natural good is attained only in human bodies.
*Defense:* The text already says the parasite "stays on this side of the scale," separates what He wills from what He permits, and rests "God does not give her to it" on a teaching that it says is "not something we see."
*Rejoinder:* It is conceded honestly.
**Held.**

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**As written: NONE.**

No Fatal or Serious objection survives. Each place where my school disagrees on substance (the waiver, the debt to the dead, transcending humanity, the further "why" behind kind-worth) is either labelled in the text as the book's own claim or claimed only as a clue. At those points the text answers at parity or better.

**Minor items**
1. W2: the "accident or a wisdom" binary leaves out the Aristotelian third option. The C row states it, but W2 does not (R1).
2. W2: "What neither explains is the reach" does not say what the reach asks of the "one nature" answer, and it sits uneasily with the closing parenthesis (R6).
3. A heading: the routing parenthesis sends "real but need no Mind" only to the evolutionary claim (R4).
4. W1(2): Foot is credited only for the five, not for her argument that no "best state of affairs" licenses the deed (R5, optional).

**Misstatements of my school**
1. C (flourishing): "it can say she belongs and is owed our care, not that she is for the same thing as you." Neo-Aristotelians can say she has the same form and the same end. The true point is that on our view the end is an achievement she cannot reach, and our measure grades her (R2).
2. C (God row): "the flourishing view ... which also stop at one." We do not stop at one source (R3).
3. By omission: the C row lists only our euthanasia cost. Our margin cost (capability thresholds, Nussbaum, verify; Hursthouse on abortion) is real and makes the text stronger against us if named (R7).

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Whether equal worth by kind needs a further "why" (God's image and call) or whether that demand is illegitimate (McDowell).
- Whether her life stays a good to her in extremity (Church; *De Anima* II.4) or not (Foot; NE IX.9).
- Whether a debt truly owed must be receivable (W1(1)) or can be tragically unpayable (Nussbaum).
- Whether resurrection-life keeps human excellence (q.67 a.1) or leaves it behind (Nussbaum).
- Whether the existence of rational natures needs a Source: a cosmological question on which the moral argument adds fit, not force.

New: "So the question is not whether our nature could have been otherwise, but whether it is an accident, an order that needs no mind behind it, or a wisdom."

**R2 (C, The flourishing of our kind).**
Old: "And the end it measures us by is a life of reason well lived, which the newborn who will never think can never reach: it can say she belongs and is owed our care, not that she is for the same thing as you (W1(2))."

The end the Church names surpasses every human power and is given as a gift (CCC 1722), so that strength and mind set no one's end (W1(2))."

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
1. **The end is given, not achieved.** This is the text's strongest point against eudaimonism, and it is currently only implicit. If the final end is a gift that surpasses every creature's powers (CCC 1722), ability sets no one's end, and the newborn and the genius are equally recipients. If the end is an activity, ability grades how far each one reaches it. Making this explicit (R2) turns a misstatement into a real advantage.
2. **"Willed for itself" against form-centred goodness.** Natural normativity derives an individual's good from its kind's life-cycle. GS 24 ("the only creature on earth God willed for itself") answers a pressure Foot's critics press on her. The text could use this in the flourishing row. It should give the Foot/Hursthouse reply that reason transforms the natural ends, and claim parity-plus, not victory.
3. **Aristotle's own ending point reaches past the human.** NE X.7–8 (contemplation as divine, "immortalizing so far as we can," verify wording) shows that the school's founder did not stop at the human. His god, though, knows only itself and is not loved back (*Met.* Λ; *MM* II.11, verify), which makes him a fit companion to the Spinoza row in W3.
4. **"For living things, to live is to be"** (*De Anima* II.4, 415b13) is the best Aristotelian support for the no-waiver line: to aim at his death is to aim at him. Use it knowing that NE IX.9 qualifies it.
5. **Aquinas on the virtues in heaven** (ST I-II q.67 a.1, verify) is the precise reply to Nussbaum's "Transcending Humanity" in W3.
6. **Raimond Gaita** (secular; *A Common Humanity*): the love of saints reveals the full humanity of the afflicted, and he admits that "sacred" in his usage resists full secular translation (verify). He is the most serious secular witness to "the infant no one holds" and is worth a sentence in the care row or ours.
7. **The parasite, sharpened.** A human-obligate parasite whose natural good exists only in human bodies is the hardest case for "a person it never gives away." The text already concedes the weight. The sharper form is worth acknowledging.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent."
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."
- "Equal worth is not equal duty ... the saints are higher than I am in how well they love, never in what they are worth."
- "A will that invents values can command, but it cannot bind."
- "Heaven and Earth give life to things; God wanted *you*."
- "The rebel can take her side; he cannot keep her."
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it."

## 8. Score

**8 / 10.** My school already grants nearly all of A. The text states our view fairly except for one sentence about the newborn's end and one about "stopping at one." It claims only fit, never force, where we really disagree, so nothing my school can bring defeats it as written.

**Honest ceiling** for any theistic case against my school: about **8–9**. A theistic case can show a real explanatory surplus at the margins: the end given rather than achieved, the person willed for itself rather than for its kind, the dead kept. But a school that holds A1–A7 on natural grounds and refuses, on principle, the demand for a ground outside the life of reason cannot be refuted by moral data. It can only be shown to be stopping earlier than it might.
