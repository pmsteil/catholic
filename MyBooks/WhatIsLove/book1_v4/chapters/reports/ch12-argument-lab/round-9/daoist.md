# Blind review: the Daoist school (Laozi and Zhuangzi)

## 1. School and voice

I speak for the *Daodejing* and the *Zhuangzi* (inner chapters first, with the Yangist and later chapters where they help), read with Graham, Hansen, Ivanhoe, Kohn and Ziporyn. The Way that can be spoken is not the constant Way. Heaven and Earth are not humane: they treat the ten thousand things as straw dogs (DDJ 5). Benevolence and righteousness appear when the great Way is abandoned (DDJ 18, 38). The sage acts without forcing (wu wei), lets each thing be self-so (ziran), and rears without ruling (DDJ 10, 51). He holds that "this" and "that" are each deemed from a standpoint (Qiwulun), and he meets his own death and his wife's with equanimity before the transformations (Zhuangzi 6, 18). I am not a theist, but I am not a nihilist either. I do not attack morality from below, as the debunker does; I put it in its place from beside it. My question for the text is whether its starting points (owing, wronging, pardon, being wanted, being kept) assume a personal and juridical picture of value that is exactly what is at issue between us.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** I do not start from what is owed. I start from the Way of Heaven, which takes from what has too much and gives to what lacks, as one draws a bow; the way of men takes from those who lack to serve those who have too much (DDJ 77). The people starve because rulers tax too much (DDJ 75). Justice is the ruler not interfering with each thing's self-so course, and the levelling of surplus. → **Holds as a principle of governance; breaks on "owed to her."** It yields no claim that belongs to a person in her own right. It also strains on the ziran/wei distinction (see Exchange 4).

**2. A hard dilemma resolved.** *Case:* bandits demand one innocent villager and threaten to burn the village if he is not handed over. My answer comes from the Great King Danfu (Zhuangzi 28, also told in Mencius 1B15): rather than make his people die for the land, he left Bin, and the people followed him. You do not spend the people for the things that nourish the people. Do not hand him over; yield, scatter, go elsewhere. Do not contend (bu zheng), and use force only as an inauspicious tool you take up with mourning (DDJ 30–31). A second case: a dying father in great pain asks to be killed. The answer is not to add to life and not to harm oneself with likes and dislikes (Zhuangzi 5, Huizi dialogue): relieve the pain, stop the forcing treatments, sit with him, let the course run, and do not cut in the master carpenter's place (DDJ 74). → **Coherent, and it borrows nothing.** It draws on ci, the motherly compassion that is the first of the three treasures (DDJ 67), which is our own and not the book's "mercy." But it **thins** in a true tragic dilemma where no one can yield and every path kills. There it rests on the sage's uncodifiable skill (Cook Ding), which gives the rest of us no principle we can check.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** I ground equal regard in three things: the Way is wholly present in everything, even in ants, weeds, tiles and dung (Zhuangzi 22); no standpoint ranks things (Qiwulun); and the sage is good to the good and good to the not-good alike, and takes all the people as his children (DDJ 49; cf. 27, 62). The cripples and the ugly men of Zhuangzi 5 and Shu the hunchback of Zhuangzi 4 show that worth does not follow use or ability; the useless tree lives out its years. → **Holds for "equal," "unconditional" and "not lost by crime."** It **breaks on "human" and on "inviolable."** My equality is a levelling of all things: the child is no more than the fawn, and the rat's liver is as good a destiny as a man's. What I ground is a disposition of non-harm, not a dignity that is a claim against others. I deny human exceptionalism, openly.

**4. Ultimate flourishing.** Returning to the root (DDJ 16) and nourishing life; free and easy wandering; a heart that works like a mirror, which neither sends off nor welcomes and responds without storing (Zhuangzi 7); and a yes to the transformations, death included (Zhuangzi 6). The guiding principles are wu wei, ziran, the three treasures (compassion, frugality, not daring to be first), the soft overcoming the hard, and knowing what is enough. → **Coherent, and it borrows nothing.** It pays openly: there is no one who knows me as myself, no personal survival, and no one who can release what I did to another.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1. The epistemic gate of A.**
*Attack:* A trusts "what still seems true to us after honest reflection." Qiwulun answers that every school's reflection confirms its own deemings. The Confucians affirm what the Mohists deny, and each side's honest reflection holds. "Wrong even if every culture approved" (A1) claims a place to stand outside all standpoints, and that is the very thing in dispute.
*Defense:* The text claims no neutrality ("None is beyond denial… This is not a vote"). The principle is defeasible and invites reflection on how a belief was produced. Zhuangzi himself trusts something: the "clarity" (ming) that sees from the axis of the Way. Zhuangzi 4 and 10 also condemn the tyrant and the great thief who steals benevolence along with the state, and those are not standpoint-free judgments either.
*Rejoinder:* Zhuangzi's affirmations are a "going along with this" (yinshi), not standpoint-free truths. Still, every account trusts some seeing, and I would not let the text call mine arbitrary. **Held** (standoff at parity).

**Exchange 2. The ledger of guilt (A2, A3, W1(1)).**
*Attack:* The text treats guilt that "asks her pardon" as a datum. Laozi treats the ledger as a symptom. When a great grievance is settled, some grievance always remains. So the sage holds the left half of the tally, the creditor's half, and does not press for payment; those with virtue keep to the tally, and those without it keep to collecting (DDJ 79). He answers resentment with virtue (DDJ 63). Zhuangzi says it is better to forget both the praise of Yao and the blame of Jie, and to change along with the Way, than to keep the accounts (Zhuangzi 6). The text reduces all this to "forgetting heals guilt" (W1(1); C, the Way), but my school speaks mainly from the creditor's side: do not write the debt.
*Defense:* Catholic teaching agrees with DDJ 79 on the creditor's side. Forgiveness is the release of a debt ("forgive us our debts, As we also have forgiven our debtors," Matthew 6:12), and the lord who releases the servant does not exact (Matthew 18:27). But a debt released is first a debt, and A2's case of the secret shows that someone was wronged before anyone forgives. The text also labels W1(1) a clue that "weighs as much as the reader finds."
*Rejoinder:* Then the text should say what my school actually says. Its parenthesis about who finds the clue weightless names only the reader who holds that "owed" voices refined feeling. It should name mine too: the reader who holds that the ledger of dues is itself what appears when the Way is lost. **Held with repair** (R2, R7).

**Exchange 3. "Wanted" built into the datum (W1(2), the evil section).**
*Attack:* A4 says only that worth is equal, prior to recognition, and not to be spent. By W1(2) and the evil section the datum has become "a worth that is each one's own, *wanted for her own sake*," and the text says an impersonal source "wants none of them." That rigs the comparison: only a Person can want. Zhuangzi denies the value the word carries. The very place where the Way is damaged is where love-as-preference is formed (Qiwulun, on Zhao Wen's lute). On my account, partial wanting is the wound, not the crown.
*Defense:* The text's ground for the line in W1(2) is not "wanting." It is that no one may take what is not his to take, and that her worth follows what she is. "Wanted" comes from W3 and W1(1), and each is labelled a clue. The text concedes that ziran gives each thing its own self-so, "and not only its kind." It presents the difference as a parting ("We part over whether the Giver knows her"), not as a proof.
*Rejoinder:* The parting is honest. But one sentence of the evil section counts "wanted for her own sake" as a datum that the impersonal accounts fit less well. Against my school that begs the question, because it scores the theory by a value the theory alone supplies. **Held with repair** (R6). The underlying dispute, whether being wanted or being let be is the higher gift, is a standoff.

**Exchange 4. "Not ours to take" without a Lord (A6, W1(2), "A God who is Love").**
*Attack:* The text says Kant and the Theravāda hold the line even at a man's own request, and that neither grounds it in a worth she was given. It overlooks a closer rival. There is always the master executioner who kills, and whoever kills in his place is like one who cuts in place of the master carpenter, and seldom escapes cutting his own hand (DDJ 74). The world is a sacred vessel that cannot be worked on or grasped (DDJ 29). And the sage does not "add to life" (Zhuangzi 5). Those three together give exactly the shape of W1(2)'s argument ("He who gives life its being, and can give it back, does her no wrong… we, who can do neither, could only take it") with no Person in it. Wu wei also explains doing versus allowing more naturally than any count of totals.
*Defense:* DDJ 74 is in context about rulers' executions, and it is partly prudential (you will cut your hand). It says the killer oversteps the course, not that he wrongs *her*. A6's charge against the total-counters applies to it as well. There is also a crack in the Daoist ground. If everything is self-so, as Guo Xiang reads Zhuangzi, then the killer's act is self-so too, and the ziran/wei distinction that makes killing a usurpation cannot be stated.
*Rejoinder:* I grant both points. The ziran/wei problem is real and old in my school (Graham presses it). But the text's distinctive gain is then narrower than it claims. An impersonal "not ours to take" is available. What only the text has is "a wrong done to her" plus a Lord who has jurisdiction. It should say so. **Held with repair** (R3), and my own school breaks at the ziran/wei point.

**Exchange 5. "Nor does God possess."**
*Attack:* The Daoist row says: "Nor does God possess: He willed her for her own sake and gave her life to her keeping (GS 24; CCC 2280)." But CCC 2280 says the opposite in its letter. God "remains the sovereign Master of life"; we are "stewards, not owners." The text's own W1(2) rests the line against a man's own request on that very sovereignty ("held in trust from the One who alone is Lord of life"). The Way rears and does not rule (DDJ 10, 51). The text's God rears *and* rules, and His ruling is the decisive premise of A6's most contested clause. Calling it non-possession hides the real point where we part, and it misreads the text's own citation.
*Defense:* God's dominion is not acquisitive. He gains nothing from her (ST I q.44 a.4) and governs her for her own sake (SCG III.112), so He does not "possess" in the DDJ 51 sense of holding for one's own use.
*Rejoinder:* That distinction is sound, but the text does not draw it. As written, the sentence denies what CCC 2280 affirms and what W1(2) needs. **Failed as written; Held with repair** (R1).

**Exchange 6. Evil and the straw dogs.**
*Attack:* The pattern the evil section concedes (pain where survival puts it, the wolf and the fawn, the parasite and the child, God willing "some things to be naturally corrupted") is exactly what "Heaven and Earth are not humane" describes, and it needs no remainder, no hope, and no defense of a Will. The text's "a person it never gives away" is asserted, not seen, while the parasite goes on taking.
*Defense:* The text concedes all of this in its own words: a Way that is not humane "owes no one an answer," and the parasite claim is "a teaching, not something we see," left "on this side of the scale." Its three replies are offered as bearing on the weight, not as removing it. The Daoist pays as well: when she dies, his yes lets her go.
*Rejoinder:* There is one overstatement. The evil section says a yes said to whatever happens "cannot take her side." But it then exempts Zhuangzi, rightly, because his yes is to the changes and not to the tyrant, and the sage's compassion takes her side while she lives (DDJ 27, 67). The true contrast is the text's own line: he can take her side, he cannot keep her. The text already says this of the rebel, so the general sentence is now only loosely worded. **Held** (standoff; the text's concession is honest).

**Exchange 7. The forge (W3).**
*Attack:* Master Lai's metal that leaps up in the forge and demands to be made a famous sword (Zhuangzi 6) is not only the man who refuses change. It is anyone who demands to stay *himself*. "We shall all be changed" and still "go on being herself, body and all" asks the smith to change the metal and keep its name.
*Defense:* W3 separates the longing that grasps from the longing to be given. The Christian demands nothing; he hopes to receive. Master Lai also accepts whatever the Creator of things (zaowuzhe) makes of him, a rat's liver or an insect's arm. The Christian accepts being changed by Another, and hopes that what Another makes is still the one He loved. W3 itself says that a longing does not prove its answer.
*Rejoinder:* Whether hoping to be kept is still a fixing is the question between us, and the text marks it as a clue. **Held** (standoff).

**Exchange 8. One Source, but not a law (W2).**
*Attack:* I agree there is one Source of things and of the mind that knows them (DDJ 25, 42). I deny it is a law. The "fit" W2 finds between moral law and mind is the fit between conventional deeming and conventional minds. Wang Ni already asked whose standard of dwelling, food and beauty is right: the man's, the eel's, the deer's or the fish's (Zhuangzi 2). Darwin's bees are my witness, not the text's. W2's list of those who agree with the witness and part elsewhere leaves my school out.
*Defense:* Wang Ni's relativity concerns goods fitted to kinds, and W2 grants that. Its claim is narrower: gratuitous cruelty and treating a person as a thing would be wrong for any creature that can reason and suffer. W2 says it "weighs, it does not settle." If the Daoist denies that reach, he pays for it under A1, which the comparison records.
*Rejoinder:* Yes, I pay under A1 in its juridical form. The omission is still a misplacement of my school. **Held with repair** (R5).

## 4. Ledger

- **A heading:** Holds with repair. The epistemic principle is defeasible and claims no neutrality (Exchange 1). The list of deniers under-reports Daoist dissent: Zhuangzi would deny A1's standpoint-free "wrong" (on Hansen's reading; Ivanhoe contests it, verify), not only the debt to the dead. See R8.
- **A1:** Holds, at a standoff. Daoism condemns the cruelty of rulers but not as a standpoint-free shi/fei judgment. The parity is honest.
- **A2:** Holds. The secret case is well built to separate wronging from worsening. A Daoist would say the teller harmed his own virtue (de) and the bond, not "her" as a creditor. That is a standoff, and the text records it under "refined feeling"-type readers. The structures-of-sin paragraph is accurate (CCC 1868–1869).
- **A3:** Holds. "No one can consent his own enslavement into being right" is close to the Yangist chapters, which hold that life is not to be traded for the empire (Zhuangzi 28). "We still owe her" is contested by my school, and the text names that.
- **A4:** Holds. As written it does not assert human superiority over the fawn, so a Daoist can grant most of it (DDJ 49, Zhuangzi 4–5). Where I part is on inviolability as a claim against others, and on the human privilege that comes later in W1(2).
- **A5:** Holds. The formal first principle survives DDJ 2 (when all know the good as good, there is already the not-good), because even the Daoist pursues something under the aspect of good (nourishing life, the Way). Aquinas's list is attributed to him, not required of the reader.
- **A6:** Holds with repair. The cases are fair and the Church's teaching is accurately stated. It overlooks the Daoist "not ours to usurp" ground (DDJ 74) when it surveys who holds the line (Exchange 4; R3).
- **A7:** Holds. A Daoist reframes the binding as the unobstructed, mirror-like heart: "there must first be a genuine person before there can be genuine knowledge" (Zhuangzi 6, paraphrase). That reframing does not refute A7. Zhuangzi's ironic "goblet words" dodge the tu quoque used against the debunker, but W2 does not aim that tu quoque at him.
- **W1 frame:** Holds. The order of cause and fruit and the necessary truth are both fairly described as "no one the guilt could be owed to." Agreement that guilt is owed to her is placed honestly.
- **W1(1):** Holds with repair. The three roads are fair, and Xunzi and Mozi are well used. "Forgetting may heal, as Zhuangzi taught" misplaces my school, whose remedy is on the creditor's side and dissolves the ledger itself (DDJ 79, 63; Zhuangzi 6). The weighting parenthesis should name the Daoist reader (R2, R7). The Catholic paragraph is accurate (CCC 1459, 958; Spe Salvi 44, 47–48).
- **W1(2):** Holds with repair. The concession that ziran gives "each thing its own self-so, and not only its kind" is fair. The ground for the line is lordship, which is honest here but contradicted by the Daoist row (R1). "A worth no deed confers and no order wants" uses "wants" as a rhetorical weight that the argument itself does not need. The OT-violence paragraph is accurate to Dei Verbum 15 and Verbum Domini 42.
- **W1 closing ("This Lawgiver is not a commander"):** Holds. The no-voluntarism position is accurate (VS 36, 40–41), and no Daoist objection arises.
- **W2:** Holds with repair. The Daoist accepts one Source and denies that it is a law, and the parenthetical classification omits him (R5). "It weighs, it does not settle" is right.
- **W3 (longing):** Holds. The forge is quoted fairly, and the grasping/given distinction is a real reply (Exchange 7). The Sartre and Spinoza handling is fair.
- **W3 (hiddenness):** Holds. It is honest about cost and accurate on CCC 847–848, LG 16, GS 22 and CCC 1257/1261. A Daoist would add that the Way's not being found is what the Way is like (DDJ 14, 41: the great image has no form). That is a standoff.
- **C intro:** Holds. The fairness pledge on origins is kept toward my school.
- **C, Nothing binds:** Holds (outside my remit).
- **C, Feeling refined:** Holds (outside my remit). The Smith reference is apt.
- **C, Our best words:** Holds (outside my remit).
- **C, Reason's own law:** Holds (outside my remit). The Kant clemency point is well made.
- **C, What we owe each other:** Holds (outside my remit).
- **C, Care between persons:** Holds. Daoist ci (DDJ 67), the mother who guards, is a near neighbour and meets the same "infant no one holds" question.
- **C, Utilitarianism:** Holds (outside my remit).
- **C, Flourishing of our kind:** Holds (outside my remit).
- **C, Godless realism:** Holds (outside my remit).
- **C, Impersonal sacred order:** Holds. It is fair to Zhu Xi and to Sima Qian's use of DDJ 79. The moon in ten thousand rivers is accurately Neo-Confucian.
- **C, The Way that gives and does not claim:** Holds with repair. DDJ 5, 10/51, 18/38, 55, 27 and 74 are accurately cited. Three repairs are needed: "Nor does God possess" (R1, a real fault); "heals guilt by forgetting" (R2); and the omission of DDJ 49 and 67, which makes the Daoist's equal regard look thinner than it is (R4). The final sentence, "We part over whether the Giver knows her, and whether she is kept or let go," is exactly right.
- **C, God or Nature:** Holds (outside my remit).
- **C, The Buddha's Dhamma:** Holds (outside my remit).
- **C, History and class:** Holds (outside my remit).
- **C, Power and rank:** Holds (outside my remit).
- **C, Freedom that makes its values:** Holds (outside my remit).
- **C, A God who is Love:** Holds with repair. CCC 206, "something like the refusal of a name," is a fine bridge to DDJ 1 and 25. The survey of who holds the line at a man's own request omits DDJ 74 (R3). "Against… the nameless Way… it is the one that wills each of us for her own sake" is fair as a contrast.
- **Evil, paragraph 1 ("On the other side"):** Holds. The concession that a Way that is not humane "owes no one an answer" is honest and accurate. Hell and Marian grace are stated accurately (CCC 1033–1037, 491–493).
- **Evil, First (privation):** Holds with repair. The Zhuangzi parenthesis is fair. The sentence that counts "wanted for her own sake" as fit-data begs the question against impersonal sources (R6).
- **Evil, Second (permission not a trade):** Holds. It is accurate on CCC 311–312, 324 and ST I q.19 a.9, and candid that the parasite claim is "a teaching, not something we see."
- **Evil, Third (God who suffers with):** Holds. A Daoist has no counterpart and offers none. That is a standoff.
- **Conclusion:** Holds. It is calibrated (CCC 31, 35–37, 847). The hillside newborn is the right test. My answer is that she was harmed as fully as the strong child beside her, and that a sage would not leave her there. Whether "wronged" adds anything is where we part.

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**Serious (local, easily repaired).**
1. **"Nor does God possess" contradicts CCC 2280 and the text's own W1(2).** The Daoist row denies God possesses her and cites CCC 2280. CCC 2280 says God "remains the sovereign Master of life" and that we are "stewards, not owners," and W1(2) rests the no-consent line on exactly that lordship. As written, the text claims a convergence with "rears but does not rule" (DDJ 10, 51) that its own decisive premise denies. That misplaces the real point at which my school and the book part. The distinction that would answer it (dominion that is non-acquisitive and exercised for her good) is sound, but it is not in the text. See R1.

**Minor.**
2. The evil section counts "a worth … wanted for her own sake" as a datum that impersonal sources fit less well. Against the Daoist, that scores the rival by a value only the theory supplies (R6).
3. The survey of who holds the line even at a man's own request ("A God who is Love"; W1(2)) omits the Daoist "master executioner" ground (DDJ 74, 29), which has the same shape as W1(2)'s "we could only take it." The text's distinctive gain is "a wrong to her" plus jurisdiction, not "not ours to take" (R3).
4. W2's parenthetical classification of who agrees with the one-Source witness omits the Daoist, who accepts one Source and denies that it is a law (R5).
5. The W1(1) weighting parenthesis names only the refined-feeling reader as one for whom the clue weighs little. The Daoist is a second such reader, on different grounds (R7).

**Misstatements of my school.**
- "Forgetting may heal, as Zhuangzi taught" (W1(1)) and "it heals guilt by forgetting, not by pardon" (C). Zhuangzi's forgetting (wang, zuowang) is a forgetting of the frame of praise and blame, of benevolence and ritual, and of self. It is not a technique for quieting remorse. Laozi's teaching on grievance is the creditor's restraint (DDJ 79) and answering resentment with virtue (DDJ 63). (R2.)
- The C row shows Daoist equal regard only through DDJ 27. It omits DDJ 49 (good to the good and to the not-good alike) and DDJ 67 (compassion as the first treasure), so the Way looks less able to reach the enemy and the newborn than it is. (R4.)
- "A Way that gives… gives without wanting" (W1(2)) fits Laozi (DDJ 51). On Guo Xiang's and Ziporyn's reading of Zhuangzi, there is no giver at all: things are "self-so" and transform alone (duhua), so "gives without wanting" still pictures the Way as a deficient God. This is a scholarly reading, not a fault; flag it (verify).
- The A-heading list has Zhuangzi deny only the debt to the dead. On Hansen's reading he also denies A1's standpoint-free "wrong" (Zhuangzi 2, Wang Ni: the beginnings of benevolence and righteousness are hopelessly tangled). Ivanhoe reads him as more ethically committed (verify). (R8.)
- Religious Daoism, Kohn's field, has confession of sins to the Three Officials and refinement-and-deliverance rites for named dead (verify). That is a personal-cosmic receiver the text does not consider. It sits outside the philosophical school I speak for, so I note it but do not press it.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects).**
- Whether being wanted and kept by Someone is a higher good than being let be in the transformations. Qiwulun says love-as-preference is where the Way is damaged; the text says love's first word is "it is good that you exist."
- Whether "wronged" adds anything to "harmed, and the harmer's virtue lost."
- Whether a human-specific inviolability is a truth, or a ranking that the levelling of all things corrects.
- Evil: the Daoist bears it more lightly and lets the dead go; the theist bears it more heavily and hopes to keep them. The text states both sides fairly.
- My own breaks: the ziran/wei distinction under Guo Xiang's reading; no "owed to her"; thin guidance in tragic dilemmas.

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1 (Serious; C, the Way):**
Old: "Nor does God possess: He willed her for her own sake and gave her life to her keeping (GS 24; CCC 2280)."
New: "Nor does God possess her for any gain of His own, for He lacks nothing: He willed her for her own sake and gave her life to her keeping (GS 24; CCC 2280). But He remains its Lord, and there the Way parts from Him most plainly: it rears and does not rule (*Daodejing* 10, 51)."

**R2 (C, the Way):**
Old: "But it heals guilt by forgetting, not by pardon (A2, A3), and lets the dead be transformed (W3)."
New: "But it meets a grievance by not keeping the ledger: the sage holds the creditor's tally and does not press for payment, and answers resentment with virtue (*Daodejing* 79, 63); better, Zhuangzi says, to forget both the praise and the blame and change along with the Way. So there is no one to ask pardon of in the end, and no one to give it (A2, A3), and it lets the dead be transformed (W3)."

And in W1(1):
Old: "or forgetting may heal, as Zhuangzi taught,"
New: "or a forgetting of the whole ledger of praise and blame may dissolve, as Zhuangzi taught,"

**R3 ("A God who is Love"):**
Old: "Kant and the Theravāda hold that line too, Kant from a worth beyond price, the Theravāda from the killer's heart and her chance to be freed, but neither from a worth she was given."
New: "Kant and the Theravāda hold that line too, Kant from a worth beyond price, the Theravāda from the killer's heart and her chance to be freed; and Laozi comes near it, for to kill in the Executioner's place is to usurp a course that is not ours (*Daodejing* 74). But none of them grounds it in a worth she was given, and Laozi's speaks of overstepping, not of a wrong done to her."

**R4 (C, the Way):**
Old: "a sage who abandons no one (A1, A4)"
New: "a sage who abandons no one, who is good to the good and to the not-good alike and holds all the people as his children, and whose first treasure is compassion (A1, A4; *Daodejing* 27, 49, 67)"

**R5 (W2, closing parenthesis, insert before "W1 and W3 give the reasons"):**
New sentence: "And the Daoist, for whom one nameless Source gives rise to all things and to the mind that knows them, agrees that there is one Source but denies that it is a law: on his account, benevolence and righteousness appear when the Way is lost (*Daodejing* 18, 25, 38). For him the question is the one in A1 and in C."

**R6 (Evil, First):**
Old: "a worth that is each one's own, wanted for her own sake and not to be spent for others"
New: "a worth that is each one's own and not to be spent for others, which this book holds is a worth wanted for her own sake"

**R7 (W1(1), weighting parenthesis):**
Old: "(For a reader who holds that our word \"owed\" voices refined feeling, it weighs little, since on his account no judgment of what is owed can tell him who is still there.)"
New: "(For a reader who holds that our word \"owed\" voices refined feeling, it weighs little, since on his account no judgment of what is owed can tell him who is still there; and little too for one who, with Laozi, holds that the ledger of what is owed is what appears when the Way is lost.)"

**R8 (A heading):**
Old: "Zhuangzi would deny that we still owe the one who is gone;"
New: "Zhuangzi would deny that any judgment of right and wrong stands free of the standpoint that makes it, and that we still owe the one who is gone;"

## 7. New insights; lines worth keeping

**Insights for the book.**
- **DDJ 79 for the chapter on mercy.** The sage who holds the creditor's tally and does not exact is a non-Christian witness to the creditor side of forgiveness. It sits well beside Matthew 18:27. The Christian difference is that the debt is real before it is released.
- **Zhuangzi 5, "not adding to life."** Do not hasten death, and do not force life on with likes and dislikes. This converges, from a wholly different ground, with CCC 2278–2279 (no aggressive overtreatment, no aiming at death). It is a useful witness that the Church's line on end-of-life care is not merely sectarian.
- **The cripples of Zhuangzi 5, Shu the hunchback and the useless tree.** These are the strongest classical non-theist witnesses that worth does not follow use or ability. They would strengthen A4's "whatever he can or cannot do," and they cut against Seneca's practice.
- **Danfu leaving Bin (Zhuangzi 28; Mencius 1B15).** A ruler refuses to spend his people for the land. It pairs with Mencius 2A2 under A6.
- **DDJ 29, the world as a "sacred vessel" that cannot be grasped.** A bridge to Chapter 3 (love is sacred) that a Daoist reader would recognise.
- **The real line of parting.** With Daoism, the decisive difference is not personal versus impersonal in general. It is "rears and rules" versus "rears and does not rule." The text's strongest and most contested claim (life is not hers to dispose of) rests on God's lordship, so it should own that openly (R1).

**Lines worth keeping.**
- "Heaven and Earth give life to things; God wanted *you*."
- "We part over whether the Giver knows her, and whether she is kept or let go."
- "Zhuangzi's yes is narrower: it is said to the changes of life and death, not to the tyrant's cruelty, which his Way condemns; but when she dies, it lets her go."
- "The rebel can take her side; he cannot keep her."
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."
- "A Way that is not humane gives life and lets each thing go, and owes no one an answer." (an honest concession; keep it)
- "His name is at once a name revealed and something like the refusal of a name" (CCC 206). Set beside the nameless Way, it is the text's best gesture toward my school.

## 8. Score

**8 / 10.** The text treats Laozi and Zhuangzi accurately on almost every point. It concedes the evil question where we really are stronger and frames our deepest disagreement as a parting rather than a win. It loses ground only on one internal inconsistency ("Nor does God possess" against CCC 2280) and on a few places where it undersells our resources (DDJ 49, 67, 74, 79). With R1–R8 it would be about 8.5–9.

**Honest ceiling for any theistic case against my school: about 9.** The final question, whether the highest good is to be wanted and kept by Someone or to be let be in the transformations, is a difference over ultimate value that argument can bring to parity and make vivid but cannot compel. This text already reaches that parity on nearly every line.
