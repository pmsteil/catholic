# Blind review: the evolutionary debunker / error theorist

## 1. School and voice

I argue for the school that holds two things. First, the content of our moral beliefs, and the feeling that they bind us whatever we want, is fully explained by natural selection, by cultural evolution and by reasoning that holds evolved attitudes to consistency. None of that explanation needs the beliefs to be true (Sharon Street, "A Darwinian Dilemma for Realist Theories of Value," *Phil. Studies* 2006; Richard Joyce, *The Evolution of Morality*, 2006; Michael Ruse, *Taking Darwin Seriously*, 1986). Second, moral judgments claim a categorical authority over us that nothing in the world has, so the positive ones are systematically false (J. L. Mackie, *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong*, 1977; Jonas Olson, *Moral Error Theory*, 2014). The cognitive science of religion (CSR) adds a matching account of why minds like ours sense watchers, keep speaking to the dead and long to be known (Justin Barrett 2004; Jesse Bering 2011; Ara Norenzayan, *Big Gods*, 2013). Street herself is a constructivist and not an error theorist. I use her dilemma against realism and the error theory as my own positive view. I do not treat debunking as a genetic fallacy. It is an *undercutting* defeater: it removes the support for a belief without showing the belief false.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** No one is *owed* anything in the categorical sense the book means. Following Mackie, justice is a human invention: a set of rules that answers to the circumstances of justice (limited sympathy, rough equality of vulnerability, moderate scarcity), so that strangers can cooperate. What someone is "owed" is what those rules, which we all have strong hypothetical reasons to uphold, assign to him. → **Holds** as a coherent view. It **borrows** the word "owed" only in a deflated, institutional sense, and I say so openly. It **breaks** against A1 and A3 as the text states them, because I deny exactly what they assert.

**2. A hard dilemma.** Take the Manchester conjoined twins, *Re A* (2000). If they are separated, Mary dies and Jodie very probably lives. If they are not, both die within months, and the parents refused consent. I find no fact of the matter. I would decide from the concerns we share and that survive reflection: that more life is better than less; that killing must not become a general tool (the hospital acts once, under a court's eye, and sets no policy of spending the weak); that parents have standing; that the decision must be one we could defend publicly to anyone affected. Here those concerns favor separation, with the parents heard and overruled only by a public process. → **Holds** as a procedure, and it uses no definition from the book: honesty is a commitment I hold, fairness is a convention, and compassion is a sentiment. It **breaks** if you ask for the *right* answer. I say there is only a better or worse answer by our refined lights, and Catholic moralists who hold the opposite view are not making a factual error that I can expose.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** As an objective property, I **deny** it. As a commitment, I endorse it and would defend it hard. Graded-worth regimes have been catastrophic, and a bright line is easier to enforce and harder to abuse than any sliding scale. That defense is instrumental and depends on our attitudes. If a whole people stopped caring about the infant no one holds, nothing would make that people *wrong* in the categorical sense. I would oppose them with everything I have, but not from a fact. → **Breaks** against A4 as stated, and I pay that openly. It is **coherent** and **borrows** nothing.

**4. Ultimate human flourishing.** Nothing is ultimately good for humans in the sense of an end set by nature or by a Maker. What is good for us, in Street's sense, is what follows from the standpoint of beings who value as we do: love and friendship, understanding, pleasure, absorbing work, the well-being of those we care about, and a self-image we can live with. My guiding principles are honesty with myself (for anyone who cares about truth, which includes everyone in this debate), compassion, and fair cooperation. → **Holds** as an account of proximate goods. It **breaks** at "ultimately", and I accept that this word has no referent.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1: the text's own epistemic rule.**
*Attack.* The A heading makes a belief trustworthy "until something more certain shows it false, or shows that what produced the belief had nothing to do with its truth." I meet the second condition. The best explanation of why humans believe that harming kin is wrong, that cheats must be punished and that help must be returned is the adaptive one. That explanation would produce exactly the same beliefs whether or not there were any stance-independent moral facts (Street's adaptive-link account).
*Defense.* The text's standard is the correct one, and it grants the form of the debunking argument. However, to show that the producing process had *nothing* to do with truth, the debunker must rule out a "third factor." Survival may have taught us to attend to pain, trust and betrayal *because* those are the very features that make acts wrong (David Enoch 2011; Erik Wielenberg, *Ethics* 2010; the text gives this answer in W2). For the theist there is also a deeper third factor. God governs through secondary causes, and evolution is one of them (CCC 302, 306–308). He orders those causes toward rational creatures who share in the eternal law (ST I-II q.91 a.2), so the fit between the law and the mind that reads it is not luck.
*Rejoinder.* Both third-factor stories are coherent. Neither explains why we hold *these* beliefs any better than the adaptive story does. Their only job is to save the beliefs' truth. So they can *defend* realism or theism if one already holds it, but they cannot turn moral knowledge into *evidence* for God (Guy Kahane, *Noûs* 2011, makes this kind of point about debunking arguments; verify). Note also that the text's standard asks me to show the process had "nothing to do with" truth. If God exists, I can never show that without first assuming God does not exist. The fair standard is whether the *best* account of the belief's origin would have produced it whether or not it was true.
**Held with repair.** The defense reaches parity. The heading's wording should be made neutral (Repair R1).

**Exchange 2: the reach of conscience (W2).**
*Attack.* We judge that gratuitous cruelty would be wrong "for any mind in any world." That reach adds nothing. It is the same evolved attitude, carried outward by the same consistency reasoning that generalizes everything else. Darwin's hive-bee people (*Descent of Man*, ch. 4) would feel the same reach about killing their brothers.
*Defense.* The text already concedes the point: "If he is right, the reach is no witness against him." It uses reach only against the godless realist, who already accepts moral knowledge and so owes an account of it.
**Held.** The text does not overclaim, and it puts the argument where it belongs.

**Exchange 3: companions in guilt (A7).**
*Attack.* W2 says I rely "on the ought of evidence in the very argument" I make, and that survival "did not select for that either." Both claims fail. (a) My argument needs no *categorical* epistemic ought. It needs two things: a descriptive claim (the process that produces moral beliefs is insensitive to moral truth), and an epistemic norm that is either built into what belief is (belief aims at truth) or holds for anyone who wants the truth. Olson argues that epistemic reasons reduce to non-categorical ones (*Moral Error Theory*, ch. 8; verify). Bart Streumer (*Unbelievable Errors*, 2017) goes further: error theory about *all* normativity may be true even though no one can believe it. Either way, the argument's soundness does not depend on anyone's being obliged to accept it. (b) Survival and social life did select for evidence-responsive belief and for public norms of honesty. On one leading account, human reasoning evolved for giving and judging reasons in argument (Hugo Mercier and Dan Sperber, *The Enigma of Reason*, 2017). The *felt* categorical force of the epistemic ought can be explained in the same way as the moral one.
*Defense.* Terence Cuneo (*The Normative Web*, 2007) argues that epistemic reasons are as categorical as moral ones. A man has reason to believe what the evidence shows even when he does not care. The aim built into belief explains why beliefs are judged by truth, but not why anyone ought to keep inquiring when he fears the result. The text makes this exact point in the C row. A restricted error theorist like Mackie or Joyce, who drops the moral ought but keeps the epistemic one, owes a principled reason for the asymmetry. A global error theorist has to say that no one ought to believe his own conclusion. In the Catholic frame, the duty to seek the truth binds every person because the intellect is ordered to truth by its nature (CCC 2104; *Veritatis Splendor* 34).
*Rejoinder.* Against Mackie and Joyce, the asymmetry charge has force. That is a real cost to the restricted form of my school, and I record it. Against Olson and Streumer, it shows only that we bite a second bullet. The bullet is the same kind as A1 (a categorical seeming we deny), so it adds no new type of cost. Moreover, everyone actually engaged in this inquiry does care about truth, so the hypothetical force of my argument reaches every reader it needs to reach.
**Held with repair.** This is an honest standoff at parity. The text's claim that the debunker *relies on* the categorical ought overstates it (Repair R2).

**Exchange 4: "the belief survival least predicts" (A4, W2, conclusion).** This is the decisive exchange.
*Attack.* W2 rests the case against me on A7 and on "A4, the belief survival least predicts." A4 says this is the belief "an account of us as built only for survival least predicts." No one in my school holds that account. Joyce, Street, Kitcher (*The Ethical Project*, 2011) and Henrich (*The WEIRDest People in the World*, 2020) all include cultural evolution and reflective consistency. The full account makes a prediction about regard for every human, including the unwanted, disabled newborn. It predicts that this regard would be absent or weak for most of human history, would arise late, would travel with particular universalist ideologies, would fail where interest pulls, and would stay contested at the edges. The text's own evidence matches that prediction point for point: Seneca on drowning weak newborns, Christians' slowness about slavery, Singer and McMahan today, and "history shows how easily it is lost." Infanticide followed the pattern parental-investment theory predicts across many societies (Sarah Blaffer Hrdy, *Mother Nature*, 1999; Martin Daly and Margo Wilson, *Homicide*, 1988). Hunter-gatherers enforce fierce egalitarianism against would-be dominants (Christopher Boehm, *Hierarchy in the Forest*, 1999), and consistency reasoning extends it. A belief that instinct alone does not predict is not, for that reason, evidence of truth.
*Defense.* The disabled newborn who will never contribute adds nothing to group cooperation, so cultural group selection predicts care for productive members, not for her. Consistency reasoning has to *choose a ground*, "human" rather than "mine" or "useful," and evolution does not fix that choice. Theism also predicts the history, as the text says: equal worth is seen by reason, darkened by sin, and seen more fully in the light of the Gospel (DI 10–13; CCC 37; ST I-II q.94 a.6).
*Rejoinder.* A bright-line rule of equal worth has its own adaptive logic. It works as insurance (anyone may become the weak one) and as a coordination point (graded worth invites abuse by the powerful), so the useless member is protected *by the rule* even though she is not the rule's reason. More to the point, the defense concedes that both hypotheses predict the observed history. Then A4 gives no evidence *against* me in the likelihood sense. Its whole remaining force is the reader's direct confidence that the infant was wronged. That confidence is largely the inheritance of the very tradition being argued for, and the text admits that equal worth spread where Christianity spread. I do not say this makes the belief false. That would be a genealogy I would not accept used against me, and I keep the fairness rule. I say only that it cannot count twice.
**Failed as written.** The claim "least predicts" holds only against a theory of instinct alone, which no one in my school defends. **Held with repair** if A4 and W2 are restated so that the weight sits openly on the reader's judgment, where the conclusion already places it (Repairs R3, R4).

**Exchange 5: the Moorean conclusion.**
*Attack.* The conclusion asks which you are surer of: that the abandoned infant was wronged, or any argument that she was not. But your degree of certainty is itself an output of the instrument under question. You cannot vouch for a gauge by reading the gauge.
*Defense.* Every argument ends in seemings, the debunker's included. His empirical premises, that particular moral contents are adaptations, are far less secure than "she was wronged." Much of evolutionary moral psychology is contested reconstruction (Philip Kitcher and William FitzPatrick are among its critics; verify). Perceptual seemings are also evolved, and we trust them on the whole. Taken globally, debunking proves too much. The text applies its own rule evenly: a seeming stands *until* it is shown to come from a process unrelated to its truth.
*Rejoinder.* My premise is weaker than the defense says. I need only that the best causal history of the belief, genetic and cultural together, nowhere requires its truth. Perception differs: it has independent checks in prediction, instruments, and convergence across species and methods. Moral seemings have none, because their "convergence" is the convergence of one evolved attitude. Still, I grant that a reader who is more certain the infant was wronged than he is of my premise is not irrational.
**Held.** This is an honest standoff, and the text presents it as one ("there the case ends in a choice, not a proof").

**Exchange 6: guilt toward the dead and the longing to be known (W1(1), W3).**
*Attack.* CSR explains both: attachment, the continuing-bonds mechanisms of grief, agency detection, and a mind that pictures being watched. The text admits the likelihoods are equal ("a mind made for living bonds would ask whether or not she hears"). If P(E | God) ≈ P(E | no God), then E is not evidence, and a "clue of fit" is not a category confirmation theory recognizes.
*Defense.* The text agrees that the feeling is not evidence. The weight comes from a conceptual premise: a debt *truly owed* to someone is one she must be able to receive. That is analysis of the concept, not an inference from the feeling, and the text scales the weight explicitly ("it weighs as much as the reader finds…").
*Rejoinder.* Then the error theorist *accepts* the conceptual premise and reasons the other way. In its full sense, "owed to her" presupposes someone there to receive it. The world provides no such person, so the concept is in error. That is the Anscombe point, and Mackie's. Here the text and I share a premise and part at the last step.
**Held.** The text already grades this as a clue that weighs little for a non-realist reader. It should name the error theorist's modus tollens explicitly (Repair R6).

**Exchange 7: queerness moved, not removed.**
*Attack.* Mackie's argument from queerness says that objective prescriptivity, a fact that by its nature gives every agent a reason, is unlike anything else we know. Putting it in God's nature ("goodness is His nature") moves the strangeness; it does not dissolve it.
*Defense.* The text does not claim theism removes the mystery: "it is one mystery, and a mystery that explains." On the Thomist view, the good is being itself under the aspect of what is desirable (ST I q.5 a.1). Prescriptivity is then not a strange extra property attached to neutral facts. It is what being is to a will, and it finds its source in Him who is subsistent being and the final end of all things (ST I q.44 a.4).
*Rejoinder.* That is the queer property, now given a theological home. But I record something that favors the text's structure. In *The Miracle of Theism* (1982, ch. 6), Mackie himself granted, as I recall, that *if* objective prescriptive values existed, they would make a god's existence more probable. His own exit was to deny the values (paraphrase; verify).
**Held.** The dialectic between this text and my school is exactly what the text says it is: modus ponens against modus tollens.

**Exchange 8: the distribution of evil and of conscience.**
*Attack.* Naturalism predicts *without help* that pain falls where survival puts it and that conscience is warmest toward kin and those who can repay. Theism needs further hypotheses (sin, the Fall, a creation "on its way"). In Bayesian terms, these data favor me.
*Defense.* The text concedes both points in so many words and puts them "on the other side of the scale." The further hypotheses are long-standing doctrine, not inventions for this argument (CCC 400, 405, 310).
*Rejoinder.* Doctrine that is independent of this argument is still unsupported *by reason alone*, which is the court the case appeals to.
**Held.** The text pays this cost honestly. It is weight, not a defect.

**Exchange 9: the means/side-effect alarm (A6).**
*Attack.* A6 answers a personal-force version of Joshua Greene's debunking ("no one is pushed in the riot"). Greene's later hypothesis is that an automatic alarm responds to harm *planned as a means* and is blind to harm foreseen as a side effect (*Moral Tribes*, 2013, the "modular myopia" proposal; verify). That covers the riot and the hidden experiment. It also predicts that the line weakens with scale and distance, which is what A6 reports: many who would never hang the innocent man would bomb a city.
*Defense.* "A history settles neither." The consequentialist regard for the many has a genealogy too, and the line is offered as this book's own claim, not a shared premise.
*Rejoinder.* Against an error theorist who debunks both sides, the parity reply works. The text should still answer the current version of Greene's view, not the superseded one.
**Held with repair** (Repair R5).

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**Serious.**

1. **"The belief survival least predicts" (A4; reused in W2 as one of the two legs of the case against the debunker).** The claim holds against a theory of instinct alone, which no evolutionary debunker holds. Against the actual school (gene–culture coevolution plus consistency reasoning), the history the text itself cites is predicted: the regard comes late, is fragile, depends on culture, and fails at the margins. Theism predicts the same history with the help of sin and revelation. So A4 has no likelihood advantage over my account, and its remaining weight is the reader's direct confidence. That is a legitimate weight, and the conclusion already uses it correctly. But in W2, A4 is presented as a datum my account "least predicts," and that misstates my school at the one place the case says it rests its argument against me. The premises of this objection (the text's own history, the stated positions of Joyce, Street and Kitcher) are at least as plausible as the claim attacked. The text cannot answer it at parity *as written*. It can answer it at parity once the weight is relocated, so the fix is cheap (R3, R4).

**Minor.**

- W2's "he relies on the ought of evidence in the very argument he makes, and survival did not select for that either" overstates. The debunking argument needs only a non-categorical epistemic norm, and evolved reasoning plus social honesty norms explain the felt categorical force of the epistemic ought. The real result is a cost to the *restricted* error theorist and a parity standoff with the global one (R2).
- The A heading's defeater standard, "had nothing to do with its truth," sets a burden the debunker can meet only by first assuming atheism (R1).
- In A6, the reply to Greene answers the personal-force version, not the means/side-effect version (R5).
- In W1(1), the parenthetical names only the expressivist reader. The error theorist, who shares the text's conceptual premise and draws the opposite conclusion, should be named (R6).

**Misstatements of my school.**

- "Mackie denies that any of them is objectively true" (A heading). Mackie affirms the is/ought gap in A5. He targets categorical moral prescriptivity, not every sentence in A (R7).
- "An account of us as built only for survival" (A4). No debunker holds this; see Serious 1.
- Street's view is filed under "Feeling, refined," but it is constructivism, not expressivism. Its distinctive cost, that an ideally coherent agent with other starting values may have reason to do what we call cruel, is not stated (R8).
- The error-theory row omits that error theorists do not recommend ceasing to care: Mackie's inventing, Joyce's fictionalism, Olson's conservationism. Without that, a reader may think the error theorist's practice is worse than it is (R8).

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects).**

- **Companions in guilt.** Is the epistemic ought categorical (Cuneo) or reducible (Olson), or does error theory about it simply have to be lived with (Streumer)?
- **Moorean confidence versus the debunker's premise.** The reader decides which he is surer of. The text says so.
- **Third-factor explanations.** These are available to the realist and the theist alike. They defend moral knowledge for one who already holds it, but they do not turn it into evidence for God.
- **Queerness.** Theism gives objective prescriptivity a home; it does not remove its strangeness. Mackie, on my reading, granted the conditional the text relies on (verify).
- **The Anscombe premise.** The text and I agree that full moral obligation needs a Lawgiver. We part over modus ponens or modus tollens. That is the deepest truth about this dispute, and the text sees it.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact old → new)

**R1 (A heading).**
Old: "or shows that what produced the belief had nothing to do with its truth."
New: "or shows that the best account of what produced the belief would have produced it whether or not it was true."

**R2 (W2).**
Old: "Against him the case rests on A7, for he relies on the ought of evidence in the very argument he makes, and survival did not select for that either; and on A4, the belief survival least predicts (see the conclusion)."
New: "Against him the case rests on two things, and neither settles the matter. First, A7: he asks us to give up beliefs because the evidence requires it. If that requirement is only the aim built into believing, or advice for those who want the truth, he can still make his case, but he must say that no one who fears where the evidence leads ought to follow it. The ablest error theorists accept that cost, or argue that it is no cost; those who keep the ought of evidence while denying the ought of morals must say why the one survives and the other does not. Second, A4: a regard for every human being that our instincts least predict, which he must explain by culture and consistency, and which the reader may be surer of than of any premise in the debunker's argument (see the conclusion)."

**R3 (A4).**
Old: "So this is the belief that an account of us as built only for survival least predicts."
New: "So this is the belief our instincts least predict. The evolutionary debunker does not rest on instinct alone: he adds culture, and the reasoning that holds us to consistency, and these can carry a regard for our own out to strangers. What that account predicts is a regard that comes late, travels with particular cultures, and gives way where interest pulls; and that is much of what history shows. So the belief's history does not decide between us. What remains is the question the conclusion asks: which are we surer of, that the infant no one held was wronged, or the argument that says we cannot know it?"

**R4 (W2, in the debunker sentence before R2's text).** No change is needed if R2 is adopted. If R2 is not adopted, at minimum:
Old: "and on A4, the belief survival least predicts (see the conclusion)."
New: "and on A4, a belief our instincts least predict, whose weight against him lies in the reader's own certainty (see the conclusion)."

**R5 (A6).**
Old: "Some say this certainty is only the flinch of a mind built to recoil from violence up close; but no one is pushed in the riot or in the hidden experiment, and the line holds there too;"
New: "Some say this certainty is only the flinch of a mind built to recoil from violence up close; but no one is pushed in the riot or in the hidden experiment, and the line holds there too. Others say the alarm sounds whenever harm is planned as a means, and is silent when harm comes as a side effect; that would explain the line in all three cases, and even why it weakens as numbers grow and distance increases. But it would equally explain the line if the line were true: whether the difference between spending a person and foreseeing a harm is real is the question, not its answer;"

**R6 (W1(1)).**
Old: "(For a reader who holds that our word \"owed\" voices refined feeling, it weighs little, since on his account no judgment of what is owed can tell him who is still there.)"
New: "(For a reader who holds that our word \"owed\" voices refined feeling, it weighs little, since on his account no judgment of what is owed can tell him who is still there. For the error theorist it weighs nothing, and here he and this book share a premise: he too holds that a debt owed to her, in the full sense, needs someone there to receive it. He concludes that the word is in error; this book, that she is there.)"

**R7 (A heading).**
Old: "Mackie denies that any of them is objectively true;"
New: "Mackie denies that any moral claim among them is objectively true, since each claims an authority over us that, he holds, nothing in the world could have;"

**R8 (C, "Nothing really binds").**
Old: "(error theory, often joined to an evolutionary account of conscience and of religion; others use the same evolutionary argument to show that our values bind us only from within, a view weighed under the next heading) is consistent and borrows nothing."
New: "(error theory, often joined to an evolutionary account of conscience and of religion; others, such as Sharon Street, use the same evolutionary argument to show that our values bind us only from within, so that a fully consistent person who began from other values could have reason to do what we call cruel, a view weighed with the next heading) is consistent and borrows nothing. Nor does it tell us to stop caring: Mackie urged that we go on making morality as a human device, and others keep moral speech as a useful fiction or simply go on using it."

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights.**

- **The error theorist is the case's nearest relative, not its farthest rival.** He accepts Anscombe's diagnosis that "obligation," in its full sense, presupposes a Lawgiver. He accepts the conceptual claim in W1(1) that a debt truly owed needs a receiver, and he accepts that invented values cannot bind. Mackie himself, by my reading of *The Miracle of Theism*, granted that objective values would favor theism (verify). The book can say outright that against this school the argument is a single fork: *either* the infant was really wronged and a Lawgiver is the best home for that fact, *or* she was not. That is stronger and more honest than any claim of evidential advantage, and the conclusion is already almost there.
- **Separate defending moral knowledge from using it as evidence.** Providence working through secondary causes (CCC 302–308) is a good defeater-defeater for the believer facing debunking. It cannot also serve as evidence for God, because it presupposes the knowledge at issue. W2 nearly keeps this line, and a sentence would make it explicit.
- **The history of equal dignity is a shared datum, not a weapon.** Both accounts predict a late, fragile, contested history. The book's real asset is not that history but the reader's present certainty about the infant on the hillside, and the conclusion should be the only place that certainty is spent.
- **The companions-in-guilt argument divides my school.** It bites hard against Mackie- and Joyce-style restricted error theory, which must explain why epistemic categoricity survives and moral categoricity does not. It is only a parity cost for global error theorists (Olson, Streumer). The book could say so in one clause and gain precision.

## 8. Score

**7 / 10.** Against my school the text does not win, and it does not claim to. It concedes the undercutting form of debunking, the equal likelihoods for guilt and longing, and naturalism's better fit to the distribution of pain and conscience. It ends in an honestly labeled choice. Its one real slip is presenting equal dignity as the belief "survival least predicts," and fixing that is cheap.

**Honest ceiling for any theistic case against this school: 8 / 10.** No theistic argument can force a consistent error theorist past a stalemate. The most it can do is price his costs fully (A1, A4, the categorical ought of evidence) and show that the reader's Moorean certainty is rational. With R1–R8, this text reaches that ceiling.
