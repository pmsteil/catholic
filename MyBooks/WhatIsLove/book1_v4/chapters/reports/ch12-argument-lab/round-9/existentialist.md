# Blind review: the atheist existentialist school

## 1. School and voice

I speak for Sartre, Beauvoir and Camus together, and say so where they part. Sartre: if there is no God to conceive us, existence comes before essence; there is no human nature, only a universal human condition (being free, in a world, among others, mortal), and values come into the world through freedom, which is total and has no degrees. Beauvoir (*The Ethics of Ambiguity*): freedom is the source of all value; to will oneself free is also to will others free; oppression is cutting another off from their open future; there is no ready-made ethics, and violence, when it cannot be avoided, is a failure to be owned, never justified by an abstract future (verify). Camus (*The Myth of Sisyphus*, *The Rebel*, *The Plague*, *The Just Assassins*): the absurd is the gap between our demand for meaning and the world's silence; the leap to God is "philosophical suicide"; revolt says No, and in that No finds a limit and a value common to all ("I rebel, therefore we exist," verify), and with it solidarity without God or hope. I attack the starting points as well as the steps, and I say plainly where my school pays.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** Beauvoir: every person is a freedom, a reaching beyond what is given toward an open future. Justice is what each freedom is owed so that it can go on reaching: not to be reduced to a thing, a tool or an obstacle. Oppression is the primary injustice. Camus: in revolt the slave who says "this far and no farther" affirms a value that is not his alone. **Verdict: holds in part, borrows at the hinge.** The step from "my freedom needs others' freedom to have meaning" to "I *ought* to will their freedom even when I could gain by enslaving them" is the weak point, as Beauvoir's critics have long said. Sartre's "in choosing for myself I choose for all" borrows Kant's universal form without Kant's ground. Camus gets the step only by admitting a common human nature, which is the text's ground, not ours.

**2. A hard dilemma.** Camus's Kaliayev (*The Just Assassins*) is ready to throw the bomb at the Grand Duke's carriage, sees two children inside, and does not throw. Camus's principle is the limit that revolt finds in itself: a revolt that kills the innocent denies the common value it rose up to defend. If one kills at all, one pays with one's own life. Sartre's student, torn between his mother and the Free French, gets the reply that no principle decides, so he must choose and own the choice. **Verdict: Camus holds, but he leans on lucidity (truth), the refusal of oppression (justice), measure (a thin mercy) and Rieux's plain decency (self-gift without God).** These are thinner versions of the book's four, not inventions of our own. Sartre's "no principle decides" is honest but breaks as guidance. And Sartre's preface to Fanon, which let the line bend for liberation, shows what happens when the limit is dropped.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** Freedom has no degrees: the genius and the severely disabled person who is conscious are equally free and equally a reaching beyond themselves. Beauvoir counts the child a freedom not yet taken up, so the newborn left on the hillside is wronged as fully as the grown man. The enemy is a freedom too, and Camus opposed the death penalty (*Reflections on the Guillotine*). **Verdict: holds for every actual or nascent freedom, and breaks at the edge.** For the one who never will be, or never again will be, conscious, we owe honor to the person she was or care for the body she is, but we cannot say she has a worth equal to yours "prior to anyone's recognition." Dignity on our account is also conditional, because it rests on being a freedom. It is not unconditional in the book's sense.

**4. Ending point for human flourishing.** There is no ultimate good, because there is no end we are *for*. The good is freedom willing itself and others' freedom, without end. Camus's version is lucidity, revolt, freedom and passion, and solidarity with those who suffer: "one must imagine Sisyphus happy" (verify). **Verdict: coherent as a denial, so it borrows nothing here, but it pays.** It has no answer for the dead, for the forgotten, or for the longing to be received whole. We call that longing nostalgia, and we refuse it on purpose.

## 3. Debate highlights

**E1. Starting points: moral "seemings" are the spirit of seriousness.**
*Attack.* A's rule, to trust what still seems true after honest reflection, is what Sartre and Beauvoir call the spirit of seriousness. It treats values as if they were properties of things, waiting to be found, and it does so to escape the anguish of being their source.
*Reply.* That is a diagnosis of motive, a genealogy, and by the fairness rule it cuts both ways: anguish is also a mood, and it proves nothing either. Phenomenology is your own method. Sartre and Beauvoir condemned the torture in Algeria whatever France approved, and they did not condemn it as a project they happened to have. They condemned it as wrong for anyone. The rule in A asks only that a seeming stand until something more certain defeats it.
*Rejoinder.* We condemned it as a commitment made for all, and it was no less absolute for being chosen. But I must be honest: Sartre also granted that if men chose Fascism tomorrow and others let them, Fascism would then be "the truth of man" (*Existentialism Is a Humanism*, verify). That gives up A1's "wrong even if every culture approved."
**Held.** The rule in A stands, and my school pays at A1. Our practice spoke more truly than our theory.

**E2. A7 against authenticity.**
*Attack.* "We are bound to the truth" is a law; we have only lucidity, which is a way of existing, not a rule.
*Reply.* Your whole ethics rests on calling bad faith a lie to oneself and an error others may judge. Camus's absurd man keeps one value, refusing to cheat. That is A7, unchosen and binding on every consciousness, and you rely on it in every argument you make against the believer.
*Rejoinder.* Lucidity follows from what consciousness is: it is transparent to itself, so self-deception is a strain within it. But "one *ought* not to flee" is not a description of that strain. I concede that our "ought" of lucidity is not something any freedom made. What I do not concede is that A7 leads to a Mind. The text agrees: in the Power and rank row, it says the dilemma "does not settle" that question.
**Held.** A7 binds my school, and the text claims no more from it than it can carry.

**E3. "A will that invents values can command, but it cannot bind" (W1 closing; the Freedom row, "a law no freedom chose").**
*Attack.* By the text's own account God did not choose that cruelty is evil; the law is His nature. So an unchosen law is just as much your ground as ours. Being unchosen cannot be what tells against us.
*Reply.* Agreed, and the W1 frame already concedes that binding alone "does not decide between the accounts." The point against your school is internal. You hold that every value comes through freedom, and yet Beauvoir needs a requirement that binds every freedom to will others' freedom, which no freedom made. That contradicts your thesis, not ours.
*Rejoinder.* That is fair. But the Freedom row's wording ("a law no freedom chose (W2)") makes it sound as if being unchosen were the defect, and it points to W2, which is about mind and law having one Source, not about this.
**Held with repair** (R2).

**E4. W3: Sartre's analysis covers the longing "to be given."**
*Attack.* The text says Sartre's verdict, that we are a lack longing to be God, is true only of the longing that grasps. But Sartre analyzed the wish to be loved directly (*Being and Nothingness*, the chapter on concrete relations with others, verify). To want to be loved is to want another freedom to choose me as its absolute end and so to found my being, to justify my contingency. "Tell me it is good that I exist" is that wish in its receiving form. It is the same project, and it fails for the same reason.
*Reply.* Sartre's analysis assumes that every Other is a rival freedom beside me, whose look turns me into an object, so that to be founded by the Other is to be captured. The Creator is not a rival beside us. He gives being. To be founded by Him is not a contradiction one must try to make into a self; it is simply what a creature is (ST I q.45; the non-competitive view of transcendence). The longing fails on Sartre's terms only if there is no such Giver, so his verdict assumes what is in question.
*Rejoinder.* Then each side assumes its own answer, and the longing is evidence for neither. The text already says it is "not evidence," so the argument survives. But the text misstates Sartre by implying he dealt only with grasping.
**Held with repair** (R1).

**E5. A God-given end makes humans artifacts (W1(2), "measured against one end that is God Himself").**
*Attack.* This is Sartre's paper-knife: an object made for a purpose by a maker whose idea of it came first. Grounding dignity in being *made for* an end gives a human being the dignity of a well-designed tool. Our dignity is that we are for nothing, and that is exactly why no one may use us.
*Reply.* The text blocks the artifact reading in so many words: the human person is "the only creature on earth God willed for itself" (GS 24), "not for the species, not for the total, not for us." The end is communion, a gift she shares in, not a job she performs. And "for nothing" cannot reach the one who will never be conscious, while an equal call that "does not wait on what anyone can do" does.
*Rejoinder.* I concede the artifact charge fails against GS 24. I hold that at the edge I owe her honor, not equal worth. The text is right that this is where my school pays, though whether it is a cost depends on whether A4 is true at that edge, which is the question.
**Held.** The disagreement at the edge is a standoff at parity.

**E6. Camus and Ivan: the more absolute A1 and A4 are, the stronger the case against God.**
*Attack.* The conclusion asks the reader to trust his surest moral seeming, that the child on the hillside was wronged, over any theory. Rieux trusts the same kind of seeming: a Creator who could have spared this child, and did not, is not to be loved. The text answers that "not seeing God's reasons is weak evidence that He has none." That is skeptical theism: it discounts a moral seeming by appealing to our limits, the very move the text will not let the debunker make against A4 in W2.
*Reply.* The two moves differ in scope. The debunker says our moral seemings track nothing anywhere. The text says we know enough of goodness to know what a good God would value, "which is why this weight is real," and that what we lack is a view of how each particular evil is answered in a story the book holds is not finished. It also keeps Ivan's best point inside its own case: no one pardons in her place, not even God (W1(1), and the Kant row's rule of clemency); the order of the whole cannot by itself justify what was done to her; "God never spends anyone as a mere means." It calls hell the heaviest weight on the scale and lets the reader turn the question back on it.
*Rejoinder.* I accept that the two scopes differ and that the text gives our objection full weight. The weighing itself is where we part.
**Held**, as an honest standoff the text itself states.

**E7. Camus's charge of the leap: the converging case is a disguised leap.**
*Attack.* Collecting "clues of fit" and then speaking of certainty is the leap Camus diagnosed in Kierkegaard and Jaspers.
*Reply.* The text names the leap as faith and keeps it apart. Reason "can predispose to faith... but does not replace it"; W1(1) and W3 are "a clue, not a proof"; W2 "weighs, it does not settle"; evil "weighs... heavily." It refuses comfort as a reason to believe (A7).
*Rejoinder.* All true of the body of the text. But the conclusion's "not because certainty is out of reach" reads as if this case could carry a reader to certainty, which is more than its own ledger supports. The doctrine is accurate: certainty is possible by reason (Dei Filius; CCC 36). The phrasing invites a reader to rest it on this argument.
**Held with repair** (R6; Minor).

**E8. Sartre's charge that God is a contradiction (the last C row).**
*Attack.* "A God who is Love... the One whose very being is to be": a being that is conscious and also wholly what it is, the in-itself-for-itself, is on Sartre's ontology a contradiction. Consciousness is a gap, never at one with itself. So the stopping point is not merely unproved; it is impossible.
*Reply.* Sartre's argument takes the structure of human consciousness and makes it the structure of all knowing. Aquinas's God is neither a thing nor a for-itself. He is pure act, and His knowing is identical with His being (ST I q.14 a.4). Sartre's premise assumes what it is meant to prove.
*Rejoinder.* Each side then extends its own account of consciousness, so this is a standoff. The text never names the charge, though Sartre is its chief representative of my school.
**Held** (standoff); optional repair R5.

**E9. The debt to the dead: is the wish for her pardon a wish for absolution (W1(1))?**
*Attack.* For Sartre the dead are handed over to the living, who carry their meaning. We walk the second road with open eyes. To want to be forgiven *by her* is to want one's past settled by another's verdict, which is a flight from bearing what cannot be forgiven.
*Reply.* The Catholic answer in W1(1) does not let anyone off. Absolution "does not remedy all the disorders" (CCC 1459), repair is still owed, and God does not give her pardon in her place. Guilt that asks her pardon faces her; it does not flee. And the text counts this as a clue that weighs only as much as the reader finds that a debt owed to her must one day be receivable.
*Rejoinder.* I find that it need not be receivable; the text has priced exactly that.
**Held** (standoff at parity).

## 4. Ledger

- **A heading (frame and the list of those who deny):** Holds. Its account of Sartre ("denies that any is found rather than made by freedom") is fair for Sartre; Camus of *The Rebel* would affirm A1 as found (the Freedom row says so). The epistemic rule survives the charge of "seriousness" (E1).
- **A1:** Holds. My school affirms it in practice (Algeria); Sartre's theory pays at "even if every culture approved" (E1).
- **A2:** Holds. Sartre would agree that guilt faces a person and that we answer for what we take part in; the secret case wrongs her even on our view, through fidelity to her freedom.
- **A3:** Holds. No one can absolve us in another's place: we agree.
- **A4:** Holds as offered. We grant it for every actual and nascent freedom and contest it only for the permanently non-conscious. "Least predicts" is aimed at survival-only accounts, not at us.
- **A5:** Holds. It is neutral, and Sartre grants it (capacities and desires do not settle choice). It explicitly leaves the question of natural ends to W.
- **A6:** Holds. It clearly sets apart the book's own claims from shared ones. Camus's Kaliayev is a non-theist witness to its core.
- **A7:** Holds, and it binds my school (E2).
- **W1 frame:** Holds. Its concession that binding and guilt do not decide between the accounts is accurate and disarms the voluntarism charge.
- **W1(1):** Holds. Calibrated as a clue; my school is on the second road; standoff (E9).
- **W1(2):** Holds. GS 24 defeats the artifact charge (E5); the edge case is a standoff; the "not hers to waive" claim is clearly labeled as the book's.
- **W1 closing:** Holds. "Cannot bind" lands as an internal cost to Sartre's thesis (E3).
- **W2:** Holds. "Weighs, it does not settle." My school rejects the demand for a reason why there are minds at all (Sartre: being is contingent and superfluous), a standoff the text's own wording allows.
- **W3 (longing):** Holds with repair. It misstates Sartre's analysis of love (E4, R1).
- **W3 (hiddenness):** Holds. Honest about the cost; for Camus this is the silence that defines the absurd, and the text does not count it as evidence either way.
- **C, frame:** Holds. The fairness pledge is kept throughout.
- **C, Nothing really binds:** Holds. Its cost at A7 is the same one my school pays.
- **C, Feeling, refined:** Holds.
- **C, Our best words, widening:** Holds. Rorty would say widening needs no "why"; the text names that as its stopping point.
- **C, Reason's own law:** Holds.
- **C, What we owe each other:** Holds.
- **C, Care between persons:** Holds. "The infant no one holds" is fair and strong.
- **C, Well-being, counted impartially:** Holds.
- **C, The flourishing of our kind:** Holds.
- **C, Necessary truths that belong to no one:** Holds.
- **C, An impersonal sacred order:** Holds (outside my school; no defect seen).
- **C, The Way that gives and does not claim:** Holds.
- **C, God, or Nature:** Holds.
- **C, The Buddha's Dhamma:** Holds.
- **C, History and class:** Holds. Sartre's *Critique* would sit here as well as in the Freedom row; no defect.
- **C, Power and rank:** Holds.
- **C, Freedom that makes its values:** Holds with repair. Its claims are accurate, but "a law no freedom chose (W2)" points to the wrong section and frames the cost loosely (R2). It omits the human condition, our real universal, and the child as a freedom not yet taken up (R3). It could name Sartre's Fascism admission as the cleaner statement of our price (R4).
- **C, A God who is Love:** Holds with repair (Minor). It does not name Sartre's charge that the in-itself-for-itself is a contradiction (R5).
- **Evil, first paragraph ("On the other side of the scale"):** Holds. It is the most honest part of the text; hell is named as the heaviest weight; Rieux's case is given its full force.
- **Evil, "First" (privation):** Holds. It modestly claims only that evil is no evidence of a dark god, and leaves indifference to the whole case.
- **Evil, "Second" (permission is not a trade):** Holds. Whether God wills the order without willing the harm is invisible from below, and the text says so ("a teaching, not something we see").
- **Evil, "Third" (God suffers with):** Holds. "Not a further proof."
- **Evil, closing (rival hopes, Adorno):** Holds. Camus's lack of hope is consistent with this paragraph's claim that hopes for those who come after "offer memory, not repair."
- **Conclusion:** Holds with repair (Minor). The phrasing "not because certainty is out of reach" outruns the case's own calibration (E7, R6).

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**As written: NONE.**

No Fatal or Serious objection from my school stands. Its strongest attacks (moral seemings as "seriousness," the absurd and the leap, the artifact charge, Camus and Ivan on evil, Sartre's charge that God is a contradiction) either fail, or end in standoffs at parity that the text has already conceded in its own words.

**Minor:**
1. W3 misstates Sartre. It implies his "useless passion" verdict applies only to grasping, but Sartre analyzed the wish to be loved, to be founded by another, as the same project. The step still holds once it is answered with the Creator who is no rival (R1).
2. In the Freedom row, "(W2)" points to the wrong section, and "a law no freedom chose" reads as if being unchosen were the defect, when the text's own law is unchosen too. The real cost is that Sartre's thesis needs a binding requirement that freedom did not make (R2).
3. The Freedom row omits Sartre's universal human condition and Beauvoir's child as a freedom not yet taken up. Without them our answer to A4 looks thinner than it is, and the true edge, the permanently non-conscious, is harder to see (R3).
4. The last C row does not name Sartre's in-itself-for-itself charge against the coherence of God (R5).
5. The conclusion's "not because certainty is out of reach" risks overclaiming relative to the case's own ledger (R6).

**Misstatements of my school:** item 1 above, and in part item 3. Everything else is accurate: Algeria; "only a Creator could give us a nature we are for"; freedom has no degrees; "its masters let the line bend for liberation" (Sartre's Fanon preface; Beauvoir's acceptance of violence that is owned rather than justified); Camus nearer through a common nature; Rieux at the child's deathbed.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):** whether the permanently non-conscious has equal worth (E5); whether a debt to the dead must be receivable (E9); whether the demand for a reason why minds exist is legitimate or mere nostalgia (W2); whether the longing to be told "it is good that you exist" shows anything (E4); whether God is coherent (E8); and the weighing of evil (E6).

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1 (W3).**
Old: "That is true of the longing that grasps, the wish to be like God without God (Genesis 3:5; CCC 398); it is not true of the longing to be given, to be told by another that it is good that we exist."
New: "He said the same of the wish to be loved: to want another's freedom to found our being, which must fail as well. That is true of the longing that grasps, the wish to be like God without God (Genesis 3:5; CCC 398), and true of any love that asks a rival freedom beside us to found us. It is not true of the longing to be given, to be told that it is good that we exist by the One who gives us being and is no rival beside us; whether there is such a One is what this case weighs."

**R2 (C, Freedom that makes its values).**
Old: "and what binds one freedom to will another's is a law no freedom chose (W2)."
New: "and what binds one freedom to will another's, as Beauvoir held it must, is not made by any freedom: it binds as a law binds, which is what this account set out to deny (W1, A7)."

**R3 (same row).**
Old: "Its best answer to A4 is that freedom has no degrees."
New: "Its best answer to A4 is that freedom has no degrees, and that while there is no human nature there is a human condition shared by all; Beauvoir counts the child a freedom not yet taken up, so the newborn on the hillside is wronged as fully as you."

**R4 (same row; strengthens the honest price).**
Old: "Its price: one who never will be, or never again will be, conscious has only the worth our freedom gives her (A4);"
New: "Its price: Sartre granted that if men chose Fascism tomorrow, it would then be the truth of man, which gives up A1's 'even if every culture approved'; one who never will be, or never again will be, conscious has only the worth our freedom gives her (A4);"
(Check the Sartre wording against *Existentialism Is a Humanism* before it enters the book.)

**R5 (C, A God who is Love).**
Old: "who needs nothing outside Himself to explain Him."
New: "who needs nothing outside Himself to explain Him. (Sartre held that such a being, conscious and wholly what it is, is a contradiction; that holds only if every knower is as we are, never one with what it is, and in Him knowing and being are one, ST I q.14 a.4.)"

**R6 (Conclusion).**
Old: "This book offers the case for weighing, not because certainty is out of reach, but because each reader must reach it with his own reason, and many of us find the way hard (CCC 37)."
New: "This book offers the case for weighing. Certainty is not out of reach, the Church teaches; but this case claims less, two of its witnesses are clues and evil weighs heavily against it, and each reader must reach certainty with his own reason, as many of us find hard (CCC 37)."

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights.**
- Set Sartre's practice beside his theory: he condemned torture in Algeria as wrong whatever France approved, yet granted that a Fascism men chose would become "the truth of man." That pairing shows A1 at work inside its strongest denier more cleanly than any other witness the text has.
- Kaliayev in Camus's *The Just Assassins* is a non-theist witness for A6's core: no killing children for the cause, even a just one. It belongs beside Mencius.
- Camus's *Myth of Sisyphus* rejects suicide, though as consent to the absurd, not as a betrayal of a trust. He is a non-theist who reaches part of A6's last line on a different ground; worth one clause, marked as a different ground.
- The best Catholic answer to Sartre's analysis of love is the non-competitive Creator. Being founded by another is a contradiction only if the other is a rival being beside us. This also deepens W3's distinction between grasping and being given.
- Beauvoir's child, a freedom not yet taken up, moves the real disagreement with my school from the newborn to the permanently non-conscious. Saying so makes the conclusion's hillside test sharper, not weaker.

**Lines worth keeping.**
- "The rebel can take her side; he cannot keep her."
- "God says no to what was done to her, and yes to her."
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it."
- "But that yes is said by me, not to me."
- "It does not remove all mystery; it is one mystery, and a mystery that explains."

## 8. Score

**8 / 10.** The text states my school accurately apart from one slip on Sartre's analysis of love. It turns A7 and our own Algeria practice against our theory, and it concedes every weighing it cannot win, so no Serious objection survives.

**Honest ceiling for any theistic case against my school: 8.** An existentialist who bites Sartre's bullet on A1, refuses the demand for a reason why minds exist, and treats the longing as nostalgia cannot be compelled by any moral argument. Such a case can only show, as this one does, what that refusal costs.
