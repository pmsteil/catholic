# Blind review — the Confucian school

## 1. School and voice

I speak for the Ru tradition across its main lines: the classical teachers (Confucius, Mencius, Xunzi), the Song–Ming Neo-Confucians (Zhang Zai, the Cheng brothers, Zhu Xi on principle; Wang Yangming on the heart), the New Confucians (Mou Zongsan, Tu Weiming), and Western-language scholars who read them closely (P. J. Ivanhoe, Stephen Angle, Irene Bloom, Joseph Chan, Bryan Van Norden; and Henry Rosemont and Roger Ames for role ethics). I oppose the argument as a non-theist, though not as a materialist. My tradition agrees with nearly all of section A. It grounds human worth in a nature Heaven confers on everyone (*Zhongyong* 1: what Heaven mandates is called the nature). It holds the line against killing one innocent person even to gain the whole realm (*Mencius* 2A2). And it puts the source of the moral law and of the mind that knows it in one place: principle (Zhu Xi), or the heart that is principle (Wang, Mou). What I deny is that this source is a Someone who knows and wants each person. I also deny that the moral data need such a Someone. So I am the rival the text calls "among the strongest", and I have checked whether its account of me is fair. All paraphrases of Confucian texts and modern scholars below are mine and should be checked ("verify").

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** Justice comes from two sources. One is the heart that cannot bear another's suffering, and the sense of shame at what is unfitting, which every human heart has as a sprout (*Mencius* 2A6). The other is righteousness (*yi*): the fitting response to each person in his relation and station, shaped by ritual (*li*). What anyone is owed comes from what he is (a bearer of Heaven's nature) and from where he stands toward me (the five relations, *Mencius* 3A4). **Holds** for the core: never kill the innocent, never take what is not yours, keep faith with the dead. **Strains** with strangers who stand in no relation to me. There the tradition extends family feeling outward (*tui*, 1A7) and falls back on the shared nature. That is coherent, but the reach is thinner than the book's "owed to each one by right".

**2. A hard dilemma.** Take a modern version of *Mencius* 7A35. A man learns that his aged father once killed a stranger in a hit-and-run and was never caught. By Confucian principles he may not use his office or lie to the court to shield his father: Shun does not stop his judge from arresting Gusou. He need not denounce his father to the police (*Analects* 13.18). He must remonstrate with his father gently and keep at it (4.18), press him to confess and make amends to the victim's family, and if necessary give up his own position and share his father's fate, as Shun would have carried his father off to the sea. When rules collide, discretion (*quan*) weighs them, the way you take your drowning sister-in-law by the hand even though ritual forbids touching her (4A17). **Holds** on its own terms, and it borrows nothing from the book's definitions: *ren*, *yi*, *li* and *quan* are native terms. But it **diverges** from the book's justice. The victim's claim gets an answer through remonstrance and restitution, not through impartial disclosure. Modern Confucians argue among themselves over whether this breeds favoritism: Liu Qingping says it does, Guo Qiyong defends the tradition (verify).

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** Every human being has Heaven's whole nature (Zhu Xi: the one moon whole in every river). This is the "nobility of Heaven" that no ruler confers (6A16). The sage and I are of one kind (6A7), and the great man keeps the heart of a newborn (4B12). Zhang Zai's *Western Inscription* calls the weary, the disabled, the orphaned and the widowed "my brothers in distress who have no one to turn to" (verify wording). The newborn, the dying and the disabled are covered by nature, not capacity. The enemy and the criminal keep their nature even when it has been stripped bare, like Ox Mountain (6A8). **Holds** for equality. **Strains in two places.** First, Mencius says that one who lacks the heart of compassion "is not human" (2A6). This is usually read as normative rhetoric, but it sounds like a capacity test. Second, in Zhu Xi what separates humans from other things is clear or murky *qi*, which looks like capacity again. The tradition also endorsed capital punishment and treated a tyrant as "a mere fellow" (1B8). So it supports "dignity of nature never lost". It does not support every conclusion the Church now draws from that.

**4. Ultimate flourishing.** Ultimate flourishing is to fulfil the nature Heaven gave by cultivating the heart: to know Heaven by fully developing the heart and to serve Heaven by preserving it (7A1). Its fullest form is the humaneness that forms one body with Heaven, Earth and the myriad things (Cheng Hao, Wang Yangming's *Inquiry on the Great Learning*), lived out in family, ritual and a well-ordered society that cares for the widow, the orphan and the disabled (*Liji*, "Liyun"). The guiding principles are *ren*, *yi*, *li*, *zhi* and *xin*, filial piety, and the Mean. At death, in Zhang Zai's words: living, I follow and serve; dying, I am at peace. **Coherent, but it breaks in one place.** For the person who will never think, sagehood is something she can never reach, and the tradition offers no end beyond this life. She is fully *in* the moral community but not *for* the same end as the sage. The book presses exactly this point, correctly (W1(2), C "flourishing" and "God who is Love").

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1. Mencius' child at the well against A4's "belief survival least predicts".**
*Attack.* A4 says our instincts favor the healthy and our own, so equal worth is the belief least predicted. Mencius says that anyone who suddenly sees a child about to fall into a well feels alarm and pity. The feeling is not about pleasing the parents or gaining a reputation, and it does not ask whose child it is (2A6). Equal regard is the most natural sprout in the human heart, not an anomaly.
*Defense.* A4's sentence targets an account of us "as built only for survival", which Mencius' account is not. Mencius also saw the failure the book describes: King Xuan pitied the ox he could see and sent the unseen sheep to slaughter, and the gentleman keeps away from the kitchen (1A7). The sprout favors what is in front of us, and must be extended. China's long struggle against infanticide of girls, which Confucian literati condemned, shows the extension failing (verify).
*Rejoinder.* Granted. The sentence is not aimed at us, and Mencius in fact supports it. But as written, "Our instincts favor the healthy…" reads as a claim about all of human nature, and my tradition disputes that.
**Held with repair (R1).**

**Exchange 2. "Taken as given": Heaven gives, too.**
*Attack.* The "God who is Love" row says rivals who hold the line, Mencius by name, "rest on a standing or a humaneness they take as given". We do not take it as given. We take it as *given by Heaven*: the heart that thinks is "what Heaven has given me" (6A15), and the nature is Heaven's mandate. Our final stopping point, Heaven-principle, is no more brute than God as subsistent being. Zhu Xi says the Supreme Ultimate needs nothing outside itself, just as the book says God does. So the claimed asymmetry is unfair.
*Defense.* W1(2) already concedes the point: "A law or an order gives a nature to every member of a kind; it does not want any one of them." The book's claim against you is not "gift versus no gift". It is "gift wanted for this one versus gift to the kind", and "a call to one end versus a nature to fulfil".
*Rejoinder.* Then W1(2) is fair and the God-row sentence is loose. It should state the difference the text actually draws.
**Held with repair (R7).**

**Exchange 3. An impartial principle grounds equality better than a Person who chooses.**
*Attack.* A Person who wills individually can prefer. Scripture speaks of loving Jacob and hating Esau (Romans 9:13). The text itself admits that some great theologians held God could command the killing of children (1 Samuel 15:3), and Aquinas held that by God's command death may be inflicted on anyone, guilty or innocent, without injustice (ST I-II q.94 a.5 ad 2, paraphrase). So the theistic ground for the absolute line contains one being for whom the line does not hold. Principle has no favorites and commands no one's death. As a ground for *equal* worth, it is at least at parity and arguably cleaner.
*Defense.* Catholic teaching is that God wills each person for her own sake (GS 24), calls all to one end (GS 29) and desires all to be saved (1 Timothy 2:4). Election in Scripture concerns a role in the history of salvation, not a ranking of worth (non-magisterial reading, verify). God taking back the being He gave is not taking what is not His. The line binds creatures, and no one may claim a divine command to cross it (CCC 67, 2258). The text reads the herem passages as incomplete revelation (*Dei Verbum* 15; *Verbum Domini* 42) and does not approve them. And your Heaven also "lets each one go": every innocent dies under the constant course of things (Xunzi 17).
*Rejoinder.* On equality, parity stands: both accounts stop at a source that gives the same nature to all. On the absolute line the text is honest, because it states the theological admission openly instead of hiding it. I cannot turn this into a defect of the text, only into a cost the reader should weigh, and the text already tells him to.
**Held (standoff at parity on grounding equality).**

**Exchange 4. "For the same thing": the person who will never think.**
*Attack.* The Catholic "one end that is God Himself" is surplus that cannot be checked. Worth comes from nature. The never-thinking child has Heaven's whole nature, and the *Western Inscription* makes her my sister. Nothing more is needed to say she is wronged when she is exposed.
*Defense.* The book agrees that worth rests on what she is, and the conclusion asks only that she was wronged "no less". But W1(2) asks a further question: what is she *for*? On your account the human end is sagehood, reached by cultivating the sprouts. Mencius says that one who lacks them "is not human". Zhu Xi says murky *qi* keeps principle from showing. She can be cared for, but she can never take part in the end. On the Catholic account the end is a vision *given*, not achieved. The Church teaches that it is given to the baptized infant who dies before she can choose (*Benedictus Deus*; CCC 1023, 1250), and she hopes it for the unbaptized (CCC 1261). So the end does not wait on what anyone can do.
*Rejoinder.* Our one-body humaneness counts her as a member, not as an occasion for our virtue. That is true, but it gives her no end of her own beyond being cared for. This is my school's weakest point against this text, and the text finds it fairly. The Catholic advantage here is better fit, not proof.
**Held (text's point stands against my school).**

**Exchange 5. "Not hers to waive": the Confucian ground in trust.**
*Attack.* W1(2) says "few say why the line is not even hers to waive", and names only Kant and the Theravāda. We say why. The body is received from one's parents and one does not dare harm it (*Classic of Filial Piety* 1). Zengzi at his death showed his students his unharmed hands and feet (*Analects* 8.3). Guarding oneself is the root of all guarding (*Mencius* 4A19). This is a view of life as held in trust, not owned, of the same kind as CCC 2280, without a Creator. It even comes from a life she was *given*.
*Defense.* The comparison concerns an *exceptionless* line. Your trust runs to parents and ancestors, who are not lords of life and could never give it back. Your tradition also gives it up for righteousness: it praises giving up one's life to complete humaneness (*Analects* 15.9; *Mencius* 6A10), and it honored some who took their own lives for loyalty or chastity. So it does not hold the line the Church holds.
*Rejoinder.* That is correct, and A6 rightly calls the full line "this book's claim". But the text should name our ground, because it is the nearest non-theistic analogue to the trust language it relies on.
**Held with repair (R8).**

**Exchange 6. The dead: does a debt have to be received to be real?**
*Attack.* W1(1) says Xunzi's rites are "true of the living; it does not reach her". That assumes a debt is discharged only when the creditor receives it. A debt of righteousness is discharged by right conduct and reverence. Confucius sacrificed "as if they were present" (3.12), and the reverence itself was the payment. Ji Zha of Wu hung his sword on the tree at the dead Lord of Xu's grave because he had promised it in his heart (*Shiji* 31; verify), and no one says he failed. Our moral psychology centers on correcting the fault (*Analects* 15.30), not on being pardoned.
*Defense.* The text separates *paying* from *being forgiven*. It agrees that "what he owes her is the same on the second road and the third". It claims only that guilt *asks* pardon, which only she can give. It says this proves nothing, because a mind made for living bonds would ask anyway, and it weighs the point only "as much as the reader finds" it.
*Rejoinder.* The hedging is accurate and sufficient. My premise (correcting the fault completes the moral work) and the book's (guilt asks her pardon) are each as plausible as the other. The text says as much.
**Held (honest standoff; the text does not overclaim).**

**Exchange 7. Hiddenness and evil, from Sima Qian to two thousand years of China.**
*Attack.* By the book's own definition, love wants to be known back. Yet for most of recorded Chinese history, the most disciplined moral contemplatives on earth found principle, not a Person. Sima Qian set Bo Yi starving beside Robber Zhi living out his years and asked whether the Way of Heaven is just. My Heaven-principle intends no one and so owes no answer. The book's God must answer for each child.
*Defense.* The text says all of this itself: the Neo-Confucians found "a principle… rather than a Person", the definition "makes the question harder", it has "a real cost", and the evil section concedes that some rivals "bear this whole weight more easily". It answers with the Church's teaching on salvation without explicit knowledge (LG 16; GS 22; CCC 847–848), and on evil with a setting, not an explanation (CCC 309–314). It also turns the problem back: the more your Heaven is said to have a heart that gives life (Zhu Xi's *Treatise on Ren*), and the more the *Western Inscription* calls it father and mother, the more Sima Qian's question applies to you too.
*Rejoinder.* The turn is fair. Our answer is to "establish the mandate": long life or short, cultivate oneself and wait (7A1). That is a stance with no promise attached. It is coherent, but it gives up "kept", which is what the text says it gives up.
**Held (parity standoff, honestly scaled).**

**Exchange 8. Role ethics against "her own sake".**
*Attack (Rosemont and Ames).* A person is constituted by roles: daughter, mother, friend. "Willed for her own sake", abstracted from those roles, is a Western individualism imported into the question.
*Defense.* W3 anticipates this: "even if we are made of our relationships, the longing remains." Catholic personhood is relational at its root (communion; CCC 1878) and also individual (GS 24). And role ethics is a disputed reading of the tradition itself; Ivanhoe, Angle and others resist it (verify).
**Held.**

## 4. Ledger

- **A3.** Holds.
- **A4.** Holds with repair. Mencius anticipates the "instinct fails" point and partly contests it (R1). The citation of CCC 2267 could be read as requiring the abolitionist conclusion, which traditional Confucians and many who grant A4 reject (R2).
- **A5.** Holds. There are Confucian parallels: the slight difference between humans and animals (4B19) and the five relations (3A4).
- **A6.** Holds. *Mencius* 2A2 is cited correctly. The text correctly states that the self-killing line is the book's claim, not a shared one.
- **A7.** Holds. Sincerity (*cheng*) is the way of Heaven (*Zhongyong* 20). Concealment for a father (13.18) concerns speech, not belief against evidence.

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**As written: NONE.**

I found no Fatal or Serious objection from the Confucian side. Where my school is strongest (equal worth grounded in a nature Heaven gives; the line at every number; the dead kept by rites; the source of law and mind in one place), the text either grants the point or treats the remaining question as a clue weighed at parity, not as proof.

**Minor items**
1. A4, "Our instincts favor the healthy, the beautiful and our own", reads as a claim about all of human nature. Mencius 2A6 disputes it, and Mencius 1A7 supports the narrower point the text actually needs (R1).
2. The God-row's "rest on … a humaneness they take as given" understates Mencius, whose humaneness is Heaven's gift (6A15). W1(2) states the real difference correctly (R7).
3. W1(2), "few say why the line is not even hers to waive", passes over the Confucian trust ground in the *Classic of Filial Piety*. That ground is the nearest non-theistic analogue to CCC 2280 (R8).
4. A4 cites CCC 2267 among the premises a non-Christian is asked to grant. The dignity claim is grantable; the Church's present conclusion on the death penalty is a further claim and should be labelled as the book's own (R2).
5. W1(1), "Xunzi's answer…": Xunzi gives this view of the rites (ch. 19) but does not reply to Mozi's line directly (R3).

**Misstatements or incomplete statements of my school**
- C "impersonal sacred order" treats Confucius' appeals to a knowing Heaven only as "strain". Historically, the Heaven of the Analects belongs to the Zhou Heaven that gave and withdrew the mandate, which was closer to an agent (see Eno, *The Confucian Creation of Heaven*, verify). Zhu Xi had to read such passages through his gloss that Heaven is principle (note on *Analects* 3.13, verify). Saying so strengthens the text's point (R4).
- On Mou Zongsan, "no one besides me who knows me as myself" is defensible but incomplete. For Mou, the infinite heart has an intellectual intuition that knows each thing as it is in itself. The real dispute is whether that knower is *other* than my deepest self (R6).
- The row omits the *Western Inscription*, the Confucian text closest to the book's A4 and to its family language for God (R5).

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Whether guilt toward the dead must be *received* to be fully answered (Exchange 6).
- Whether an impartial, non-intending source or a Person who wills each grounds *equality* better (Exchange 3).
- Whether the longing to be known wholly is a clue or a wish (W3 against Zhang Zai's peace at death).
- Hiddenness among the Neo-Confucian contemplatives, and Sima Qian's question (Exchange 7).

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1 (A4).**
Old: "Our instincts favor the healthy, the beautiful and our own; where instinct fails, for the infant no one is drawn to hold, we learn whether what we meant was worth or only attraction."
New: "Our instincts favor the healthy, the beautiful and our own, and most of all what is before our eyes. Mencius saw that anyone would rush to save a strange child about to fall into a well, and saw too that a king could pity the ox he saw led to slaughter and spare it for a sheep he did not see (*Mencius* 2A6, 1A7). Where instinct fails, for the infant no one is drawn to hold, we learn whether what we meant was worth or only attraction."

**R2 (A4 parenthesis).**
Old: "A6 names both as this book's claims, not shared ones.)"
New: "A6 names both as this book's claims, not shared ones. So is the conclusion the Church now draws, that the death penalty is inadmissible (CCC 2267); many who hold that no crime destroys a man's dignity do not draw it.)"

**R3 (W1(1)).**

Kant did."

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **Use Mencius as a witness, not only as a rival.** The child at the well (2A6) and the ox and the sheep (1A7) together make the book's A4 point better than Seneca does alone. Everyone has the sprout of equal regard, and it fails exactly where the child cannot be seen.
- **Ji Zha's sword** (*Shiji* 31, verify) is a famous Chinese story of keeping a promise made only in one's heart to a man already dead. It fits beside the secret in A2 and the debt in A3, and shows the intuition is not Western.
- **The *Western Inscription*** is the best non-Christian text for the book's family language. It calls Heaven and Earth father and mother, and it names the disabled as kin. The book can honor it and still ask its question: can a father and mother who know no one be father and mother?
- **The trust ground in the *Classic of Filial Piety*** shows that "life held in trust, not owned" is not a Christian-only intuition. What is distinctive in the Church's version is the One to whom the trust is held: the giver who could give life back.
- **"Establishing the mandate" (7A1)** is the Confucian counterpart to "a setting, not an explanation". It is a stance before suffering with no promise attached. Set beside CCC 309–314, it shows exactly where the two part: both refuse to explain; only one hopes.
- **The weakest point in the Confucian position** is the end for the person who will never think (Exchange 4). The book already finds it. It could say so in one sentence in the Confucian row, not only in the flourishing row.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "Heaven and Earth give life to things; God wanted *you*."
- "When one river is drained, the moon never knew which river it was."
- "Such an order is free of the problem of evil only if it intends no one in particular."
- "Minds under threat reach for a knowing Heaven, ours as well; this shows where the account strains for its own teachers, not that it fails." This applies the fairness rule correctly.
- "Equal worth is not equal duty." A Confucian can sign this.
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."

## 8. Score

**8 / 10.** The text knows my school well, states it accurately on Zhu Xi, Xunzi, Mozi, Mencius 2A2, Mou and Sima Qian, and its distinctive claims against me (an end given, not achieved; a giver who intends each person; the dead kept) are honestly presented as better fit, not proof. What it lacks is the Confucian material that would make it fairer still (the *Western Inscription*, filial trust, the child at the well), not anything that would overturn it.

**Honest ceiling for any theistic case against the Confucian school: about 8.** A Confucian grants almost all the moral data. A theistic case can win only on the personal surplus (the end of the never-thinking, the dead, being wanted, communion), and evil and hiddenness weigh against those at least as heavily as they weigh for them. A careful case can reach a strong standoff with a real advantage on the question of the end, and no further.
