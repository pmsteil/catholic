# Blind review: "A GOOD GOD WOULD NEVER ALLOW THIS"

## 1. School and voice

I argue as the atheist who presses the problem of evil and divine hiddenness at full strength: Mackie's logical problem (a God who could make free creatures who always choose the good), Rowe's evidential argument from intense suffering that serves no visible good, Draper's comparison (the pattern of pain and pleasure is far more probable if nothing in charge of nature cares about sentient beings either way than if a perfectly good God does), Schellenberg's argument that a perfectly loving God would never let anyone open to Him go without the relationship, Maitzen on what theodicy does to ordinary moral duty and on how much belief depends on where you are born, Stephen Law's evil-god challenge, Marilyn Adams and Thomas Talbott on hell as a problem of evil for Christians, Morriston and Wielenberg on the commanded killings, and Ivan Karamazov, who hands back the ticket. On the positive side I am a godless moral realist in Wielenberg's manner (verify): there are basic ethical facts that are necessary and need no ground. I grant almost all of A. My quarrel is with the step from A to a Lawgiver who is Love, and with the claim that the counterweight does not overturn it.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

1. **Starting point for justice.** Persons are owed what their nature makes good or bad for them: a being with a life that can go well or badly for her, who can be hurt, deceived and used, has claims against being hurt, deceived and used. That this is so is a basic necessary ethical fact, as basic as the theist's "God is the Good." **Holds**, at parity: I stop at a first principle, and the text grants that every account does (C, godless realism row). What I cannot say is why this fact and the minds that know it fit; I count that as luck plus the reach of reason, and the text's W2 rightly calls it contested.
2. **A hard dilemma.** Conjoined twins (the Manchester case of 2000, verify): one twin can survive separation, the other cannot survive either way, and without surgery both die within months. My principles: each twin's claim is weighed one by one (no total); neither may be used as a mere means; there is a real difference between killing and failing to save, and between aiming at a death and foreseeing it; where one cannot be saved on any course, saving the one who can is permitted and probably required. I would separate. **Borrows**: this is justice owed to each, and truthfulness about what the surgery does, close to the text's justice and truth. It does not borrow mercy as self-gift or communion. Catholic moralists themselves divided on this case (verify), so it shows our method, not a gap in theirs.
3. **Equal, unconditional dignity.** I hold it: every human being has the same basic moral standing because she is a member of a kind whose members have a rational nature, and because she is one of us, born of us. **Bends**: for the infant who will never think, my ground is membership of a kind, and I must take as given that membership carries full and equal standing. McMahan, an atheist I respect, denies it. Here the text's W1(2) presses me fairly; I hold the belief more firmly than any ground I can give it.
4. **Ultimate flourishing.** Lives of love, knowledge, work, play and justice among persons, here, and nothing beyond them. Guiding principles: do no wrong to anyone; reduce suffering; keep faith with the dead in memory and act. **Holds, with the cost the text names**: on my account the debt to the dead can be honored and never received, and the child already spent gets memory, not repair. I do not need it to be otherwise for my view to be coherent; I would want it to be otherwise, and A7 forbids me to believe it because I want it.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1. The pattern of pain (Draper).**
*Attack.* Pain in nature falls where survival puts it, conscience is warmest toward kin and creditors, and belief in God follows birthplace. All three are what we expect if nothing in charge of nature cares, and far less expected if a perfectly good, all-powerful Person governs "from the least to the great events of the world" (CCC 303). That is a likelihood ratio, not a "noseeum."
*Defense (text).* The text concedes all three, in its own words ("a world with no one behind it predicts it without help"), and keeps them on the scale. Its claim is only that the positive case outweighs them, and it presents that as a judgment.
*Rejoinder.* By the text's own accounting, two of its witnesses (the debt to the dead, the longing) are "clues of fit, not evidence," since science explains them "whether or not anyone hears" (C, final row). So the positive side against Draper rests on W1(2) and on W2 and A7, and W2 "weighs, it does not settle." The weighing is closer than the conclusion's tone suggests, but the conclusion claims only a judgment and invites the reader to weigh otherwise.
**Held.** The concession is honest. The scale is close to even, and the text says so.

**Exchange 2. Rowe and the two answers.**
*Attack.* The fawn burned in the forest fire and the two-year-old dying of cancer serve no good we can see that an all-powerful God could not get without them.
*Defense (text, "Second").* Two answers must be kept apart. *Why* He permits is the setting: a world of real causes, where creatures act on their own and can harm. *What* He brings from it is a further thing, a good offered to her. And since that good is finished only in eternal life, we would not expect to see how each evil is answered; so not seeing His reasons is weak evidence that He has none.
*Rejoinder.* The separation is what protects the text, and it does not hold. Either God's reason for permitting this evil is the setting alone, and then the reason is in plain view and can be judged: does a world of real causes need this cancer, or any cancer in two-year-olds? The text gives no argument that it does (van Inwagen's claim that there is no minimum amount of evil and no sharp line at which one more prevention costs the order nothing would be the argument, and it is absent). Or the reason includes the unseen good drawn from this very evil, and then the permission has a greater-good structure, the very "trade" the text denies. The skeptical-theist step works only on the second horn: what is unseen is the good, not the setting. And the text's own premises sharpen Rowe for the infant: the good of her own freedom does not apply to her, since she dies before she can choose, and the highest good she is offered God gives as pure gift, without suffering, to the baptized child who dies before choosing (CCC 1250; *Benedictus Deus*). So her suffering is, by the text's own teaching, not needed for her own final good.
**Held with repair.** As written this is a Serious objection (§5, S1). The defense can be made good: concede the greater-good form the Catechism itself uses, add the argument that a world of stable causes has no minimum, and say plainly that the infant's natural suffering is not explained, only set and hoped for.

**Exchange 3. Mackie, Mary and the happy fault.**
*Attack.* Mackie asked why God did not make free creatures who always freely choose the good. Plantinga's answer was that perhaps He could not. The text closes that door itself: God kept Mary from every sin "without forcing her yes," so "He can bring a free creature to say yes without overriding her."
*Defense (text).* The text accepts that, and answers the question as the Catechism does: only with what He brought from the fall, "Christ's grace gave us more than the devil's envy took away" (CCC 412, St. Leo). That is not a defense of God's permission by freedom; it is a confession that freedom does not finish the answer.
*Rejoinder.* Then the answer to the first sin is the happy-fault answer, and CCC 412 states its form in so many words: "God permits evil in order to draw forth some greater good." Every downstream horror, the parasite in the child included (the text ties that hold to the loss of the first harmony), is permitted within an order chosen for the sake of the good of redemption. That is the structure Ivan rejects: a harmony bought with a child's tears. The text answers that the good is offered to the child herself and that God does not pardon in her place what was done to her (which, to its credit, already absorbs Ivan's second point, that the mother may not forgive the torturer for her child's suffering). It does not answer his first: that no harmony offered afterward makes it right to have permitted the torture before.
**Held with repair**, ending at an honest standoff over Ivan. The text should keep the happy fault and drop the claim that permission is "not a trade," or say exactly what kind of trade it is not.

**Exchange 4. Maitzen: does theodicy cancel the duty to rescue?**
*Attack.* If God permits only evils from which He draws a good for the sufferer, then a child's suffering is, on the whole, good for her, and I who could prevent it deprive her of that good. Ordinary morality, which says I must pull her from the fire, then contradicts theism.
*Defense (text and Catholic teaching).* The text says "His permission never excuses ours" (CCC 2269), and the good drawn is not earned by the evil. The best version: God draws good from whatever happens, including our rescue, so the good is not tied to our failure; God's permission is the permission of the One who can repair, raise and restore, and ours would be the permission of those who can do none of these; and we are among the real causes He made, so our duty is part of the very setting that explains His permission.
*Rejoinder.* That defense works, but only by conceding that the evil was not needed for her good, which returns us to Exchange 2's first horn: then the only reason for permitting it is the setting, and whether the setting needs it is visible and unshown.
**Held with repair.** The text asserts the conclusion; it should give the reason in one sentence (the good is not tied to our failure; we are among the causes).

**Exchange 5. The commanded killings (Morriston, Wielenberg).**
*Attack.* 1 Samuel 15:3 commands Israel to kill "infant and suckling." The text reports that "some great theologians held that God, as Lord of life, could so command without injustice" (St. Thomas's ground is that all die by God's decree on account of original sin, so death may be inflicted at His command on guilty and innocent alike, ST I-II q.94 a.5 ad 2). And the text itself says, a few lines earlier, that He who gives life "does her no wrong when He calls it home." So either God could authorize the killing of an innocent child, or He could not.
*Defense (text).* No one may now claim such a command (CCC 67); the Church reads these pages in the light of Christ as "incomplete and temporary" (DV 15; VD 42); this book reads them as the record of a people God was still teaching; and the line is stated as having "no exceptions for any of us." God's prerogatives are not ours; that is no voluntarism, since the law does not change, only who has the right to take a life He gave.
*Rejoinder.* That is coherent, but it costs the comparison the text most wants to win. W1(2) says "what protects her is not a weight... it is that no number of others has any right to take her life." If the Lord of life can confer that right, then what protects her is that He does not confer it, and her protection is a policy of the Giver, not a feature of her worth. "A worth she was given" (the text's advantage over Kant and the Theravāda) is then exactly a worth the Giver can recall into others' hands. Kant's ground binds every agent: on his view a rational being may never be used merely as a means by anyone, not even by God (*Critique of Practical Reason* 5:131, verify). On the inviolability of the child, the rival the text ranks below it is stronger, unless the text says plainly that God could not command what *Evangelium Vitae* 57 calls always gravely immoral. And the text's own ground for the line, "held in trust from the One who alone is Lord of life," makes the line's absoluteness hang on God's ownership, which is what makes the command thinkable.
**Failed as written; Held with repair.** The book must decide. A Catholic may decline St. Thomas's view on this point; the text currently reports it and walks past it (§5, S2).

**Exchange 6. Hell (Adams, Talbott).**
*Attack.* Talbott's three claims cannot all be true: God wills that every person be saved; God's will cannot be finally defeated; some are lost forever. The text grants the second claim's strongest form itself (Mary: God can win a free yes without overriding). Adams adds that a God who is good to each must give each a life that is a great good to her on the whole, and eternal loss is not that. And the Gospel says of one man that it would have been better for him if he had not been born (Matthew 26:24), against a book whose God says first to each that it is good that she exists.
*Defense (Catholic teaching).* God wills all to be saved (1 Tim 2:4) by the will that bears on each as she is in herself, and permits a refusal held to the end by the will that bears on all that she freely does (ST I q.19 a.6 ad 1; CCC 1037); no one is lost without full knowledge and consent (CCC 1033, 1857, 1859–1860); being is a good even where its end is lost; and the Church teaches no one by name is lost. The text puts all this on its own scale and calls hell "the heaviest thing there."
*Rejoinder.* The distinction names the problem; it does not dissolve it, since God can bring the yes and does not. And W1(1)'s advantage over the second road ("what can never be repaid, no one ever releases") is lost on the Catholic road for every wrongdoer who refuses to the end: his victim never receives what he owes her either.
**Held.** The text concedes this openly, which is the most a theist can do here. Two small repairs (the antecedent/consequent distinction; the note on W1(1)).

**Exchange 7. Hiddenness (Schellenberg, Maitzen).**
*Attack.* A perfectly loving God would never let anyone who is not resisting Him go without the conscious, two-way relationship love seeks, because that relationship is itself a great good, now. There are such people, and their distribution follows birthplace, not honesty. So there is no such God. This is a deductive argument, not a weight.
*Defense (text, W3).* God is known through persons first, and has given us the dignity of making Him known (CCC 306, 848); He offers each person a share in the Paschal Mystery in a way known to Him (GS 22; LG 16); the knowing is begun now and finished at the end. And the text concedes its own definition makes the question harder: "a love that binds into communion wants to be known back."
*Rejoinder.* The mediation answer predicts exactly the demographic pattern, which is why it cannot count as evidence for the God it defends; and an implicit relation without belief is not the knowing back the text's own definition wants.
**Held as an honest standoff at parity**, with a repair: the text should say which premise it denies (the "now" in the first), and that it grants the cost.

**Exchange 8. Law's evil god.**
*Attack.* Every theodicy has a mirror for an evil god: he gives us freedom so that we do more evil, love so that its loss hurts, a longing for a love that never comes. Debts to the dead that can never be paid, worth that makes its destruction a real loss: what better torments?
*Defense (text).* Evil is a privation; there can be a supreme Good but no supreme evil (ST I q.49 a.3), since every choice of evil reaches for a lesser good the chooser lacks, and a will that lacks nothing has nothing to grasp at. The text says this rests on what evil is, and that a reader who thinks malice can be as full as love must weigh a dark god against the whole case.
*Rejoinder.* The privation answer is the right answer and it is strong; but it is metaphysics argued in Chapter 1, not something the moral data show. Against the indifferent source the text then lists three things none of them "fits as well": the debt to the dead, the worth that is each one's own, and the longing. Two of those three the text elsewhere calls clues, not evidence.
**Held with repair** (a wording fix to the evil section's second paragraph).

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**S1 (Serious). The two-answers defense in the evil section's "Second" does not hold together, and it is the one point the text says lessens evil "as evidence."**
The text says God's permission "is not a trade," that *why* He permits (the setting of real causes) and *what* He brings from evil must be kept apart, and that "not seeing God's reasons is weak evidence that He has none." But (a) for the first sin, which the text ties to the hold of the parasite on the child, the text's own answer to "why did He not prevent it," as He prevented Mary's sin without forcing her, is only what He brought from it; the Catechism paragraph it cites says "God permits evil in order to draw forth some greater good" (CCC 412). That is the greater-good form the text denies. (b) If the reason for permission is the setting alone, the reason is visible, and the text offers no argument that the setting needs this much pain (or any in infants); the skeptical-theist step then has nothing to work on. If the reason includes unseen goods drawn from that very evil, the separation fails and Ivan and Maitzen apply. (c) For the infant who dies of disease before she can choose, the good of her own freedom is absent and her final good God gives as pure gift (CCC 1250; *Benedictus Deus*), so on the text's own teaching her suffering is not needed for her good. The premises of this objection are the text's own and the Catechism's. The text can repair it (§6), but it cannot answer it as written.

**S2 (Serious). The Lord-of-life clause and the unanswered question about the commanded killings undercut W1(2)'s central claim and its advantage over Kant.**
W1(2) says what protects her is "that no number of others has any right to take her life," and the final C row claims an advantage over Kant and the Theravāda: a worth she was given. But the same paragraph says God "does her no wrong when He calls it home," grounds the line in a trust held from the "Lord of life," and reports, without accepting or rejecting it, that great theologians held God could command the killing of children (1 Sam 15:3). If He could, the right to take her life can be conferred on others, and her protection is the Giver's policy, not her worth; and Kant's ground, which binds even God (verify), protects her better. The text can answer this only by deciding, which a Catholic may do (*Evangelium Vitae* 57: the direct and voluntary killing of an innocent human being is always gravely immoral); as written it does not.

**Minor items.**
- **S3 (Minor).** The evil section's answer to an indifferent source and to Spinoza's God lists the debt to the dead and the longing as things they cannot "fit as well," but the final C row says those two "are clues of fit, not evidence." The discriminating work is done by W1(2) and W2; say so.
- "Free will (Chapter 1) answers much of the evil we do to one another" overstates: given Mary, free will answers whose the evil is (CCC 311), not why God permits it. The text admits as much two sentences later; the first sentence should match.
- Hell: the text should give the Catholic distinction between God's will toward each as she is in herself and His will in view of all she freely does (ST I q.19 a.6 ad 1), which is the accurate Thomist reply to Talbott, and note that it names the weight without removing it.
- W1(1): the critique of the second road applies equally to the Catholic road for the unrepentant wrongdoer; one clause.
- Hiddenness: the strongest form (Schellenberg) is deductive; the text should say it denies the "now" in its first premise, not merely that it lacks "a full answer."
- Animals: Aquinas's lion and ass (ST I q.22 a.2 ad 2) concern death; the hundreds of millions of years of agony before man are not covered by it, and the Church calls it contrary to human dignity to cause animals to suffer or die needlessly (CCC 2418). The text already says the fawn's hope is thinner; it should say the standard it would apply to us applies here.
- Ivan Karamazov is the classic form of the cost question and is not named; Camus's doctor is.
- Mary's case is said to show God can win a free yes "without overriding her"; that is accurate to Catholic teaching on grace, but readers on the Molinist side will hear it as the Thomist answer. The text already says the Church lets her theologians differ. Fine as is.

**Misstatements of my school.**
- "Not seeing God's reasons is weak evidence that He has none" casts the evidential argument as a "noseeum" inference. Its strongest form (Draper) compares how likely the whole pattern is on each hypothesis and needs no claim about unseen reasons. The next sentence ("the pattern of pain in nature still weighs") partly fixes this; the repair in §6 completes it.
- Otherwise the text states my school fairly, and in places (birthplace, Mary, hell, the fawn) more fairly than many atheists do.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects).**
- Ivan: whether any good offered to the child afterward makes it right to have permitted the torture before. The text says it is weighed, not settled; I agree.
- Draper's pattern against the positive case: a close weighing; the text claims a judgment, not a result.
- Schellenberg: once the text says which premise it denies, this is a standoff over whether a relationship begun by grace before the name is known counts as the relationship love seeks.
- Hell (Talbott, Adams): the antecedent/consequent distinction against the triad; neither side moves the other.
- Law's evil god: decided by the privation account of evil, which I dispute and the text argues elsewhere.

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1 (S1, the heading of "Second").**
Old: "*Second*, God's permission is not a trade, and two answers must be kept apart here."
New: "*Second*, God's permission is not a trade in which she is spent for someone else's gain, and two answers must be kept apart here."

**R2 (S1, faith's addition, aligned with CCC 412).**
Old: "faith adds that He permits no evil from which He will not draw a good, though the evil never earns that good and never becomes good itself (CCC 311, 312, 324, 412)."
New: "faith adds that He permits no evil from which He will not draw a good; the Catechism, with St. Thomas, says that He permits evil in order to draw forth some greater good (CCC 412; ST III q.1 a.3 ad 3). But the evil never earns that good and never becomes good itself (CCC 311, 312, 324), and the good is offered to her, not taken from her for someone else."

**R3 (S1, the skeptical-theist sentence; also fixes the misstatement).**
Old: "So for one weighing by reason alone, not seeing God's reasons is weak evidence that He has none. It is not no evidence: the pattern of pain in nature still weighs."
New: "So for one weighing by reason alone, not seeing *how* each evil is answered is weak evidence that it is not. Whether a world of real causes needs this much pain is another question, and there the evidence is not weak. Some argue that such a world leaves no sharp line at which one more prevented agony would cost the order nothing (verify: van Inwagen); but the child who dies of disease before she can choose is not served by the good of her own freedom, and the highest good she is offered God can give as pure gift (CCC 1250, 1261). The pattern of pain in nature still weighs, and that child weighs with it."

**R4 (Ivan named, evil paragraph 1).**
Old: "whether it is worth this world's cost is part of the weighing, not settled by it."
New: "whether it is worth this world's cost is part of the weighing, not settled by it. Ivan Karamazov's refusal, to hand back his ticket to any harmony bought with one tortured child's tears, is the sharpest form of that question. This book does not claim to answer it; it claims only that the harmony hoped for is offered to the child herself, and that God does not forgive in her place what was done to her."

**R5 (free will, evil paragraph 1).**
Old: "Free will (Chapter 1) answers much of the evil we do to one another: it is ours, not His."
New: "Free will (Chapter 1) answers whose the evil we do to one another is: it is ours, not His (CCC 311). It does not by itself answer why He permits it, since He can, as with Mary, bring a free will to its yes."

**R6 (S2, the commanded killings, W1(2)).**
Old: "and some great theologians held that God, as Lord of life, could so command without injustice."
New: "and some great theologians held that God, as Lord of life, could so command without injustice, since all die by His decree on account of the first sin (ST I-II q.94 a.5 ad 2). This book does not follow them here. The direct and voluntary killing of an innocent human being is always gravely immoral (*Evangelium Vitae* 57), and the One who willed each child for her own sake does not put the taking of her life into our hands. His calling a life home is His own act, and He gives that right to no one."

**R7 (S2, the protection clause, W1(2)).**
Old: "it is that no number of others has any right to take her life."
New: "it is that no number of others has any right to take her life, and the One who alone has that right wills her for her own sake and gives it to no one."

**R8 (S2, Kant row; fairness).** After "humanity has a worth beyond price, which admits no equivalent and so cannot be traded even for many." insert: "Kant held that no one, not even God, may use a rational being merely as a means (*Critique of Practical Reason* 5:131, verify); this book agrees, and holds that God never does (W1(2))."

**R9 (hell, evil paragraph 1).**
Old: "why one will is won by His grace and another refuses it to the end, the Church does not explain, and she lets her theologians differ over it."
New: "why one will is won by His grace and another refuses it to the end, the Church does not explain, and she lets her theologians differ over it. St. Thomas distinguishes God's will that each be saved, which looks at her as she is in herself, from His will in view of all that she freely does, which permits a refusal held to the end (ST I q.19 a.6 ad 1; CCC 1037). That names the weight; it does not lift it."

**R10 (W1(1), the Catholic road for the unrepentant).**
Old: "as though nothing had happened (*Spe Salvi* 44);"
New: "as though nothing had happened (*Spe Salvi* 44), and if he refuses to the end, what he owes her is never received, on this road as on the second (see the section on evil);"

**R11 (hiddenness, W3).**
Old: "and for many it does not come in this life at all."
New: "and for many it does not come in this life at all. In its strongest form the objection says that a perfectly loving God would never let anyone open to Him go without that knowing, now, so that every such seeker counts against Him. This book denies only the *now*: it holds that the relationship can be begun by grace before the name is known and is completed at the end; and it grants that this is less than the knowing back its own definition of love wants."

**R12 (S3, evil section "First").**
Old: "Those are answered only by the whole case, and none of them fits as well a debt that outlives every creditor on earth, a worth that is each one's own, wanted for her own sake and not to be spent for others"
New: "Those are answered only by the whole case: chiefly by a worth that is each one's own, wanted for her own sake and not to be spent for others"
and after the closing parenthesis "(an impersonal source can make all things equal, as Zhuangzi's Way does, but it wants none of them)," replace ", or a longing to hear from Someone that it is good that *you* exist." with "(W1(2)), and by the law and the mind that reads it (W2). A debt that outlives every creditor on earth, and a longing to hear from Someone that it is good that *you* exist, fit a loving God better than any of them, but as clues, not evidence (W1(1), W3)."

**R13 (animals, evil "Second").**
Old: "the lion lives by the slaying of other animals (ST I q.22 a.2 ad 2)."
New: "the lion lives by the slaying of other animals (ST I q.22 a.2 ad 2). St. Thomas speaks there of death, not of long agony, and the Church holds it contrary to human dignity to cause animals to suffer or die needlessly (CCC 2418); by that measure the pain of the ages before man stays on this side of the scale."

## 7. New insights; lines worth keeping

**Insights for the book.**
- The Mary concession is the honest center of the evil section. It closes the door Plantinga left open against Mackie, and the text walks through it to CCC 412. Build on it openly: the Catholic answer to the first sin is the happy fault, not the free will defense, and saying so is stronger than half-saying it.
- The text already answers half of Ivan without naming him: God does not forgive in her place what was done to her. Name Ivan and say which half is answered.
- The exposed infant is both the text's best witness and my best evidence. The conclusion sees this. The chapter would gain by saying it once in plain words: the same child who shows that worth follows what she is, and not her strength, is the child no all-powerful hand saved; the book's claim is that her being kept, not her being spared, is what God's love for her means.
- The Lord-of-life sentence cuts both ways. Grounding the line in God's ownership makes the commanded killings thinkable; grounding it in God's willing her for her own sake makes them unthinkable. Lead with the second.
- Draper's comparison is the form of my argument that the text should face by name, because its own scorecard ("clues, not evidence"; "weighs, does not settle") is already written in his terms.

**Lines worth keeping.**
- "God says no to what was done to her, and yes to her."
- "The parasite takes from the child; God does not give her to it."
- "For the fawn that hope is thinner than it is for the child, and this book does not pretend otherwise."
- "He did not keep her from what was done to her; that weight is on the other side of the scale, and this book weighs it there."
- "So hell stays on this side of the scale, and for a book that says God's first word to each of us is that it is good that we exist, it is the heaviest thing there."
- "Which is he surer of, that a child left to die was wronged, or that a good God could have reasons to let her die?"
- "The rebel can take her side; he cannot keep her."
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."

## 8. Score

**7 / 10.** The text concedes nearly every datum my school relies on, at full strength and sometimes against itself, and claims only a judgment; its two Serious faults (the why/what split that CCC 412 undoes, and the unsettled question of the commanded killings) are real but repairable in a few sentences, and with R1–R13 I would put it at 8.
**Honest ceiling for any theistic case against my school: 8.** No theistic case can do better than a close weighing here, because the suffering of those who never choose is, on the theist's own teaching, not needed for their final good, and Ivan's question cannot be answered by reason, only by a hope.
