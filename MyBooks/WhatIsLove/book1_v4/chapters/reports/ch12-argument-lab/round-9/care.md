# Blind review: the ethics of care

## 1. School and voice

I argue for the ethics of care as its best defenders hold it. Carol Gilligan heard a different moral voice. Nel Noddings set out caring as a relation that the cared-for completes. Annette Baier made trust the centre of morality. Virginia Held treats care as both a practice and a value, with justice nested inside it. Joan Tronto made care political: attentiveness, responsibility, competence and responsiveness, and later "caring with". Eva Feder Kittay works from dependency and from her daughter's profound disability, with "connection-based equality" and the principle of *doulia* (verify both terms as hers). Sara Ruddick extended maternal thinking to the enemy. On this view morality starts in the fact that every one of us was held before we could do anything. The test of any ethic is the dependent and the one who holds her. I speak as a non-theist. I owe the text fairness, and I owe my own school the same honesty.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** What anyone is owed comes from need, vulnerability and the relations a person stands in. Above all it comes from the dependency every human life begins in. Justice is the fair sharing of care, both its burdens and its benefits, inside that web. Kittay's *doulia* says the carer is owed care in turn (Held, Tronto, Kittay; verify). → **Holds** as a coherent starting point. It **borrows** a universality that a purely relational base does not obviously supply: the duty runs to everyone's dependency, not only to the people who happen to be mine.

**2. A hard dilemma resolved.** A daughter is the sole carer of her mother, who has advanced dementia. Years ago the mother said "never put me in a home." The daughter is collapsing, and her own children are going without. Care resolves it this way. Attend to what the mother needs and responds to now, which is no longer what she once feared. Count the carer and her children as people the care must also reach. Move the mother to residential care with daily visits, so the relation is kept and not abandoned. → **Holds**, without the book's four terms as the book defines them. It overlaps them heavily, though: attentiveness is truthfulness about the other, the fair spread of care is justice, responsiveness is close to mercy, and bounded self-giving is close to sacrifice. Care *limits* self-gift by the carer's own needs (Gilligan's mature stage). That is a real point of tension with A6, but it does not break the answer.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** Kittay's answer is that each of us is "some mother's child": a dependent who could live only because someone cared. Equality is equality in that connection and in being owed care fitted to need. It is not equality in capacity. Ruddick extends this to the enemy: he too is some mother's son. → **Holds** for the newborn, the dying and the disabled. It **strains** at two points. One is the enemy, since Noddings limits obligation to those with whom caring can be completed (verify). The other is *unconditional*, *equal* worth as distinct from claims proportioned to need. Why this relation, rather than capacity, gives *full and equal* standing is finally taken as given, as the value of care itself is.

**4. Ultimate good and guiding principles.** The ultimate good is lives sustained in good relations of care: being held, holding, being known and responded to. The goal is a society organised so that everyone receives care and can give it. The guiding principles are attentiveness, responsibility, competence, responsiveness and solidarity (Tronto). → **Holds** for this life. It **breaks** at the grave and in the past. For the child already lost, care can offer memory and better institutions for the next child, and nothing for her. The text says this fairly in its section on evil.

## 3. Debate highlights

**E1. The dilemma in the care row.**
*Attack.* The row says care "stops at the infant no one holds. If she is owed only within a relation of care, hers was refused; if she is owed care by all, that is a worth already there, which care answers and does not give." The first horn runs together two things: a relation as an actual practice of caring, and a relation as a standing tie that makes care owed. The abandoned newborn is still her mother's child and still one of us. On care ethics, refusing care owed within a standing relation is the paradigm wrong, a betrayal of trust (the text's own A2). So "hers was refused" does not show the account has nothing to say. It *is* the account's verdict: she was wronged. That holds even on Noddings, since the mother who exposes her is in relation by birth. On Tronto's view, a society that leaves her on the hillside has failed a care it owed her as a whole.
*Defense.* "Relation of care" means Noddings's caring encounter. Biological motherhood is a fact, not yet a moral relation. Why it generates a claim is exactly what is at issue. And the second horn concedes the book's point: the claim comes before any caring.
*Rejoinder.* Kittay and Held both deny that biological motherhood is "a fact, not yet a moral relation." Even granting the defense, it shows only that care must say *why* this relation gives full standing. That is the stopping point the text already gives the contractualist ("why that relation, and not her powers, gives her full and equal standing, it takes as given"). It is parity, not a stop at the infant. The second horn grants a claim prior to caring, and care accepts that: her need and her place among us come before anyone answers them. That shows a stopping point, not that a Mind is needed. → **Failed as written; held with repair** (repair R1: restate the row at parity with the contractualist row).

**E2. "No order wants her", but carers do.**
*Attack.* The central contrast in W1(2) is that a law, an order or a Way "does not want any one of them." Care is none of these. It is persons wanting persons. So W1(2)'s argument passes care by.
*Defense.* For the infant no one wanted, care can say only that she *ought* to have been wanted. The book says she *was* wanted, by the One who made her (Jeremiah 1:5; GS 24). That is a fact claim, not an ought. An equal call that does not depend on anyone's partial love explains an *equal* worth. Care's actual wanting is partial by design.
*Rejoinder.* An equal claim does not need equal actual wanting. Connection-based equality grounds the claim in a dependency everyone shares. Still, I concede the difference is real: on my account no one wanted *this* child, and on the book's account Someone did. Whether actually being wanted adds grounding beyond "ought to be wanted" is the crux, and the text frames it as care's *shape*, not as proof. → **Held** (a standoff of fit, honestly framed).

**E3. The severely disabled as test case.**
*Attack.* The text repeatedly writes "the newborn who will never think." Kittay's criticism of Singer and McMahan applies here. Philosophers describe profoundly disabled people by deficit, with a confidence they have not earned, and use them as test cases. Her daughter does not reason discursively, but she responds, delights, loves music and loves people (Kittay, *Learning from My Daughter*, verify). Also, W1(2) makes equal worth depend on a shared end, communion with God. How does a person who will never reason reach that end?
*Defense.* The book's whole point is that her worth does not follow capacity. The deficit phrase is the rivals' test, used to show that their grounds cannot reach her. The end is reached as gift, not achievement: God can give the seeing to the infant and to the one who will never think (CCC 1250, 1261).
*Rejoinder.* The second answer is strong. It answers "for the same thing" better than the secular Aristotelian can, and I grant it. But care asks why equal worth needs a *single* end at all. Equal consideration, fitted to each person's needs, does not require sameness of telos. *Defense, second round:* without one measure, "equal" has no content: equal in what? *Rejoinder:* equal in being owed full membership and care fitted to need. That is content, though I take it as given. The phrasing is still a fault. "Will never think" is false of many such people. "Will never reason" says what the rivals actually mean. → **Held with repair** (R3).

**E4. The justice voice and the missing care datum.**
*Attack (Gilligan).* The A-list is almost entirely in the justice voice: wrong, guilt, owed, pardon, lines, numbers, the surgeon, the riot, the experiment. A4 states dignity as negative constraints: never used, never killed. Yet the conclusion's final test is an *abandonment*: she was "left there." That wrong is the failure of a positive care. It is the datum care owns, and the A-list never states it.
*Defense.* A5 names dependency and carers (CCC 2208–2209). A6 says real good for another asks something of the giver. Exposure intended to kill is killing, and the Church treats it as infanticide (CCC 2271). W3 is built on being wanted.
*Rejoinder.* All true, but scattered. If A4 said she is "owed care and not only spared harm," the conclusion's hillside test would follow from a stated premise that nearly every rival grants. It would also enlist care as a witness instead of leaving it to one row. → **Held with repair** (R2).

**E5. The Carer who did not save her.**
*Attack.* By care's own standard, anyone who wanted her and could have saved her, and did not, failed her. "She is held by Him" (W3) is no holding in the sense she needed: no one fed or warmed her. Noddings argued in *Women and Evil* (verify) that the traditional theodicy, and above all eternal hell, cannot be squared with caring. The care row offers God's wanting as the answer at the very point where theism's cost is highest.
*Defense.* The text does not hide this. W1(1) says "He did not keep her from what was done to her; that weight is on the other side of the scale." The section on evil names child suffering and hell as the heaviest weights and claims only a setting, not an explanation (CCC 309–314). It refuses to treat her as a means (SCG III.112). It answers with a God who suffers with her, who raises the dead, and who acts through "the hands that pull a child from the fire" (CCC 306, 2269). Scripture itself speaks of a love that holds when parents fail: "For my father and my mother have forsaken me, but the LORD will take me up" (Psalm 27:10).
*Rejoinder.* That is honest, and it is the right placement. My objection to the case as a whole does not stand: it puts the weight where it belongs and does not claim to lift it. The care row, read alone, uses the hillside infant as theism's advantage and does not say in the same breath that she is also theism's heaviest cost. → **Held with repair** (one clause in R1); the underlying weight remains a real standoff.

**E6. Sacrifice and the carer.**
*Attack.* Feminist care ethics warns that ideals of self-sacrifice have been used to extract care work from women without limit. A6 ("real good for another always asks something of the giver") and the book's absolute duty of food and water put lifelong burdens on carers.
*Defense.* The text says those who hold us need holding too (A5; CCC 2209). Burdensome or disproportionate treatment may be refused (CCC 2278). Food and water are owed only while they nourish without causing harm or intolerable suffering (*Samaritanus Bonus* V.3). A gift that is forced is no gift (Ch. 4).
*Rejoinder.* That is adequate. Care ethicists also split on assisted death: Kittay and disability scholars share the Church's fear of pressure on the dependent (verify). The text flags the absolute as its own claim. → **Held.**

**E7. The debt to the dead.**
*Attack.* Care discharges the debt to the dead by caring for what she cared for. That is the text's second road, and it is enough.
*Defense.* On that road, "what the living pay, she is not there to take up or answer."
*Rejoinder.* Here my own school gives ground. Noddings holds that caring is *completed* by the cared-for's reception and response (verify). On care's own terms, a debt of love with no one left to receive it is incomplete. Care can live with that as tragedy, but it cannot deny the incompleteness. The text rightly claims only a clue. → **Held** (a standoff at parity; care's own account supports the text's description).

**E8. Relation or nature?**
*Attack.* The book grounds worth in "what she is" and also in being *wanted* and *called*. The second is a relational ground. Either the book has adopted care's relational ontology, and its talk of a worth "in what she is" adds nothing, or it has two grounds that may come apart.
*Defense.* In Catholic thought the creature's being is itself a relation. Creation, in the creature, is "a certain relation to the Creator as to the principle of its being" (ST I q.45 a.3). What she is *is* received being, so nature and relation do not come apart. Her worth follows her nature because her nature is a gift that is still being given.
*Rejoinder.* This is coherent and, frankly, elegant. It is a relational ontology of dignity that answers McMahan, who grounds worth in capacity, and care's stopping point at once. It does rest on metaphysics my premises do not grant. That is parity of first principles, not a defect. → **Held.**

## 4. Ledger

- **A heading.** Holds. The epistemic rule is fair to both sides, and the list of deniers is honest. No care ethicist is wrongly listed as a denier. (Noddings's views on the unborn would cut against the Church, but A4 as stated speaks of the born.)
- **A1.** Holds. Care affirms it.
- **A2.** Holds strongly. The secret told to a stranger is a pure betrayal of trust (Baier). Structures of sin match Tronto's structural account of care failures.
- **A3.** Holds.
- **A4.** Holds with repair. The negative constraints are right, but the positive care owed to the dependent is missing, and the conclusion relies on it (R2). The line about the infant "no one is drawn to hold" is excellent.
- **A5.** Holds. It is the one item that names dependency and the carer's need. Care welcomes it.
- **A6.** Holds for the shared core. The book's own claims are flagged as such. Care warns about sacrificial ideals, but the text's proportionality answers that (E6).
- **A7.** Holds.
- **W1 frame.** Holds. "We agree she is owed. We part at the grave, and over what gives her the claim at all" is fair to care.
- **W1(1).** Holds. Noddings's completion-by-reception supports it (E7). It is honestly framed as a clue.
- **W1(2).** Holds with repair. The "no order wants" contrast does not touch care (E2), and the deficit phrasing needs fixing (R3). The equal-call argument and "every child was wanted by the One who did" are the text at its best.
- **W1 closing.** Holds.
- **W2.** Holds as a contested question of fit. It is not care's ground to contest.
- **W3.** Holds. "Only a person can love us back" is care's own claim. The infant "held by Him" holds only together with the section on evil (E5).
- **C: Nothing really binds.** Holds.
- **C: Feeling, refined.** Holds. It is fair to Hume and Smith, and so to Baier's Humean care ethics. The phrase "the newborn who will never think" needs R3.
- **C: Our best words, widening.** Holds.
- **C: Reason's own law.** Holds.
- **C: What we owe each other.** Holds. It is the template the care row should match.
- **C: Care between persons.** Fails as written: a false dilemma at its first horn (E1), it omits Tronto's political answer, and it does not note the evil cost at this exact case. Holds with repair R1.
- **C: Well-being, counted impartially.** Holds.
- **C: Flourishing of our kind.** Holds with repair R3. It rightly notes Aristotle on deformed infants.
- **C: Necessary truths.** Holds.
- **C: Impersonal sacred order.** Holds.
- **C: The Way that gives.** Holds.
- **C: God, or Nature.** Holds with repair R3.
- **C: The Buddha's Dhamma.** Holds with repair R3 ("the one who will never think").
- **C: History and class.** Holds.
- **C: Power and rank.** Holds.
- **C: Freedom that makes its values.** Holds.
- **C: A God who is Love.** Holds. "Against the ethics of care … it is the one relation no one can refuse her" is fair and strong.
- **Evil, paragraph 1 (the weight).** Holds. It is honest about child suffering, the pattern of conscience, and hell as the heaviest weight. Noddings's objection is placed on the scale, not answered away.
- **Evil, "First" (privation).** Holds as metaphysics. Care would resist any implication that pain is less real, and the text explicitly denies that implication.
- **Evil, "Second" (permission, not trade).** Holds. "Whether it could be justified to her" is the right test, and it is honestly answered with a hope.
- **Evil, "Third" (God suffers with).** Holds. Care prizes presence, and this is care-shaped.
- **Evil, rivals' hopes.** Holds. It applies to care's hope for a caring society too: it is for those who come after.
- **Conclusion.** Holds. Care grants the hillside judgment and that her claim does not follow "how much anyone cared for her." The inference that her worth "follows what she is" is one care can accept in relational form. The step from there to God rests on the whole case, as the text says.

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**Serious (repairable): the care row misstates the school at the decisive case.** The row says care "stops at the infant no one holds," using a dilemma whose first horn runs together a relation as practice and a relation as standing tie. On care's best versions (Kittay, Held, Tronto), the abandoned infant is wronged *because* care owed within standing relations, and by society as a whole, was refused. Care's real stopping point is the one the text already assigns the contractualist: it takes as given why that relation gives full and equal standing. As written, the row claims an advantage it has not earned. The book's final test case is this same infant, which is why I count this as Serious and not Minor. R1 repairs it, and the issue then becomes an honest standoff.

**Minor**
- A4 states dignity only as negative constraints. The positive care owed to the dependent, on which the conclusion's abandonment test relies, is never stated as a premise (R2).
- The phrase "the newborn who will never think" and its variants (Feeling row, Flourishing row, Spinoza row, Dhamma row) describe the profoundly disabled by a deficit that is often false, and they adopt the rivals' framing (R3).
- The care row credits "carers need care too" to CCC 2208–2209 alone. Kittay's *doulia* is the school's own form of the point and deserves naming.
- The care row offers God's wanting as the answer at the hillside without saying, in the row, that the same case is theism's heaviest cost. This is covered elsewhere (W1(1), the section on evil), so it is Minor (folded into R1).

**Misstatements of my school**
- "It stops at the infant no one holds": an overstatement (above).
- The row reduces care to interpersonal relations and leaves out Tronto's political account. That account is the school's strongest answer to the uncared-for child.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Whether being *actually wanted* by God grounds anything beyond being one who *ought* to be wanted (E2).
- The value of care, or of the human dependency relation, as a first principle versus a God who is Love (E8).
- A Carer who wanted her and did not save her, and eternal hell (Noddings). The text places both on the scale and claims no explanation (E5).
- The request for death, where care ethicists are themselves divided (E6).

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1: care row (C, "Care between persons").**
Old: "and that carers need care too (CCC 2208–2209). With Kittay it says why worth does not follow ability: each of us is some mother's child (A4). But it stops at the infant no one holds. If she is owed only within a relation of care, hers was refused; if she is owed care by all, that is a worth already there, which care answers and does not give. This book's answer has care's shape: the One who made her wanted her before anyone saw her (W1(2))."
New: "and that carers need care too (Kittay's principle of *doulia*; CCC 2208–2209). With Kittay it says why worth does not follow ability: each of us is some mother's child (A4). It reaches even the infant no one holds: the care she was owed belongs to relations that betrayal does not dissolve, for she is still her mother's child and still one of us, and, with Joan Tronto, a people that leaves her on the hillside has failed a care it owed her together. But it stops at two things. Why that relation, and not her powers, gives her full and equal standing, its best defenders take as given, as they take the worth of care itself; there it meets the contractualist's question. And of the infant no one wanted, it can say that she ought to have been wanted, not that anyone did want her. This book's answer has care's shape: the One who made her wanted her before anyone saw her (W1(2)). That He did not save her from the hillside is the heaviest weight on the other side of the scale, and this book weighs it there."

**R2: A4.**
Old: "equal, prior to anyone's recognition, never to be used as a mere means,"
New: "equal, prior to anyone's recognition, owed care and not only spared harm, never to be used as a mere means,"

**R3: deficit phrasing (four places).**
Old (Feeling row): "whether the newborn who will never think is worth as much as you" → New: "whether the child who will never reason is worth as much as you"
Old (Flourishing row): "which the newborn who will never think can never reach" → New: "which the child who will never reason can never reach"
Old (Spinoza row): "so the newborn who will never think has less" → New: "so the child who will never reason has less"
Old (Dhamma row): "to the infant and to the one who will never think" → New: "to the infant and to the one who will never reason"

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **The case's strongest datum has care's shape.** Its best moments are not about obligation but about being *wanted*: "every child was wanted by the One who did," "it is good that you exist," "the one relation no one can refuse her." A condensed chapter could open with the held infant, with A5's dependency, before the surgeon and the riot. Care ethicists are then witnesses for the case and not only a rival in it.
- **Creation as relation (ST I q.45 a.3).** Her being is itself received from God. This removes the apparent tension between grounding worth in "what she is" and grounding it in being wanted and called. It answers McMahan's capacity view and care's stopping point together: a relational ontology of dignity with a Giver. It belongs in W1(2).
- **Ezekiel 16:4–6.** Scripture's own image of the exposed newborn is Israel "cast out on the open field … on the day that you were born," to whom God says, "in your blood, 'Live'" (RSV; verify the exact wording). It is the hillside infant of the conclusion, in revelation's own voice. With Psalm 27:10 and Isaiah 49:15, it gives the care row's last sentence a biblical root.
- **Noddings's completion-by-reception** supports W1(1): on care's own terms, love's debt asks for someone to receive it.
- **Kittay's connection-based equality and *doulia*** match A4's "equal worth is not equal duty" and CCC 2209. Naming them shows the book listening to the school that has thought hardest about the severely disabled.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "where instinct fails, for the infant no one is drawn to hold, we learn whether what we meant was worth or only attraction."
- "We also begin, and many of us end, wholly in others' hands, and those who hold us need holding too."
- "Not every child is wanted by those who made her; every child was wanted by the One who did, before anyone saw her."
- "Against the ethics of care, which also begins with this one, it is the one relation no one can refuse her."
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it."
- "He did not keep her from what was done to her; that weight is on the other side of the scale."

## 8. Score

**7/10 as written; 8/10 with R1–R3.** The text is honest, it takes the dependent as its hardest case, and its theistic answer actually has care's shape. Its one direct engagement with my school, though, uses a false dilemma that care's best defenders (Kittay, Held, Tronto) answer at once.

**Honest ceiling for any theistic case against the ethics of care: 8.** Care reaches the newborn and the disabled with a stopping point no worse than God's. Theism's real advantage, that this child was actually wanted, falls on the very case where the problem of evil weighs heaviest.
