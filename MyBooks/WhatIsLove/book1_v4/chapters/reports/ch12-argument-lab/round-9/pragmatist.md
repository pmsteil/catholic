# Blind review: the Pragmatist / Anti-representationalist and Relativist school

## 1. School and voice

I speak for the line that runs from William James and John Dewey to Richard Rorty, together with the moral relativism of Gilbert Harman and David Wong. My claim is not that cruelty is fine. I say it is wrong, and wrong even if every culture approved. My claim is that the question "what makes it true?" does no work. Our moral vocabulary is a set of tools we have built and keep revising for living together. It gets justified by how it serves us and the people we are learning to count as "us," and not by matching a moral order that exists independently of us. "Necessity" and "reach" are features of how we use the words: we judge every possible world by our own lights, because those are the only lights anyone has. Harman adds that the "ought" we address to an agent assumes a shared, implicit agreement. Wong adds that there is more than one true morality, though human nature rules some of them out. I write as a fair opponent and a non-theist. My test is James's: what difference would it make in practice if this were true?

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** I derive justice from no principle. Rorty treats justice as loyalty extended to a wider group (verify: "Justice as a Larger Loyalty," 1997). We start from the solidarities we have and widen them through stories, through dealing with each other, and through democratic inquiry (Dewey). Harman says what is owed is fixed by the implicit agreements people actually act on. **Verdict: coherent and borrows nothing.** It breaks for anyone outside every existing loyalty: such a person is owed something only once we choose to extend loyalty to them. I accept that cost and hold the commitment unconditionally from inside, but I cannot claim it is anything more than a commitment.

**2. A hard dilemma.** The case: the Manchester conjoined twins (*Re A*, Court of Appeal, 2000, verify). Separating them would kill Mary and might save Jodie. Leaving them joined would kill both. The devout parents refused consent, and the court authorized the surgery. A Deweyan resolves this by inquiry. Rehearse each course and its consequences in imagination. Consult everyone affected and the practices already in place (here, the legal doctrine of necessity). Decide what can be lived with and defended to the people it touches, and stay open to revising the decision. I would separate them. **Verdict: coherent.** I do use honesty, fairness and compassion, but as revisable goods, not as the book defines them. What breaks is any claim that this answer is *the* right one apart from our deliberation. I can say only that it was the best answer we could defend. (Catholic moralists were divided on whether separation was direct killing or a foreseen side effect. Verify before relying on either reading.)

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** I deny it has a ground. I do not deny the commitment. Rorty says the human-rights culture rests on no shared human nature such as rationality. It rests on sentimental education, which teaches us to see the hillside infant as one of us (verify: "Human Rights, Rationality, and Sentimentality," 1993). "Unconditional" names the way we pledge it: we refuse to make it conditional. It does not name a property she has. James's view that every actual demand creates an obligation does ground obligations in the infant's own wants (verify: "The Moral Philosopher and the Moral Life," 1891). But it weights obligations by demand, so it cannot give *equal* worth to someone who will never be conscious, or no longer is. **Verdict: coherent as a vow. It breaks at "prior to anyone's recognition."** I can say she was wronged before anyone said so, because I say it now, about then. I cannot say that anything beyond some community's speech keeps it true.

**4. Ending point for flourishing.** I deny that there is an ultimate good. Dewey holds that growth (the continual enrichment and widening of experience) is the only moral end, and that ends are always ends-in-view, revised as we go. Rorty's liberal hope is less cruelty, more freedom, private self-creation and public solidarity. James's meliorism holds that the world can be made better, but nothing guarantees it. **Verdict: coherent. It breaks at the grave.** Growth offers nothing to the child who is already dead. My honest word for that is tragedy.

## 3. Debate highlights

**E1. The starting point: "truths, not the best words."**
*Attack.* The A heading sets up a false choice. Its own rule, "What still seems true to us after honest reflection ... we have reason to trust until something more certain shows it false," is Peirce's anti-Cartesian maxim, and every pragmatist accepts it. But the rule licenses trust in first-order moral beliefs (A1–A7). It does not license the second-order gloss that they are "not ... the best words one community has come to so far." That gloss is the very thing in dispute, so it is not a datum. Calling it one loads the scale before anything is weighed.
*Defense.* The text does not demand the gloss. It states it openly, adds "None is beyond denial," names Rorty, Harman and Wong, and sends the dispute to C. A reader whom honest reflection leaves sure that the slave was wronged whatever anyone said may trust that conviction by the same rule. The rule does not tie trust to first-order claims only.
*Rejoinder.* Agreed. Then A is shared ground only at the first-order level, and the heading misdescribes Rorty: he does not deny that A is "true of anything beyond our widening 'we'." He denies that truth is something to explain, and he keeps "true" as distinct from "justified to us" (the cautionary use). **Held with repair** (R2).

**E2. "Necessity" and "reach" (W1 frame, W2).**
*Attack.* "Wrong of necessity" and "wrong for any mind that can reason and suffer, in any world" are not data about a moral order. They come from how we use the words: we apply our concept of cruelty to imagined worlds because we have no other concept to apply. Darwin's hive-bees are wrong by our lights and we are wrong by theirs. Saying such minds "might have fixed on the wrong ones" assumes a standard that is independent of every mind. That begs the question.
*Defense.* W2 does not put this to the pragmatist. It says outright that pragmatists "need no meeting of law and mind; for them the question is the one in C." The reach question is aimed at the realist, who does believe in such a standard. Also, the pragmatist himself says "wrong even if every culture approved," so in practice he grants the reach he denies in theory.
*Rejoinder.* Granted. Fixing our own standpoint and judging every world from it is how a pragmatist talks about counterfactuals, and it costs him nothing. W2 does not attack us, so we have no complaint about it. **Held.**

**E3. The slave and the infant on the hillside (C, "Our best words").**
*Attack.* The row makes three charges, and two of them misstate my school. (a) "The slave was not yet wronged in a way any truth could record." I say now, truly, that he was wronged then. Our statements are about the past, and nothing about my ethnocentrism concerns when the wronging happened. (b) "The infant no one holds is safe exactly as far as our stories reach her." That runs together protection and standing. On *every* account she is protected exactly as far as people act on what they believe. The text admits God "did not keep her from what was done to her." Today our widest stories do reach every human being.
*Defense.* The text means standing, not protection: a truth about her wronging that holds whether or not any vocabulary exists to state it. Rorty himself conceded something close to this. He granted that pragmatists cannot say an injustice was already there waiting to be discovered before anyone had the words for it (verify: "Feminism and Pragmatism," 1991).
*Rejoinder.* Then the row should say exactly that, and the price becomes one I dispute: "nothing but our continuing to speak this way makes it hold." I say that once the practices are fully described, nothing is missing that could make a difference to anyone. The row asserts a cost without showing that, by my own test, it is one. **Held with repair** (R1). The corrected charge stands at parity, and E6 shows where the text can win it.

**E4. "Why 'we' should go on widening rather than shrink, it cannot say."**
*Attack.* That is false of Rorty. He says why: we are liberals, people for whom cruelty is the worst thing we do (he takes this from Judith Shklar, verify), and widening is what that self-image demands. What he cannot do is give a non-circular reason that would move someone who does not share it. The text cannot do that either. It says of Hume's knave, "No argument compels such a man, ours included," and of the one who gives up A4, it "cannot compel" him.
*Defense.* The difference is in what the reason *is*, which the Feeling row already sees: "not who must judge, but what the truth of the judgment comes to." If the theist is right, the shrinker is failing her and failing the One who wanted her, whether or not he ever grants it. There is a practical difference too. The lone abolitionist, set against his whole society, can say he answers to something it does not. On Rorty's view he answers only to a future audience that may never exist. Rorty's reading of Orwell is a case in point. He grants that the torturer can break Winston down so that no inner truth is left to betray (verify: *Contingency, Irony, and Solidarity*, ch. 8). That is the price stated by my school's own master.
*Rejoinder.* "If the theist is right" carries the whole argument. A difference in what a reason is makes no difference to what it does to the shrinker. Still, the Orwell concession is honest, and it is the strongest material the text has against me. The row does not use it. **Held with repair** (R1). The row as written fails ("cannot say"). The corrected claim is a standoff that the text can tip with W1(1).

**E5. Bound to the truth (A7).**
*Attack.* Rorty argues that truth is not a goal of inquiry, because we cannot tell when we have reached it except by being justified. What binds us is justifying ourselves to ever wider audiences. James argued that "believe truth" and "shun error" are two separate commands, and that when the evidence cannot settle a live, forced, momentous option, our passional nature may decide. The text's own conclusion, which "ends in a choice, not a proof," is a Jamesian will to believe.
*Defense.* A7 forbids believing *against* the evidence and refusing to look out of fear. James forbids both too. The Church teaches that faith goes beyond reason and never against it (CCC 159), and that is no embarrassment. Rorty's replacement, "justify to wider audiences," has no answer to the whistleblower whose whole audience prefers the lie. The audience-based account can bind him only if the audience is an ideal one: Peirce's opinion at the end of inquiry, or Putnam's idealized justification. A truth that would survive all inquiry, though no one may ever hold it, is the godless realist's position in C, and W2's question comes back.
*Rejoinder.* Peirce's ideal belongs to pragmatism. It guides inquiry without requiring a Mind. That is a real stopping point, and W2 already rates its own argument as weighing, not settling. **Held.** A7 survives, and the dispute lands at parity where W2 says it does.

**E6. The debt to the dead (W1(1)).**
*Attack.* I take the second road: we honor her memory, keep her wishes and care for the people she loved. Wanting her to be there to forgive is wanting the universe to be different from what it is.
*Defense.* By my school's own test this is exactly where the God question pays out. James held that looking backward, materialism and theism come to the same thing, but looking forward they part: one ends in the dissolution of every ideal, the other promises an ideal order kept for good (verify: *Pragmatism*, Lecture III). So the text is not asking an idle question about what makes things true. It is asking one with practical consequences, about whether she is kept. And it rates the answer honestly: "a clue, not a proof."
*Rejoinder.* Agreed. A difference in what we would want is not evidence, and the text says so itself (A7, W3's "That is not evidence"). **Held.** This is the text's real lever against my school, and C should use it (R1).

**E7. A rival the text does not weigh: James's finite God.**
*Attack.* James proposed a God who is personal and good but not all-powerful: a helper in a fight that has not yet been won (verify: *A Pluralistic Universe*, 1909; *Pragmatism*, Lecture VIII). That God fits W1 and W3 (Someone who knows and cares) and carries the evil section's weight much more easily. Animal pain, the parasite, and the question why He did not stop the first sin all stop being charges against Him. A best-explanation case that leaves out the rival with the lightest evil-load has not finished its comparison.
*Defense.* The text already has what it needs. W1(2) grounds her worth in the gift of her *being*, and a finite God who is part of the world did not give her being. W2 places eternal truths in God's own wisdom, and a finite God's wisdom does not contain every possible nature. W1(1) needs Someone who can promise to keep her, and James's God offers no guarantee.
*Rejoinder.* Then it is a trade-off: lighter on evil, weaker on grounding and on hope. That answer holds, but the text needs one sentence that says it. **Held with repair** (R3; Minor).

**E8. Harman on the shape of justice (A6, W1(2)).**
*Attack.* W1(2) asks "why justice has that shape" and credits Foot with the asymmetry between doing harm and failing to help. Harman explained that asymmetry without God: morality is an implicit bargain between rich and poor, so the duty not to harm is strong and the duty to help is weak (verify: "Moral Relativism Defended," 1975).
*Defense.* That is an account of how the belief arose, and the text has bound itself not to use such accounts against its rivals. More to the point, the bargain predicts the line bending wherever the powerful gain. It also predicts weak duties toward the infant, who has nothing to bargain with. That is exactly the price A4 names.
*Rejoinder.* Harman accepts it: the infant is protected through her parents' interests. That openly gives up A4's "prior to anyone's recognition." **Held.** The relativist pays the price the text says he pays.

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**Serious: the C row "Our best words, widening" misstates the price it charges.**
1. "Why 'we' should go on widening rather than shrink, it cannot say." This is false of Rorty, who says why from within (the liberal hatred of cruelty). The text concedes that its own case cannot compel the shrinker either, so as written the line charges my school a cost the text also pays. The true difference is what the reason is. The text says this about quasi-realists but not about us.
2. "Not yet wronged in a way any truth could record" and "safe exactly as far as our stories reach her" read as charges about record and protection. On my account the slave's wronging is recorded truly now. On every account her protection depends on what people do. The real charge, that nothing but our continuing practice makes her claim hold, is left unsaid.
3. The row asserts the cost without showing it is one by the pragmatist's own test. The text has that showing available (James's forward-looking difference; Rorty's Orwell concession) and does not use it.

The objection is valid, its premises are well documented in the literature, and the text as written cannot answer it at parity, because the row's sentences are false of the school as its best defenders state it. That breaks the text's own rule ("Each is stated as its best defenders state it"). It is fully repairable (R1), and once repaired the row holds at parity and is tipped by W1(1).

No Fatal objection stands.

**Minor**
- A heading: "Rorty ... denies that any is true of anything beyond our widening 'we'" misstates him. He denies that truth is relative and denies that there is anything to say about what makes a truth true. Justification, not truth, is relative to an audience (R2).
- "Harman and Wong hold them true only relative to a morality": Wong's pluralism is bounded by human nature, and some moralities are simply inadequate. A1 may well hold in every adequate morality (R2).
- James's finite God is missing from the evil section's list of rivals who bear the weight more easily (R3).
- Harman's bargaining account of why justice has its shape (doing vs. allowing) goes unmentioned in W1(2). The text's answer (it predicts bending, and fails the infant) is ready to hand. Optional.

**Misstatements of my school**
- Rorty treated as a relativist about truth (A heading; C row grouping). He rejected the label.
- "Cannot say" why to widen. He says why, circularly and openly.
- The slave "not wronged in a way any truth could record." He would record it truly now. What he gives up is that the wronging holds independently of any vocabulary.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Whether "what makes A true?" is a good question at all. The text cannot refute my refusal to ask it. It can only show that the answer changes something in practice, which it does in W1(1) and W3.
- Whether a reason that holds of the shrinker whether or not he accepts it is better than one held from within. That turns on the metaethics in dispute.
- Whether the second road (memory, honoring, care) is enough for the dead. My school says it has to be. The text says guilt asks for more, and rates that as a clue.
- A7: whether truth binds beyond the ideal end of inquiry. Peirce's regulative ideal and the text's Mind are rival stopping points, which is what W2 says.

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1 (Serious): C row, "Our best words, widening."**
Old:
> **Our best words, widening** (pragmatism, above all Richard Rorty; and the relativism of Gilbert Harman and David Wong, for whom moral truth is relative to a morality) does not deny A; it declines to ask what makes A true. It says torturing a child is wrong, from within the words we have come to. Moral progress, it holds, is the widening of "we," won more by stories than by proofs, and the Church grants much of that history (W3). Its price is the infant on the hillside. If worth is what our widest "we" extends, then before anyone extended it the slave was not yet wronged in a way any truth could record, and the infant no one holds is safe exactly as far as our stories reach her. Why "we" should go on widening rather than shrink, it cannot say.

New:
> **Our best words, widening** (pragmatism, above all Richard Rorty, with Dewey and James behind him; and nearby the relativism of Gilbert Harman and David Wong, for whom moral truth is relative to a morality, though for Wong human nature rules some moralities out) does not deny A; it declines to ask what makes A true, holding that no answer would do any work that our practices of praise, blame and giving reasons do not already do. It says torturing a child is wrong, and would be wrong if every culture approved, from within the words we have come to; Rorty does not say our agreement makes it so, only that nothing else does either. Moral progress, it holds, is the widening of "we," won more by stories than by proofs, and the Church grants much of that history (W3). It can say, and say truly, that the slave was wronged; what it gives up is that anything but our having come to speak this way makes it so. Its price is the infant on the hillside: no one then had the words that reach her, and on this account her claim holds as long as some "we" goes on saying it, and in nothing that would remain if every such "we" were gone. Rorty was honest about that price: reading Orwell, he granted that the torturer can break a man until no inner truth is left to betray. Asked why "we" should widen rather than shrink, it answers from within: because we are people who count cruelty the worst thing we do. It has nothing to say to one who is not, and this book's case cannot compel him either. The difference is what the answer is: on its account the one who shrinks departs from us; on ours, he departs from her, and from the One who wanted her. The pragmatist will say that difference makes no difference. William James, who gave pragmatism its test, thought otherwise: looking back, he held, the two accounts come to the same, but looking forward, one promises that what is good is kept and the other does not. That is the difference W1(1) and W3 weigh: whether she is still someone.

(Before publication, verify the Orwell paraphrase against *Contingency, Irony, and Solidarity* ch. 8 and the James paraphrase against *Pragmatism* Lecture III.)

**R2 (Minor): A heading.**
Old: `Rorty asserts most of them but denies that any is true of anything beyond our widening "we," and Harman and Wong hold them true only relative to a morality;`
New: `Rorty asserts most of them but holds that nothing beyond our widening "we" and its reasons makes them true, and declines to ask what does; Harman and Wong hold them true only relative to a morality (for Wong, one of the several that human nature allows);`

**R3 (Minor): Evil ¶1, after the Way sentence.**
Old: `A Way that is not humane gives life and lets each thing go, and owes no one an answer.`
New: `A Way that is not humane gives life and lets each thing go, and owes no one an answer. William James proposed a God who is good and personal but not almighty, a helper in a fight not yet won; such a God bears this weight more lightly than ours, but He did not give her being, so her worth is not His gift (W1(2)), and He cannot promise to keep her (W1(1)).`

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **Use pragmatism's own test against pragmatism.** James held that the God question pays out in what we can hope for looking forward (Lecture III). That makes W1(1) and W3 the right ground on which to meet Rorty. On that ground, "declines to ask what makes A true" is no longer a principled refusal. It is a refusal to look at a difference the method itself counts.
- **Rorty's Orwell chapter** is the most honest statement anywhere of what the infant on the hillside costs a non-theist: there is no inner core the torturer cannot destroy. It fits the book's line "The rebel can take her side; he cannot keep her" exactly.
- **The pragmatist family is divided on precisely the slave case.** Putnam, Habermas, and Misak's Peircean pragmatism all left Rorty because a truth claim has to reach beyond its context, or the reformer could never be right against his age (verify each). Where they land is the godless-realist row, so the book can show that pragmatism's own heirs end up at the W2 question.
- **"The Moral Philosopher and the Moral Life"** (James, 1891) argues that obligations are owed only to claimants, and that the most stable and demanding moral universe is possible only if there is a divine thinker whose demands take in everyone's (verify). That is a pragmatist witness close to W1. Use it with care, since James's God there is not the Creator.
- **The twins case** (*Re A*, 2000) is a concrete place where the book's "the absolute can cost lives" can be tested honestly. Catholic moralists divided over it, so if Chapter 11 uses it, it should state both readings.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."
- "So the difference is not who must judge, but what the truth of the judgment comes to." This is the right framing for every deflationary rival, mine included.
- "The rebel can take her side; he cannot keep her."
- "Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent."
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it."
- "So, like the longing in W3, this is a clue, not a proof." This calibration is why my school finds little to attack outside C.

## 8. Score

**7 / 10 as written; 8 with R1.** Outside C the text is well calibrated against my school: it exempts us where it should and calls its strongest differences clues. Its one row on pragmatism overclaims ("cannot say") and does not use the pragmatist's own test, which would make its real lever, the kept dead, hit harder.

**Honest ceiling for any theistic case against my school: 8.** No argument can force the pragmatist to ask the grounding question. The most any theistic case can do is show, by James's own test, that the answer changes what can be hoped for the dead and the unheld, and then leave the reader to weigh a hope against a vow.
