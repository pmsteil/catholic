# Blind review: the utilitarian school

## 1. School and voice

I speak for the utilitarian tradition at its strongest today. That means Sidgwick's rationalist hedonism as Katarzyna de Lazari-Radek and Peter Singer revived it in *The Point of View of the Universe* (2014), Hare's two-level preference utilitarianism (*Moral Thinking*, 1981), Brad Hooker's rule consequentialism (*Ideal Code, Real World*, 2000), and Smart's frank act utilitarianism (*Utilitarianism: For and Against*, 1973). We are not error theorists. Most of us now hold that there is a self-evident truth: from the point of view of the universe, the good of any one being counts for no more than the like good of any other. On that view, right action is whatever does best for all who can fare well or badly, or follows the code that would. We grant everything consequences can earn. What we refuse is any moral premium that no one's welfare ever feels.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** Our starting point is equal consideration of interests. What anyone is owed is whatever the rules most protective of everyone's vital interests (security, subsistence, freedom from violence) give her. Mill, *Utilitarianism* ch. 5, treats justice as the name for the most important and most strictly binding class of social utilities (verify). **Holds** on its own terms and borrows nothing. But "owed" is derivative here: the claim is hers because honoring claims like it does the most good. The text's charge that we cannot make a wrong primitively *directed* lands, and we accept that as a cost rather than an incoherence.

**2. A hard dilemma.** A competent patient with advanced ALS, months from death, refuses further ventilation and repeatedly asks for help to die. Our principles: (a) every affected interest counts equally; (b) her informed, settled preference is the best evidence of her welfare; (c) at the rule level, safeguards against pressure on the poor, the disabled and the depressed, judged by the evidence from jurisdictions that permit the practice (Oregon, the Netherlands) rather than by imagined slopes. Conclusion: permit it, with safeguards. We use truthfulness (informed consent) and compassion (relief of suffering), but as dispositions justified by welfare, not as covenantal truth or mercy in the book's sense. **Holds**, without borrowing. It contradicts the book's own claim in A6/W1(2), which the text labels as the book's claim and not a shared premise, so this is a disagreement, not a refutation.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** We affirm equal *consideration*: the newborn's pain, the dying man's, the disabled child's and the enemy's count exactly as much as like pain in anyone. We deny equal *value of life* independent of capacity. Singer (*Practical Ethics*) holds that a life's value depends on the being's capacities for future-directed preference or welfare, so it is not equal across all humans, and that species membership as such carries no moral weight (verify). Hooker and Hare would build very strong rule-level protections for every born human. **Breaks with A4 openly**, but it breaks coherently. Our ground (Sidgwick's axiom of benevolence) is a first principle held as self-evident, the same footing as the godless realist's. We borrow nothing, and we pay the price the conclusion names. Where it bites hardest is the infant no one holds. We protect her firmly, but we cannot say her life is *worth* as much as yours.

**4. Ultimate good and guiding principles.** The ultimate good is the well-being of all sentient beings. For Sidgwick and de Lazari-Radek and Singer, that means happiness: desirable conscious states. For Hare and the earlier Singer it means satisfied preferences. Hooker allows a pluralist list: pleasure, friendship, knowledge, achievement, autonomy. The guiding principles are impartiality, equal weight, and doing (or following the code that does) the most good. **Holds** as a system. The hedonist version **strains** at the experience machine and at the secret case (A2), where nothing anyone experiences is worse and yet we sense a wrong. De Lazari-Radek and Singer accept that consequence and do not dodge it (verify).

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1: the secret told to a stranger (A2).**
*Attack.* The case is clever, but the difference between the letter blown into the street and the friend who tells is a fact about the *agent*. The teller showed a disposition (willingness to break a trust for a laugh), and if that disposition were common it would destroy confidences everywhere. Rule consequentialism condemns him even if this telling did slightly better. "Wronged her" is how our intuitive-level morality names violations of rules that protect trust.
*Defense.* That explains why the teller is *faulty*. It does not explain why *she* is the one whose pardon he needs. A2 separates three things: a mistake needs correcting, a flawed character needs reforming, a wrong done to a person needs forgiving. A disposition-fault calls for reform. Only a wrong done to her calls for her pardon. And the standing to release a promise in advance belongs to her (A3), not to whoever manages the code.
*Rejoinder.* An optimal code would give promisees the power to release, because the promisee knows her own interests best.
*Defense.* Then her standing is a delegation from the total, and it is revocable whenever revoking it would do better. We hold that she has it in her own right.
*Verdict:* **Held.** We can rebuild directed wrong only as something derived from the total, which is exactly the text's claim. One repair is needed (see §5, item M1): the C row overstates the point against the rule-based form of the theory.

**Exchange 2: the surgeon, and whose wrong it is (A6).**
*Attack.* "Almost everyone sees these at once." That is precisely the kind of verdict that has a truth-independent genealogy. People who would never kill an innocent are trusted more as partners (there is experimental work on deontologists as preferred partners; verify), so a heuristic against sacrificing anyone spreads whether or not it tracks the truth. Your reply to Greene's "personal force" story (no one is pushed in the riot) defeats only one version of the debunking argument.
*Defense.* The fairness rule cuts both ways. If you may debunk the line, the text may debunk the arithmetic: "the regard for the many that would spend the one has a history of its own." A history settles neither.
*Rejoinder.* Not that history. De Lazari-Radek and Singer's whole argument is that *impartial* benevolence toward every sentient being is exactly what kin selection and reciprocity do *not* predict, so it survives debunking where the partial intuitions do not (*Point of View of the Universe*, ch. 7; verify). Your cited history ("kin and favors returned") explains partial regard, which we already set aside. As written, the parity claim aims at the wrong target.
*Best defense available.* (i) There is a stronger genealogy of impartiality: kin sympathy extended outward by reasoning to consistency. Guy Kahane and others argue it makes impartial benevolence just as debunkable (verify), and W2's own debunker paragraph already uses this "same consistency" move. (ii) Better still: the principle you say survives debunking, that each one's good counts as much as anyone's, is A4's cousin, and the book holds it too. It does not license spending anyone. To get from equal weight to "therefore kill one to save five" you need a further premise, that the right act is the one that maximizes the sum. That further premise is the one the surgeon's case denies, and nothing in your debunking argument protects it.
*Verdict:* **Held with repair.** The parity holds, but only with the better genealogy and the equal-weight point stated (repair R6).

**Exchange 3: the killer's heart (W1(2), A6).**
*Attack.* "Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent." Your tradition lets a city be bombed when the deaths of innocents are foreseen but not intended and are proportionate. Those victims are just as innocent, just as dead and just as unconsenting as the patient on the surgeon's table. So the Catholic line tracks the *structure of the agent's will*, which is precisely what the text faults the Theravāda for ("it speaks of the killer's heart ... not of a worth she was given"). Worse, the ground offered, "No one may take what is not his to take," covers foreseen killing too. Taken literally it would forbid what the Church permits.
*Defense.* The line tracks the victim's *status*, not the agent's feelings. To aim at her death is to make her an instrument, to subordinate her to others' ends against her being willed for her own sake (GS 24; *Summa contra Gentiles* III.112). The side-effect victim is not used; she is wronged if the harm is disproportionate or careless, and the Church condemns indiscriminate destruction of cities (CCC 2314). This is the "inviolability" account that Quinn and Kamm defend as a status the victim has, not a scruple of the agent (verify). The text's next sentence also says "directly" (CCC 2258).
*Rejoinder.* Then the *shape* of the line (aiming versus foreseeing) comes from the means principle, which the text concedes Kant shares. The Creator adds only the claim that it holds at every number. Say so. And the sentence about taking needs its "directly." The closeness problem (where does "intending the explosion" end and "intending her death" begin?) belongs to every deontologist, not specially to you.
*Verdict:* **Held with repair** (R7). The standoff over double effect is at parity with secular deontology, not a defect peculiar to the text.

**Exchange 4: "survival least predicts" (A4, W2, Conclusion).**
*Attack.* You argue that equal worth is the belief survival least predicts, and so it is a deliverance of reason. We run the same argument for impartial benevolence. If the argument works for you, it works for us, and impartial concern across species is *less* predicted by survival than the protection of one's own species. Favoring one's own kind is just kin and in-group bias generalized. So the species boundary inside A4 ("worth follows kind") is the part most open to debunking. The marginal-cases argument then asks: why does the anencephalic infant have infinite dignity while a chimpanzee with a richer inner life has none?
*Defense.* The datum A4 relies on is not "humans over animals." It is *equality among humans across capacity*: the infant no one holds is worth what the genius is worth. Survival does not predict that (it would favor productive members), and neither does the utilitarian calculus, which explicitly denies it. So it is a datum independent of both. The Church does not make animals mere things: they are owed kindness, and causing them needless suffering is against human dignity (CCC 2416–2418). The text grounds the kind-line in an explanation it offers for the datum (an equal call), not in the datum itself.
*Rejoinder.* Fair. But then the comparative claim must be stated as being about *intra-human* equality, not as a blanket "least predicted" against every rival's core principle. As things stand, a reader of de Lazari-Radek and Singer will think the text has not noticed that they use the identical form of argument.
*Verdict:* **Held with repair** (R8). On the substance, this is an honest standoff, which the conclusion concedes ("it cannot compel the one who gives up A4").

**Exchange 5: equal wrong, unequal loss (Conclusion).**
*Attack.* The conclusion asks whether the weak newborn left on the hillside was wronged "no less than ... a grown man ... however differently one counts what each of them lost." But if the losses differ and the wrongs are equal, the wrong has come loose from anything that matters *to the victim*. That is McMahan's point, and ours: a wrong measured by nothing the victim loses looks like a moral premium no one's life ever registers.
*Defense.* The wrong is measured by *who* she is, not by the size of the lost future. A grown man and a dying man of ninety lose very different amounts, yet almost everyone holds that killing either is murder, equally. Your own rule-level protections treat them as equal for that reason. The text's point is that the equality we enforce in law and in conscience is not a useful fiction. And the alternative is to say the ninety-year-old's murder is a lesser murder. That cost is at least as hard to pay as yours.
*Rejoinder.* We would say it is a lesser *loss* and an equal *crime*, because the code must be equal. You will reply that this is the accommodation-without-explanation that C accuses every rival of.
*Verdict:* **Held.** This is an honest standoff at parity, and the text concedes it.

**Exchange 6: truthfulness (A7 and the C row).**
*Attack.* The C row says utilitarianism "makes truthfulness a servant of the total" and cites esoteric morality. But A7 is about *belief and inquiry*: one may not believe against the evidence. Sidgwick and de Lazari-Radek and Singer never say the enlightened should believe against the evidence. Their esotericism concerns what is *published* and what others are left believing. Your own Church allows silence and discreet language and holds that "no one is bound to reveal the truth to someone who does not have the right to know it" (CCC 2489). As for Sidgwick's resignation: his writing on subscription defended it partly on the high social value of trust in what teachers profess, which is a utilitarian ground.
*Defense.* The real contrast is sharper than the row states. The Church forbids every lie, by its nature (CCC 2485), while utilitarianism permits, and in principle requires, the lie or the induced false belief whenever it does more good. A utilitarian who could give a dying man a comforting falsehood, or take a pill that installed one in himself, must in principle favor it.
*Rejoinder.* Agreed. Practical reasons can favor *inducing* a belief; epistemic reasons never favor *the belief*. On that distinction we are no worse off than anyone who accepts Pascal's wager.
*Verdict:* **Held with repair** (R5). Aim the charge at lying and at induced falsehood, and grant the Church's own permission to stay silent.

**Exchange 7: evil, animals and hell (the evil section).**
*Attack.* From the point of view of the universe, a fawn's agony matters as much as a child's agony of equal intensity. The text grants that God's order "does give one creature's loss to another's life" among animals. A perfectly good being who wills that order is spending sentient beings for the whole, the very thing the text condemns when we do it to persons. Hundreds of millions of years of that, with pain falling "where survival would put it," is evidence. And hell is, by our measure, an infinite disvalue.
*Defense.* The text says all of this itself, at full weight: "it is not no evidence"; "for the fawn that hope is thinner ... this book does not pretend otherwise"; hell is "the heaviest thing there." It does not trade the evil away. It keeps God's permission distinct from God's willing (ST I q.19 a.9; CCC 311), denies that God ever gives a *person* away (*Summa contra Gentiles* III.112), and lets Romans 8:19–22 be a thin hope, not a proof. Your axiom that animal and human suffering weigh equally is itself the premise in dispute.
*Rejoinder.* Then for a reader who holds that axiom, the scale tips the other way. The text's conclusion grants exactly that ("An honest reader may weigh it differently").
*Verdict:* **Held**, as an honest accounting. The weight is real and remains on the scale, which is where the text puts it.

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**As written: NONE.**

I pressed for a Fatal or Serious objection at three points: the double-effect tu quoque (Exchange 3), the debunking asymmetry (Exchange 2), and A4's species line (Exchange 4). Each time the text, or Catholic teaching consistent with it, answers at least to parity. The deepest disputes (aggregation against the separateness of persons, and worth by kind against worth by capacity) are standoffs the text openly declares.

**Minor:**
- **M1. Rule consequentialism misstated.** The C row treats Hooker as giving only reasons to *hold* firm rules, and then says the theory "reduces wronging to worsening" and that a slightly better telling would wrong no one. On Hooker's view an act is wrong when it breaks the optimal code *even if it does better this time*. The true charge is that the code, and so the claim, is grounded in the total. Hooker's code also includes a rule to prevent disaster, so at large numbers his line bends too (R1–R3).
- **M2. Replaceability overstated.** A utilitarian *can* say "it is good that you exist," because your life goes well. What it cannot say is that you could not be replaced without loss by an equally happy life (R4).
- **M3. A7 charge mis-aimed.** Esotericism concerns publication and others' beliefs, not believing against evidence oneself, and the Church also permits withholding the truth (CCC 2489). The sharp contrast is lying: the Church forbids it by its nature (CCC 2485), while utilitarianism permits it in principle (R5).
- **M4. A6's genealogical parity cites the weak history.** Kin and reciprocity explain partial regard, which de Lazari-Radek and Singer already set aside (R6).
- **M5. "No one may take what is not his to take"** lacks "directly," so read literally it forbids what the Church permits under double effect. The shape of the line (aiming against foreseeing) should be credited to the means principle the book shares with Kant (R7).
- **M6. "Least predicts" (A4)** should be explicitly about intra-human equality, since the same form of argument underwrites the utilitarian axiom (R8).
- **M7. CCC 2269 paraphrase (A6).** The Catechism's wording is closer to "a scandalous injustice and a grave offense" than to "a grave injustice." This is not my school's point, but it is accurate to check.

**Misstatements of my school:** M1 and M2 above (both partial). Everything else is accurate: Mill on slaves and women, Singer on animals and the distant poor, Hare's two levels, Sidgwick's esotericism and his resignation of the Trinity fellowship (1869), de Lazari-Radek and Singer's defense of secrecy (*Ratio*, 2010; verify), and Singer's expanding circle crediting reason.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):** aggregation against the separateness of persons in the transplant and magistrate cases; the absolute "at every number" against ex ante justifiability to each person; equal value of life across capacity (A4 and the conclusion); posthumous wrongs and the debt to the dead (W1(1)); assisted death at request (the book's claim, labeled as such); the weight of animal suffering in the evil section; double effect's closeness problem (shared by every deontologist).

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1 (C, Well-being row).**
Old: "But it reduces wronging to worsening."
New: "But it explains wronging only by worsening: even its rule form, which holds an act wrong when it breaks the rules best for all to accept, though it does better this time, grounds those rules, and so her claim, in the total."

**R2 (same row).**
Old: "counting wishes, it can blame the teller, but only because he could have done better overall, so that, had the telling brought a little more good to someone else, no one, she included, would have been wronged at all."
New: "counting wishes, it can blame the teller, but on its act form only because he could have done better overall, so that, had the telling brought a little more good to someone else, no one, she included, would have been wronged at all; on its rule form he broke a rule best for all to keep, and her claim is the rule's, held for the sake of the total, not hers in her own right."

**R3 (same row).**
Old: "It makes every person, in principle, someone who may be deliberately killed for better totals (A4, A6)."
New: "It makes every person, in principle, someone who may be deliberately killed for better totals: case by case in its act form, and in its rule form whenever the numbers grow large enough to call on its rule to prevent disaster (A4, A6)."

**R4 (same row).**
Old: "it can say that it is good that a happy life exists, not that it is good that *you* do, for one such life may in principle replace another (W3)."
New: "it can say that it is good that you exist, because your life goes well, but not that you could not be replaced without loss by another life that goes as well (W3)."

**R5 (same row).**
Old: "And it makes truthfulness a servant of the total: where a falsehood, believed or left in place, would do more good, it must in principle favor the falsehood (A7). Some of its masters have allowed that its own truth might be better kept from the public (Sidgwick; de Lazari-Radek and Singer defend that secrecy still)."
New: "And it makes truthfulness a servant of the total: where a lie, or a falsehood induced or left in place, would do more good, it must in principle favor it (A7). Some of its masters have allowed that its own truth might be better kept from the public (Sidgwick; de Lazari-Radek and Singer defend that secrecy still). The Church too allows that the truth need not be told to one who has no right to know it (CCC 2489); she never allows the lie (CCC 2485)."

**R6 (A6).**
Old: "and the regard for the many that would spend the one has a history of its own, in kin and in favors returned. A history settles neither."
New: "and the regard for the many that would spend the one has a history of its own: sympathy learned among kin and those who return favors, carried outward by the same reasoning to consistency that carries the line. A history settles neither. (Those who hold that impartial concern for all alone survives such histories, as de Lazari-Radek and Singer do, defend what this book holds too, that each one's good counts as much as anyone's (A4); the step from there to spending one for the sum is a further premise, and it is the one the surgeon's case denies.)"

**R7 (W1(2)).**
Old: "No one may take what is not his to take, even to keep others from taking more."
New: "No one may directly take what is not his to take, even to keep others from taking more. (Why the line runs at aiming and not at foreseeing, Kant's 'never merely as a means' says as well as this book; what the Creator adds is why it holds at every number.)"

**R8 (A4).**
Old: "So this is the belief that an account of us as built only for survival least predicts."
New: "So this is, of all the beliefs in A, the one that an account of us as built only for survival least predicts: not concern for many, which can grow from concern for our own, but equal worth in the one who can give nothing back, as much as in the one who can give the most."

**R9 (A6, CCC 2269 paraphrase).**
Old: "and a society that accepts murderous famines without trying to remedy them commits a grave injustice (CCC 2269)."
New: "and a society that accepts murderous famines without trying to remedy them commits a scandalous injustice and a grave offense (CCC 2269)."

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights.**
1. **Sidgwick's dualism of practical reason.** The greatest utilitarian ended *The Methods of Ethics* unable to reconcile rational egoism with rational benevolence. He wrote that without some assurance of a moral order in the universe, the "Cosmos of Duty" seemed reduced to a "Chaos" (Concluding Chapter; verify). This is a secular rationalist's own witness to W2's claim that "this law holds together." Use it modestly: Sidgwick concluded with uncertainty, not with theism, and de Lazari-Radek and Singer answer him by debunking egoism.
2. **"The point of view of the universe."** The strongest secular impartialism names its standard as a standpoint no one occupies. The book may ask, as a clue and not an argument, whose point of view that is. The utilitarian will answer that it is an idealization, like the frictionless plane. The book should grant that reading.
3. **Meet the survival lottery head-on.** John Harris's "The Survival Lottery" (1975) and ex ante contractualism (Johann Frick) give the strongest form of our challenge to A6: a policy that raises *everyone's* chance of living, including the one spent. The book's answer, that being made a lottery ticket for others' organs is to have one's life made a resource, deserves a sentence in A6 or W1(2).
4. **Name the precise point of agreement.** Bentham's dictum, which Mill reports as "everybody to count for one, nobody for more than one," and A4's "no one is worth less" coincide. The two accounts part at exactly two points: whether goods may be *summed* across persons so that one is spent for the sum, and whether a *life's* worth follows capacity. Stating this sharpens the case and shows fairness.
5. **Utilitarianism was born partly theological.** John Gay and William Paley grounded utility in God's will for human happiness (verify). This is not a genealogy to be used against us. It is a reminder that the dispute is not God against utility, but whether persons may be spent.
6. **Kolbe and the firefighter.** We will say that self-sacrifice for others and asking for death differ only in their consequences. The book's distinction (laying down a life and accepting death, versus aiming at it) is already stated well. A sentence on why the *aim* matters to *her* good, and not just to the agent's purity, would forestall the "killer's heart" retort.

**Lines worth keeping.**
- The secret told to the stranger and the letter blown into the street: the strongest single case against my school in the text.
- "Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent."
- "The absolute can cost lives, even many. Without it, no innocent is safe, in principle, from being spent."
- "Equal worth is not equal duty."
- "Weighing is not a license" (with CCC 2269). My school would applaud the indirect-homicide line on famine.
- "A history settles neither."
- "So for one weighing by reason alone, not seeing God's reasons is weak evidence that He has none. It is not no evidence."
- The Sidgwick resignation sentence: generous, and fair if R5 is adopted.
