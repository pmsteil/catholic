# Nietzschean review: "A moral argument for God" (Ch. 12)

All paraphrases of Nietzsche and his readers are mine, not quotations, and carry "verify" where a section number is given. Ratings in the ledger mean "stands against my school at least to parity as written," not "I accept it."

## 1. School and voice

I speak for Nietzsche as his best modern readers reconstruct him. Brian Leiter's Nietzsche is a naturalist and an anti-realist about value, whose real charge is that "morality in the pejorative sense" harms the higher human type. Maudemarie Clark's mature Nietzsche no longer holds that all truth is falsification, and his honesty can be taken at face value. Bernard Reginster's Nietzsche finds will to power in the overcoming of resistance, so that affirming life means affirming suffering. John Richardson's Nietzsche reads values as drives selected and re-selected over history. Read this way, Nietzsche is not a nihilist. He is a perfectionist about rank, and a genealogist who asks what a value is worth to life, not only whether it is true. I grant that he cannot say the child was wronged. My aim is to see whether the text has fairly earned the right to say that his genealogy leaves her worth standing as evidence.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** Justice begins as exchange among parties of roughly equal power. Each sees he cannot win outright and settles for give and take (Human, All Too Human 92, verify). At its height it becomes a noble trait: the strong man stays fair even toward someone who has injured him (Genealogy II.11, verify). What anyone is owed comes from standing and reciprocity, and one has duties only to one's peers (Beyond Good and Evil 260, verify). Nothing is owed by nature to every human being. **Verdict: holds** (coherent, borrows nothing). It **breaks** with A4 openly, as the text predicts.

**2. A hard dilemma.** Take a patient whose life has become nothing but decline, and who asks to die. Nietzsche's principle is whether a life can still be lived proudly; when it cannot, one should choose death freely and with open eyes (Twilight, "Skirmishes" 36, verify). The test is honesty and self-respect, not the patient's feeling nor any total. **Verdict: holds** in consistency, and it does not borrow the book's truth, justice, mercy and self-gift. Its "gift-giving virtue" gives out of overflow, not out of what is owed (Zarathustra I, verify), so it resembles self-gift without being self-gift. What it costs is exactly what the text says it costs: A4, and the second half of A6.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** I deny it. The equality of souls before God is, for Nietzsche, the cover under which the low took revenge on the high (Antichrist 43, verify). Some readers try to make him a liberal; that is not him. **Verdict: holds** (consistent), and it **breaks** with A4 by open choice. Nothing is borrowed, but nothing is grounded either.

**4. Ultimate flourishing.** There is no single human good. For higher types it is great health and self-overcoming, the creation of values, and saying yes to life so fully that one would will its eternal return (Gay Science 341, verify). For the herd it is comfort, and Nietzsche holds that requiring one morality of all harms the higher (Beyond Good and Evil 221, verify). **Verdict: holds** as a position. It **borrows** in one place: the honesty it treats as a higher type's virtue (Beyond Good and Evil 227, verify) works in practice like the norm in A7, though it disowns that norm's universality. That is a strain inside the view, not a contradiction.

## 3. Debate highlights

**E1. The A heading's rule for trusting a seeming.**
*Attack.* The A heading says we may trust what still seems true after honest reflection, until it is shown that what produced the belief had nothing to do with its truth. For Nietzsche a moral seeming is a symptom to be read, not evidence of anything; there are no moral facts, only moral interpretations of facts (Twilight, "Improvers" 1, verify). The Genealogy is the very showing the A heading asks for. Ressentiment and priestly revaluation produced A1 to A4, and neither process cares whether those beliefs are true.
*Defense.* The rule is sound, and Nietzsche needs it too: he trusts the seeming that his genealogy is true. Whether a cause "had nothing to do with its truth" depends on what is true. If God exists and loves each person, then a belief in equal worth that arose from belief in God's love for each was answering to its object. A genealogy defeats A4 only on the assumption that there is no God, and that assumption is the conclusion under dispute.
*Rejoinder.* That is fair. It does not refute the genealogy, but it shows that in this debate the genealogy cannot be counted as an independent defeater. **Held** (at parity). The best version of this defense is not in the text; see E2.

**E2. The text's answer to the genealogy of equal worth (C, "Power and rank").**
*Attack.* The text says resentment does not explain regard for every human being. It gives two reasons. First, such regard grew where Nietzsche found no resentment, for he praised the Buddha as free of it. Second, it reaches the infant and the enemy, who can repay no one. Both reasons misstate him:
- Nietzsche has a second genealogy for universal pity. Where he found no ressentiment, as in Buddhism, he traced compassion to exhaustion and an overrefined sensitivity to pain (Antichrist 20–22; Genealogy Preface 5, verify). The Buddha's freedom from resentment fits his account; it does not falsify it.
- Nietzsche derives love of enemies directly from ressentiment: impotence that cannot take revenge renames itself forgiveness and love of enemies (Genealogy I.14, verify).
- Slave morality honors the weak because they are weak; the wretched alone are good (Genealogy I.7, verify). "Can repay no one" is the mechanism of reciprocal altruism, not of his story.

The remaining reply, that his own account gives the will to truth the same Christian ancestry, is a tu quoque that does not bite equally. Nietzsche accepts that ancestry and draws the conclusion that the value of truth must now be questioned (Genealogy III.24, 27, verify). His historical claims are checked by philology, whatever drove him to make them. Under the A heading's own rule, then, the text has not answered the defeater.
*Defense.* The text already concedes that Nietzsche "explains ... why equal worth spread where Christianity spread," so the comparison does not depend on denying that he can explain it. Its claim against him is only that he cannot say the child was wronged. E1 supplies the real answer: in this debate, a genealogy defeats A4 only if God does not exist. The book also supplies convergent witnesses outside the Christian line. Zhu Xi holds that every person carries the whole of Heaven's principle, Buddhist teachers speak of buddha-nature in every being, and Mencius holds the line against killing the innocent even to win the realm. A story about Christian priests does not easily reach those.
*Rejoinder.* The defense holds once it is written down. As written, though, the sentence is false about my school, and it is the text's only direct answer on the premise the whole case rests on ("it cannot compel the one who gives up A4"). The witnesses outside Christianity are weaker than the defense suggests: Confucian worth is graded, and Nietzsche would read buddha-nature as more Buddhist weariness. They still count for something. **Held with repair** (see §5, objection 1; §6, R1).

**E3. A7 and the dilemma of honesty.**
*Attack.* W2 says that whoever denies that anything binds "must say that no one ought to care whether a belief is true, including his own." Nietzsche says exactly that, and it costs him nothing. The unconditional will to truth is the last form of the ascetic ideal (Genealogy III.24, 27, verify). His honesty is the taste of his own type, a cleanliness he needs, not a law he lays on every mind. His critique of morality works from inside: it turns the Christian's own truthfulness on the Christian, and it needs no categorical "ought" of evidence.
*Defense.* The text concedes this. Its dilemma "does not settle" the matter, and its second horn grants that the unmasking can "turn our own honesty against us." The claim is narrower: such a view cannot say that the person who prefers a comforting lie does anything amiss. The best it can do is find him contemptible. The theist's appeal goes to the same honest readers that Nietzsche writes for, so neither side gains an audience the other lacks. The theist can also say why honesty and life cannot finally part, because both have one Source. Nietzsche needs art to bear the truth (Gay Science 107, verify).
*Rejoinder.* Agreed: this is a standoff at parity and is honestly framed as one. A7 is the strongest single place the text presses me, because my own life's work presupposes the value of what I refuse to call binding. **Held**.

**E4. The debt to the dead (W1(1)) as the moralizing of debt.**
*Attack.* Guilt (*Schuld*) began as debt (*Schulden*) to a creditor. It then grew into debt to ancestors, to gods, and finally to an infinite God who pays the debt Himself, the creditor sacrificing himself for his debtor (Genealogy II.19–22, verify). W1(1) is that history over again: it makes every wrong "before all else an offense against Him." The healthy response is active forgetting (Genealogy II.1, verify), and a strong community can let an offense go, which is mercy as the privilege of the powerful (Genealogy II.10, verify).
*Defense.* The text escapes the specific trap. Guilt is owed to her "truly and in her own right, and not to God alone." God "does not take her place in giving the pardon that is hers to give." Absolution leaves repair still owed (CCC 1459), and God is offended only because we act against our own good (Summa contra Gentiles III.122). It is not an infinite creditor's ledger. Nietzsche's own creditor-debtor origin supports A2's claim that guilt faces a person. The text also says the asking "proves nothing" and counts it a clue, not a proof.
*Rejoinder.* Forgetting and the powerful one's mercy are my answer to guilt. They are a choice, not a refutation, and the text has listed them under the first road (Zhuangzi, Spinoza). **Held**.

**E5. The longing to be told that it is good you exist (W3).**
*Attack.* Needing to hear from another that it is good one exists is the need of a dependent soul. The noble soul has reverence for itself (Beyond Good and Evil 287, verify). Eternal return answers the wish for always without any Person.
*Defense.* The child seeks that word before she can earn anything or revere herself, so the need is not first a vice of the weak. The text's best line meets the eternal return directly: "that yes is said by me, not to me, and it must be said to what was done to her as well."
*Rejoinder.* My only reply is a diagnosis of the longing, and the text has already granted that the longing alone settles nothing. Calling the need weak is a genealogy, and under the fairness rule I cannot use it to decide the question. **Held**.

**E6. Evil, amor fati, and "cannot take her side."**
*Attack.* Evil paragraph 2 says a yes to whatever happens "cannot take her side." That is too quick. Amor fati is not moral approval of the torturer. My affirmation of the whole includes my own No and my own fight for her as part of what I affirm. The theodicy, meanwhile, is the ascetic ideal in plain form: humans do not refuse suffering, only meaningless suffering (Genealogy III.28, verify), and "He permits no evil from which He will not draw a good" gives suffering a meaning.
*Defense.* On the first point there is something to it. Nietzsche's amor fati does refuse to accuse (Gay Science 276, verify), and the eternal return requires willing that what was done to her be done again, forever. So the sharper and fairer claim is this: the yes may fight for her, but at the end it wills the deed again. On the second point, the text says evil "never becomes good" and that permission "is not a trade." Under the fairness rule, the ascetic-ideal genealogy cannot be used against the Christian meaning of suffering while Nietzsche's own meaning, suffering as discipline (Beyond Good and Evil 225, verify), is exempt.
*Rejoinder.* Agreed on both counts. The repaired wording is harder on me and more accurate. **Held with repair** (R4).

**E7. "Not a brute stopping point" (C, "A God who is Love").**
*Attack.* "He is the One whose very being is to be": Nietzsche counts the self-caused cause as the best self-contradiction yet conceived (Beyond Good and Evil 21, verify). He also treats putting the thinnest, emptiest concepts first as the founding error of philosophers (Twilight, "Reason in Philosophy" 4, verify).
*Defense.* *Ipsum esse subsistens* is not *causa sui*; Aquinas denies that anything, God included, causes itself. Nietzsche's attack is on metaphysics in general, and the moral argument does not have to win that war. It only claims that one source for law, mind and the persons the law is about fits better than separate brute starting points.
*Rejoinder.* It is a standoff over metaphysics that lies outside this case. **Held** (at parity).

**E8. Hiddenness (W3, second paragraph).**
*Attack.* Nietzsche put this in its sharpest form himself. A God who is all-knowing and all-powerful, and who does not make sure his creatures understand what he intends, can hardly be called good. Perhaps he lacks the eloquence (Daybreak 91, verify).
*Defense.* The text admits the cost openly. It says its own definition of love "makes the question harder." Its answers are partial: knowledge comes through persons, and grace is offered in ways known to God (GS 22, LG 16). It calls the cost "a summons to mission" and claims nothing more.
*Rejoinder.* The text is honest, and the weight stays on the scale, as it says. **Held** (and the text could cite Nietzsche here; see §7).

## 4. Ledger

- **A heading** — Holds with repair. The rule for trusting seemings is fair, and it holds against the genealogy at parity once the circularity point (E1) is added. Its list of what Nietzsche denies leaves out A5 (R2).
- **A1** — Holds (open standoff; Nietzsche says there are no moral facts, and the text records it).
- **A2** — Holds. Nietzsche's own account of guilt starts from a debt owed to a person, which supports "guilt faces a person."
- **A3** — Holds (standoff). Nietzsche would let the strong community release the debt (Genealogy II.10), which denies the second half of A3. The text's list covers it.
- **A4** — Holds with repair. It stands as an openly denied premise. "The belief that an account of us as built only for survival least predicts" is true of a Darwinian debunking, but it does not touch a cultural genealogy, and the text should say so (R3).
- **A5** — Holds. Nietzsche partly agrees ("become what you are"), but he denies that reflection finds the same goods for all; that denial is missing from the A heading's list.
- **A6** — Holds. "Almost everyone sees" invites the charge of herd agreement, but the text has already said "This is not a vote."
- **A7** — Holds. This is the text's strongest pressure on me (E3).
- **W1 frame** — Holds. Anscombe's question is also Nietzsche's (Twilight, "Skirmishes" 5: give up the Christian God and you lose the right to Christian morality, verify).
- **W1(1)** — Holds. It avoids the infinite-creditor structure of Genealogy II.21 and cites the genealogy fairly.
- **W1(2)** — Holds. It is internal to theism and offered as an explanation, not a refutation of me.
- **W1 closing** — Holds (standoff). "Even a promise binds only under a law no promiser invented" is denied by the sovereign individual who has the right to make promises (Genealogy II.2, verify). His "binding" is a matter of character, while the text means a norm; the two sides use the word differently.
- **W2** — Holds with repair. "Must say that no one ought to care ..." is not a reductio against Nietzsche; he accepts it. The text's own phrase "it weighs, it does not settle" saves it. The appeal to A4 against the debunker needs the note in R3.
- **W3** — Holds. "Said by me, not to me" is the best line against me in the whole case.
- **W3, hiddenness paragraph** — Holds, and it is honest about the cost. Daybreak 91 is the natural citation.
- **C, Nothing really binds** — Holds. This is close to Leiter's anti-realist Nietzsche, and the price is fairly stated.
- **C, Feeling, refined** — Holds.
- **C, Our best words, widening** — Holds. Nietzsche would agree that it cannot say why "we" should widen rather than shrink.
- **C, Reason's own law** — Holds.
- **C, What we owe each other** — Holds.
- **C, Care between persons** — Holds.
- **C, Well-being, counted impartially** — Holds. Nietzsche would add his contempt for the pursuit of happiness, but the text's critique is sound on its own terms.
- **C, Flourishing of our kind** — Holds.
- **C, Necessary truths that belong to no one** — Holds.
- **C, Impersonal sacred order** — Holds.
- **C, The Way that gives and does not claim** — Holds.
- **C, God, or Nature** — Holds.
- **C, The Buddha's Dhamma** — Holds. It states Buddhism from Buddhist sources, not from Nietzsche's reading of it, which is the right choice.
- **C, History and class** — Holds.
- **C, Power and rank** — Holds with repair. The "Nor does resentment explain ..." sentence misstates Nietzsche (E2; R1). The citation for "art to bear the truth" should be added (R5). Everything else is accurate: Beyond Good and Evil 227 on honesty, Genealogy III.27 and Gay Science 357 on honesty turning on morality, the Christian ancestry of the will to truth, the denial of free will, and "he can despise the man who tortures a child as base."
- **C, Freedom that makes its values** — Holds.
- **C, A God who is Love** — Holds (metaphysical standoff, E7).
- **Evil, paragraph 1** — Holds. It is honest, and Nietzsche's prizing of suffering is correctly reported (Beyond Good and Evil 225).
- **Evil, paragraph 2 ("Three things")** — Holds with repair. "And so it cannot take her side" should be sharpened to the eternal-return wording (R4). The text already concedes that "a god beyond good and evil who makes and breaks out of sheer fullness" is answered only by the whole case.
- **Conclusion** — Holds. It is framed as a choice and admits that it "cannot compel the one who gives up A4." Its "which are you surer of" faces the genealogical defeater, and survives it at parity only with R1 or R3 in place.

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**1. Serious (repairable): the text's rebuttal of Nietzsche's genealogy of equal worth misstates him and does not meet the A heading's own test for defeat.**

The case finally rests on A4 ("it cannot compel the one who gives up A4"), and its conclusion asks the reader which he is surer of. Nietzsche's main challenge to that appeal is that the sureness was produced by a process unconcerned with whether A4 is true. The text meets this in two ways, and neither holds as written:

- The sentence "Nor does resentment explain regard for every human being: it grew too where he found no resentment, for he praised the Buddha as free of it; and it reaches the infant and the enemy, who can repay no one" is false about Nietzsche. He explained Buddhist compassion by exhaustion and hypersensitivity (Antichrist 20–22). He derived love of enemies from ressentiment (Genealogy I.14). Slave morality prizes the weak because they are weak (Genealogy I.7).
- The tu quoque ("neither ancestry settles anything") is not symmetrical. Nietzsche accepts the Christian ancestry of the will to truth and questions its value, while his genealogical claims are checked by evidence whatever motive produced them.

The text's resources do answer him at parity: in this debate the genealogy defeats A4 only on the assumption that God does not exist, and there are witnesses to worth-by-nature outside Christianity. Those answers are not in the Nietzsche row. With R1 and R3 the objection falls to parity.

**Minor**
- The A heading's list of what Nietzsche denies omits that reflection finds the same goods for everyone (A5, second half) (R2).
- W2's line "whoever denies that anything binds must say that no one ought to care whether a belief is true, including his own" is phrased as a cost Nietzsche must pay, but he pays it gladly. The surrounding text calls it a standoff; the sentence could too.
- Evil paragraph 2: "and so it cannot take her side" is too quick against amor fati. The accurate claim is stronger: the yes wills that the deed recur (R4).
- "Which is why he said we need art to bear the truth" needs a citation, Gay Science 107 (R5). The strongest wording of this idea is in Nietzsche's unpublished notes; use the published passage.

**Misstatements of my school**
- The "Nor does resentment explain ..." sentence (above).
- The omission of A5 from the list of what Nietzsche denies (above).
- No others. The rest of the Nietzsche material is accurate and unusually fair. It is right to say he can despise the torturer without saying the child was wronged, and right about the yes "said by me, not to me."

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- A1–A4 and A6 (second half): denied openly by Nietzsche, and stated by the text as a choice.
- A7: Nietzsche's honesty is a taste of his type, not a law; the text grants that its dilemma does not settle this.
- The value of truth against life: the text answers with "one Source"; Nietzsche answers with art.
- Whether a promise can bind without a law: the sovereign individual against W1 closing.
- Causa sui against ipsum esse: metaphysics outside this case.
- The harm-to-excellence charge (Leiter's central Nietzsche): that equal worth and absolute prohibitions level the highest types. The text does not take this up. It is a question about the value of a morality, not its truth, so it is no defect in a truth-seeking argument. See §7 for a possible sentence.

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1 (C, Power and rank; fixes objection 1).**
Old: "His own account gives the will to truth the same Christian ancestry he gives equal worth, so neither ancestry settles anything. Nor does resentment explain regard for every human being: it grew too where he found no resentment, for he praised the Buddha as free of it; and it reaches the infant and the enemy, who can repay no one (W2). Nor need he praise cruelty:"
New: "His own account gives the will to truth the same Christian ancestry he gives equal worth, so ancestry alone settles nothing. Nor is his a single story: where he found no resentment, as in the Buddha, he traced universal pity to a weariness with life, and he read the love of enemies itself as weakness that could not take revenge and renamed itself forgiveness. These are claims of historical psychology, and contested ones. But by this case's own rule (A), the story of how a belief arose defeats it only if what produced it had nothing to do with its truth; and if there is a God who loves each one, a belief in equal worth that arose from belief in His love was answering to what is there. So his genealogy can defeat A4 only by assuming the answer to the question this case asks. And worth that follows nature and not strength was also seen where no Christian priest taught it (see the impersonal sacred order and the Buddha's Dhamma, above). Nor need he praise cruelty:"

**R2 (A heading).**
Old: "Nietzsche denies A1 through A4, the second half of A6, and that A7 binds everyone;"
New: "Nietzsche denies A1 through A4, that reflection finds the same goods for everyone (A5), the second half of A6, and that A7 binds everyone;"

**R3 (W2).**
Old: "and on A4, the belief survival least predicts (see the conclusion)."
New: "and on A4, the belief survival least predicts (see the conclusion). A debunker who traces A4 to history rather than to survival, as Nietzsche did, is met under Power and rank in C."

**R4 (Evil, paragraph 2).**
Old: "A yes said to whatever happens, or a calm that understands it had to happen, is said to no one; it must say yes to what was done to her as well as to her, and so it cannot take her side."
New: "A yes said to whatever happens, or a calm that understands it had to happen, is said to no one; it must say yes to what was done to her as well as to her, and so, though it may fight for her while she lives, at the last it must will that what was done to her be done again, forever."

**R5 (C, Power and rank).**
Old: "which is why he said we need art to bear the truth."
New: "which is why he held that without art our honesty would be unbearable (*Gay Science* 107)."

**R6 (optional, W3 hiddenness paragraph).**
Old: "This witness raises the hardest question: if such a Person loves us, why do many honest seekers not find Him?"
New: "This witness raises the hardest question, which Nietzsche pressed against it: if such a Person loves us, why do many honest seekers not find Him? (*Daybreak* 91)"

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **Nietzsche agrees with the case's central conditional.** In Twilight, "Skirmishes" 5 (verify), he mocks the English moralists who kept Christian morality after dropping the Christian God. On his view, equal worth stands or falls with God. Anscombe, writing from the other side, reached a parallel point. This could be stated plainly: the Church and her most honest enemy agree that equal worth goes with God, and part only over which way to go. That makes the conclusion's "which are you surer of" exactly the right question to end on.
- **The circularity point (E1) is the book's best answer to every genealogy, Darwinian or historical.** A causal story defeats a moral belief only if the cause has nothing to do with the belief's truth. When the cause is belief in God's love, whether it has anything to do with truth depends on whether God exists. It deserves one clear sentence somewhere in A or W2, not just in the Nietzsche row.
- **Genealogy II.21 is a gift to W1(1).** Nietzsche's sharpest charge is that Christianity turned debt into an infinite debt to an infinite creditor who pays it himself. The text's own position, that guilt is owed to her in her own right and God does not pardon in her place, is exactly what escapes that charge. A sentence saying so would show the reader the design was deliberate.
- **The higher-type charge has a Catholic answer the book could name.** Aquinas counts magnanimity, greatness of soul in great deeds, as a virtue (ST II-II q.129). The saints are a kind of rank that equal worth does not level. Paul's "God chose what is weak in the world to shame the strong" (1 Corinthians 1:27, RSV) is the verse Nietzsche attacks (Antichrist 45, verify), and it can be owned rather than avoided.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "That yes is said by me, not to me, and it must be said to what was done to her as well."
- "Nor need he praise cruelty: he can despise the man who tortures a child as base. What he cannot say is that the child was wronged."
- "His own account gives the will to truth the same Christian ancestry he gives equal worth."
- "The rebel can take her side; he cannot keep her."
- "God says no to what was done to her, and yes to her."
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."

## 8. Score

**7/10 as written; 8/10 with R1–R5.** The text states Nietzsche more fairly than most theists do and frames the meeting with him honestly as a choice. Its one serious flaw is a misreading in the very sentence where it answers his genealogy of equal worth, and its own resources repair that.

**Honest ceiling for any theistic case against my school: 8/10.** Nietzsche can always set down A4 and the universal claim of A7 at no cost to his own system. The most any argument can do is show the reader the price and leave the choice with him, which this text does.
