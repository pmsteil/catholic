# Censor's report: "A moral argument for God" (Chapter 12, *What Is Love?*)

## Scope

A pre-imprimatur read of /tmp/claude-0/lab9/ARGUMENT.md, done the way a censor deputed by a bishop would do it. I read the whole text sentence by sentence: the Form note, A heading, A1–A7, W1 (frame, (1), (2), closing), W2, W3 (both paragraphs), every C row, the section on evil (both paragraphs) and the Conclusion. I checked it against Scripture (RSVCE), the Catechism, the councils (Florence, Trent, Vatican I, Vatican II), the encyclicals and other magisterial documents, and St. Thomas. The question at each point was whether anything is contrary to faith or morals, or so imprecise that a faithful reader could take it in a sense contrary to faith or morals. Points of philosophy, and the accuracy of what the text says about non-Christian authors, are outside a censor's remit. I note them only where they touch doctrine. I verified citations on vatican.va where the site could be reached. Section 2 sorts every citation by how it was checked.

## Bottom line

One Error:

- **E1 (C, "Reason's own law," the clemency sentence).** The text says God "keeps in both halves" Kant's rule that a ruler may never remit what one subject did to another, and that "the one forgiven must still ... make satisfaction for his sins (CCC 1459)." This is stated of everyone God forgives. Baptism is the case it gets wrong. Baptism remits "all sins ... as well as all punishment for sin" (CCC 1263). The Council of Florence (*Exultate Deo*) teaches that no satisfaction is to be imposed on the baptized for past sins, and so does St. Thomas (ST III q.68 a.5, whose ad 3 keeps the duty of restitution but not satisfaction). The phrase "both halves" also implies that God does not remit sins committed between persons, which contradicts the text's own "He forgives whole." The fix is one sentence (see section 3, R1).

Nothing else rises to an Error. The items marked "Needs care" below are corrections a censor would ask for, or strongly recommend, before the nihil obstat. None of them, taken in context, states anything contrary to faith or morals.

## 3. Replacement wording

**R1 (E1, Kant row).**
Old: "And his rule for clemency, that a ruler may remit a crime against himself but never what one subject did to another, God keeps in both halves: the sin, first an offense against Himself, He forgives whole (CCC 1440–1441); but not as impunity, for the one forgiven must still repair what he can and make satisfaction for his sins (CCC 1459), and what was done to her He does not pardon in her place."
New: "And his rule for clemency, that a ruler may remit a crime against himself but never what one subject did to another, God answers in His own way: the sin, first an offense against Himself, He forgives whole (CCC 1440–1441), and in Baptism He remits with it all the punishment it deserved (CCC 1263); yet His pardon is not indifference to her, for the one forgiven must still repair what he can, and after Baptism must also make satisfaction for his sins (CCC 1459), and the pardon that is hers to give He does not give in her place."

**R2 (N1, 1 Samuel 15).**
Old: "Scripture tells of wars in which Israel was told to destroy whole peoples, children too (1 Samuel 15:3), and some great theologians held that God, as Lord of life, could so command without injustice. The Church reads those pages in the light of Christ, as part of a revelation suited to the moral level of distant times, which contains \"things which are incomplete and temporary\" and recounts violence and massacre without always condemning them (*Dei Verbum* 15; *Verbum Domini* 42). This book reads them as the record of a people God was still teaching, not as His approval of killing children, and measures them by Christ,"
New: "Scripture tells of wars in which Israel was told to destroy whole peoples, children too (1 Samuel 15:3). St. Thomas, with others before him, held that God, as Lord of life, could so command without injustice, since every life is His gift and the one who obeyed would act by God's right and not his own (ST I-II q.94 a.5 ad 2; II-II q.64 a.6 ad 1). That is the principle just stated, and it gives no man a right of his own over innocent life. The Church has not settled how those pages are to be read. She asks that they be read in their historical and literary setting and in the light of Christ, as part of a revelation suited to the cultural and moral level of distant times, which contains \"things which are incomplete and temporary\" and recounts violence and massacre without explicitly denouncing them (*Dei Verbum* 15; *Verbum Domini* 42). This book reads them as the record of a people God was still teaching, not as a warrant for anyone, then or now, to kill children in His name, for He has no pleasure in the death of any one (Ezekiel 18:32), and it measures them by Christ,"

**R3 (N2, the unbaptized infant).**
Old: "and if she dies unbaptized, the Church, which holds that God is not bound by His sacraments, entrusts her to His mercy and hopes that there is a way of salvation for her (CCC 1257, 1261)."
New: "and if she dies unbaptized, the Church, which holds that Baptism is necessary for salvation and that God, who bound salvation to it, is not Himself bound by His sacraments, entrusts her to His mercy and hopes that there is a way of salvation for her; all the more urgently she asks that no child be kept from Baptism (CCC 1257, 1261)."

**R4 (N3, grace and freedom).**
Old: "why one will is won by His grace and another refuses it to the end, the Church does not explain, and she lets her theologians differ over it."
New: "why one will is won by His grace and another refuses it to the end, the Church does not explain, and she lets her theologians differ over it. She holds only that the one who is saved is saved by God's gift, and the one who is lost is lost by his own willful turning away and his persistence in it to the end, never by any lack in God's will to save him (CCC 1037; 1 Timothy 2:4)."

**R5 (N4, reason and the certainty of faith).** After "Evil weighs against that conclusion, heavily; in this book's judgment it does not overturn it." insert:
"(This is reason's weighing, offered to every reader. The believer's own assent does not wait on it: he believes because of the authority of God who reveals, and faith is more certain than all human knowledge, CCC 156–157.)"

**R6 (N5, A6).** After "and his asking never makes it right for anyone to kill him." insert:
"(Grave anguish or fear can lessen the responsibility of one who takes his own life, and the Church does not despair of his salvation, but prays for him: CCC 2282–2283.)"

**R7 (N6, W1 frame).**
Old: "this book agrees: guilt is owed to her, truly and in her own right, and not to God alone (A2)."
New: "this book agrees: pardon must be asked of her and amends made to her, truly and in her own right, and not of God alone (A2)."

**R8 (N7, W1 frame).**
Old: "(Not every *ought* wrongs someone when it is broken, A7; these two rest on the wrongs that do.)"
New: "(Not every *ought* wrongs another human person when it is broken, though every sin offends God, A7; these two rest on the wrongs done to a person.)"

**R9 (N8, evil section).**
Old: "and so we must answer for the child, as anyone who loved her and could have spared her would have to."
New: "and so we who say it must give an account of the child, the question we would put to anyone who loved her and could have spared her."
Old: "The best rivals rightly ask of Him what they ask of us, whether it could be justified to her;"
New: "The best rivals ask of Him what they ask of us, whether it could be justified to her, and reason may fairly put that question, though God is answerable to no creature's court (Job 38:1–4);"

**R10 (N9, W1(2)).**

New: "Not every child is wanted by those who conceived her; every child was wanted by the One who made her, before anyone saw her (Jeremiah 1:5)."

## Verdict

Not quite as it stands. A censor would be bound to return it for the one correction in E1, a single sentence in the Kant row that, as worded, contradicts the Church's teaching on what Baptism remits. With R1 applied, I would expect a nihil obstat. Nothing in the text is contrary to faith or morals in intent, and on the points the bishop's office would look at hardest it is unusually exact: the authorship of the natural law, CCC 2258, euthanasia and ordinary care, legitimate defense, the treatment of other religions, and the refusal to presume unbelievers culpable. R2 through R6 are the corrections a careful censor would also ask for before signing, the 1 Samuel passage (R2) and the infant and Baptism (R3) above all. R7 through R12 are refinements.
