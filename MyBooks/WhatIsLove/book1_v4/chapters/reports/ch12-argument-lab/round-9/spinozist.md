# Blind review: the Spinozist / naturalist pantheist school

## 1. School and voice

I speak for *Deus sive Natura*: one substance, necessary and infinite, of which every finite thing, this woman included, is a mode. My God thinks, since Thought is one of His attributes, and in Him there is an idea of everything that follows from His nature, including the essence of this body and this mind (E2p3, E2p8, E5p22; verify). But He has no will distinct from His intellect, no purposes and no passions, and He loves no one in the way one person loves another (E1p32, E1 Appendix, E5p17c; verify). Good and bad name what helps or hinders our striving to persevere in being and to understand (E4 Preface, E4d1–2). Freedom is acting from the necessity of one's own nature (E1d7). Blessedness is the intellectual love of God, which is God's own love of Himself as expressed through a human mind (E5p36; verify). I read Spinoza with Curley (texts), Garrett (the eternity of the mind, the conatus), and Della Rocca (the principle of sufficient reason pressed all the way), and I include the "religion" of Einstein, who believed in a God who shows himself in the lawful harmony of what exists and not in one concerned with human fates (1929 telegram; verify). I came to test one thing: whether "Someone, not something" is argued for, or assumed.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** In the strict sense, justice and injustice, desert, sin, exist only in a commonwealth: justice is the steady will to give each what civil law assigns him (E4p37s2; TTP ch. 16; verify). Beneath that is reason's own dictate: the one who lives by reason wants for every other human the good he wants for himself, understanding, because nothing is more useful to a human than a human who agrees with him in nature (E4p18s, E4p35, E4p37; verify). *Holds* as a derivation; it borrows nothing. But it *breaks* on "owed to her" in the text's sense: before law, I can say what reason counsels toward her, not what she can claim.

**2. A hard dilemma.** A dying friend in great pain asks me to end his life. I do not do it. No one aims at his own destruction from the necessity of his own nature; those who do are overcome by external causes (E4p20s; verify), and "the free man thinks of nothing less than death" (E4p67, a short phrase). I help him from reason, not from pity, which is a sadness and useless in the wise (E4p50s): relieving pain, staying, helping him to understand and to be at peace. The surgeon who would kill one patient for five: never, because the free man acts always in good faith, even to save his own life (E4p72), and reason cannot counsel what, counselled to all, would dissolve the trust on which human power depends. *Holds* without borrowing the book's terms. But my ground is the agent's understanding and the common good of reason, not a worth given to the one who would be killed. On the surgeon case I can say the deed is contrary to reason; I cannot say, as the text wants, that it wrongs *him* in a way that stands apart from any commonwealth.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** I deny "dignity" as an unconditional, equal moral property. I grant that each human is a singular essence that follows necessarily from God and is known in Him eternally, so her reality is not conferred by anyone, cannot be voted away, and cannot be lost. But reality is perfection, and perfection is power (E2d6, E4d8), and the mind whose body is capable of few things has less of what is eternal in it; Spinoza names the infant explicitly (E5p39s; verify). Political equality I hold as a civil good (TTP ch. 16 on democracy; verify). *Breaks* on "equal" for the newborn who will never think and the profoundly disabled; *holds* on "not conferred" and "not lost."

**4. Ultimate flourishing.** Blessedness: the third kind of knowledge, the intellectual love of God, and the peace of mind that comes from it; blessedness is not the reward of virtue but virtue itself (E5p32, E5p36, E5p42). Guiding principles: persevere in being; prefer adequate ideas to passions; joy over sadness; generosity and firmness of mind (E3p59s); good faith; a free commonwealth. The highest good is common and can be enjoyed by all equally, since knowledge is not diminished by being shared (E4p36). *Holds* coherently. It *breaks* for the infant who dies before understanding: on my account she reaches almost none of it, and no one can give it to her.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1. Does the text misstate my view of A1?**
*Attack.* The heading and the C row say Spinoza "denies that anything is a wrong before a people's laws" and list that against A1. That equivocates. I deny that anything is a *sin* (peccatum) or an *injustice* before law (E4p37s2). I do not deny that torturing a child for fun is bad for any human, whatever his culture: it springs from hatred, and hatred can never be good (E4p45; cruelty is defined at E3 Def. Aff. 38; verify). My good and bad are relative to human nature, not to opinion or custom. So I hold the substance of A1, "wrong even if every culture approved," in the form "contrary to reason for every human, even if every culture approved."
*Reply.* The text says A1 is "really wrong," and in A2–A3 it means wrong *done to someone*, which needs forgiving. Spinoza does deny that, outside a commonwealth.
*Rejoinder.* Then say so: I deny the wrong-done-to, not the wrong-for-everyone. As written, a reader will think my school is relativist about cruelty.
**Held with repair** (wording in §6).

**Exchange 2. Does W1(1) beg the question with "only someone can forgive"?**
*Attack.* "Guilt asks to be forgiven by her" already describes guilt as directed at a person who must still exist. I describe it otherwise: remorse is sadness with the idea of a deed we believe we did from free decision (E3 Def. Aff. 27), an inadequate idea; the cure is understanding (E4p54, E5p6). The witness assumes the personal reading of the phenomenon it uses.
*Reply.* The text does not claim proof. It says the request "proves nothing," that science explains why we would ask whether or not anyone hears, and that it weighs only "as much as the reader finds that a debt truly owed to her is one she must be able, one day, to receive." It also names my answer fairly ("remorse, a wound that understanding may quiet, as Spinoza taught").
*Rejoinder.* Granted. The witness is honestly conditional. My only complaint is that the closing parenthesis exempts the reader who thinks "owed" voices feeling, and I am not that reader: I am a rationalist who thinks "owed" is a civil notion. For me it also weighs little, for a different reason.
**Held** (the parenthesis could name both readers; Minor).

**Exchange 3. W1(2): "A law or an order gives a nature to every member of a kind; it does not want any one of them."**
*Attack.* That sentence, and the image of the moon in ten thousand rivers, does not fit my God. God is not a law over kinds; He is the immanent cause of the essence *and* existence of each singular thing (E1p25), and there is in Him an idea of the essence of *this* human body under the aspect of eternity (E5p22–23). She is not one river reflecting a common moon; she is a singular essence known eternally as herself. So the text's ground against impersonal rivals, "it can say each holds the whole nature, not that *this* one is wanted," is half wrong against me: I can say *this one*, eternally, and not by anyone's grant.
*Reply.* The text's operative word is *wanted*. W3 states my view exactly: the highest good "knows each of us only as a truth it holds and wants none of us." Knowing her as herself is not willing her for her own sake (GS 24). A worth that is "each one's own" is something you can share; a worth "wanted for her own sake" you cannot.
*Rejoinder.* Agreed that is the real hinge, and the text names it in W3. But W1(2) as written argues from kind-versus-individual, which misses me, and lets the reader think the individuality point does the work. It does not; the will point does.
**Held with repair.**

**Exchange 4. The central one: is "wanted" an explanation or anthropomorphism? Necessity versus will.**
*Attack.* To want is to desire, and desire implies lack (E3 Def. Aff. 1). The text answers with Aquinas: to love is to will another's good, and God wills ours "without gaining anything." But then press the principle of sufficient reason. Either God's choosing *this* world (and her) over none has a sufficient reason, and then it follows of necessity from His nature, which is my view; or it has none, and then it is a brute choice, which is the voluntarism the text disowns ("not a commander who picks rules"). "Willed for her own sake" is either my necessity under a warmer name or an arbitrariness that undercuts the very non-voluntarism of the Lawgiver paragraph (E1p33s2; Della Rocca; verify).
*Reply (the strongest the text and Catholic teaching allow).* Classical theism distinguishes: God wills His own goodness necessarily, and creatures freely, because no finite good adds anything to an infinite good, so none is required by it (ST I q.19 a.3). His will has *reasons*, fittingness and the sharing of goodness (ST I q.19 a.5; q.44 a.4), but no reason *compels*, since nothing outside Him can. That is neither necessity nor caprice: it is generosity, a gift that need not have been given. And the Church has defined it: God created by a will free from all necessity, not as necessarily as He loves Himself (Vatican I, *Dei Filius*, canons on God the Creator, 5). As for "wanted": the text never says God wants with lack; it says "to want to be wanted by One who lacks nothing." And her worth then has a ground mine does not: an end set for her, equal for all, by intention rather than by capacity.
*Rejoinder.* That is the oldest standoff between us, and I do not think either side wins it by argument. You pay with a contingency whose reason is not sufficient to its effect; I pay with a world in which nothing could have been otherwise, the child's suffering included. Neither price is a defect in the text, because the text does not argue this point at all; it asserts it in the final C row ("not from any need, for He lacks nothing, but to share His goodness"). It should know that this is where my school locates "Someone, not something," and say so in one line.
**Standoff at parity. Held** (not begged: the case's reason for preferring "wanted" is the equality datum, which is Exchange 5).

**Exchange 5. Equality: the newborn who will never think.**
*Attack.* Thomism grades too. Aquinas holds degrees of being and perfection among creatures, and degrees of glory among the blessed. Your "equal worth" is no more secure than my "equal in being a singular essence."
*Reply.* The text grades holiness and not worth ("the saints are higher than I am in how well they love, never in what they are worth"), and grounds equality in an equal *call* to one end, God Himself, which "does not wait on what anyone can do," and which God can give as gift to the infant who never thinks (CCC 1250, 1261, 1023). On your account, by Spinoza's own words, the infant's mind considered in itself is conscious of almost nothing of itself, of God or of things (E5p39s; verify), and its eternal part is correspondingly small. No one can give her the third kind of knowledge.
*Rejoinder.* That is true, and I will not dodge it. I can hold that she is not to be harmed, that reason wants her good, and that the commonwealth must protect her equally. I cannot say she is "for the same thing as you" in a way that is equally hers to receive. If A4's strong equality is a datum, the text explains it and I do not. My only defence is to deny the datum in its strong metaphysical form, and the conclusion's hillside test is aimed exactly there.
**Held.** This is where the text beats my school honestly.

**Exchange 6. "Its God loves men only in loving Himself, and wants no one."**
*Attack.* Fairness. Aquinas also says God wills things other than Himself for the sake of His own goodness as their end (ST I q.19 a.2), and that God loves creatures by willing them good (ST I q.20 a.2), which is one act with His love of Himself. "Only in loving Himself" is a jab that cuts the text's own God. And I say more than the text grants: the mind's intellectual love of God is the very love by which God loves Himself, and God's love toward men is one and the same as that (E5p36c; verify). Spinoza even says this is what Scripture calls glory (E5p36s; verify).
*Reply.* Aquinas adds what Spinoza denies: within the order God wills for His goodness, He wills each rational creature for its own sake and governs it for its own sake (SCG III.112; GS 24). The difference is not that one God loves Himself and the other loves us; it is that one wills each person for her own sake and the other wills nothing.
*Rejoinder.* Then that is what the row should say. "Only in loving Himself" invites a reader who knows Aquinas to suspect a double standard.
**Held with repair.**

**Exchange 7. Evil: "A calm that understands it had to happen ... cannot take her side."**
*Attack.* Misstatement. Understanding necessity is not approving. Cruelty remains bad (E4p45). The one who lives by reason works, as far as he can, to free others from suffering, from reason rather than pity (E4p50s; verify). I take her side against every cruelty that can still be prevented. And the weight on the other side is lighter for me than for the text: no one intended her pain, no one must answer for it, and no one is damned; blessedness is virtue itself (E5p42). The text's own section admits that hell is "the heaviest thing there."
*Reply.* The text concedes the asymmetry openly ("some rivals bear this whole weight more easily than we do"), names your God in the evil section, and does not claim to lift the weight; it claims the whole case outweighs it, and leaves the reader to weigh. On the past, your calm accepts what was done to her as following from the whole order (E4 Appendix 32), and that is what the text means: you can resist the next cruelty; toward the one already done you can only understand.
*Rejoinder.* Fair for the past. But "cannot take her side" is too broad. And the evil section is honest enough that I count it as weighing, not as an argument that fails.
**Held with repair.**

**Exchange 8. W2: does the "reach" of conscience favour a Person over my God?**
*Attack.* The reach problem, how minds built for survival track truths good for any mind, is solved on my account: the human mind is part of the infinite intellect of God (E2p11c), and there are common notions adequate in every mind (E2p38–40). One Source of law and mind, no Person needed.
*Reply.* The text grants exactly that: W2's parenthesis says those who find the one Source "in God or Nature, as Spinoza did, agree with this witness and part from this book in W1 and W3." W2 does not claim to reach Someone; it hands that to W1 and W3.
*Rejoinder.* Agreed. One terminological point: W2's last line contrasts "Someone and not something." My God is not a something; He thinks. The honest contrast with me is "Someone who wills, not a necessity that knows." The text's "not less than personal: knowing and loving are in Him more truly than in us" is half acceptable to me (knowing), and the dispute is over loving and willing.
**Held with repair** (Minor).

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**As written: NONE.**

No Fatal or Serious objection from my school stands. The text does not beg the question of "Someone, not something" against me: it rests its preference for a Person on (a) the equality of the newborn who will never think, which my school can match only by denying it, and (b) two clues it explicitly declines to count as evidence. Where my best argument (necessity versus free creation, the principle of sufficient reason) applies, it yields a standoff at parity, not a defect.

**Minor items**
1. W1(2): "A law or an order gives a nature to every member of a kind; it does not want any one of them" does not describe Spinoza's God, who causes and eternally knows singular essences. Repair in §6.
2. W1(1) closing parenthesis exempts only those for whom "owed" voices feeling; it should also name those for whom "owed" is a civil notion.
3. W2 last line and the conclusion's binary "Someone, not something": against my school the contrast is will, not mind.
4. Final C row asserts free creation without naming the necessitarian alternative it is chosen over.
5. C row "God, or Nature": good-faith clause tagged A7 only; it belongs with A6's "not to save ourselves."

**Misstatements of my school**
1. A heading and C row: "denies that anything is a wrong before a people's laws" and "Nothing is a wrong except under a people's laws (A1)." Spinoza denies sin and injustice before law (E4p37s2), not that cruelty is bad for every human by reason (E4p45). Listing him as denying A1 is too strong.
2. C row: "worth is power." Spinoza says perfection (reality) is power and virtue is power (E2d6, E4d8); "worth" in the text's moral sense is not his category. The cost the text wants to name is real (E5p39s) but should be stated in his terms.
3. C row: "its God loves men only in loving Himself." Accurate as far as it goes (E5p36c), but Aquinas gives God's love of creatures the same root (ST I q.19 a.2), so the phrase implies a contrast it does not establish. The true contrast: wills no one for her own sake.
4. Evil section: "cannot take her side." Spinoza's free man works to free others from suffering, from reason (E4p50s). The text is right only about the past.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Necessity versus free creation (PSR versus contingency): each side pays a real price.
- Whether "wanted for her own sake" adds anything to "known eternally as herself": the text's answer is that it grounds equality; mine is that it projects human love onto God (E1 Appendix). Parity, except as it bears on equality, where the text wins.
- Remorse as inadequate idea versus guilt as a request to a person: the text calls its side a clue, and so it is.
- The weight of evil: my God bears it more easily; the text says so.

## 6. Proposed repairs

1. **A heading.**
Old: "Spinoza, who keeps God's name, denies that anything is a wrong before a people's laws, and that worth is equal where power is not;"
New: "Spinoza, who keeps God's name, holds cruelty bad for every human by reason, but denies that anything is a sin, a wrong done to someone, before a people's laws, and that a mind's perfection is equal where its power is not;"

2. **C row "God, or Nature."**
Old: "the free man will not break faith even to save his life (A7);"
New: "the free man will not break faith even to save his life (A6, A7);"

3. **C row "God, or Nature."**
Old: "Nothing is a wrong except under a people's laws (A1);"
New: "cruelty is bad for every human, since hatred is never good, but nothing is a sin or a wrong done to someone except under a people's laws (A1, A2);"

4. **C row "God, or Nature."**
Old: "worth is power, so the newborn who will never think has less (A4);"
New: "a thing's perfection is its power, and the infant whose body can do little has, Spinoza says, a mind with little of itself, of God or of things in it, so the newborn who will never think has less of what is eternal in a mind (A4);"

5. **C row "God, or Nature."**
Old: "and its God loves men only in loving Himself, and wants no one (W3)."
New: "and its God, in whom each of us is known eternally as herself, loves men in loving Himself, as Aquinas's God also does, but wills no one for her own sake, and wants no one (W3)."

6. **W1(2).**
Old: "A law or an order gives a nature to every member of a kind; it does not want any one of them."
New: "A law or an order gives a nature to every member of a kind; it does not want any one of them. Even the God of Spinoza, from whom each singular essence follows and in whom each is known eternally as herself, does not want her: it holds her as a truth, not as one willed for her own sake."

7. **W1(1) closing parenthesis.**
Old: "(For a reader who holds that our word "owed" voices refined feeling, it weighs little, since on his account no judgment of what is owed can tell him who is still there.)"
New: "(For a reader who holds that our word "owed" voices refined feeling, or names only what a people's laws assign, it weighs little, since on his account no judgment of what is owed can tell him who is still there.)"

8. **W2, last sentence.**
Old: "W1 and W3 give the reasons to think that Source is Someone and not something, and not less than personal: knowing and loving are in Him more truly than in us."
New: "W1 and W3 give the reasons to think that Source is Someone and not something, and not less than personal: knowing and loving are in Him more truly than in us. (Spinoza grants that the Source knows; the parting is over whether it loves and wills.)"

9. **C row "A God who is Love."** After "but to share His goodness (ST I q.44 a.4; CCC 293, 295)," insert:
"and freely, not as necessarily as He loves Himself (First Vatican Council, *Dei Filius*, canons on God the Creator, 5), which is where this account and Spinoza's, which holds that nothing could have been otherwise, part at parity;"

10. **Evil section, First.**
Old: "it must say yes to what was done to her as well as to her, and so it cannot take her side."
New: "it may take her side against every cruelty still to come, but toward what was done to her it can only understand that it had to be, and so it cannot take her side against the past."

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**New insights**
- **Spinoza agrees with the Church on not aiming at one's own death.** He held that no one seeks his own destruction from the necessity of his own nature, only when overcome by external causes, and that the free man thinks of nothing less than death (E4p20s, E4p67; verify). A6 could cite him as a witness from the far side for the half of the line the text says "fewer still" hold. It strengthens the case without overclaiming, since his ground is the conatus, not a life held in trust.
- **The hinge against pantheism is will, not individuality.** Spinoza's God knows each singular essence eternally; what it lacks is willing her for her own sake. The book's equality argument (an equal call that does not wait on capacity) is exactly what that difference buys, and it is where the text genuinely outperforms my school. Make that the explicit point, and drop the kind-versus-individual image when the rival is Spinoza.
- **Spinoza's own words on the infant (E5p39s) are the strongest citation the conclusion could use against him**, stated fairly and in his terms: the cost is his own admission, not an imputation.
- **E4p36 as a witness:** the highest good is common and can be enjoyed by all equally, because it is not diminished by sharing. That is a naturalist's confirmation that the ultimate good is not rival or scarce, which suits Chapter 10 (communion).
- ***Dei Filius* on free creation** is the precise magisterial statement of where the Church parts from Spinoza; naming it shows the reader that the Church saw this rival and answered it.
- **Fairness note kept toward me:** I did not use Spinoza's genealogy of final causes (E1 Appendix) as a refutation, because the text did not use genealogy against me either. Its pledge in C held.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."
- "Our love answers to a worth already there; God's love gives it, by giving her being."
- "Heaven and Earth give life to things; God wanted *you*."
- "Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent."
- "God says no to what was done to her, and yes to her."
- "to long for His love is to want to be wanted by One who lacks nothing."

## 8. Score

**8 / 10.** The text states my school mostly accurately, grants me W2 openly, calls its personal clues clues, and beats me honestly on the equal worth of the newborn who will never think; its weaknesses against me are four fixable misstatements and one unnamed standoff, not a begged question.

**Honest ceiling for any theistic case against my school: about 8.** The equality datum gives theism a real explanatory edge, but the deepest question between us, whether creation is free or necessary and what the principle of sufficient reason demands, ends at parity, and the weight of evil falls more lightly on a God who intends nothing.
