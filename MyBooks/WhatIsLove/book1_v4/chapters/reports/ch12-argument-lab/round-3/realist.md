# Argument Lab, Round 3: Non-Naturalist Secular Moral Realism vs. v3

## 1. School and voice

This is the godless normative realism of Erik Wielenberg (*Robust Ethics*, 2014), David Enoch (*Taking Morality Seriously*, 2011), Derek Parfit (*On What Matters*, 2011), Russ Shafer-Landau and Michael Huemer. It is joined here by the theory of directed duties: Stephen Darwall's second-personal standpoint, R. Jay Wallace's *The Moral Nexus* (2019) and Michael Thompson on "bipolar" obligation. It also uses the third-factor reply to Sharon Street's Darwinian dilemma, and Kit Fine's view that necessity lives in the essences of things. On this view moral truths are objective, necessary, grounded in non-moral features (a child's pain is bad because of what pain is), and known by reflection. They need no God. In Rounds 1 and 2 this school was the book's ally against relativism and its sharpest critic on W1. In Round 3 it grants nearly every repair v3 made. It presses four things: the new "owed or only ripening" test, the new dilemma about the dead, "worth follows the call," and the new places where v3 says the realist cannot account for necessity and fit.

---

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** *Answer:* Justice is giving each person what he is owed, and much of what is owed is owed *to* someone: directed duties whose breaking wrongs a particular person, who then has standing to complain, demand amends and forgive (Darwall, Wallace, Thompson). What she is owed is fixed by necessary truths about persons. These truths are basic; nothing stands behind them. *Verdict:* **Holds as a working principle. It posits its ground.** This is *suum cuique* without its metaphysics. In Round 3 it matters that this school, unlike karma, already says guilt is *owed to someone*: the victim. The book's new test must face that (Exchange 1).

**2. A hard case.** *Answer:* The conjoined twins case, Re A (2000), as in Round 2. Mary may not be killed as a means. Jodie is not bound to keep sustaining Mary with her own body. Separation removes a burden rather than using Mary as a tool. *Verdict:* **Coherent, and it borrows justice and mercy in all but name**, as Romans 2:14–15 predicts. Catholic moralists divided on the case too, so the realist and the Catholic disagree only at the edges.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** *Answer:* The best version is now kind-based and essentialist. Every human being has the rational nature essentially, whether or not its use is blocked (Liao). Worth follows the essence, not its exercise, and essences do not come in degrees. *Verdict:* **Holds better than in Round 2, but it still posits.** Kind membership is all-or-nothing, so this view can deliver exact equality. What it cannot say is why the essence, and not its use, should be the measure of worth. The realist takes that as a further brute truth. The theist can give a reason (Exchange 3), so our side still leads, but the lead is narrower than Round 2 said.

**4. The ultimate good.** *Answer:* An objective list (knowledge, friendship, achievement, beauty, autonomy, pleasure) lived with integrity. Meaning comes from within a life; death ends it (Wielenberg). *Verdict:* **Coherent as a plan for a life. It breaks on unity and on the victims.** It does not say why these goods belong together, and it has nothing to say to the child who died before any good on the list reached her. That is a gap in explanation, not a contradiction.

### New exchanges

**Exchange 1: the ripening test files the realist on the wrong side.**
*Realist:* Your new test is "is guilt owed to someone, or does it only ripen?" Then you say: "An order of cause and fruit, a necessary truth, the shape of our kind: each can say the deed was wrong and its fruit will come. None of them is anyone the guilt could be owed to." I never said guilt is owed to a necessary truth, and I never said its "fruit will come." That is karma's grammar, not mine. On my view guilt is owed to *her*, the one I wronged. The necessary truth is the content of what I owe her ("she may not be used"), not the one I owe it to. Darwall, Wallace and Thompson have built a whole literature on exactly this: directed duties, owed to the victim, with her standing to blame and forgive. So your test does not separate me from you while she lives. We both answer "owed to someone," and it is the same someone.
*Defense:* Granted. The test sorts karma and the error theorist from both of us. It does not sort the realist from the theist. The test cannot be what decides between those two. What can decide is two narrower questions: what happens to the debt when the one owed it is gone, and what gives her the standing in the first place. Those are exactly W1(1) and W1(2). The frame should say so.
*Result:* **Serious as written; held with repair.** As written, the frame's decisive test misdescribes this school against v3's own rule that each rival is "stated as its best defenders state it." Repair R1.

**Exchange 2: A3 and the dilemma about the dead cannot both stand.**
*Realist:* A3, which you offer as something everyone can grant, says: when she is gone, "we still owe her something we can no longer pay." W1(1) then says: "Either the debt died with her ... or it did not, and she is still someone." So the debt survives only if she does. Put the two together. If A3 is true and the dilemma is valid, A3 already implies that the dead survive, and then A3 is not neutral. If A3 is neutral, the dilemma leaves out a third road, and that road is mine. The debt did not die, and she is not still someone. It is owed to the woman who lived, and it can never be paid. Your own A2 shows this road is coherent. The ashes are buried after she has died; "nothing in anyone's experience is different, and still she was wronged." That is a wrong done to someone who, on A2's neutral terms, no longer exists. Feinberg and Pitcher explain such wrongs as wrongs to the person as she was before death. If she can be wronged after death without surviving, she can be owed after death without surviving.
*Defense:* That is fair, and the repair is better than the original. The real division is not "died / survives." It is whether the debt can ever be answered. On the realist's road, the debt is owed forever to someone who can never hear it, accept amends, or forgive. That is tragic, but it is honest, and its best defenders hold it without flinching. Raimond Gaita calls remorse the clear-eyed sight of the reality of the one we wronged; Bernard Williams would not soften it. So the honest dilemma is: either the debt can never be answered, or she is still someone and it can. Then the clue v3 already claims is intact. Guilt is shaped to seek pardon from the one wronged. It goes on seeking after everyone on earth who could answer is gone, and one account says that seeking will always miss its mark while the other says it can be met.
*Realist rejoinder:* Guilt's shape fits living victims. Its reaching past the grave is the attitude overshooting, as grief does.
*Defense:* Grief overshooting is the expressivist's and the debunker's reading, and v3 lets them make it. We do not use a genealogy against you that we refuse to let you use against us. On your own view, though, the debt is real, not an overshoot. So you are left holding a real debt that is necessarily unpayable. That is a cost you can pay, and we say so, but it is a cost. This is a standoff, at parity, of the kind v3 already labels "a clue."
*Result:* **Serious as written (an invalid "either/or" whose missing branch the version's own A2 and A3 supply). Held with repair; at parity afterward.** Repair R2.

**Exchange 3: "Worth follows the call." Which call?**
*Realist:* W1(2) says "if her worth lies in what she is," and two sentences later says "worth follows the call, not the capacity." Which is it? If "the call" is the supernatural calling (GS 29: "one and the same destiny, which is divine"), your own Church says it is a free gift beyond nature. Pius XII condemned those who "destroy the gratuity of the supernatural order" by holding that God "cannot create intellectual beings without ordering and calling them to the beatific vision" (*Humani Generis* 26). So God could have made human beings without that call. Would they have had equal dignity? Surely yes. Then the supernatural call is not what grounds equal worth. If instead "the call" means what a rational nature is for, that is a fact about the nature, and my kind-based view already has it.
*Defense:* The question is a good one, and the answer sharpens the line rather than withdrawing it. The call that grounds worth is the ordering of every rational nature to God as its last end: to know and love Him. Aquinas gives exactly this as one reason persons alone are willed for their own sake. Only intellectual creatures can reach the last end of the universe, God, by knowing and loving Him (*Summa Contra Gentiles* III.112). That ordering belongs to the nature in any order God might have made, so *Humani Generis* is not touched. In the actual order it is raised into the one divine calling of GS 29. Then "not the capacity" means "not the individual's present use of it." And here the theist has something the realist lacks: a *reason* why the essence and not its exercise is the measure. Worth is measured by what a thing is for. If every human being is for the same end, and that end is God Himself, then the genius and the newborn are for the same thing. Whatever differs in what they can do now makes no difference to what they are for.
*Realist rejoinder:* SCG III.112's first two reasons are "dominion over its own act" and freedom. That is capacity, at the level of the kind.
*Defense:* Yes, of the kind, and that is what v3 means. The newborn has the nature whose acts are her own, though she cannot yet perform them. Your kind-based view and ours agree on that much. We part on the measure. You posit that the kind's capacity is the measure. We explain it: the kind is the measure because the kind is what is ordered to God.
*Result:* **Held with repair. Our side still leads, but more narrowly than Round 2 said.** As written, "call" invites the *Humani Generis* objection and appears to compete with "what she is." This is Minor (clarity and doctrinal precision), because the intended reading is available and correct. Repair R3.

**Exchange 4: "Wanted" proves too much, and one line slips.**
*Realist:* God "loves all existing things" (ST I q.20 a.2, your own text). Stones are wanted too. So "wanted" cannot be what sets the child apart. And "Everyone is someone's child; she was wanted before anyone saw her" reads, to an ordinary reader, as a claim about parents. That is false of the abandoned infant on the hillside your conclusion invokes. It is the same false universal ("every child is loved") that v3 rightly removed from W3.
*Defense:* Both points granted. What sets her apart is GS 24's "willed *for itself*," not just "wanted." The second point is a wording slip. Better: "Even the child no one wanted was wanted before anyone saw her."
*Result:* **Held with repair. Minor.** Repair R3 (second part).

**Exchange 5: the divine ideas. Do they say where necessity lives, or only move it?**
*Realist:* You now say necessary moral truths live "in God's own wisdom, where every possible nature is first known (ST I q.15)." First, that only relocates them. Every truth I hold to be basic now appears as one of God's ideas, with the same content and the same number. Second, I do say where necessity lives: in the natures of pain, persons and promises themselves. Kit Fine's essentialism ("Essence and Modality," 1994) is the leading account of metaphysical necessity, and it needs no mind. Third, your own Aquinas says God has many ideas (q.15 a.2).
*Defense:* Aquinas: many ideas, one essence. The ideas are many only as the many ways God's one being can be shared by creatures (q.15 a.2). So the moral truths are not a list that happens to be stored in a Mind. They are all truths about one thing: what it is for a creature of a given nature to share in the Good. That unifies by derivation, not by count, and the realist has nothing like it. He has no reason why all the basic moral truths concern the good of beings. The precise text, moreover, is not q.15 but q.16 a.7: truth has eternity only in the divine intellect. And truths that are first a Mind's thoughts are by their nature knowable. That links straight to W2.
*Realist rejoinder:* A Platonic truth needs no knower. Its knowability is a separate question.
*Defense:* Agreed, and that question is W2's.
*Result:* **Held, at parity, with a citation repair.** But this exchange shows that the C line's "it does not say where its necessity lives" is false of this school (Exchange 6).

**Exchange 6: the C lines restate two advantages the lab already conceded were even.**
*Realist:* Three things in C. (a) "It explains that the law holds; it does not say where its necessity lives." I do say where: in the essences of the things the truths are about (Fine; Wielenberg's basic ethical facts; Shafer-Landau). You may think that stopping point is worse than yours. But "does not say" is false, and Round 2 already settled that both stopping points are brute. (b) "Against the realist it is one stopping point instead of many," followed one sentence later by "Its claim is reach, not count." Round 2 conceded that it is one against a few, and that both sides need a first principle ("good is to be done" is no more derived than mine). (c) "Who could ever release or forgive a debt when no one on earth is left." On your own A3 and W1(1), no one *releases* the gravest debt, not even God. He forgives only the part done against Himself. You charge me with lacking what you also deny exists.
*Defense:* All three are granted. The honest asymmetry is not that the realist is silent about necessity, or that he has more axioms. It is that his stopping point does one job: the law holds of itself. The theist's does three: the law, the beings it is about, and the minds that read it all come from one Source. And "release" should go. What the realist lacks at the grave is someone who can still answer, not a releaser.
*Result:* **Serious as written** (it misdescribes the rival in the punchline of its own row, and contradicts a parity the lab already settled). **Held with repair.** Repairs R6 and R7.

**Exchange 7: W2 drops the third-factor reply and then claims to have answered it.**
*Realist:* v2 credited my best reply to the fit question: survival and well-being are themselves good, so a mind shaped by survival was roughly aimed at the good. v3 removes that. Then it says the rival "must still say why, in a world of matter and chance, there came to be minds made to read an order that holds for every mind." And its closing parenthesis says: "This answers those who say moral facts simply exist." I have said why. Wielenberg's third factor: the same capacities that let a creature form beliefs about rights (reason, self-awareness, the ability to suffer and to act for reasons) are what *make* it a bearer of rights. So it is no accident that the beings who can see the moral truths are the beings those truths are about. Enoch's version: survival is (roughly) good, so selection pushed us toward the good. And by your own concessions in W2, evolution explains why our reasoning is mostly reliable, and consistency is what drives conscience to correct itself. Reliable general reasoning, held to consistency and turned on necessary truths about pain and persons, is all I need. That leaves only "why are there minds at all?" That is the argument from reason or from consciousness, not the moral argument.
*Defense:* The omission is a real fault. The rival's best answer should be stated, and "this answers" claims more than the text shows. But the real question survives the third factor. The third factor explains why evolved minds notice the features that make deeds wrong (pain, trust, betrayal). It does not explain how such minds can see that those features would make a deed wrong for any mind in any world. That is a grasp of necessary truths that have no causes and leave no trace a selection process could track. It is the same puzzle that hangs over the mind that reads mathematics (Benacerraf, Field). One Maker of law and mind answers both: on the theist view, necessary truths are first thoughts, and minds are made in the image of the Mind that thinks them.
*Realist rejoinder:* Clarke-Doane (*Morality and Mathematics*, 2020) argues that the reliability challenge is weak for necessary truths. They could not have been otherwise, so the worry that we would believe them even if they were false has no grip.
*Defense:* That is contested in the literature, and the book need not settle it. Stated honestly, W2 is a question of fit that both sides can press. It weighs; it does not settle. With the reply named and the question narrowed to how far conscience can reach, it stands at parity, which is all a converging case needs from it.
*Result:* **Serious as written** (it omits the rival's best reply to the very question it poses, and claims to have answered him). **Held with repair, at parity.** Repair R4.

**Exchange 8: the unity of the law is explained by its form, and your own encyclical says so.**
*Realist:* W2: the law's "demands ... never require us to do evil," offered as evidence of one Source. But *Veritatis Splendor* 52 gives the reason, and it needs no Source: a person "can never be hindered from not doing certain actions, especially if he is prepared to die rather than to do evil." Prohibitions can always be kept together, because one can always refrain. Positive duties that conflict (two drowning, one can be saved) never require evil either, because ought implies can. Ross gets the same result with prima facie duties. So this kind of unity follows from the form of the precepts. It is no evidence of a single author.
*Defense:* Granted for the clause as written. The unity that would count as evidence is v1's richer claim, which v3 thinned out: the goods themselves fit together. Truth needs mercy to be told well, justice needs mercy to stay just, and mercy needs truth to be mercy (Chapters 5–9). That is not a fact about prohibitions. It is a fact about what the goods are, and it is the book's own theme.
*Result:* **Held with repair. Minor** (as written the clause adds no weight, but W2 does not rest on it). Repair R5.

**Exchange 9: the redundant reason returns in W1's closing.**
*Realist:* The W1 frame says, rightly, "the wrong is wrong first because of what it does to the child." The closing says "the law binds because He is good." Those answer the same question in two ways. Which makes the deed binding: the child's pain, or God's goodness?
*Defense:* Different questions, as in Round 2. The wrong is wrong because of what it does to her. That there is a *her*, made for an end, and a law about what she is owed, is because He is good. That the wrong can be forgiven is because He is Someone. The sentence should say that.
*Result:* **Held with repair. Minor.** Repair R8.

**Exchange 10: fairness check on the Dhamma row.**
*Realist:* "A law that judges hearts looks like the work of a mind." My necessary truths are sensitive to intention (the same act is murder or accident depending on the will), and they are mindless. If the inference works against karma, it works against me, and v3 does not argue that in the row.
*Defense:* The inference is not drawn from truths that are sensitive to intention. It is drawn from a *process* that sorts outcomes by intention across lives. Karma delivers what fits; a necessary truth delivers nothing. Gravity does not read intentions; karma, as a causal order, is said to. That is a fair, specific inference about a causal mechanism, and it does not reach the realist.
*Result:* **Held.** The fairness rule is kept.

**Exchange 11: the fawn, and "not a trade."**
*Realist:* My view sharpens the problem of evil: pain is necessarily bad, the fawn's included. Aquinas says plainly that the order of the whole requires that some things fail, and that the lion would not live without the slaying of animals (ST I q.22 a.2 ad 2; q.48 a.2). For non-persons, that is a greater-good trade. v3's "God's permission is not a trade" and "No one is the price of someone else's good" hold of persons only. And on your own tradition God, as Lord of life, may take any life (II-II q.64 a.6 ad 1).
*Defense:* Correct, and v3 already carries it honestly: "the pattern of pain in nature still weighs." "No one" means persons. The text should say so in a clause, so that it does not seem to claim more. God's lordship over life is the lordship of the giver who keeps her. In His hands death is not her end, and nothing is taken from her for another's profit.
*Result:* **Held at parity by concession. Minor wording.** Repair R9.

**Exchange 12: the A heading's epistemology cuts both ways.**
*Realist (Huemer):* "What we cannot give up after honest reflection we have reason to trust." That is phenomenal conservatism, which I hold. But it also licenses my seeming that the wrongness of torture needs nothing behind it, and Rowe's seeming that some suffering is pointless.
*Defense:* The book does not ask the reader to doubt A1 without God, and it counts the second seeming as weight (evil section). The counterpossible point (Round 2, Exchange 13) still stands: our sense of what would be true if there were no God is not a reliable guide to what depends on God.
*Result:* **Held.**

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**Fatal against v3 as written:** none.

**Serious against v3 as written:** three. All three are about how the rival is described or how an inference is set up, not about the substance of the case, and each has a repair of a few sentences:

1. **W1's decisive test misfiles the realist (frame).** Directed-duty realism holds that guilt is owed to the victim, a someone. "None of them is anyone the guilt could be owed to" is false of this school, so the test does not decide between realism and theism while the victim lives. *Serious*, because it is the version's own stated test for deciding between the accounts. Repair R1.
2. **W1(1)'s dilemma is not exhaustive, and the version's own A2 and A3 supply the missing branch.** A debt owed forever to the dead, unanswerable, is coherent (posthumous wrongs; the person as she was), and A3 asserts it as neutral. Either A3 presupposes survival, or "either it died with her, or she is still someone" is invalid. *Serious*. Repair R2. Afterward: a clue, at parity.
3. **The realist is misdescribed in W2 and C.** W2 drops the third-factor reply and claims to answer the realist. The C realist line says the realist "does not say where its necessity lives," and the God line says "one stopping point instead of many," both against parities Round 2 settled. *Serious* (the rival is not "stated as its best defenders state it"). Repairs R4, R6, R7.

**Minor:** "call" in W1(2) needs fixing as the ordering of every rational nature to God (*Humani Generis* 26 risk); "Everyone is someone's child; she was wanted" reads as a false universal; "the law binds because He is good" brings back the redundant reason; the unity clause in W2 is explained by VS 52; cite ST I q.16 a.7 alongside q.15; "release" in C; A6 "finally safe"; A7 scope sentence in W1; Romans 2:14–15 cited for "because"; "no one is the price" should be marked as about persons; "a God who suffers" (for the doctrinal censor).

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**

- *Both stopping points are brute:* basic ethical truths grounded in essences, or God's essence with the divine ideas. Our side's edge is breadth (one source for law, beings and minds), not fewer axioms.

## 7. New insights; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
1. **The ripening test is the right test against karma and the error theorist, and the wrong one against the realist.** Directed-duty realism already says "owed to her." Against it the case narrows to two points, and both are good ones for the book: the grave, and the source of her standing.
2. **The honest dilemma at the grave is "answerable or not," not "died or survives."** A3 and A2 commit the book to debts owed to the dead on neutral terms. The tragic realist accepts that such a debt can never be answered. The book's clue is that guilt is shaped to be answered.
3. **"Call" must be the ordering of the nature to God, not only the gift of grace.** This keeps W1(2) clear of *Humani Generis* 26 and gives the theist what the realist lacks: a reason why the essence, not its use, is the measure of worth. If every human being is for the same end, worth does not rise and fall with ability.
4. **Divine ideas unify by derivation, not by count.** All moral truths become truths about one thing: how a creature shares in the Good (ST I q.15 a.2; q.16 a.7). That is the real asymmetry with realism, and it is breadth, not fewer axioms.
5. **The third factor answers fit, not reach.** The theist's real card in W2 is the access problem for necessary truths, shared with mathematics. Play it, and grant that it is contested.
6. **VS 52 already explains why prohibitions never conflict.** The unity worth citing as evidence is the fit of the goods themselves, which is the book's own theme.
7. **Chapter-text flag (still open from Round 2):** chapter_12_current.md, "Every clause of it is something only a person can do ... and a gift can only come from a Giver," and "One last skeptic ... no *thing* ... has ever laid a claim on anyone. Only a Someone can," repeat the Round 1 gap. Directed-duty realism grants that claims are laid by persons, namely the victim. When the chapter is condensed, rework both along the lines of R1 and R2.
