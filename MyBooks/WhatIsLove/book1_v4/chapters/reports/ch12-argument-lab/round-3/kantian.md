# Round 3 — Kantian Constructivism vs. v3

## 1. School and voice

The opponent speaks for the Kantian tradition at full strength: Kant himself (*Groundwork*, second *Critique*, *Critique of Judgment* §§86–87, *Metaphysics of Morals*, *Religion within the Bounds of Mere Reason*, the Collins lectures, the 1791 theodicy essay) and his heirs (Korsgaard, O'Neill, Hill, Herman, Wood, Reath, Kleingeld and Willaschek). The core claims: the moral law is the law of a free will as such; humanity is an end in itself, with dignity and not price; and God is a postulate that practical reason must hope for, never the ground that makes duty binding.

This round the Kantian finds that v3 has fixed every Serious finding from Round 2. So he presses new ground. Is the made-or-found dilemma a false dilemma? Does the book read too much into *Religion* 6:98–99? Is guilt owed to someone, or only judged by reason's law? Is "participated theonomy" heteronomy under a softer name? Does "worth follows the call" hang dignity on a will, or on a vocation that can be refused? He presses all of these with Kant's own texts. Most of them turn into evidence *for* the book's reading of guilt. None stands as a Serious defect.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges (pressed in new places this round)

**1. Starting point for justice.**
*Kantian answer:* Each human being has one innate right "by virtue of his humanity": to be free of anyone else's forcing choice, so far as that fits everyone's freedom under a universal law (*Doctrine of Right* 6:237). What anyone is owed is set out from that right, together with the duties of virtue (respect and beneficence), which are owed to every human being as such.
*Verdict: Holds, without borrowing.* It is a real starting point. It reaches the enemy and the vicious (see 3) and the dead (§35). Its limit shows up at mercy (see 2).

**2. A hard case, resolved by their own principles.**
*The case:* A murderer is convicted. The state can keep everyone safe by imprisoning him for life. May it, or must it, execute him?
*Kantian answer (Kant's own):* It must. Justice requires like for like. Kant's island example says that a society about to disband must still execute the last murderer in its prisons, so that the blood-guilt does not cling to the people (*Doctrine of Right* 6:333). The sovereign's right to pardon is the most slippery of rights. He may use it only for crimes against himself, because pardoning what one subject did to another would be a grave injustice to the victim (6:337).
*Verdict: Coherent, and it does not borrow. But this is justice with mercy torn away, which is exactly the break Chapters 6–8 predict.* Many modern Kantians reject the death penalty. They do it by appealing to the convict's own dignity, so mercy comes back in under the name of respect. The Church's teaching is that mercy enters justice for the sake of the guilty man's dignity (CCC 2267). One by-product deserves note: Kant's rule on pardon (forgive only what was done against you) is the very structure of v3's W1(1) (see Exchange 5).

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.**
*Kantian answer:* Morality, and humanity insofar as it is capable of morality, has dignity, a worth above all price that admits no equivalent (*Groundwork* 4:434–435). It belongs to every person: to the newborn, whom the parents brought into the world without his consent (§28); to the dead, in their good name (§35); and to the vicious man, from whom I may never withhold all respect as a human being, so that degrading punishments are forbidden (*Doctrine of Virtue* 6:463).
*Verdict: Holds for the enemy and the vicious, and better than most rivals there. For the newborn and the profoundly impaired, it holds only by borrowing.* "Capable of morality" has to mean a rational *nature* that is present where no reasoning is happening. That is a claim about what a being is *for*. Late Korsgaard reaches every sentient creature by making worth something valuers must confer. That keeps equality, but it gives up "prior to anyone's recognition" (A4). It also erases the line between humans and other animals, which *Dignitas Infinita* keeps.

**4. The ultimate end of human flourishing.**
*Kantian answer:* The highest good: virtue, with happiness in proportion to it, in an ethical commonwealth. Secular Kantians say a realm of ends, a just society of self-governing persons.
*Verdict: Kant borrows openly; his secular heirs give the borrowing up and pay for it.* In the *Critique of Judgment* §87, Kant pictures a righteous man, Spinoza for example, who is firmly persuaded that there is no God and no future life. The honest people he meets suffer every evil nature sends, like everyone else, until one wide grave swallows the honest and the dishonest together. Kant says such a man must either give up his moral end as impossible or assume a moral author of the world (paraphrase; verify 5:452). The secular realm of ends is coherent. It is also a kingdom that the dead never enter, and v3's W1(1) is built on exactly that point.

### Exchange 1: The made-or-found dilemma is a false dilemma

- *Kantian:* Your dilemma says the law is either made by reason or found by it. Kant's best readers (Reath, Kleingeld and Willaschek) say it is neither. A free will and a will under moral laws are one and the same (*Groundwork* 4:447). The law is the form of a free will's own working, as the laws of logic are the form of thinking. "Found" makes it sound like an order outside reason that reason comes across. It is not outside reason; it *is* reason working in practice. And your "the question of W2 returns" misfires against me. W2 asks why minds came to read an order that holds for every mind. For me that fit is not a lucky match that needs explaining. A will that could not "read" the law would not be a rational will at all.
- *Patrick's side:* Grant the reciprocity thesis in full. Then ask Kant's own question: does the law hold for every *possible* rational will, whether or not any exists? Kant says yes. It holds for all rational beings as such and not only for humans, and it would bind even if no one had ever kept it (*Groundwork* 4:389, 4:408). Then the law's validity does not wait on any actual reasoner. No reasoner set it, and each one finds himself already under it. That is all the "found" horn claims. Calling it "the form of reason" moves the question without answering it: where does a necessity about every possible will hold, before there are any wills? And W2's other question does come back, in a different form. The fit between law and will is analytic, granted. That free wills exist at all, in a world of nature, is not. Kant himself said we cannot comprehend how freedom is possible; the most we can comprehend is that it is incomprehensible (*Groundwork* 4:459–463).
- *Kantian rejoinder:* Asking where a necessity "lives" is speculative metaphysics, and practical reason does not need an answer to it.
- *Reply:* That is the standing disagreement over the limits of reason (*Dei Filius* against the first *Critique*). It is held at parity, not won by either side. The row as written stands. Because Kant's readers will cry "false dilemma," it should name the constitutive view once and show that it falls on the found horn.
- **Verdict: Held. Minor wording repair (§6, item 2).**

### Exchange 2: *Religion* 6:98–99 is overread

- *Kantian:* You cite the passage correctly. An ethical community cannot have the people as its lawgiver, because laws of virtue concern inner dispositions that no people can see. It can be thought of only as a people under a moral Ruler who knows the heart, and its true duties as His commands. But Kant adds, in the same place, that these laws must not be thought of as coming from that Ruler's will, or they would be mere statutes and not ethical laws. The book he wrote is a religion within the bounds of *mere reason*: the idea of God there is an idea of reason. So "he reached past reason alone" is false to the title. "At the dead" is false too. In §35 Kant grounds the good name of the dead in the person as a rational being, considered apart from his bodily existence, and he says plainly that this rests on no assumption about a future life. "At the newborn" is at best half true. In §28 Kant says only that we cannot form a concept of how a physical act brings a free being into existence. He does not call on God there.
- *Patrick's side:* Conceded as to wording. The row pledges to state each rival as its best defenders state it, and the last sentence overstates Kant. But the substance stands, and it is sharper than the overstatement. Kant did not make the Ruler the *author* of the law, and neither does the book (Chapter 3; W1 close: "He did not choose whether cruelty is evil"). Kant's point is about where guilt and goodness are *inward*, beyond any human court. There, he found that the moral life must be thought of as lived under Someone who knows the heart. That is W1's datum: guilt seeks a person, and keeps seeking after everyone on earth who could answer is gone.
- **Verdict: Held with repair. Minor (§6, item 1).**

### Exchange 3: Guilt is owed to someone, the victim, and I pass your test

- *Kantian:* W1 says the deciding test is "is guilt owed to someone, or does it only ripen?" I answer: owed to someone, the person I wronged. Duties of right are owed to persons. Darwall and Scanlon answer the same way. Your test separates you from karma, and from nothing else. Your frame names only impersonal rivals ("an order of cause and fruit, a necessary truth, the shape of our kind") and then says the test decides between the accounts. It does not decide between you and me.
- *Patrick's side:* Granted for the frame sentence. The test bites only when the victim is gone, and that is where W1(1) takes it. When she has died, no one on earth can forgive in her place, and still we owe her.
- *Kantian:* There too I have an answer. In §35 the dead are still owed, as persons considered apart from their bodies. So I take your second horn: "she is still someone." I take it as a practical idea, without claiming that she exists, and without God.
- *Reply:* Then which is it? If the noumenal person is only an idea we must think, the debt is owed to an idea, which means to no one. That is "the debt died with her" under another name. If she is real, outside time, she still cannot be answered to, healed, or give her forgiveness. A creditor who can never be answered is the dead debt with a better name. The person in W1(1) who matters is the one who can be raised, so that she is there to be answered to and to forgive in her own name. Kant himself did not rest at the noumenal creditor. For the highest good he postulated immortality and God together (*Critique of Practical Reason* 5:122–132). At the wide grave (*Critique of Judgment* §87) he said the righteous atheist must either give up his end or assume a moral author of the world.
- *Kantian rejoinder:* Survival does not need God. McTaggart believed in immortality and not in God.
- *Reply:* That is true, and v3 is honest about it. W1(1) concludes "she is still someone" first, and it calls the rest a clue. But a contingent creature that survives the loss of her body needs something to keep her in being. Raising her "body and all" and making her whole is not something a soul does for itself. And the few atheists who hope for survival leave the rest of W1(1) unexplained: the wrong against the One whose love made her precious (CCC 1850), and the Judge who can hear her.
- **Verdict: Held. The W1 frame needs one Minor wording repair so that the someone/Someone step is explicit (§6, item 3).**

### Exchange 4: Guilt is owed to reason's law, not to a creditor

- *Kantian:* Your own A7 shows that an ought can bind with no creditor. The man who believes against the evidence in private wrongs no one, yet he is guilty. Kant's duties to oneself (not lying, not degrading oneself) are the same. So "guilty" does not entail "owed to someone." It is the verdict of reason's tribunal on a will that broke its own law. When the victim is dead, what remains is that verdict, and the remorse a rational being rightly feels at it. Nothing in it calls for a creditor.
- *Patrick's side:* A2 was careful here. Guilt for *wronging* faces a person ("I did this to *her*"); being bound to the truth (A7) is a different thing. But look at how Kant himself analyzes even the cases with no creditor:
  1. Duties to oneself look contradictory to him, because the one who binds and the one who is bound are the same. He solves this by splitting the self into two: the self as rational legislator binds the self as bound (*Doctrine of Virtue* 6:417–418). He needs a second party even there.
  2. Conscience, he says, is an inner court. The accuser and judge must be thought of as another person, an ideal one. Since that judge must know the heart and bind every duty, it must be thought of as God (6:438–440, paraphrase; verify).
  3. He calls guilt a debt (*Schuld*) that cannot be handed to anyone else, the most personal of all debts (*Religion* 6:72).
  4. When he asks how the converted sinner can stand justified, he answers that it is by the verdict of a Judge who knows the heart, a sentence of grace (6:75–76).

  So the philosopher of autonomy, describing guilt, finds a court, a Judge who knows the heart, a debt and grace. That is W1's datum in his own words.
- *Kantian rejoinder:* All of that is the *representation* reason must form. A necessary idea is not evidence that its object exists.
- *Reply:* That is the deepest standoff between us, and it is honestly at parity. But notice how close the positions are. The book claims a clue that can predispose one to faith (CCC 35), not knowledge that compels. Kant claims a rational moral faith, held with practical certainty. We agree on the shape of guilt and on where it points. We differ on whether reason may count what it cannot help thinking as a witness. *Dei Filius* says it may, and the first *Critique* says it may not.
- **Verdict: Held (parity). Gain: Kant's own analysis of conscience strengthens A2 and W1(1).**

### Exchange 5: Forgiveness is either corrupting or redundant

- *Kantian:* A holy Judge cannot simply let a debt go. That would be indulgence, and it would undercut moral effort. If the wrongdoer's heart truly changes, the change does the moral work, and forgiveness adds nothing. So "only a person can forgive" names either something unjust or something idle.
- *Patrick's side:* Neither. In A3 forgiveness heals the bond; it does not cancel desert or make the wrong not wrong. *Spe Salvi* 44 says grace does not make wrong into right, and the wrongdoer does not sit beside his victim as though nothing had happened. A change of heart mends the wrongdoer's *will*. It cannot mend the *relation*, which has two ends, and only the one at the other end can restore it. Kant himself needed the Judge's sentence of grace on top of the change of heart. His rule for the sovereign is the structure of W1(1): pardon only what was done against you, never what one subject did to another (6:337). God forgives the offense against Himself, and He keeps the victim so that she can forgive in her own name. Kant's own law of pardon says the same.
- **Verdict: Held. An insight for the book (§7).**

### Exchange 6: "The law binds because He is good": idle or heteronomous?

- *Kantian:* W1's close says both "Our share in that law is reason's own light, not a foreign rule (VS 41)" and "The law binds because He is good." Either the law binds me because my reason sees it, and then God's goodness is idle as a ground of obligation; or it binds me because of His goodness, and then reason is a messenger for an outside source, which is heteronomy. And "He is good" is either measured by the law, which makes "binds because He is good" circular, or measured by some concept of perfection apart from the law, which is empty and secretly presupposes morality (*Groundwork* 4:443).
- *Patrick's side:* The dilemma misdescribes Kant before it misdescribes us. For Kant too, the law does not bind me *because* I recognize it. It is valid for every rational will, and my awareness of it is only the "fact of reason." Validity and recognition are two things in both accounts. The question is only what validity is. For Kant it is reason as such. For Aquinas it is the eternal wisdom of the One who is the Good, which my reason shares in. So "not a foreign rule" is true in the same way for both: my reason is no more foreign to God's wisdom, for Aquinas, than it is to reason as such, for Kant. As for the circle: Aquinas does not define God's goodness through the law, nor through desire. Good is understood through being and end (ST I q.5 a.1), and the first precept, "good is to be done," is not a hypothetical "if you want…". It is self-evident and categorical (ST I-II q.94 a.2). And Kant's own distinction between the lawgiver and the author of the law (*Metaphysics of Morals* 6:227) is the one Chapter 3 needs. God is not the author who picks the law; the law is His wisdom, because goodness is His nature. Genesis 18:25, "Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?", which v3 cites, shows a Judge who is answerable to a justice that is His own nature.
- *Kantian rejoinder:* "Participation" is metaphysics beyond any experience.
- *Reply:* The critical limit again, at parity. VS 40–41 and Kant agree on more than either side admits: reason does not create values, and the law is reason's own.
- **Verdict: Held (parity).**

### Exchange 7: The Creator's authority from making

- *Kantian:* Patrick grounds God's authority in His power to create and to establish what we are for. Kant's §28 says that bringing a free being into existence gives the parents duties toward it, not ownership. A free being cannot be someone's product. By the same rule, creating persons gives God duties toward them, not authority over them. And "establish what we are for" is heteronomy: our end handed to us from outside.
- *Patrick's side:* v3 does not ground the authority in making as such. It grounds it in His being the Good: "because He is Himself the Good He asks of us." The ends are set "in the very natures He created." They are not laid on a self that already existed and had ends of its own. No prior self exists whose law could be overruled. And what God wills, on GS 24, is that she exist *for her own sake*. In Kant's terms, the Creator wills her as an end in herself, the very standing that §28 protects. Kant's argument against ownership is a Catholic one too: no one owns a person, and God does not hold her as a chattel either (see Exchange 9).
- **Verdict: Held.**

### Exchange 8: "Worth follows the call"

- *Kantian, three hits:*
  1. *Conditional worth.* Whatever is valuable because someone wants it has only conditional worth (*Groundwork* 4:428). "She was wanted for her own sake" makes her worth hang on a will, God's, and that is the structure of price, not of dignity.
  2. *A call can be gratuitous and refusable.* GS 29's "one calling" is the divine destiny. The Church insists that God could have made intellectual creatures without calling them to the vision of Himself (*Humani Generis* 26). If worth follows *that* call, such creatures would have no worth. And v3 names hell (CCC 1033). If a person finally refuses the call, does worth that "follows the call" go with it? Aquinas once said that the sinner falls from human dignity (ST II-II q.64 a.2 ad 3). Kant's ground, humanity in the person, cannot be lost even by the vicious (6:463).
  3. *Kant already explains "never spent."* "Being human … does not explain why what belongs may never be spent." Dignity above all price, which admits no equivalent, explains exactly that.
- *Patrick's side:*
  1. God's love is not desire for something that already exists, which is the kind of wanting that gives conditional worth. It gives the being that has the worth (ST I q.20 a.2). What He wills is that she be an end in herself (GS 24). Her worth is in her and caused by Him, not pinned on her from outside.
  2. A fair point about wording, not about substance. In W1(2), "call" has to mean being willed for her own sake, given with her being, as *Dignitas Infinita* grounds dignity in each person's being willed, created and loved by God. That dignity cannot be lost (DI 15), even by her own refusal. DI distinguishes the moral dignity a person can lose from the ontological dignity she cannot (DI 7). As written, "worth follows the call, not the capacity" can be read as the supernatural vocation alone. It should be tied back to her being (§6, item 5).
  3. Granted. W1(2) is aimed at kind, order and karma, and it does not claim to beat Kant here. Against Kant the honest result is parity. Dignity above price is either conferred by valuers (the regress argument, which loses "prior to recognition") or simply asserted as a fact of reason, which is a stopping point like ours. What theism adds is *this* one: not only that every rational nature has worth, but that she was wanted.
- **Verdict: Held with repair. Minor (§6, item 5).**

### Exchange 9: "The One who made her precious": an owner?

- *Kantian:* Saying that a wrong to her is also a wrong to her Maker is the old property argument. Aquinas likened suicide to killing another man's slave (ST II-II q.64 a.5). If God is wronged as an owner, the victim becomes His chattel, and you have degraded her to explain her worth.
- *Patrick's side:* That is not the claim. CCC 1850 says sin sets itself against God's *love* for us. The wrong to God is like the wrong done to a mother when her child is hurt: she is wronged because she loves the child, not because she owns it. One word blocks the misreading: "the One whose love made her precious."
- **Verdict: Held with repair. Minor (§6, item 4).**

### Exchange 10: Smaller hits on A and W2

- *A3:* "No one could consent a child's torture into being right." A child cannot consent at all, so the example proves nothing about the limits of consent. Kant's paradigm is adult self-degradation: no one can consent to being enslaved. **Minor** (§6, item 6).
- *W2, unity:* Kant explains why the law never requires evil: duties cannot truly collide (*Metaphysics of Morals* 6:224), because one formal law cannot contradict itself. *Reply:* Kant buys that unity at the price of content, which is Hegel's empty-formalism charge, and his derivations of the material duties remain contested. The book's unity is a unity of substantive goods (life, truth, friendship). W2's scope note directs it at the realist and the debunker. **Held (parity).**
- *Evil, "He wills the order of the whole":* Is the child's loss then a means to the whole, which the Formula of Humanity forbids? *Reply:* v3 has already closed this: "no one is the price of someone else's good, not even in God's hands," and the good hoped for is a good *for her*. Permitting harm within a world of real causes is not doing it. The weight that remains belongs to the evil school, and v3 owns it. Kant's 1791 essay sides with Job's honesty against his friends' excuses, and v3's "That does not explain why" is the Joban stance. **Held.**

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**As written: NONE.**

No Fatal or Serious objection from this school stands against v3 as written. Every Serious finding from Round 2 has been fixed. **Minor (wording only; the arguments stand without them):**
1. **C Kant row, last sentence** overreads Kant: §35 rests on no assumption about a future life; §28 does not call on God; *Religion* is "within mere reason" (Exchange 2).
2. **C Kant row, made-or-found:** name the constitutive view, so that Kantians cannot cry "false dilemma" (Exchange 1).
3. **W1 frame:** the test "owed or only ripens" does not separate the theist from Kant while the victim lives. Make the move to the dead, and to Someone, explicit (Exchange 3).
4. **W1(1):** "the One who made her precious" invites an ownership reading. Say "whose love" (Exchange 9).
5. **W1(2):** "worth follows the call" can be read as the gratuitous, refusable supernatural vocation alone. Tie it to being and to dignity that cannot be lost. Say by whom she was wanted (Exchange 8).
6. **A3:** the child's torture does not test the limits of consent. Add an adult case (Exchange 10).
7. **W1 frame:** move the Romans 2:14–15 citation to where it fits (the law is known without knowing its Author).

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**
- **Postulate vs ground.** Kant says a necessary idea of reason (a Judge who knows hearts) gives rational faith, not evidence. The book says it is a clue in a converging case (CCC 31, 35; *Dei Filius*). Both agree on the shape of guilt and on where it points.
- **Where necessity lives.** The law's home is either reason as such or the reason of Someone. Each side's stopping point is one stopping point.
- **Form vs end.** Kant reads any grounding in the Good as heteronomy. Aquinas reads the first precept as categorical and the Good as our own end. Neither refutes the other.
- **Self-standing dignity.** The fact of reason vs worth as gift. Parity, with theism alone explaining *this* one being wanted.
- **The noumenal creditor vs the kept person.** Kant's dead are owed but can never be answered. The book's dead can be. Kant's own postulates lean toward the book.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**1. C, Kant row: replace the last sentence** ("Kant himself wrote … he reached past reason alone.") with:
> Kant himself held that a people cannot be its own lawgiver in matters of the heart, which no human court can see, so that an ethical community can be thought of only as a people under a moral Ruler who knows the heart, with its true duties as His commands (*Religion* 6:98–99). He did not make that Ruler the author of the law, and neither does this book (Chapter 3). But where guilt and goodness are inward, the philosopher of autonomy found that he had to think of Someone.

**2. C, Kant row: replace** "If made, reason could unmake it; if found, it is an order reason did not set, and the question of W2 returns." with:
> If made, reason could unmake it. If it is the very form of a free will, as Kant's best readers say, it still holds for every possible reasoner before any exists: no reasoner set it, and each finds himself under it. Then the question of W2 returns: where does a law for every possible mind hold, and how did minds that live by it arise in a world of nature? Kant answered that how freedom is possible we cannot understand.

**3. W1 frame: replace** "None of them is anyone the guilt could be owed to. Two witnesses say guilt is owed." with:
> None of them is anyone the guilt could be owed to. The best accounts without God answer that guilt is owed to the one we wronged, and so far we agree. The test bites when she is gone. Two witnesses say guilt is still owed then, and point to Someone.

**4. W1(1): replace** "a wrong against the One who made her precious" with:
> a wrong against the One whose love made her precious

**5. W1(2): replace** "Worth follows the call, not the capacity, and an equal call explains an equal worth. Everyone is someone's child; she was wanted before anyone saw her." with:
> Worth follows being willed and called, not capacity. The call comes with her being, and the worth it gives cannot be lost, not even by her own refusal (*Dignitas Infinita* 15). An equal call explains an equal worth. Not every child is wanted by those who made her; every child was wanted by the One who did, before anyone saw her.

**6. A3: replace** "But no one could consent a child's torture into being right, beforehand or after." with:
> But no one could consent his own enslavement, or a child's torture, into being right, beforehand or after.

**7. W1 frame: replace** "The wrong is wrong first because of what it does to the child (Romans 2:14–15), and not even God makes cruelty right" with:
> The wrong is wrong first because of what it does to the child, and those who do not know the Lawgiver can know it (Romans 2:14–15); not even God makes cruelty right

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **Kant's law of pardon is W1(1)'s structure.** The sovereign may pardon only crimes against himself, never what one subject did to another, or he wrongs the victim (*Doctrine of Right* 6:337). God forgives the offense against Himself and keeps the victim to forgive in her own name. A secular philosopher's rule of justice confirms that this is the just shape of forgiveness, not a dodge.
- **Kant's analysis of conscience is a witness for A2 and W1.** Conscience is an inner court whose judge must be thought of as another person who knows the heart, ultimately God (*Doctrine of Virtue* 6:438–440). Guilt is a debt only the culprit can bear (*Religion* 6:72). Justification is a Judge's sentence of grace (6:75–76). Kant denies only that a necessary idea counts as evidence. The book's "clue" sits between Kant's "rational faith" and a proof. Cite these as an opposing witness, never as authorities.
- **The wide grave (*Critique of Judgment* §87)** is the best opposing-witness text for W1(1) and the conclusion. Kant says the righteous atheist, watching the honest swallowed by the same grave as the wicked, must either give up his moral end or assume a moral author of the world.
- **Kant's island (6:333)** is a clean example for Chapters 6–8 of justice with mercy torn away. CCC 2267 shows mercy entering justice for the guilty man's own dignity.
- **Kant and Chapter 3 agree more than the chapter shows.** He holds that God is lawgiver but not author (*Metaphysics of Morals* 6:227). Radical evil, putting self-love above the law, is Chapter 1's "lesser good over the greatest Good." His "no collision of duties" is W2's unity. His dignity above price is A4 and A6. The real dispute is narrow: is the law's home reason as such, or the reason of Someone?
- **Optional, for W3 or W1(1):** Kant's highest-good argument is a duty-based companion to the longing in W3. We are bound to will a world in which the just are not finally crushed, and we cannot coherently will what we hold to be impossible. It satisfies A7, because it rests on the will's coherence, not on comfort.
- **Flag for Chapter 9, beyond this lab:** Kant's 6:72 (the debt of sin cannot be handed to another) is aimed at vicarious satisfaction. The chapter on sacrifice should meet it.
