# Round 3: Contractualism and the Second-Personal Standpoint vs. v3

## 1. School and voice

The voice is a composite of T. M. Scanlon (an act is wrong if any principle that allowed it could reasonably be rejected by someone affected; reasons are truths that need no metaphysical home), Stephen Darwall (obligation is the authority of free and equal persons to make claims on one another; second-personal reasons cannot be built out of impersonal ones), John Rawls (justice as fairness; reflective equilibrium), and R. Jay Wallace (blame and guilt as the reactive attitudes through which we hold one another to account). This round also uses Strawson's point that third parties hold wrongdoers to account by indignation, which is the stand-in for resentment, and the Feinberg–Pitcher account of wrongs to the dead. Positions are paraphrased throughout. There are no direct quotations from these authors.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges (Round 3 form)

**1. Starting point for justice.**
*Their answer:* We start inside the relationship of persons who answer to one another. Anyone who makes a demand has already granted that the person addressed may ask for a justification in return. What anyone is owed is what follows from principles that no one affected could reasonably reject. Those principles are fixed by reasons, not by anyone's actual consent. Wallace adds that guilt is how this accountability looks from the inside: the wrongdoer holds himself to the demand the victim could rightly press.
*Verdict: Holds, and it now meets the book halfway.* This school agrees with v3's A2 that guilt faces a person. It derives "guilt is owed" without God: owed to the one wronged. This is the central fact for W1 (Exchange 1). Its limit is the one Darwall admits himself: the circle shows that anyone who makes a claim already grants equal standing. It does not show why there are beings who have that standing.

**2. A hard case, resolved by their own principles.**
*Case:* South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission offered perpetrators amnesty in return for full disclosure. Several victims' families, the Biko family among them, challenged the amnesty provisions in court (*AZAPO v. President of the RSA*, 1996). *(Verify the case and the later refusal of amnesty in the Biko matter before use.)*
*Their answer:* A principle of conditional amnesty could be justified to the families, given what was at stake: a country on the edge of civil war, and the truth that would otherwise never come out. So the state may waive punishment. But amnesty is not forgiveness. The state cannot forgive what was done to Steve Biko, and neither can the commission. Only he could have, and he is dead. His family keeps the standing to forgive what was done to them, or to withhold it.
*Verdict: Holds on its own terms, and it supports A3 exactly.* "A nation can pardon; it cannot forgive." It does not borrow the book's definitions. Truth enters as a condition of justification, justice as justifiability to each person, and mercy only as a permission the victim may grant. This school has no account of mercy as a gift that heals, and its founders do not claim one.

**3. Ground of equal, unconditional dignity.**
*Their answer, stated more exactly than in Round 2:* Scanlon allows that being born of human parents gives strong reason to count a being among those to whom we must justify ourselves. We then ask what a trustee could reasonably reject on her behalf. That trustee is a step in reasoning, not a volunteer. So the abandoned child is owed exactly what the cherished child is owed, because she too was born of human parents. Equal dignity is equal standing in the space of justification.
*Verdict: The scope holds, but the ground stops at a reason it cannot explain.* My Round 2 report claimed the abandoned child was protected only "by courtesy." That was a misreading, and this school now presses the correction (Exchange 5). The real limit lies elsewhere. *Why* being born of human parents places her among those owed a justification is, on Scanlon's own account, a further truth about reasons that simply holds. It explains nothing further. At that point contractualism becomes godless realism about reasons and faces the realist's question (W2; C, godless realism).

**4. Ultimate good for humans.**
*Their answer:* Rawls's "social union of social unions," in which each person's good is completed in a shared life. Scanlon's answer to "why be moral?" is the value of standing with everyone in relations of mutual recognition.
*Verdict: Coherent and bounded.* It is a finite communion of persons who know one another. It ends at death and has nothing to say to the kinless dead. It names no one who receives the whole of anyone (W3). It does not borrow, and it does not reach.

## 3. Debate highlights (escalating)

**Exchange 1. Second-personal accountability already explains "guilt is owed."**
*Contractualist:* W1 sets its test: "is guilt owed to someone, or does it only ripen?" It lists the accounts that fail: "an order of cause and fruit, a necessary truth, the shape of our kind." My school is not on the list, and it passes the test. On my view guilt is owed, and owed to someone: the person wronged. Your own A2 says so ("Guilt faces a person: I did this to *her*"). So the test does not "decide between the accounts," as the frame claims. It divides accounts built on persons from impersonal ones, and you and I are on the same side of that line. Then "Two witnesses say guilt is owed" proves what I already grant. The step from "owed to someone" to "owed to Someone" is never made by the test.
*Defense:* Granted. The test does its work against the order, the necessary truth and the shape of our kind, and it does not touch you. Against you the question narrows. Guilt is owed to her. But (1) what happens to that debt when she is gone, and (2) why is she someone to whom no one may ever spend her for others, even when no one on earth wants her? Those are the two witnesses. The frame should say that the test narrows the question, not that it settles it.
*Contractualist:* Then W1 against my school rests entirely on the dead victim and the unwanted child.
*Defense:* Yes. That is where it should rest.
*Verdict: Held with repair (Minor).* This is a scope error in the frame, the same kind of omission as Round 2's fork. It carries no inference to God, though; the witnesses carry that. Repair in section 6.

**Exchange 2. W1(1)'s dilemma is false by the book's own A2 and A3.**
*Contractualist:* W1(1) says: "Either the debt died with her, and what is left is only remorse ... or it did not, and she is still someone." But your A2 (the ashes) and A3 ("we still owe her something we can no longer pay") are offered as truths that readers without God share. I share them. I hold, with Feinberg and Pitcher, that the dead can be wronged and the debt survives. The one wronged is the woman who lived, not some survivor. So there is a third option, and it is mine: the debt did not die, and she is not "still someone" in any sense beyond being the one owed. Either your "still someone" means only "still owed," and then the talk of people speaking to their dead adds nothing. Or it means she still exists, and then your dilemma is false. Worse, the first option misdescribes the best view without God as "only remorse," when your own A3 says that view keeps the debt.
*Defense:* Granted. The honest form has three options. The debt died with her (error theory). It survives, and she is owed forever by someone who can never release: a wound that will never be healed by the one it was done to. Or she is still someone who can be met. The middle option is yours, and it is coherent. It is also the bleak option the reader is asked to weigh. W1(1) never claimed more than a clue, and nothing in the clue rests on the dilemma being two-way: guilt "keeps seeking" her after everyone on earth who could forgive is gone, and that seeking fits the third option better than the second.
*Contractualist rejoinder:* Speaking to the dead is apostrophe. We address the woman who was, as we address her in a eulogy.
*Defense:* Perhaps. That is why it is weighed as a clue and not as evidence of survival. But people do more than mourn. They ask *pardon*, which only someone who can still answer can give.
*Verdict: Held with repair (Minor; borderline).* As written, the dilemma is valid only if "still someone" is read thinly, and the text then slides to the thick reading. Restoring the middle option makes the passage more honest and leaves the clue's weight unchanged. Also: "after everyone on earth who could answer is gone" should say "who could forgive." On Darwall's and Strawson's view the moral community still holds the wrongdoer to account through indignation. What it cannot do is release him.

**Exchange 3. "He keeps her": Ivan's question turned around.**
*Contractualist:* If God raises her so that she can "forgive in her own name," then either her forgiveness is certain or it is not. If certain, because grace perfects her will in glory, then God has secured the murderer's release through her, and "in her own name" is a formality: forgiveness in her place, routed through her. If not certain, then the forgiven penitent's peace depends on her choice, and you have tied a victim's final state to whether she forgives her murderer. Either way she bears one more burden for his sake, and that is a principle she could reasonably reject. Your A3 makes it worse: "The one we wronged can forgive us, and should." Wallace and many others hold that forgiveness is elective and is never owed to the wrongdoer. "Should" puts a disputed and largely Christian duty, love of enemies, into a premise offered to everyone. The brief forbids that.
*Defense:* On the dilemma: neither horn. Grace does not compete with freedom; it is what makes freedom whole (Chapter 4; CCC 1742). A saint's love is no less hers for being given by grace, and Maria Goretti's forgiveness was her own. The hope is that she *will be able* to forgive, not that she will be made to. And the text does not promise a cheap ending: "the wrongdoer does not sit at the final banquet beside his victim as though nothing had happened" (*Spe Salvi* 44). If she withholds forgiveness, his debt to her stands, and God does not override her. That is the seriousness A2 asked for. On A3: granted. The duty to forgive is Christian teaching (Chapter 8), and A3 does not need it.
*Contractualist:* Then you admit her forgiveness may never come.
*Defense:* We admit it may come late, and that only she can give it. Your view says it can never come at all. That difference is the clue.
*Verdict: Held, at parity. A3's "and should": Minor; remove it.*

**Exchange 4. God-grounded worth makes her claim second-hand (Pufendorf's problem).**
*Contractualist:* Darwall's deepest objection to grounding morality in God: if her claim on me ultimately comes from God's giving, then her authority to demand is borrowed, and the wrong to her becomes at bottom a wrong to God. That is exactly the directed wrong your A2 insisted on keeping, now lost. And an authority that binds only once we recognize it presupposes our own standing to recognize it, so the standing comes first and God second.
*Defense:* v3 already blocks both halves. First, "the wrong is wrong first because of what it does to the child," and "the victim's claim is truly her own." A gift is the one way something can come from another and still be wholly your own. A borrowed thing must be returned; a given thing belongs to the one who receives it. Second, the closing paragraph denies that God's authority works by command: our share in the law "is reason's own light, not a foreign rule" (*Veritatis Splendor* 41). We recognize the law with the same reason it addresses. No external command needs recognizing first.
*Verdict: Held.* (One wording risk: the closing sentence "it can be forgiven because He is Someone" may let a reader slide back to God forgiving the whole wrong. Minor; see section 6.)

**Exchange 5. The C row misdescribes this school, and contradicts itself.**
*Contractualist:* The row says contractualism "grounds the newborn's standing in her being born one of us," and then says "the abandoned child whom no one would choose to speak for is owed only what someone agrees to owe her." Both cannot be true. The abandoned child was born one of us. If being born one of us grounds standing, she has it. And "owed only what someone agrees to owe her" is Hobbes and Gauthier, the contract of actual bargainers. It is not Scanlon, whose book title the row borrows. On my view nothing turns on anyone's agreement or anyone's choice to speak for her. We ask what could be justified to her. The trustee is a step in that reasoning, not a volunteer. Your C introduction promises that "each is stated as its best defenders state it." This row does not do that, and it is the only place v3 says where my school stops.
*Defense (first try):* But someone has to build the trustee. Scanlon himself says whether to include beings born of human parents is a substantive moral question, and *we* answer it.
*Contractualist:* We answer it by finding reasons, not by agreeing. You would not say the realist's truths are "only what someone agrees to" because realists are the ones who state them.
*Defense:* Fair; the first try fails. The true point is narrower and stronger. Contractualism reaches the abandoned child, but only by a reason it cannot explain: why being born of human parents puts her among those to whom we must answer, and why answering to each person is what right and wrong *are*. Scanlon treats both as truths about reasons that simply hold. There contractualism becomes godless realism and owes the realist's answer to where that necessity lives (W2). The theist explains both at once: each person is willed for her own sake (GS 24), and that is why each must be answered one by one.
*Contractualist:* That I accept as a fair statement of where we stop.
*Verdict: Failed as written; Held with repair.* **Serious.** This is the book's whole verdict on its nearest rival. As written, it states that rival in a form its best defenders reject, and it contradicts its own first clause.

**Exchange 6. W1(2): "It does not explain why what belongs may never be spent," but my school does.**
*Contractualist:* W1(2) says being human explains why the newborn belongs but "does not explain why what belongs may never be spent." Contractualism explains exactly that. Wrongness is a matter of what can be justified to each person, and complaints are compared one person at a time, never added up. A principle that let a surgeon spend one patient for five could be rejected by the one, and also by everyone who has reason to want the assurance that he will not be used for parts. Frances Kamm adds that persons have a *status*, inviolability, which is not a quantity of value to be promoted. A worth to be respected is not a worth to be maximized. Your C row on Kant already credits "never merely as a means" with standing beside A4 and A6. So W1(2)'s gap claim is false of the two rivals you credit most.
*Defense:* Granted against you; the sentence is true of kind-membership alone, which is what it was aimed at. But follow the explanation down. Why does wrongness consist in what can be justified to each? Scanlon's answer is the value of standing in mutual recognition with others. That is a value of relationship, and the infant left on the hillside has none except by proxy. Why do persons have the status of inviolability? Kamm says it is fitting to their worth, which is the question again. The theist's answer is one sentence: each is willed for her own sake (GS 24), so she can never be a means to someone else's good. That explains the anti-aggregation you and I share, and it explains why it reaches those who can enter no relation of justification.
*Contractualist:* That is an explanation at the same depth as mine, with one more stopping point behind it.
*Defense:* One stopping point behind both of your two (A6's "never spent" and A4's scope). That is the claim in C: reach, not count.
*Verdict: Held with repair (Minor).* W1(2) should credit the anti-aggregation answer and move its claim to the ground. At the ground, theism has an edge in unifying the two stopping points; on the verdicts themselves the two views are at parity.

**Exchange 7. Aquinas says God loves some more. Then worth that follows God's love is unequal.**
*Contractualist:* W1(2) cites ST I q.20 a.2: our love answers to worth already there, while God's love gives it. Read the next two articles. Aquinas holds that God wills greater good to some than to others, and that this is why some things are better than others (I q.20 a.3–4). If worth follows God's love, and God gives unequally, worth is unequal. Your Thomist ground predicts the opposite of A4.
*Defense:* v3 already holds the distinction that answers this: "Worth follows the call, not the capacity, and an equal call explains an equal worth" (GS 29). God's gifts on the way differ: talents, health, length of days, even graces. The end to which each person is called, God Himself, is the same for all, and every rational creature is governed for its own sake (*Summa contra Gentiles* III.112). Dignity follows that end, not the amount of gifts along the way. That is also why the severely disabled child is not worth less: her gifts are fewer, and her call is the same.
*Verdict: Held.* (Add SCG III.112 next to GS 24; it is the Thomist text for "willed for itself.")

**Exchange 8. The evil section: "the order of the whole" against "no one is the price."**
*Contractualist:* The first evil paragraph places the child with cancer under "He wills the order of the whole, not the loss for its own sake (ST I q.19 a.9)." That is whole-over-part reasoning; Aquinas's own example elsewhere is that the lion would not live if the ass were not killed (I q.48 a.2 ad 3). Two sentences later the text says "No one is the price of someone else's good, not even in God's hands." If the child's cancer is permitted for the good of a world of real causes, which benefits everyone, she is part of that price. Scanlon's individualist restriction applies to God as well.
*Defense:* Aquinas draws the line the text needs: intellectual creatures are governed for their own sakes, and the rest of creation for theirs (SCG III.112). The order of the whole is for persons. No person is only a part of it. The wolf lives by the fawn; no child is the fawn. And this school has its own tool that agrees. On an ex ante test, a principle is justifiable to each person if it serves each one's interest as things stand beforehand. A world of real causes, in which each person's own final good is assured unless she herself refuses it, passes that test for each, the child with cancer included. That is what "a good for her, not a price paid with her for someone else" means.
*Contractualist:* "Assured" is a promise I cannot inspect.
*Defense:* Agreed, and the text says so: it is a setting, not an explanation (CCC 309, 314). Parity.
*Verdict: Held with repair (Minor).* Add the SCG III.112 clause so that the two paragraphs do not appear to contradict each other.

**Exchange 9. Reciprocity: the Judge who lets Himself be asked still does not answer.**
*Contractualist:* "The Judge lets Himself be asked" (Genesis 18:25). But when Job asks, God answers from the whirlwind without giving reasons. Second-personal authority is mutual. A judge who can be questioned but never has to justify himself is not in the relationship of persons answering to one another; he is a sovereign.
*Defense:* The authority is not equal, as between a parent and a child, and the book never says it is. But the relationship is not that of sovereign and subject either. God gives no argument; He comes in person, first to Job and finally on the Cross. The evil section is honest: "That does not explain why." Job's reply is to a presence, not to a proof.
*Verdict: Held, at parity.* Already owned in the text as weight.

**Exchange 10. Hell: could the lost reasonably reject it?**
*Contractualist:* A principle under which a free refusal becomes final forever is one the lost could reject as out of all proportion.
*Defense:* Proportion is the measure of a sentence imposed. The text names hell as self-exclusion (CCC 1033) and denies predestination to it (CCC 1037). Your school's deepest value is respect for persons as the authors of their own answers. To make anyone accept communion against her final will would be the forced gift that Chapter 4 calls no gift. The weight is real, and v3 names it.
*Verdict: Held, at parity.*

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**As written: ONE Serious objection stands. No Fatal objection.**

1. **The C row "What we owe each other" misdescribes contractualism and contradicts itself.** It credits the school with grounding the newborn in "being born one of us," then says the abandoned child is "owed only what someone agrees to owe her." She was born one of us. And Scanlon's test turns on what could be justified to her, not on anyone's agreement or choice to speak for her. This breaks the version's own promise to state each rival "as its best defenders state it," in the one place v3 says where its nearest rival stops. **Serious.** (The repair in section 6 keeps the real point, that the school stops at a reason it cannot explain, and the comparison then holds at parity with an edge for theism.)

**Minor (wording and scope; none changes a conclusion):**

2. W1 frame: "the test that does [decide]" omits accounts built on persons, which pass the owed-or-ripening test. It narrows the question; it does not settle it (Exchange 1).
3. W1(1): the two-way dilemma omits the godless option that A3 itself grants (the debt survives with no one to release it). The text slides from "still owed" to "still exists" (Exchange 2). Also "or never knew" (carried over from Round 2) and "who could answer" (should be "forgive").
4. W1(2): "does not explain why what belongs may never be spent" is false of contractualism and Kant. Move the claim to the ground (Exchange 6).
5. W1 closing: "it can be forgiven because He is Someone" should name the offense against Him, or it undoes W1(1).
6. A3: "and should" places a disputed, largely Christian duty to forgive in a shared premise (Exchange 3).
7. Evil, paragraph 1: "the order of the whole" needs the SCG III.112 clause so that it does not clash with "no one is the price" (Exchange 8).
8. W1 frame: Romans 2:14–15 is attached to the wrong clause.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**
- Whether the debt to the dead is owed to someone who still is, or to the woman who was, forever unreleasable (W1(1)). The theist offers a clue, the contractualist a coherent and bleak alternative. The reader weighs them.
- Whether "each must be answered one by one" needs a ground beyond itself. The contractualist stops at the value of mutual recognition. The theist stops at "willed for her own sake" and claims the wider reach (Exchange 6).
- Whether truths about reasons need a home (W2; Scanlon's view that they need none). This is the realist standoff.
- Whether "she will be able to forgive, in her own name" is a hope one can weigh or only a promise one cannot inspect (Exchanges 3 and 8). The text calls it a hope.
- Whether a Creator's authority, which is not equal to ours, can still be second-personal (Exchange 9).
- Whether final self-exclusion respects persons or exceeds every proportion (Exchange 10).

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

## 7. New insights for the book

- **The owed-or-ripening test puts the theist and the contractualist on the same side.** Both say guilt is owed to someone. The real divide with the nearest rival is the dead and the unwanted, and the ground behind "each one must be answered." Saying so openly makes W1 stronger, because it no longer seems to ignore the best rival.
- **A gift is the one way something can come from another and still be wholly your own.** This answers Darwall's worry that grounding worth in God makes the victim's claim second-hand. A borrowed thing goes back to its owner; a given thing stays with the one who receives it. It fits Chapter 4.
- **"A nation can pardon; it cannot forgive."** The TRC amnesty is a living illustration of A3, known to many readers. (Verify the *AZAPO* case and the Biko amnesty ruling.)
- **The Church and the contractualist share the refusal to add people up.** The book can honor that and then ask the one question the contractualist cannot answer: why must each one be answered? "Because each was willed for her own sake."
- **SCG III.112 is the Thomist text for "no one is the price."** The order of the whole is for persons. The wolf lives by the fawn; no child is the fawn.
- **Worth follows the call, not the gifts.** This answers Aquinas's own "God loves some more" (I q.20 a.4): the gifts along the way differ, but the end is the same for everyone.
- **Ivan's question has a Catholic answer that keeps the victim's veto.** She will be able to forgive; she will not be made to. If she does not, his debt to her stands. That keeps A2's seriousness intact all the way to the end.

**Lines worth keeping:**
- "A gift is the one way something can come from another and still be wholly yours."
- "A nation can pardon. It cannot forgive."
- "The dead are still owed. They cannot release."
- "We agree she is owed. We part over whether she is still someone."
- "She will be able to forgive. She will not be made to."
- "The wolf lives by the fawn; no child is the fawn."
- (from v3, keep) "He does not forgive in her place. He keeps her." / "She was wanted before anyone saw her."
