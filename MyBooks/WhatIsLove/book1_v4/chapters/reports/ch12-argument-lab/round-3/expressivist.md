# Round 3: Humean Sentimentalism / Quasi-Realist Expressivism vs. v3

## 1. School and voice

The voice is David Hume (*Treatise* Book 3; *Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals*, hereafter EPM; *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion*), Adam Smith (*Theory of Moral Sentiments*, hereafter TMS), Simon Blackburn's quasi-realism and Allan Gibbard's norm-expressivism, with David Wiggins's "sensible subjectivism" as the school's realist-leaning heir. Moral judgment voices refined attitudes and plans. The quasi-realist then earns the realist's words one by one: "true," "wrong even if all approved," "her worth does not depend on us," and now "guilt is owed to her." He adds nothing to the world. In Round 3 he checks whether v3 made the repairs his school won in Round 2. He then presses the new owed-or-ripening test, the rewritten C line, the "wanted" language of W1(2), A5's "reason sees," the conclusion, and W2's scope note.

## 2. The four standing challenges (Round 3)

**Challenge 1: starting point for justice.**
*Their answer.* Justice is an artificial virtue for Hume: conventions of property and promise that arise among people of limited generosity and moderate scarcity, approved through sympathy with the public good. For Smith, justice in the strict sense is a negative virtue grounded in fitting resentment of injury. In the modern form, a person is owed what the norms we would accept after full information and sympathetic reflection require.
*Verdict: COHERENT, and it BREAKS at the edge, as the school admits.* Hume's passage on creatures too weak to resist us (EPM §3 pt 1) holds that we would be "bound by the laws of humanity" to treat them gently, but would lie under no restraint of justice toward them (checked against the text this round; Bennett's modernized version reads "treat these creatures gently"). Note that Hume says *bound*. The weak are not left with nothing. But their protection rests on our humanity, not on any right of theirs. The modern quasi-realist widens justice to them by choice, and that is coherent. A widening chosen is still not a debt found. No change from Rounds 1 and 2.

**Challenge 2: a hard case resolved (new case, chosen to test A2 against Hume himself).**
*Case.* A man promised his dying mother that he would never sell the family farm. Ten years on, keeping it will cost his children their schooling. No one alive knows of the promise.
*Method.* For Hume, a promise binds through the convention of promising and its usefulness. A secret promise to someone now dead does nothing for the convention, because no one's trust depends on it. Strictly applied, Hume's theory leaves this promise only the natural pull of feeling for the dead. Smith calls our sympathy with the dead an illusion of the imagination (TMS I.i.1.13, paraphrase). The modern quasi-realist repairs this at first order: "I would be wronging her, and I say so whoever knows." The impartial spectator then weighs her claim against the children's needs. Most refined judges conclude: sell, with sorrow, and keep what of her wish can still be kept.
*Verdict: HOLDS in result. The founding theory BREAKS on the reason, and the modern school repairs it by commitment.* The Catholic answer reaches the same verdict for a stated reason: a promise does not bind when circumstances have changed so much that keeping it would do grave harm (ST II-II q.110 a.3 ad 5, paraphrase; verify). The difference lies in what each says about the mother. Hume's own account of promising can find no wrong done to her, though A2's ashes case says she is wronged. The quasi-realist finds the wrong by standing for it. This supports v3's C line ("its founders were honest about the cost") and A2's choice of a promise to the dead as its test case. It shows no advantage on method.

**Challenge 3: equal, unconditional dignity.**
*Their answer.* "Every human being has equal worth" voices a stance of equal regard that we require of everyone, hold across every possible world, and hold as independent of our own attitudes. The impartial spectator corrects a sympathy that is naturally graded.
*Verdict: COHERENT, and it DECLINES TO GROUND.* Every word of A4 can be said. The "because" cannot: that we value her *because* her worth is there, rather than that "worth" names what our refined valuing answers to. v3's C line now puts the difference in exactly this place. Against Peter Singer's equally refined followers, the school can say "Singer is wrong," but only from where it stands. That is parity on proof. The book claims an advantage only on what disagreement *is*, and it is right to claim no more.

**Challenge 4: the ultimate good.**
*Their answer.* Happiness is plural: a calm mind, friendship, useful work, and being able to review one's own conduct with approval. The school's best witness is Hume's own death. Smith's letter to William Strahan (1776) describes Hume dying cheerful and unafraid, and calls him as near the idea of a perfectly wise and virtuous man as human frailty allows (paraphrase; verify).
*Verdict: COHERENT, AND SMALLER.* A serene death shows that the longing in W3 can be borne. It does not show that the longing is absent or answered. W3 claims only that the longing "shows what answer would fit," so Hume's calm is not a counterexample. The honest sentimentalist reply to "answered in the end by no one" is still: yes, and that is the grown-up sorrow. It neither borrows nor explains the longing away.

### Exchange 1. "Guilt is owed to her": the W1 test does not separate us.

*Expressivist:* W1 says the test that decides between the accounts is "is guilt owed to someone, or does it only ripen?" I pass it, and I pass it at both levels. At first order: "I owe her an apology; my guilt is owed to her." At the level of explanation, the school's own theory is second-personal. Peter Strawson's reactive attitudes and Gibbard's account of apt guilt tie the fittingness of my guilt to the fittingness of *her* resentment. "Owed to her" is built into the structure of the norm. W1's frame already concedes that "justice is owed to another" on the best godless accounts, and then sets a test those accounts pass. Your list of failures ("an order of cause and fruit, a necessary truth, the shape of our kind") is true, since none of those is anyone. But no rival except karma says guilt is owed to the order. The realist, the contractualist and I all say it is owed to her.

*Patrick's side:* Granted, and the contractualist, the debunker and the utilitarian found the same this round. The test separates us from the accounts in which guilt ripens: karma, and the reading of the moral life as character-repair. While the victim lives, it does not separate us from accounts built on persons. The work against those accounts is done by the next two witnesses. The first asks what becomes of what we owe her when no one on earth can receive it. The second asks where her worth comes from.

*Expressivist:* Then, as written, the frame claims a decisive test that decides nothing against your three nearest rivals.

*Patrick's side:* As written, the frame overstates by one sentence. The test narrows the field, and W1(1) and W1(2) do the separating. The frame should say so.

*Verdict: HELD WITH REPAIR (Minor).* The book should put this school's best line into its own frame: "We agree she is owed. We part over whether she will ever be able to forgive." Wording in §6.

### Exchange 2. Then can you say "the debt does not die with her"?

*Expressivist:* Yes. A3 says "we still owe her something we can no longer pay." I affirm it at first order. So does every philosopher who holds, with Joel Feinberg and George Pitcher, that the dead can be wronged. Your own A2 asks every reader to grant that the friend whose ashes were buried in a field "was wronged," though she is dead and, on my view, no longer exists. So the common ground you laid down already contains a debt owed to someone who is no longer anyone.

*Patrick's side:* That is fair. A2 and A3 are offered to every reader, and the reader without God grants them as wrongs to the woman she was.

*Verdict: Concession recorded, and it sets up Exchange 3.*

### Exchange 3. W1(1)'s either/or: as written it deduces survival from a shared premise, then calls the result a clue.

*Expressivist:* Here is the sentence: "Either the debt died with her, and what is left is only remorse, a wound that will never be healed by the one it was done to; or it did not, and she is still someone." Three things go wrong at once.
First, the either/or leaves out the reading most secular defenders of wrongs to the dead actually hold. On that reading the debt does not die. It is owed forever to the woman she was, it can never be received, and the bond stays broken. That is my school's view.
Second, the sentence puts my view on the wrong side. It pairs "only remorse" and "a wound never healed" with *the debt died*. On my view the debt lives, and remorse is what is left *because* the debt can never be received. The C line itself says "Remorse can end the guilt; only forgiveness can heal the bond." So W1(1) assigns the feeling school the horn that denies A3, and the C line says the same school affirms nearly all of A.
Third, and worst: A3, which the reader has granted, rules out the first horn. So the two sentences together deduce by plain logic that the dead are still someone, from a premise every reader was asked to grant. Then the text says "Reason cannot prove that hope" and "this is a clue, and it weighs as one." As written, W1(1) both makes the deduction and disowns it.

*Patrick's side:* The third point is the decisive one, and it is right. The book does not mean to deduce survival from A3. It means that guilt, followed to the end, reaches for a person who can still be faced. The honest form has three readings. The debt died with her, which A3 denies. It outlives her and can never be received, which is coherent and bleak, and which you hold. Or she is still someone and can one day be faced, which is Christian hope. The clue is in what guilt does. It goes looking for her, and it does not settle for "owed forever to no one who can receive it." Remorse is the right word for the second reading, not the first.

*Expressivist:* With three readings I accept that you have a clue and not a proof. I also grant that my reading leaves the bond broken forever. I call that tragedy, and I do not call it a gap in my account.

*Patrick's side:* That is the honest standoff. The reader tests the second and third readings against the guilt he knows.

*Verdict: W1(1) as written FAILED at this sentence (Serious: it argues for survival from a shared premise by an either/or that is not complete, and it assigns this school a horn it rejects). Repaired, it HELD as a clue at parity, with fit going to our side.* The debunker logged this same sentence as Serious and the contractualist and utilitarian as Minor. This school adds the second and third points: the misplaced remorse, and the fact that A3 turns the either/or into a deduction the text then disowns. Wording in §6.

### Exchange 4. Companions in guilt, again: on W1(2), worth answers to a love on your account too.

*Expressivist:* Your C line faults me because "in the end, the word 'worth' answers to our love." Now read W1(2): "Worth follows the call, not the capacity," and "she was wanted before anyone saw her." On your account her worth answers to a love as well, God's. Linda Zagzebski, a Catholic philosopher, builds a whole ethics on this ("divine motivation theory": value grounded in God's motives). So your shape is mine, and only the lover differs. And here is a dilemma. Either her worth follows her *being*, and then "wanted" adds nothing: an order that gives being to every member of a kind explains it as well, and your rows against Heaven and the Dhamma lose their point. Or her worth follows God's *wanting*, and then worth depends on an attitude after all.

*Patrick's side:* W1(2) already names the difference: "Our love answers to a worth already there; God's love gives it, by giving her being." A creature's love can only meet worth or project it. The Creator's love is the act by which there is a *her* at all (the chapter's own gloss on ST I q.20 a.2). That takes the first horn, with one addition you leave out. God's giving being is not an order handing a nature to a kind. It is a knowing will directed at *this* one (providence reaches singulars, ST I q.22 a.2), and His will is His goodness, not a sensibility that could have felt otherwise. So "wanted" is not a feeling added on top of her being. It is how her being came to be: she was wanted *into* being. An order can give a nature, but it cannot intend *her*.

*Expressivist:* Then say "wanted into being," or the reader will hear an attitude. And you still owe the step the witness promises. W1(2) says kind "does not explain why what belongs may never be spent." Being wanted explains that she matters for herself. Why does that forbid spending her for others?

*Patrick's side:* Because what the Good itself wills for her own sake cannot be made a mere means to someone else's good without setting oneself against that will. That is the bridge, and it should be on the page. It is also why "no one is the price of someone else's good" belongs to this witness.

*Verdict: HELD WITH REPAIR (Minor).* The disanalogy is already on the page. Two small wording repairs are needed: "wanted into being" in place of a bare "wanted," and the bridge sentence from "willed for itself" to "never spent." The debunker's point that "she was wanted before anyone saw her" should be marked as the Church's answer, since many children are wanted by no one on earth, joins naturally here.

### Exchange 5. The C line at full strength: worth and feeling arising together.

*Expressivist:* The line reads: "On its account our love does not answer to her worth." Blackburn accepts that at the level of explanation. But the school's most realist heir does not. David Wiggins's "sensible subjectivism," and John McDowell's model of values as like colors, hold that worth and refined feeling arise together. Neither comes first. Our responses are *answerable* to worth: they can fit it or fail it. For that heir, "our love answers to her worth" is *true*, and still no God is needed. The line misstates the best version of the school at the clause on which it turns.

*Patrick's side:* Fair. The line was written against the quasi-realist, and it fits him. The color model is a real heir, and the line should cover it. The answer does not change much. On the color model worth is real, but it is worth *to a human heart*, as red is red to human sight. It then reaches only as far as refined human hearts agree, so "two refined hearts can still part" bites it fully. And Darwin's hive bees return: a bee-sensibility would make fratricide merit approval, so A1's "wrong for everyone" would become "wrong for hearts shaped like ours." The color heir must either accept that or reach for an order that holds for every rational heart. That is W2's question.

*Expressivist:* The color heir says only a sensibility we can endorse from within counts, and the bees' cannot be endorsed.

*Patrick's side:* Endorsed by us. That is the same circle the quasi-realist owns openly.

*Verdict: HELD WITH REPAIR (Minor).* One clause covers the color model. It is not a straw man as written, since it fits its named holders, but it leaves out the heir that would most embarrass it.

### Exchange 6. The sensible knave: a cost the book shares.

*Expressivist:* The C line counts against Hume that "he had no argument for the 'sensible knave' who keeps the rules only when it pays." Hume does give the knave an answer: he loses peace of mind, the consciousness of integrity, and a satisfactory review of his own conduct. Hume admits only that if the knave's heart does not rebel, this answer will not move him. Your conclusion admits the same of your own case: it "cannot compel the one who gives up A4." Counting against me a limit you share breaks the spirit of your own fairness rule.

*Patrick's side:* That is right. No argument compels the knave, ours included. The difference lies in what the knave is missing. On Hume's account he lacks a feeling, and if he does not miss it, nothing is lost that he will ever know. On ours he is turning from a good that is there, and from Someone, whether he feels it or not. That is an explanatory difference. It is not a refutation, and it is the same difference the rest of the line already states.

*Verdict: HELD WITH REPAIR (Minor).* Keep the knave, but state the shared limit and the real difference. As written, the clause reads as an advantage the book does not have.

### Exchange 7. Smith's judge: our side presses, and the school answers.

*Patrick's side (our attack):* The line says Smith pictured conscience as an impartial judge "above everyone we know; on this account that judge is no one." Smith said more than that. In the final edition he wrote that when the man within the breast is shaken by the world's verdict, the only consolation lies in an appeal to a still higher tribunal, "the all-seeing Judge of the world, whose eye can never be deceived" (TMS III.2.33; checked against the edition history). In the first five editions he also argued that our sense of guilt looks for an atonement nature cannot supply, and that the doctrines of revelation "coincide... with those original anticipations of nature" (TMS II.ii.3.12, eds. 1–5). Like Kant in the *Religion*, the founder reached past feeling at exactly the place where W1 stands.

*Expressivist:* Two things. Smith's appeal is consolation, not theory. His spectator is a construction of the imagination formed by society, and the higher tribunal is not used in the theory's account of how conscience works. And he *removed* the atonement passage in 1790. The mature Smith let it go. If you cite it, cite the removal. Otherwise you are doing to Smith what your fairness rule forbids: making his piety a premise in his place.

*Patrick's side:* Agreed on both counts. The book should report the higher tribunal as Smith's own reach at the edge, a sign of strain, not evidence. The atonement passage belongs in the notes with its removal stated, or not at all.

*Verdict: HELD WITH REPAIR (Minor).* As written, the line is not false, because "on this account" marks the account and not Smith. But it leaves out the strongest true sentence it could say. One clause in §6.

### Exchange 8. A5: "reason sees" is the Thomist thesis Hume denied.

*Expressivist:* A5 now says "Reason sees certain goods... as good and to be pursued, and sees that good is to be done and evil avoided (ST I-II q.94 a.2)." That is better than v2's "inclination." But A is "what we take to be true," offered to every reader. Hume's best-known claim is that morality is "more properly felt than judg'd of" (*Treatise* 3.1.2, paraphrase of a short phrase), and that reason alone cannot set an end. Much of modern decision theory agrees. You have put the Thomist account of practical reason inside a premise. In Round 2 the lab removed loaded clauses from A1 and A4 for exactly this.

*Patrick's side:* That is a fair catch, and a small one. A5's job is the is/ought gap and the list of goods. It is not the claim that the faculty that grasps them is reason rather than refined feeling. Nothing in W or C uses "reason, not feeling" against this school: W2 is scoped away from you, and C rests on explanation. The sentence can keep the goods and give the faculty its name in the tradition's voice.

*Verdict: HELD WITH REPAIR (Minor).* Say "we find" in the premise, then name Aquinas's account and Hume's. No conclusion changes.

### Exchange 9. The conclusion's last line is my own slogan.

*Expressivist:* "What you feel then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it." Blackburn says exactly this at first order. It is his standard reply to the charge that expressivism makes cruelty wrong because we dislike it: no, it is wrong because of what it does to the child, and my feeling answers to that. So the book's last word is common ground with me. It does not separate us.

*Patrick's side:* It is not aimed at you. It is addressed "to him," the one who gives up A4. Against the one who grades worth by capacity, it does its work. It does separate the book from the founder. Hume wrote that reason discovers objects "as they really stand in nature," while taste has "a productive faculty," gilding or staining natural objects with colors borrowed from internal sentiment (EPM Appendix 1; checked this round). Your school's whole program is to keep Hume's gilding at the level of explanation while saying "answer" at first order. The conclusion asks the reader which he finds when he looks at the newborn: gilding or seeing. That is the plain sense the book claims, and it is a question, not a proof.

*Expressivist:* And I will say, "both, at different levels."

*Patrick's side:* Then the reader can judge which account makes better sense of what he finds.

*Verdict: HOLDS as written.* An optional sharpening uses Hume's own image so that the last line meets the founder on his own ground (§6). It is not needed for soundness.

### Exchange 10. "Where instinct fails": refinement *is* instinct corrected.

*Expressivist:* A4 says the worth we mean holds most plainly "where instinct fails, for the infant no one is drawn to hold," and the conclusion says "the newborn no instinct favors." Those sentences answer the crude debunker, who reads morality off raw instinct. They do not touch me. "Refined" *means* instinct corrected. Hume's steady and general point of view, and Smith's impartial spectator, exist to overrule the partiality of raw sympathy. Smith opens TMS with pity for the miserable, and pity is itself an instinct that favors the helpless.

*Patrick's side:* Granted. These lines are aimed at the debunker and should not be read as a point against refined feeling. Against you, the work is done by "two refined hearts can still part," which is exactly the case where correction runs out.

*Verdict: HOLDS (neutral against this school).* "No instinct favors" overstates for another reason: the debunker has asked to soften it, and parental and pitying instincts do exist. That is a Minor wording point, already logged by the debunker.

### Exchange 11. W2's scope note puts Gibbard in both camps.

*Expressivist:* The note says W2 answers "those who say moral facts simply exist, or that conscience is only what survival built," and that the feeling school "need[s] no meeting of law and mind." But Gibbard's *Wise Choices, Apt Feelings* is an evolutionary account of how norm-acceptance arose. In explanatory terms, I *do* say conscience is what survival and culture built. The note puts me in both camps.

*Patrick's side:* The camp W2 answers is the one that infers from "built by survival" that "nothing binds." W2's own words show this ("whoever denies that anything binds must deny that too"). You say conscience was built and that its norms bind, in your voice. The note should say "and so binds no one."

*Verdict: HOLDS WITH REPAIR (Minor).* Three words. The note itself is honest and correct in substance.

### Exchange 12. Hume's rule of proportion against "a God who is Love."

*Expressivist:* *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* §11: when we infer a cause from its effect, we may give the cause only what is needed to produce the effect. Our love is finite, mixed and failing. From it you may infer at most a cause sufficient for finite, mixed love. "A God who is Love" goes far past the evidence. Philo in the *Dialogues* (Parts X–XI) grants that evil is *compatible* with a good God whose reasons are unknown, but denies that goodness can be *inferred* from a mixed world.

*Patrick's side:* The book claims what Philo allows, and no more. It does not infer God's goodness from nature. Nature's pattern of pain goes on the other side of the scale, and the text says so ("the pattern of pain in nature still weighs"). The inference runs from features of the moral life, not from the world's mix: a wrong that would hold for every rational mind, a worth that may never be spent, a debt that outlives every creditor on earth, a longing to be known wholly. These ask for a ground that is necessary, personal and good, not for a large finite one. As a general rule, Hume's proportion principle would also forbid every theoretical inference in science that goes past the observed effect. And the book claims a best explanation, "converging and convincing," not a proportioned proof.

*Expressivist:* The necessity and the never-spent worth are what I read as features of our stance. So the "ground" you infer depends on reading the data your way.

*Patrick's side:* Yes. That is the standoff in C, not a new defect.

*Verdict: HELD AT PARITY.* The C row "Its claim is reach, not count" and the evil section's concession already answer this. No change.

### Exchange 13 (caution for our side). Do not use Hume's shipwreck.

*Patrick's side (considered and withdrawn):* Hume holds that in extreme necessity the rules of justice are suspended (EPM §3 pt 1, the shipwreck and famine cases). Is that the founders' cost against A6's absolutes?

*Expressivist:* Check your own house first.

*Patrick's side:* Aquinas teaches the same of property: in urgent need, taking what one needs is not theft (ST II-II q.66 a.7). Hume's suspension concerns property rules, not the killing of the innocent. Used against him, it would break the fairness rule.

*Verdict: Not to be used.* This is recorded so that no later draft adds it.

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**As written: ONE Serious objection stands. No Fatal.**

**Serious**
1. **W1(1)'s either/or** ("Either the debt died with her, and what is left is only remorse...; or it did not, and she is still someone"). (a) It is not complete. It leaves out the reading that A2 and A3 themselves invite the reader without God to hold: the debt outlives her, owed forever to the woman she was, never to be received. (b) It places this school's "remorse" on the horn that denies A3, which the C line says this school affirms. (c) A3, already granted, rules out the first horn, so the passage in effect deduces that the dead are still someone from a shared premise. It then says reason cannot prove the hope and calls the passage a clue. As written, the witness argues more than it claims and misplaces the nearest rival. This is the same sentence the debunker logged as Serious. It is one finding, not two. Points (b) and (c) are this school's addition. It is closed by one sentence (§6), after which it is an honest standoff.

**Minor (wording and scope; none changes a conclusion)**
2. W1 frame: "the test that does [decide]" decides nothing against this school, the realist or the contractualist while the victim lives (Exchange 1).
3. C, Feeling, refined: add the color-model heir (Exchange 5); state the knave as a shared limit with a real difference (Exchange 6); add Smith's "all-seeing Judge" (Exchange 7); note Hume's "bound by the laws of humanity" and his narrow "justice" (Challenge 1).
4. W1(2): "wanted into being," and the step from "willed for itself" to "never spent" (Exchange 4). Mark "she was wanted before anyone saw her" as the Church's answer (debunker).
5. A5: "reason sees" puts the Thomist account of practical reason into a shared premise (Exchange 8).
6. W2 scope note: "and so binds no one" (Exchange 11).
7. W1 closing: "it can be forgiven" should name the wrong against Him (with the contractualist).

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- **Is there a further "why"?** The quasi-realist says moral truths are explained at first order (cruelty is wrong because of what it does to the child), and that nothing further is left to explain. The book says necessity, unity, and a worth that may never be spent ask for a ground. Neither side proves its view. The book claims the plain sense of what people mean when they say "she is worth as much as you."
- **The ideally coherent Caligula, and Singer.** Neither account compels the fully consistent dissenter. The book already says so ("cannot compel the one who gives up A4").
- **The debt to the dead.** It is owed forever to the woman she was and never received, or she is still someone and can one day be faced. The first leaves the bond broken for good, and this school calls that tragedy. The second is a hope reason cannot prove. The reader weighs them against the guilt he knows.
- **Gilding or seeing.** When we look at the newborn, the quasi-realist says we do both, at different levels. The book says we see. The phenomenology is at parity, and the book is right to end on it as a question.
- **The longing.** Hume's serene death against W3's clue. A longing that can be borne is not thereby answered, and a longing does not prove its answer.
- **Hume's indifferent source and his rule of proportion.** These hold at parity once the moral data are read the quasi-realist way. They rest on the C standoff, not on a new defect.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**W1(1): replace** "Either the debt died with her, and what is left is only remorse, a wound that will never be healed by the one it was done to; or it did not, and she is still someone. People everywhere act as though she is:" **with:**
> There are three ways to read that. The debt died with her, but then A3 is false. Or it outlives her, owed forever to the woman she was, who can never receive it; then remorse is the most we can reach, a wound that will never be healed by the one it was done to. That reading is coherent, and honest people hold it. Or she is still someone, and can one day be faced. People everywhere act as though the last is true:

(Also, with the peers: drop "or never knew," and in "after everyone on earth who could answer is gone" read "who could forgive.")

**W1 frame: replace** "None of them is anyone the guilt could be owed to. Two witnesses say guilt is owed." **with:**
> None of them is anyone the guilt could be owed to. The nearest rivals answer that guilt is owed to the one we wronged, and this book agrees: it is owed to her first (A2). While she lives, the test separates us only from the accounts in which guilt ripens. We agree she is owed; we part over whether she will ever be able to forgive, and over where her worth comes from. Two witnesses speak to that.

**W1(2): replace** "Everyone is someone's child; she was wanted before anyone saw her." **with:**
> Not every child is wanted by anyone on earth; the Church says each one was wanted into being before anyone saw her (Jeremiah 1:5). And what the Good itself wills for her own sake, no one may spend for the sake of others.

**C, Feeling, refined: replace the whole line with:**
> - **Feeling, refined** (Hume, Adam Smith, and today's quasi-realists: moral words voice our attitudes, made informed, consistent and sympathetic) can say nearly everything in A in its own voice, even "her worth does not depend on us" and "my guilt is owed to her." Where it parts from us is in *why* that is true. On its account our love answers to what she is, and in the end "worth" is the name our refined love gives to what it answers. Some of its heirs say worth and feeling arise together, like color and sight; then worth is real, but it is worth to a human heart, and it reaches only as far as refined human hearts agree. Two refined hearts can still part over whether the newborn who will never think is worth as much as you, and then equal worth wins only by being ours. Its founders were honest about the cost. Hume granted that creatures too weak to make us feel their resentment would be owed gentleness under the laws of humanity, but not justice, which for him meant the rights of those who can hold us to account. He admitted that he had nothing to say to the "sensible knave" whose heart does not rebel at cheating when it pays. No argument compels such a man, ours included; the difference is what he is missing. On Hume's account he lacks a feeling; on ours he is turning from a good that is there, and from Someone, whether he feels it or not. Smith pictured conscience as an impartial judge above everyone we know, and wrote that when that judge within is shaken, we appeal to a higher tribunal, the all-seeing Judge of the world (*Theory of Moral Sentiments* III.2.33). On the account that keeps only feeling, both judges are no one. Remorse can end the guilt; only forgiveness can heal the bond.

**A5: replace** "Reason sees certain goods (life, truth, friendship, a just common life) as good and to be pursued, and sees that good is to be done and evil avoided (ST I-II q.94 a.2)." **with:**
> On reflection we find certain goods (life, truth, friendship, a just common life) good and to be pursued, and find that good is to be done and evil avoided. The Catholic tradition calls this the first light of practical reason (ST I-II q.94 a.2); Hume called it refined feeling.

**W2 scope note: replace** "or that conscience is only what survival built." **with:**
> or that conscience is only what survival built, and so binds no one.

**W1 closing: replace** "it can be forgiven because He is Someone." **with:**
> the wrong against Him can be forgiven because He is Someone.

**Conclusion (optional; sound without it). Insert before** "What you feel then is not the source of her worth." **:**
> Hume thought that feeling gilds the world with colors borrowed from within. Look at her and ask whether you are gilding her or seeing her.

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **Smith's higher tribunal is the sentimentalist twin of Kant's *Religion* 6:98–99.** At the edge of his own theory, the founder of the impartial spectator wrote that when the judge within is shaken, the only real consolation is an appeal to "the all-seeing Judge of the world, whose eye can never be deceived" (TMS III.2.33). Use it the way v3 uses Kant: as strain at the edge, not as evidence.
- **Smith's atonement passage, and its removal.** In editions 1–5 (TMS II.ii.3.12), Smith wrote that our sense of guilt anticipates an atonement nature cannot supply, and that revelation's doctrines "coincide... with those original anticipations of nature." He cut the passage in 1790 (confirmed against the edition history on adamsmithworks.org). It is a striking parallel to W1(1), but honesty requires citing the removal with it. Notes only, if at all.
- **Hume's own image is the cleanest way to state the divide.** Hume: reason finds things as they stand; taste "gilds or stains" them with colors borrowed from within (EPM Appendix 1). The book's last line says the opposite of Hume and the same as Blackburn at first order. So the divide falls between Hume's *explanation* and the plain sense of the experience. The chapter can say so in one sentence.
- **W1's test sorts karma well and the person-based rivals not at all.** All three of the nearest rivals say "owed to her." What separates us is whether she will ever be able to forgive, and where her worth comes from. Saying that openly makes W1 stronger.
- **Hume's theory of promising cannot see the wrong in A2's ashes case.** A secret promise to the dead does nothing for the convention. The modern quasi-realist sees the wrong only by standing for it. That one case shows more about the school than a page of description.
- **Do not use Hume's shipwreck against A6.** Aquinas holds the same about property in extreme need (ST II-II q.66 a.7).
- **"Wanted into being"** answers companions-in-guilt in three words: God's love is not a feeling about her, it is how there is a her.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "We agree she is owed. We part over whether she will ever be able to forgive."
- "What the Good itself wills for her own sake, no one may spend for the sake of others."
- "She was wanted into being before anyone saw her."
- "No argument compels the knave, ours included; the difference is what he is missing."
- "Ask whether you are gilding her or seeing her."
- (from v3, keep) "Remorse can end the guilt; only forgiveness can heal the bond." / "Our love answers to a worth already there; God's love gives it." / "He does not forgive in her place. He keeps her."
