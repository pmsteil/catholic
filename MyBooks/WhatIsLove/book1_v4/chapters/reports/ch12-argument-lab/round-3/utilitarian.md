# Argument Lab, Round 3: Utilitarianism / Consequentialism vs. v3

## 1. School and voice

The opponent speaks for realist, impartialist consequentialism at full strength: Sidgwick (*The Methods of Ethics*), Hare's two-level theory (*Moral Thinking*), Parfit (*Reasons and Persons*; *On What Matters*), de Lazari-Radek and Singer (*The Point of View of the Universe*), and Railton's "sophisticated consequentialism" (1984). Its neighbours are called in where they help: Hooker's rule consequentialism, John Harris's "survival lottery," threshold deontologists who are not utilitarians but give up absolutes at catastrophe (Michael Moore, Michael Walzer). Round 2's faults have been fixed, and the school knows it. So this round does not re-argue whether constraints are true (that standoff is settled at parity). It tests whether v3 states its absolutes and its theodicy *exactly*. Where v3 speaks too broadly, the utilitarian can turn the book's own faith against it.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges (Round 3 restatement)

**Challenge 1: Starting point for justice.**
*Answer:* Bentham's rule that each counts for one and no one for more than one, which Sidgwick refined into the axiom of impartiality: the good of any one person matters no more, from the point of view of the universe, than the like good of any other. Justice is the set of rules that protect the most vital interests. It is stern because so much rests on it.
*Verdict:* **Coherent; it borrows nothing.** It breaks where Round 2 found: "owed" comes second to "best." A duty to *this* person holds only while the rule that names her pays.

**Challenge 2: A hard case resolved by its own principles.** (New case.)
*Answer:* A hijacked airliner full of passengers is minutes from a packed stadium. It can be shot down. The passengers will die either way. Shoot it down: the deaths of the passengers are certain anyway, and thousands are saved.
*Verdict:* **Holds as a decision, cleanly.** The case is real. Germany's Constitutional Court struck down a law allowing such a shoot-down in 2006, on the ground that it would treat the passengers as mere objects. Catholic moralists divide over it. Some call it intending innocent deaths. Others treat it like the vital conflict of Round 2: the act's object is stopping the aircraft, and the passengers' deaths are foreseen, not chosen. The book need not settle this. But the case shows where the utilitarian is strongest: when the innocent will die anyway, his answer is simple and the absolutist's is disputed. He does not borrow the book's facets. He pays at the secret transplant, as before.

**Challenge 3: What grounds equal, unconditional dignity?**
*Answer:* Equal *consideration* of interests, yes. Equal *worth* whatever one's capacities, no (Singer). At the practical level, Hare teaches a firm rule of human rights. Singer's "replaceability" argument is the honest consequence: if the value at stake is a quantity of well-being, and the being cannot see itself as continuing over time, one such being can in principle be replaced by another that has equal or greater well-being.
*Verdict:* **Breaks at the ground, honestly.** Replaceability is exactly what W1(2) answers: a worth that is a quantity can be made up by another quantity, but a person who was wanted for her own sake cannot be made up by anyone. This is now the book's strongest reply to this school.

**Challenge 4: The ultimate good.**
*Answer:* Well-being, seen from "the point of view of the universe." For de Lazari-Radek and Singer, that means pleasant consciousness. For Parfit it may mean an objective list.
*Verdict:* **Coherent; thin at the summit.** Impartial reason can see that each one counts. Only a person can *want* each one (W3). Railton concedes the point from inside the theory (Exchange 10).

**Exchange 1: "No one is the price of someone else's good, not even in God's hands."**
*Utilitarian:* Your evil section now says that no one is the price of someone else's good, not even in God's hands. Your own Scripture says the Son of Man came to give his life as a ransom for many (Mark 10:45), and that you were bought with a price (1 Corinthians 6:20). Caiaphas said it was expedient that one man die for the people (John 11:50), and your Church teaches that God brought the greatest good out of exactly that killing (CCC 312). The center of your faith is one innocent who is the price of the many's good, in God's hands. Your sacrifice facet (Chapter 9) and your A6 ("real good for another always asks something of the giver … sometimes far more") say the same. The firefighter who dies for a child *is* the price of her life. So the sentence contradicts your creed and your own premise. If you say "that's different," tell me how, because the sentence as written draws no distinction.
*Defense:* The distinction is real and it is Catholic. Christ was not spent by anyone. He laid down His life of His own accord (John 10:18; CCC 609). Those who killed Him did evil. God permitted it and brought good out of that very evil (CCC 312, 599–600). The firefighter, too, *gave* himself; no one gave him away. What the evil section means to forbid is being *made* the price, used as currency by another. Self-gift is the opposite of being spent. It is love's highest act.
*Rejoinder:* Then say that. As written, "no one is the price" denies the Cross as every Christian reader hears it ("He paid the price for me"), and it denies your own sacrifice facet.
*Result:* **Failed as written; held with repair. Serious.** The defense is good, but the text does not contain it. The repair also gives the book its best line on this subject: *A person may give himself; no one may be given away.*

**Exchange 2: "Not even in God's hands" vs. Aquinas's Lord of life.**
*Utilitarian:* Your own master says the opposite. Aquinas holds that by God's command death may be inflicted on anyone, guilty or innocent, without injustice, because God is Lord of life and death (ST I-II q.94 a.5 ad 2; q.100 a.8 ad 3, on Abraham and Isaac). In Exodus 12 God Himself strikes Egypt's firstborn, infants included, so that Israel may go free. That is a price paid with them for someone else's good, and the text says God did it, not that He permitted it. So the absolute binds men, not God. Your safety from the numbers holds in human hands only.
*Defense:* Two answers. (1) The Catechism grounds the human absolute on exactly this point: God alone is Lord of life, so no one may claim for himself the right to destroy an innocent human being directly (CCC 2258). That does not make God a utilitarian. It means that a life God takes is not lost to Him. He is the only one who can take a life and still give the one taken every good, and the good He hopes to give is a good *for her* (the book's own principle). A surgeon cannot do that; that is why the surgeon may not kill. (2) The "dark" passages of the Old Testament are a real problem. The Church reads them in the light of Christ and does not take them as teaching that God uses persons as currency (*Dei Verbum* 15; *Verbum Domini* 42). The chapter does not need to settle that here.
*Rejoinder:* So God is exempt from the rule you call absolute.
*Defense:* God is not exempt from goodness. He alone stands where the rule's reason does not reach: a creature has no right over another's life, and the Creator does. That is the authority W1's closing describes, the Creator's and not a commander's.
*Result:* **Held with repair; a standoff at parity on the Old Testament texts.** Keep "not even in God's hands" only together with the self-gift distinction and CCC 2258's "God alone is Lord of life." On its own, the phrase reaches beyond Aquinas.

**Exchange 3: The catastrophe case. Is A6's absolute really "what almost everyone cannot give up"?**
*Utilitarian:* Your examples (the surgeon and five patients, a scapegoat to calm a riot, an experiment that kills) are cases nearly everyone agrees on. But A6 says *never*, "not to buy a better outcome for many," and W1(2) builds on "no total." Raise the number. Most people, and many of the best anti-utilitarians, give the absolute up at catastrophe. Nozick left open whether his side constraints may be broken to avoid "catastrophic moral horror." Moore and others hold a threshold deontology. Walzer, no utilitarian, defended bombing German cities in Britain's "supreme emergency." Anscombe's own target, Truman's decision to kill civilians in order to force a surrender, was approved by most Americans at the time, and many still approve it. The exceptionless line is the Church's thesis (VS 52, 80). It is not shared data. Your A heading claims near-universal reflective assent for each item, and for A6's full content that claim is false. "The absolute can cost a life" also understates. It can cost many lives, and the Church accepts that.
*Defense:* (1) The cases A6 gives are shared, and they carry most of the weight. (2) Threshold deontology is unstable. If a million lives justify killing one innocent, why not 999,999? Larry Alexander has argued that no principled threshold exists. Then the "threshold" is just utilitarianism with a high exchange rate, or else it is the absolute. (3) Even the threshold deontologist holds that the innocent resists the totals far beyond what her welfare is worth. Utilitarianism cannot explain that resistance. W1(2) can.
*Rejoinder:* All good arguments, *for* the absolute. None of them shows that almost everyone already holds it. You fixed exactly this error in Round 2 when you moved euthanasia out of A4.
*Result:* **Failed as written; held with repair. Serious.** A6 should separate the shared core from the Church's full claim, own the price ("lives, sometimes many"), and say that the line held at every number is the book's claim. This makes the case stronger, not weaker: it turns the catastrophe case into the threshold's instability, which is the book's ground, not the opponent's.

**Exchange 4: Double effect, and the theodicy as cosmic double effect.**
*Utilitarian:* The Second reply says that permitting what a world of real causes brings about "is not doing it." For a human agent who did not make the world, perhaps not. For the Creator, who keeps every cause in being and works in every act it performs, the line between doing and permitting is at its weakest. And your own source says more. Aquinas holds that God *wills* natural corruptions, not for their own sake but by willing the good of the natural order to which they belong (ST I q.19 a.9, which v3 cites), and that in this way He causes them, so to speak, by accident (I q.49 a.2). That is double effect, with God as the agent. And double effect requires *proportion*: the good must outweigh the foreseen harm. That is a weighing. So your God weighs the fawn's agony against the good of the whole. "Not a trade" is true only in the narrow sense that the harm is not His means. Meanwhile the wolf lives by the fawn, and the fawn gets nothing back.
*Defense:* Granted, and it costs nothing. The book's line was never "consequences don't count." The line is that side effects may be weighed and persons may not be spent. The Church weighs consequences too (CCC 1754); what she forbids is choosing an evil as end or means (CCC 1756; VS 80). So the defense should rest on intending vs. foreseeing, which holds for the Creator, and not on doing vs. permitting, which is weak for Him. For persons, the book's standard is *stricter* than human double effect: the good hoped for must be a good *for her*, not merely a greater total. Animals are not under A4. Their pain stays on the scale as real weight, and v3 already says so ("the pattern of pain in nature still weighs").
*Result:* **Held with repair. Minor.** Replace "permitting … is not doing it" with intention language, and say outright: side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent.

**Exchange 5: "Never to be killed or used as a means."**
*Utilitarian:* Two loose words. (a) "Killed": the list names "the enemy," and your Church allows killing in legitimate defense and just war (CCC 2263–2265, 2309). It also allows foreseen civilian deaths in proportion, so those civilians are killed and the act is justified by a weighing. (b) "Used as a means": Kant's formula says "*merely* as a means," and v3's own Kant row quotes it that way. Your Church lets the state conscript citizens and risk their lives for the common good (CCC 2310), and lets a living donor give a kidney (CCC 2296). Both are uses of a person for others' good.
*Defense:* "For better totals" already narrows both. The aggressor is killed to stop him, not to improve a sum. Conscription serves the common good, which is not a total (CCC 1905–1912): it is the good of each person in a shared life, and the soldier owes his part of it as a duty. The donor gives with consent. Still, the words should say what the Church says: never *deliberately* killed, and never used as a *mere* means.
*Result:* **Held with repair. Minor.**

**Exchange 6: The survival lottery and "no innocent is safe from the numbers."**
*Utilitarian:* John Harris's "survival lottery" (1975) turns your line around. Behind a veil, each of us is far likelier to be one of the five who need organs than the one healthy donor. A fair lottery for donors makes *every* innocent safer. Your absolute leaves more innocents dead. Who is "safe from the numbers"?
*Defense:* "Safe" here means a status, not a probability. Under the lottery, each person's life is held on the condition that the totals never call for it. A hostage whose captor is a careful calculator may well survive, but he is not safe. Frances Kamm makes the secular version of this argument: a world in which no one may be spent is better for each person than one in which anyone may be, even if a few more die. Note too that the five are not killed by the numbers. They die of their illness. The absolute protects persons from being spent; it was never a promise of survival.
*Rejoinder:* So your "safe" is a status we do not believe in.
*Result:* **Held at parity.** This is Round 2's standoff in a new form. The line stands. It reads more exactly as "no innocent is safe from being spent" (see §6).

**Exchange 7: Sidgwick's esoteric morality. Is the C line accurate?**
*Utilitarian:* You say our masters admitted that utilitarianism's truth might have to be kept from the public. Sidgwick published his book. Hare explicitly rejected "Government House" and put both levels inside each person. And Railton (following Bales) separates the theory as a *criterion* of right action from the *decision procedure* people use. Teaching firm rules as a decision procedure is not a lie, any more than a coach's "never give up your queen" is a lie because grandmasters sometimes should.
*Defense:* Sidgwick said more than "keep the exceptions quiet." He allowed that a utilitarian might reasonably want the common run of people to keep away from the system as a whole, in case they misapply it (*Methods* IV.5; paraphrase, verify the section). De Lazari-Radek and Singer defended esoteric morality again in 2010, and Parfit allowed that consequentialism may be "self-effacing," telling us to believe some other theory. So "masters," plural, is fair. As for the decision procedure: a rule against killing the innocent does its work in temptation only if it is held as *true without exception*. That is why Hare wants it felt as inviolable. If the many held it as a heuristic, it would fail exactly when it is needed. If they hold it as exceptionless, they hold what the teacher thinks false. Hare is the honest exception, and the line should not cover him.
*Utilitarian (tu quoque):* Your Church had the Index. Vatican I forbids Catholics to suspend their assent in order to investigate (*Dei Filius*, ch. 3 and can. 6). Is that not "stopping looking because you fear what you will find" (A7)?
*Defense:* No. The Church's doctrine is entirely public. She has no teaching that some truths should be hidden because believing them would do harm. *Dei Filius* claims certainty, not concealment, and the Church commands inquiry (*Fides et Ratio*; 1 Peter 3:15). Fairness, though, asks the book to credit Sidgwick's life: he resigned his Trinity fellowship in 1869 rather than profess articles he could no longer believe. His life kept A7 better than his theory could.
*Result:* **Held with minor repair.** Say "some of its masters," name the living defenders, and leave Hare outside the charge.

**Exchange 8: The preference utilitarian and the ashes.**
*Utilitarian:* "Nothing in anyone's experience is different": granted. But on the success theory (Hare, Parfit), her life went worse because her wish was frustrated. So for us the ashes case *is* worsening, and the C line's "reduces wronging to worsening (A2: the ashes)" does not touch us.
*Defense:* Then imagine that a storm scatters the ashes over the field instead. Her wish is frustrated exactly as much, so she is worse off exactly as much, and no one has wronged her. The same setback can come with a wrong or without one. So the wrong is not the setback.
*Rejoinder:* The difference lies in the promiser's act and its effects on trust.
*Defense:* There are no effects on trust in the secret case. What is left is that he did it to *her*.
*Result:* **Held with repair (Minor; it strengthens A2).** Add the storm.

**Exchange 9: W2's engine of correction is Singer's expanding circle.**
*Utilitarian:* W2 says conscience corrects itself through "reason holding itself to consistency." That is Singer's *The Expanding Circle*, and we say the circle keeps expanding. It reaches animals, where Christian conscience is most plausibly bent by self-interest (CCC 2417 permits using animals for food and research). It also runs on against the doing/allowing line and against the role of personal force, which consistency cannot defend. By your own engine, A6 falls next.
*Defense:* Consistency is a formal test. It needs a judgment about which differences are relevant, and it does not choose that judgment for you. Recent history has run the correction both ways. After Nuremberg, reflection moved *toward* an absolute: no experiment without consent, whatever the gain. On animals, the Church already says it is contrary to human dignity to make them suffer or die needlessly (CCC 2418; *Laudato Si'* 92, 130). And W2 itself says conscience fails where self-interest pulls, which leaves the book's own practice open to correction.
*Result:* **Held at parity.** The underlying standoff is speciesism vs. worth that follows kind. It is not a defect in v3 as written.

**Exchange 10: Railton's counterfactual condition (supports W3 and Chapter 4).**
*Utilitarian:* Sophisticated consequentialists can love non-instrumentally. Railton's Juan loves his wife for her own sake and does not calculate.
*Defense:* Railton is honest that Juan's love meets a "counterfactual condition": he would change his life if it stopped being the morally best one, on the consequentialist standard. That is humane, but it is a covenant with a clause for better totals. The book's love says "it is good that you exist," and no totals clause is written into it.
*Result:* **Held.** This supports W3's "highest good that knows and loves no one" and Chapter 4's covenant. Impartial reason can see that each one counts; only a person can want each one.

**Exchange 11 (brief): W1(1)'s either/or.**
*Utilitarian:* "Either the debt died with her … or she is still someone." There is a third option. George Pitcher and Joel Feinberg hold that the debt is owed to the woman she *was*. It is real, it is permanent, and it can never be paid. That needs no surviving subject.
*Defense:* That is a debt that can never be forgiven: a wound that will never be healed by the one it was done to, which is v3's own first horn. The clue still stands: guilt keeps looking for her.
*Result:* **Held with minor repair.** Name the third option, so the dilemma is not false as written.

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**As written: TWO SERIOUS. No Fatal.**

**S1. Serious: "No one is the price of someone else's good, not even in God's hands" (evil section, Second).** As written, the sentence denies what every Christian reader affirms (Mark 10:45; 1 Corinthians 6:20; CCC 312, 599–609). It contradicts A6's own "real good for another always asks something of the giver … sometimes far more," and with it the sacrifice facet of Chapter 9. It also reaches beyond Aquinas, who holds that God, as Lord of life, may take any life without injustice (ST I-II q.94 a.5 ad 2; q.100 a.8 ad 3). The book has a full answer (self-gift is not being spent; John 10:18; CCC 2258), but the text does not contain it. A utilitarian can quote Caiaphas (John 11:50) against the sentence in one line.

**S2. Serious: A6's exceptionless absolute is presented as shared data.** A6 ("never … not to buy a better outcome for many"), A4's "never," and W1(2)'s "no total" put the Church's line held at every number (VS 52, 80) into a list headed by near-universal reflective assent. At catastrophe scale that assent fails: Nozick's hedge, threshold deontology, Walzer's supreme emergency, and wide public approval of Hiroshima. "The absolute can cost a life" understates what the Church accepts. This is the same honesty error the lab fixed in Round 2 when euthanasia was taken out of A4. The book's arguments for the absolute (the threshold is unstable; the innocent resists the totals even for the threshold deontologist) are good, but they are not in the text.

**Minor**
1. A4: "killed" → "deliberately killed"; "used as a means" → "used as a mere means" (CCC 2263–2265, 2296, 2310).
2. A6: "safe from the numbers" reads more exactly as "safe from being spent" (Harris's survival lottery).
3. Evil section, Second: rest the defense on intending vs. foreseeing; say "side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent." Aquinas says God *wills* natural corruptions incidentally, not merely permits them (ST I q.19 a.9; q.49 a.2).
4. C, utilitarianism: "tradeable" residue; storm contrast for preference views; "some of its masters," Hare excepted; credit that consequences matter (CCC 1754).
5. A2: add the storm contrast (it makes the case decisive against the success theory).
6. W1 frame: broaden "or does it only ripen"; move Romans 2:14–15 to "knowable without knowing its Author."
7. W1(1): name the third option (a debt owed forever to the woman who was).
8. C, Dhamma: one clause on why desert costs A4 and inherited death (CCC 405) does not (fairness; for the Buddhist reviewer).

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Whether constraints are true or only useful (Hare's two levels): the book rests on seeing, not feeling.
- The survival lottery: safety as a status vs. safety as odds.

## 7. New insights for the book

- **Self-gift is the opposite of being spent.** The theodicy, A6 and Chapter 9 meet in one distinction: a person may give himself, and no one may be given away. That distinction also answers Caiaphas, the strongest one-line utilitarian reading of the Cross.
- **Rest the theodicy on intention, not permission.** For the Creator, intending vs. foreseeing holds, and doing vs. permitting is weak. Aquinas's "wills incidentally" is double effect at God's scale, and the book can own it: side effects may be weighed. For persons it adds a stricter rule than human double effect: the good must be a good *for her*.
- **Turn the catastrophe case into the threshold's instability.** The absolutist's honest admission ("fewer hold the line when the numbers grow vast") becomes the strongest argument for the line: no one can say where the threshold is, and a worth that is only a share of the total cannot explain why it resists the total at all.
- **Replaceability is the hinge.** Singer's honest admission that, on his view, some beings are replaceable is exactly what W1(2) explains: a quantity can be made up by another quantity, but she cannot be made up by anyone.
- **Covenant has no totals clause.** Railton's counterfactual condition is the most honest account of love inside consequentialism, and it shows what Chapter 4's covenant adds.
- **Credit Sidgwick's life.** Fairness and A7 together: the founder of the esoteric doctrine resigned his fellowship rather than profess what he doubted. This makes the C line fair, and kinder.
- **The storm makes the ashes case decisive.** The same frustration can come with a wrong or without one, so the wrong is not the setback.
- **Genesis 18:25 already supports A6.** Abraham's plea is the anti-utilitarian one: the innocent must not be swept away with the guilty, even if the guilty are spared.
