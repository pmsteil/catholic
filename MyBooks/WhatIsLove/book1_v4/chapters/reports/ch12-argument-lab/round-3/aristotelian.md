# Argument Lab, Round 3: Secular Neo-Aristotelian Naturalism / Virtue Ethics vs. v3

## 1. School and voice

The voice is the school of Philippa Foot (*Natural Goodness*, 2001; "The Problem of Abortion and the Doctrine of the Double Effect," 1967; "Euthanasia," 1977; "Utilitarianism and the Virtues," 1985), Rosalind Hursthouse (*On Virtue Ethics*, 1999), Michael Thompson (*Life and Action*, 2008; "What Is It to Wrong Someone?", 2004), John McDowell (second nature, the space of reasons) and Martha Nussbaum (*The Fragility of Goodness*; "Transcending Humanity"; *Anger and Forgiveness*, 2016). Goodness measures a living thing against the form of life of its kind. The virtues are what a rational, social, dependent animal needs to live well. Justice is "bipolar": a wrong is done *to* someone, who has a complaint against the one who did it.

In Rounds 1 and 2 this school pressed v1's invalid step, Anscombe's misstated fork, "unconferred, so received," and a dilemma that cut the Thomist as deeply as the Aristotelian. v3 fixed all of these. Round 3 accepts the fixes and presses on what v3 now leans on: the "owed or ripen" test, W1(2)'s claim that being human "does not explain why what belongs may never be spent," "accident or wisdom," A5's handing of the question to W, the flourishing row in C, and the weight that teleology adds to evil. This is still the rival nearest the book. Aquinas built on Aristotle, so most of the fight is over the *source* of a moral order both sides largely share.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

*(Round 1 and 2 answers stand. These add what Round 3 sharpened.)*

**Q1. Starting point for justice.**
*Their answer:* Justice is the virtue that gives another what is due, and its form is bipolar. The unjust man does not only miss a standard. He wrongs *her*, and she has a complaint against *him* (Thompson; NE V.4 on corrective justice; Aquinas, ST II-II q.58 a.2, q.62). Round 3 adds forgiveness. The modern school is not silent about it. Nussbaum (*Anger and Forgiveness*) treats forgiveness at length. She distrusts the "transactional" kind (apology demanded, then pardon granted) and prefers an unconditional generosity that looks forward. Secular philosophers in the Aristotle-and-Adam-Smith line (Charles Griswold, *Forgiveness*, 2007) give a full account of forgiveness between persons as a virtue.
*Verdict:* **Holds.** It borrows only, as before, the *equality* of every party, which came from the Stoic, Christian and Kantian inheritance. Aristotle's own justice was proportioned to merit.

**Q2. A hard case.**
*Case (new for Round 3):* A pandemic ICU has no free ventilators. A man with a lifelong disability has been stable on one for weeks. A younger patient with a better outlook arrives. Should the first man's ventilator be taken from him and given to the second?
*Their answer:* At intake, when the scarce thing is given out, practical wisdom and charity may weigh the likely outcome. But taking a ventilator from a man who is using it, in order to give it to someone else, is doing harm to him for another's benefit, not just failing to help. Foot's distinction between negative and positive duties forbids it, and so does justice: he has a claim, and the newcomer has no claim on *his* ventilator. Most of the school would refuse, and they would refuse the scheme that ranks the disabled lower as well.
*Verdict:* **Coherent, and it borrows nothing beyond equal standing.** It reaches the same verdict as the Church (CCC 2276–2279) by the same structure: doing versus allowing, and the claim of the one in front of you. This matters for the ledger. In cases that are "one for others," this school *explains* the constraint. It does not merely accommodate it (Exchange 2).

**Q3. Ground of equal, unconditional dignity.**
*Their answer:* Equality comes from membership in the rational kind, from conception to death. The prohibition on spending one innocent for others comes from the structure of justice. The wrong would be done to her, the many have no claim on her life, and "the best state of affairs" is not a currency the virtues trade in (Foot, 1985).
*Verdict:* **Holds for equality. Holds for "never spent *for others*" in the strength of the constraint, though its ground stops at the shape of the virtue. It breaks, by the Church's lights, on euthanasia.** Foot let a person's life be ended when it is no longer a good to him and he waives his right to it. Here the school's own account has a real gap. Nothing in "membership plus justice" forbids ending a life *for the person's own sake*, with his consent.

**Q4. Ultimate human good.**
*Their answer:* *Eudaimonia*: a complete life of virtuous activity with friends and in a community, bounded by death. Round 3 presses Nussbaum's point further. It is not only that endless life would be "more of the same." The human virtues (courage, generosity, love that risks loss) take their *shape* from our need and our danger. A life with no risk and no need has no field for them, and the one "made whole" would no longer be the person we loved.
*Verdict:* **Coherent.** It is still the strongest secular argument that W3's longing is not a longing of human nature. v3 answers its first form. Its second form is answered in Exchange 9.

### 3b. Exchanges

**E1. Attack on the W1 frame: "owed or ripen" does not separate us from you.**
*Opponent:* Your test is "is guilt owed to someone, or does it only ripen?", and you say "the shape of our kind" is not anyone the guilt could be owed to. I never said guilt is owed to the shape of our kind. It is owed to *her*, the one I wronged. That is what bipolar justice means, and your own C row says I know "justice is owed to another and a wrong must be repaid." So I pass your test. It separates you from karma and from bare necessary truths. It does not separate you from me. Against me, the only live question is whether guilt is owed *only* to her, while she lives, or *also* to Someone who outlasts her. And one more point: you cite Romans 2:14–15 for "the wrong is wrong first because of what it does to the child." That text says the Gentiles know the law without having it. It does not say what makes a wrong wrong.
*Defense:* Granted on both counts. The frame was built against karma, and it lumped this school in by mistake. The narrowed question is the honest one, and it is exactly where W1(1) and W1(2) do their work: the debt that outlives every creditor on earth, and the second party to every wrong (CCC 1850). The citation should move to the clause it fits.
*Result:* **Held with repair (Minor).** It is a framing fault, not a broken step, because the two witnesses that follow carry the real question.

**E2. Attack on W1(2): your decisive sentence is false of this school's founder, and your own A6 cases are hers.**
*Opponent:* W1(2) says: "Being human explains why the newborn belongs. It does not explain why what belongs may never be spent: why no total, no vote and no grief can make it right to kill one innocent for others." Taken literally, membership alone does not explain it. But you imply that *we* do not explain it, and we do. The magistrate who frames an innocent man to calm a mob is Foot's own case from 1967. Your surgeon is its close cousin. She explained both without God: negative duties are stricter than positive ones, so doing harm is not the same as failing to help. Thompson adds that the wrong would be done *to her*, and the five have no claim on her life. Foot's later work adds that "the best state of affairs" is not a currency the virtues trade in, so no total of any size gives a reason to cross the line. Anscombe, whom you cite, showed that "unjust" excludes the judicial killing of the innocent at once. A secular Aristotelian can keep her absolutes and leave out her God. So "for others" is the one leg where we are strongest. And now the turn. Your own explanation, "willed for itself" (GS 24), meets the paradox of deontology. The five are also each willed for themselves. If the victim's worth is what grounds the constraint, why not kill one so that five are not killed? A worth in the victim tells you what to *promote*. It does not tell you what *you* may not do. Your account needs our structure of action, doing versus allowing and what one intends, as much as we do.
*Defense:* Most of this is right, and the book should say it. The Church shares the structure of action (double effect, ST II-II q.64 a.7; the moral object of the act, VS 78–80), and it should credit Foot by name. But two things remain. First, *why* justice has that shape. The rival's answer is that this is simply what human justice is. That is true, and it is a stopping point. Second, and more important, the Church grounds the constraint in something that is not a value to be maximized: *jurisdiction*. Innocent life is not within any human's disposal, because it is held from Another. God alone is Lord of life, and no one may claim the right to destroy an innocent human being directly (CCC 2258); we are stewards, not owners, of the life entrusted to us (CCC 2280; ST II-II q.64 a.5–6). Jurisdiction answers the paradox of deontology. You may not take what is not yours to take, even to keep others from taking more. It also reaches the one leg where this school's own account runs out: ending a life for the sake of the one who lives it, with his consent. If the life is held from Another, the person himself cannot waive it.
*Opponent's rejoinder:* Then you have made murder a trespass on God's property. The wrong to *her*, which you said comes "first," has dropped out.
*Defense:* No. The wrong is done to her first, as v3 says. It is also an offense against the One who made her precious (CCC 1850), and that is the two-party structure plus a second pole, not a replacement for the first. "Steward" is not "property." Her life is hers to live and not hers, or anyone's, to end. That is the difference between a gift held in trust and a thing owned.
*Result:* **Held with repair. Serious as written.** v3's central abductive step against this school asserts that the rival lacks an explanation it has (Foot, 1967, on A6's own cases). It also omits the ground (jurisdiction, CCC 2258, 2280) that actually gives the Church's account its reach. The C God row repeats the overclaim ("a worth that may never be spent"). Repaired, it stands at parity on "for others" and gains reach on the life that is not even its own owner's to end.

**E3. Attack on W1(2): the origin dilemma. Does being wanted add worth, or not?**
*Opponent:* You say "her worth is a gift exactly as far as her being is a gift" and "she was wanted before anyone saw her." Choose. If being wanted *adds* to her worth, then worth is conferred, and A4 said worth is "prior to anyone's recognition." If it adds nothing, then God's wanting is idle as a ground of worth: it explains that she exists, not what she is worth. A cause of her existence is not a ground of her value. Imagine two identical newborns, one made by God and one (impossibly) by chance. If they are equal in worth, the Creator explains nothing about worth.
*Defense:* The dilemma assumes that worth must be either intrinsic or conferred. Aquinas holds both, without contradiction. Each thing is good by a form that is truly its own (the rival is right: her worth is hers), and good by likeness to the divine goodness, which is its exemplar, its source and its end (ST I q.6 a.4). The "chance twin" is not a fair test on this view, because nothing exists except by receiving its act of being. And v3's real ground is not "wanted" in the sense of noticed. It is the *call*: "worth follows the call, not the capacity" (GS 29). Her worth is measured by what she is for, and she is for God. That is a formal and final ground, not a merely efficient one.
*Opponent's rejoinder:* Then "wanted" is warmth, not argument, and the call, an end that is God, is W3's conclusion. Your witnesses are leaning on each other, so they cannot be counted as independent.
*Defense:* Fair. They are not counted that way: the God row says "reach, not count." A converging case may let one clue light another, so long as none is weighed twice.
*Result:* **Held with repair (Minor).** Let the call and the likeness carry the argument, and let "wanted" carry the warmth.

**E4. Attack on W1(1): a third option for the dead.**
*Opponent:* "Either the debt died with her, and what is left is only remorse … or it did not, and she is still someone." That is a false dilemma. A serious secular literature holds that the dead can be wronged without surviving death: the wrong is to the person as she was in life (Feinberg; Pitcher; Aristotle already allows, cautiously, that the dead are touched by what befalls those they loved, NE I.10–11). Your own A3 says it best: "we still owe her something we can no longer pay." That is my view. The debt outlives her, it can never be paid or forgiven, and she is not still someone. It is not "only remorse." It is a real wrong, fixed forever.
*Defense:* The third option is real and should be named. It is the tragic view, and it is honest. Naming it makes the clue sharper, not weaker. Of the three options, only one fits the shape of guilt, which keeps seeking a *person* to answer to. On the tragic view that seeking is fitting and will never be met. On the Catholic view it is shaped for a meeting.
*Opponent's rejoinder:* Tragedy is the truth of the human condition (Nussbaum, *Fragility*). A fit with hope is not evidence.
*Defense:* Agreed. v3 already says it "weighs as" a clue. The standoff is at parity.
*Result:* **Held with repair (Minor).**

**E5. Attack on W2: neither accident nor wisdom, and why the question of "minds" proves nothing.**
*Opponent:* Three points. (a) There is a third option. Our *existence* is unplanned, and our *norms* are not accidental: they are necessary given the rational life-form, and some hold for any rational animal as such (Thompson). (b) McDowell says your closing question, why there are "minds made to read an order that holds for every mind," is a confusion. Answering to reasons is what it *is* to be a mind, so there is no fit to explain, any more than we need to explain why triangles fit the theorems about triangles. And "made to read" assumes a maker. (c) Asking why such minds came to exist at all is answered by observation selection. Only a mind can ask, so of course we find minds. Finally, a small point. You write that the core of the law "would be wrong for any creature that can reason and suffer (A1)." A1 says "wrong for everyone, even if every culture approved." In Round 2 you moved the claim about other species *out* of A1 so that W2 could argue for it. Now W2 asserts it and cites A1.
*Defense:* (a) That is v3's own structure: the fitted part of the law is contingent and the core is not. The question narrows to why instances of a necessary form arose from matter and chance. (b) Granted that what counts as a mind is defined by its norms. What is *not* defined that way is that there are any such minds. "Able to read" is the honest wording. (c) Observation selection explains why *we observe* minds. It does not explain why there are any to observe: the man who survives a firing squad of fifty marksmen still asks why they all missed. And this question is not only a theist's. The atheist Thomas Nagel (*Mind and Cosmos*, 2012) thinks it is real and that materialism cannot answer it. The A1 citation is a fair hit. W2 should give the reason: what makes cruelty wrong is the suffering of one who can be wronged, and that does not depend on the shape of a species.
*Opponent's rejoinder:* Nagel's answer is a natural teleology, not God.
*Defense:* Yes, which is why the case stays at "best explanation" and does not claim "only explanation."
*Result:* **Held with repair (Minor). Standoff at parity** on whether the arrival of rational minds needs any explanation beyond evolution.

**E6. Attack on A5 and the W1 closing: the "ought" is complete without God, so what does God add?**
*Opponent:* A5's heading, "What we are does not settle what we ought to do," denies Foot's central thesis: a form of life *does* set its standards. Your body text means "what we can do and what we want," and with that I agree, so the heading should say so. Deeper: A5 grants that reason sees "good is to be done" as self-evident (q.94 a.2). Catholic natural lawyers (Grisez, Finnis) hold that these first principles bind before anyone asks who made us. Then your W1 closing, "the wrong is necessary given what we are; what we are is His gift … the law binds because He is good," mixes two grounds. A conditional necessity ("given what we are, cruelty is wrong") holds whoever made us. God explains why the condition is met, not why the conditional binds.
*Defense:* Granted that the law binds, and is known to bind, without reference to its Author (Rom 2:14–15; VS 41). The case is about explanation, not about how we come to know the law. On the conditional: Thomism holds that necessary truths have a ground (ST I q.15; q.16 a.7), and that natures are good by likeness to the Good. The rival holds that they need none. That is the standing parity over necessity. The wording should not suggest that the gift of our existence is what makes cruelty wrong. Better: "Given what we are, the wrong is necessary. That we are at all, and that what we are is good, is His gift."
*Result:* **Held with repair (Minor).**

**E7. Attack on the C flourishing row.**
*Opponent:* (a) "It knows debt and repayment, not forgiveness." That is false of the modern school. Nussbaum wrote a book on it, and the secular Aristotle-and-Smith line (Griswold) treats forgiveness as a virtue. What we honestly lack is forgiveness when the victim is gone. Griswold himself grants that it cannot be had then. (b) "It must say whether our nature … is an accident or an order it cannot explain." You leave out our actual answer (E5): neither, because the question needs no external answer. (c) "As though a person owned his life." Foot argued half from charity (is life still a good to *him*?) and half from justice (a right he may waive). "Owned" hits only the second half.
*Defense:* (a) Granted in part. Nussbaum rejects the debt-and-release model the book means, so the line is defensible against her, but not against Griswold. The safer wording is also stronger: it points straight at the dead-victim case. (b) Add the third answer; W2 already credits it. (c) The charity half is answered in W1(2) as repaired (jurisdiction). The row can keep the waiver point.
*Result:* **Held with repair (Minor).**

**E8. Attack on the evil section: the wisdom horn owns the parasite, and "permitting is not doing" is not Aquinas's word for natural evil.**
*Opponent:* W2 says our nature is "a wisdom," not an accident. Then so is the wolf's, and so is the malaria parasite's, whose healthy life kills hundreds of thousands of small children a year. Here no defect harms a good design. The parasite's flourishing *is* the child's loss. Second, your "Second" point says that permitting "what a world of real causes brings about is not doing it." Aquinas does not say God merely permits natural corruption. He says God, by willing the order of the universe, causes corruption *per accidens* (ST I q.49 a.2), and that He keeps every creature in being and works in its every act (I q.104–105). That is double effect applied to God, not doing versus allowing. And double effect requires that the bad effect not be avoidable by a better means, a condition hard to meet for an agent with infinite options. Third, "No one is the price of someone else's good, not even in God's hands." Your Scripture says you were "bought with a price," and that the Son came to give His life as a ransom for many.
*Defense:* All three should be owned. (1) The parasite belongs on the heavy side, next to the wolf, and it should be named alongside W2's "wisdom" so that the reader does not find the link first. (2) The precise Thomist wording is "not intending": God wills the order and does not intend the loss (ST I q.19 a.9; q.49 a.2). The proportion the Church gives is CCC 324: a good from *that very* evil, and for the person, a good for *her*. Aquinas also distinguishes rational creatures, which God governs for their own sake, from other creatures, which are ordered to the whole (SCG III.112). So the fawn may be part of the order of the whole in a way the child is not. (3) The line should say what it means: no one is spent against her will. The only One who became the price did so freely (John 10:18).
*Opponent's rejoinder:* SCG III.112 makes the child's death harder for you, not easier. If persons are governed for their own sake, why let a nature built to kill them flourish?
*Defense:* That is the weight, and v3 already says that not seeing God's reasons "is weak evidence … not no evidence." The theist carries more weight on the parasite. The naturalist has a weaker hold on the tortured child (A1, W2). Each account carries what it carries.
*Result:* **Held (owned weight), with Minor repairs.**

**E9. Attack on W3: the virtues of finitude.**
*Opponent:* "Not more of the same years … herself, body and all, made whole." Then what does she *do*, made whole? Courage needs danger, generosity needs need, and love as we know it needs the risk of loss. Take those away, and the one "made whole" is not the woman we loved, whose self was formed by exactly those things.
*Defense:* The Catholic answer is not that her history is erased. The risen Christ kept His wounds (John 20:27). What a finite love lived through is kept, and healed. Aquinas holds that the virtues remain in glory in what is formal to them, the order of reason and love, though the matter they work on changes (ST I-II q.67 a.1). Charity never ends (1 Cor 13:8).
*Opponent's rejoinder:* "Courage without danger" is just a word.
*Defense:* The glorified life is hoped for, not argued. W3 is a clue and says so. The standoff is at parity.
*Result:* **Held (Minor addition recommended).**

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**Against v3 as written:**

1. **W1(2)'s decisive step overclaims against this school, and the C God row repeats the overclaim.** *Serious.* As written, W1(2) says being human "does not explain why what belongs may never be spent … for others," and offers "willed for itself" in its place. This school's best defenders explain that leg without God. A6's own magistrate case is Foot's, explained in 1967 by doing harm versus failing to help. Thompson adds bipolar justice, and Foot later showed that "the best state of affairs" gives the virtues no reason at all. Meanwhile "willed for itself," as a worth in the victim, does not by itself give a constraint on the *agent* (the paradox of deontology). The ground that actually gives the Church's account its reach is missing from W1(2): innocent life is held from Another and is not within any human's disposal, not even its owner's (CCC 2258, 2280; ST II-II q.64 a.5–6). That ground is exactly where Foot parted from the Church. Repair 6.1 brings the step to parity on "for others" and gives it real reach on the life that is not even its own owner's to end.

That is the only Fatal or Serious objection. **As written: one Serious objection (above); no Fatal objection.**

**Minor (wording and precision; none breaks a step):**

**6.1 W1(2) (replaces the whole paragraph). Answers the Serious objection.**
> (2) *A worth no deed confers and no order wants.* The victim's claim is truly her own. She did not make it, cannot sign it away, and it belongs as fully to the newborn who has done nothing as to the man who has done much. Being human explains why the newborn belongs. The best accounts without God also explain much of why she may not be killed for others: the wrong would be done to *her*, the five who need her organs have no claim on her life, and doing harm is not the same as failing to help (this is Philippa Foot's own answer to the magistrate and the mob). Asked why justice has that shape, without exception and whatever the numbers, they answer that this is simply what justice is. That is true, and it is a stopping point. An order of deeds and their fruits can only return to each what each has done; it cannot say why anyone matters before she has done anything. A law or an order gives a nature to every member of a kind; it does not want any one of them. If her worth lies in what she is, her worth is a gift exactly as far as her being is a gift, and here the moral question and the question of creation meet. Worth follows the call, not the capacity, and all share one origin and one calling (GS 29); an equal call explains an equal worth. Everyone is someone's child; she was wanted before anyone saw her.

## 7. New insights and lines worth keeping

**New insights**
- **The absolutes in A6 are this school's own ground.** Foot built the magistrate case to explain them without God. The book's edge is not that the rival cannot forbid killing one for five. It is *why* the line has no exceptions, and why even the one whose life it is may not cross it. The Church's answer there is jurisdiction, not arithmetic: life is held in trust from the Lord of life (CCC 2258, 2280). Jurisdiction also answers the old puzzle that defeats every value-only account: why not kill one so that five are not killed?
- **"Owed or ripen" separates theism from karma and from bare necessary truths, not from bipolar justice.** Against this school the question is "only to her, or also to Someone?"
- **The dead-victim case is stronger with three options than with two.** Naming the tragic view (a debt no one can ever receive) shows that only the Catholic view fits the shape of guilt.
- **Worth needs a formal and final ground, not only an efficient one.** "She was wanted" is warmth. "She is an image of the Good, and called to Him" is the argument. The origin dilemma is answered by participation (ST I q.6 a.4), not by being wanted.
- **The wisdom horn owns the parasite.** Saying so, beside W2, is better than having the reader find it.
- **For natural evil, Aquinas's word is "not intending," not "not doing"** (ST I q.49 a.2). And persons are governed for their own sake, other creatures for the whole (SCG III.112): the fawn and the child are not in the same place in God's order.
- **The risen wounds answer Nussbaum's deepest form.** Glory does not erase the history of a finite love; it keeps and heals it.
- **This round finds a fault in this school's own Round 2 repair.** The "never spent" sentence was mine. Pressed harder, it claimed more than the rival leaves out. The fairness the book promises its rivals has to apply to its friends' repairs too.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "Given what we are, the wrong is necessary. That we are at all, and that what we are is good, is His gift."
- "Her life is not ours to spend, not even for many, and not hers to throw away."
- "No one may take what is not his to take, even to keep others from taking more."
- "The only One who became the price did so freely."
- "A debt no one can ever receive."
- "The risen Christ kept His wounds."
- "Not for the species, not for the total, not for us."
