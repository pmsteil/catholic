# Argument Lab, Round 3: Buddhist opponent vs v3

## 1. School and voice

The same composite voice as Rounds 1 and 2: the Theravada Pali Canon and its commentaries (Buddhaghosa), and Indian Madhyamaka (Nagarjuna, Candrakirti, Santideva), with Mahayana sources brought in where a Madhyamaka thinker would bring them. This voice holds that there is no lasting self (anatta); that craving (tanha), rooted in ignorance, keeps the round of birth turning; that karma is intention ripening by its own order, with no one appointing it; that boundless kindness, compassion, joy in another's good and equanimity are what a purified heart is like; and that nirvana, the unconditioned (for the Madhyamaka, emptiness), is the highest good. In Round 3 it tests v3 as written, checks whether v3 took in Round 2's findings, and presses on new ground. The Church "rejects nothing that is true and holy" in it (*Nostra Aetate* 2), and this voice is argued in that spirit.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges (Round 3: fresh answers)

**1. Starting point for justice.**
*Their answer:* The reflexive principle. King Pasenadi and Queen Mallika each admit that no one is dearer to them than themselves; the Buddha replies that, searching every direction, one finds no one dearer than oneself, and since every other being holds himself just as dear, whoever loves himself should harm no one (SN 3.8; Ud 5.1). Every being trembles at the rod; putting oneself in his place, one should not strike (Dhp 129–130). And justice between persons is not only the doer's purity. When the monk Sudhamma insulted the householder Citta, the Buddha had the Sangha impose the act of reconciliation: Sudhamma had to go to Citta and ask his pardon (Vinaya, Cullavagga I). Ajatasattu confesses his transgression and the Buddha accepts it (DN 2).
*Verdict:* **Holds, and it is a real starting point, not a borrowed one.** It reaches every being through the doer's own self-regard, and it already contains an "owed" structure: the wronged person must be asked. That is important for this round, because it means Buddhism does not hold that guilt "only ripens" (Exchange 1). Where it bends is the one Round 2 found: the absolute is grounded in the doer's mind (killing always arises with hatred or delusion) more than in a claim the victim holds.

**2. A hard dilemma: a prenatal diagnosis of severe disability, and the parents are urged to end the pregnancy.**
*Their answer:* Theravada says no. The monastic code defines a human being from the first moment of mind in the womb, and a monk who procures an abortion commits an offense of the gravest class (Vinaya, Parajika 3 and its explanation). The intention to end the life arises with aversion, aversion to the burden or to the condition, so it cannot be compassion. What is owed is care: the Buddha washed the monk lying in his own filth and said that whoever would tend him should tend the sick (Vinaya, Mahavagga 8.26). Japanese practice, which permits abortion and then holds memorial rites of apology to the child, is a real divergence inside Buddhism, as practice diverges among Catholics too.
*Verdict:* **Holds, does not borrow, and reaches the Church's verdict** (CCC 2270–2274). Its principles are non-harm, the analysis of intention, and compassion. They resemble mercy and self-gift in the book's sense, but they are derived from the Buddha's own analysis of the mind, not taken from the book.

**3. Ground of equal, unconditional dignity.**
*Their answer:* Sentience and the nature of mind. Every being suffers and holds its life dear; the mind is by nature luminous and its stains are visitors (AN 1.51–52); Mahayana adds buddha-nature in all. As a mother would guard her only child with her life, so one should hold every being in a boundless heart (Sn 1.8).
*Verdict:* **Holds for equal compassion. Bends for equal dignity of persons, and the bend is now located precisely.** The Buddhist value of a *human* birth is its opportunity for awakening, and the Buddha lists, among the eight inopportune births for the holy life, birth in a good land but without understanding, unable to grasp what is well or badly said (AN 8.29). It stands in that list beside birth in hell, as an animal, or as a hungry ghost. Compassion toward such a child is undiminished. But her life is counted, in this tradition, as one in which the highest good is closed to her until another birth. That is a real difference from A4, and it is the true cost the C line was reaching for (Exchange 4). Fairness check: both traditions grade the gravity of acts (killing a human is graver than killing an animal in the Vinaya; parricide is graver in CCC 2268), so grading acts is not the cost. The cost is that access to the highest good follows capacity.

**4. Ultimate good and guiding principles.**
*Their answer:* For Theravada, nirvana, the unborn and unconditioned (Ud 8.3). For Madhyamaka, the realization of emptiness, which in the Mahayana is buddhahood: wisdom and compassion inseparable, a nirvana that does not rest in its own peace but keeps working for beings. The guides: the Eightfold Path, the four boundless states, the perfections. And a ranking the book has not yet noticed: in the Pali Canon, boundless love by itself leads to companionship with Brahma, a god (DN 13); when Sariputta taught a dying brahmin only the way to Brahma's world, the Buddha asked him why he had stopped at the lesser goal when more could be done (MN 97). Love is the road; wisdom is the summit.
*Verdict:* **Holds; coherent and principled.** It gives the book its most exact point of parting (Exchange 8): the Buddhist places something beyond love; the Catholic says love never ends (1 Corinthians 13:8, 13).

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1. W1's test, "owed or only ripening?", is a false fork for this school.**
*Buddhist:* You built the test against us, and it misses us. We never said guilt is owed to karma. It is owed to the one we wronged: the Vinaya sends Sudhamma to Citta to ask his pardon, and the formula of confession is addressed to the one offended. And the deed also ripens in the doer. Both. So your fork, "owed to someone, or does it only ripen," sets "owed" against "ripen" as though we had only the second. Your frame then says "none of them is anyone the guilt could be owed to," which is true of the order and irrelevant: the order was never the creditor.
*Patrick's side:* Granted, and the realist, Aristotelian, Kantian and Confucian have said the same this round. The test discriminates only where no one on earth can receive what is owed: when the victim is dead, or when we ask who gave her the claim. That is exactly where W1(1) and W1(2) do their work. The frame should say so.
*Verdict:* **Held with repair (Minor).** The witnesses carry the argument; the frame claims too much for the test. Repair W1-frame.

**Exchange 2. The dead victim: rebirth takes your second horn without a Keeper.**
*Buddhist:* W1(1) says: "Either the debt died with her ... or it did not, and she is still someone." We take the second horn, and we need no one to keep her. Her stream of mind goes on by its own conditions. We dedicate merit to the dead, and the departed benefit when they rejoice in it (Khp 7). So "she is still someone" is as well explained by rebirth as by resurrection. And our tradition also takes your first horn without bleakness: Angulimala killed many, was never pardoned by his victims, and was freed; what was left of his deeds ripened as stones thrown at him on his alms round (MN 86). Remorse past confession and restraint is a hindrance (kukkucca), to be let go.
*Patrick's side:* Rebirth keeps a stream. Guilt seeks *her*, by name. The reborn one will not remember what was done, cannot forgive it in her own name, and on your own account is "neither the same nor another" (*Milindapanha*). W1(1) does not say only resurrection keeps her; it says that guilt is shaped for a hope in which she is there "to be answered to, to be healed, and to forgive in her own name," and that phrase is exactly where rebirth and resurrection part. As for letting go: the Church says the same against scruples; reconciliation brings peace of conscience (CCC 1468). We differ over *how* the wound is healed, by purification or by pardon, and that is the test, fairly drawn. And notice that merit for the dead works only if the dead receive it, which is your own way of saying she is still someone. Catholics pray for their dead (CCC 958, 1032), which is our way of paying through Someone what we can no longer pay her ourselves.
*Buddhist rejoinder:* "Her, by name" is the self-view at work, kept alive by grief.
*Verdict:* **Held at parity.** This is the deep standoff over whether persons are real at the ground of things, now drawn sharply on the dead victim. W1(1) as written is fair to it, because it presents both horns and weighs itself as a clue. Minor: A3's "something we can no longer pay" should say "pay her ourselves" (both traditions pay the dead something). Optional: the Dhamma line could name rebirth as the Buddhist answer to the dead victim.

**Exchange 3. The heart-reading argument: "gravity does not read intentions."**
*Buddhist:* The argument is back, and it trades on a verb. Karma does not read intentions; intention *is* karma (AN 6.63). Gravity does not read mass either; mass is the term in its law. A law whose variable is mental is not thereby a mind, any more than "resentment breeds resentment" is a judge. Second, Vasubandhu says the variety of the world is born of action (*Abhidharmakosa* 4.1): the fit between hearts and worlds is made by the minds of beings themselves, not by a maker. Third, the Buddha named the workings of karma one of the four things not to be speculated on (AN 4.77). And notice your own line: it says karma "judges hearts," and two sentences later that its debt is "owed to no one." Which is it?
*Patrick's side:* The first point answers "reads," not "fits." A mental cause shaping the doer's own character needs no judge. But karma, as the Buddha taught it, does more: it fits *external* fortunes to the moral quality of past intentions, across lives: the killer short-lived, the generous wealthy, the angry ugly (MN 135). A world whose bodies and fortunes follow the moral quality of hearts is a world with justice built into it. Kant made exactly this argument: happiness in proportion to virtue requires an author of nature who is moral (*Critique of Practical Reason* 5:124–125); and the classical Indian theists made it against the Buddhists. To the second point: if countless minds, each ignorant, together and without knowing it, build one common world fitted to every intention of every being from beginningless time, the coordination is the very thing to be explained; "born of action" restates the fit. On the internal tension: fair. The line should say that the Buddhist denies a judge, and *then* say why the fit looks like one.
*Buddhist (Madhyamaka) rejoinder:* Asking for a coordinator is asking for a ground. The fit is dependently arisen and empty, like everything else.
*Verdict:* **Held with repair (Minor), at parity against Madhyamaka, better than parity against traditional karma.** Change the verbs ("weighs," "judges"), which put the conclusion into the description. Repair C-Dhamma.

**Exchange 4. "It answers suffering, by desert ... But that answer costs A4."**
*Buddhist:* We proposed that wording in Round 2; pressed harder, it fails, and the evil section inherits the failure. Three faults. (a) The Buddha's answer to suffering is not desert. It is the four truths: birth, aging, sickness and death are suffering, as is all that is conditioned; craving keeps the round turning; it can end; here is the way (SN 56.11). Karma explains only part of how suffering falls, and your own line admits it (SN 36.21). (b) "Costs A4" is asserted with no reason, and the reason a reader will supply, that desert lowers worth, is false of us. The sick child is owed the same compassion whatever she did before; the hell-being is still within boundless kindness; the first precept protects every being whatever its karma. (c) On the scale of evil we do *better* than you, and your evil section never says so. Pain in nature falls where survival puts it? Of course: we never said the world was made good. Your evil section answers a dark god and an indifferent source. It never weighs a rival that expects suffering and still holds A1, A3, A6 and A7.
*Patrick's side:* Granted on all three, and it is better to say so. The real costs of the Buddhist answer are different and deeper. First, it bears suffering lightly because it never said that it is good that we exist: "birth is suffering" is in the first sermon. We say "it is good that you exist" (W3), and Genesis says all He made was very good (Gen 1:31; ST I q.5 a.3); so we must answer for the child, and the Buddhist need not. That is the honest trade, and it should be on the scale. Second, the Buddhist highest good is reached by insight, and the Buddha himself counted a birth without understanding among the inopportune births (AN 8.29). For the Catholic, the highest good is a gift God can give to the infant and to the one who will never think (CCC 1250, 1261). That is where the cost to A4 really lies: access to the highest good follows capacity, not call. Third, Draper's pattern weighs on karma too, because karma predicts a moral distribution and nature shows a survival one; the Buddhist escapes it only through SN 36.21, which is to say, by not claiming the world is just. And Christ Himself refused to read the blind man's blindness or the tower of Siloam as desert (John 9:3; Luke 13:4): the Catholic carries the full weight that desert would lift.
*Buddhist rejoinder:* "It is good that you exist," said of a being who will age, sicken and die, is for us a subtle craving for existence on another's behalf.
*Verdict:* **Fails as written; held with repair.** As written, the C line's central cost against the strongest non-theist rival does not follow, and the evil section omits the rival's real strength on the very scale the conclusion asks the reader to weigh. That breaks the C opening's own promise ("each is stated as its best defenders state it"). The repair (the goodness of being; insight versus gift) is truer and stronger. Repairs C-Dhamma and Evil-weight. **Serious** (section 5).

**Exchange 5. "Its highest good is a peace that knows and loves no one."**
*Buddhist (Mahayana voice):* True of Theravada nirvana as most read it. Not true of buddhahood as most Buddhists alive hold it. The Buddha of the Lotus Sutra says he has been the father of the world for ages beyond counting and only seems to pass away, like the physician who feigns death so his poisoned sons will take the medicine (Lotus ch. 16); and the same sutra tells of a father who searches for his lost son for fifty years (ch. 4). Amitabha, whose name means infinite light and infinite life, vows to receive anyone who calls on him. A buddha is omniscient. That is a highest good that knows and loves, without a Creator. Your W3 says love would be "answered in the end by no one." We answer: by the Buddha.
*Patrick's side:* Two answers. First, your own Indian Madhyamaka is closer to our line than you allow: Candrakirti taught that in buddhahood the mind and its factors no longer operate, and Santideva compares a buddha to a wish-fulfilling gem, and to the healer's pillar that goes on curing poison long after he is gone (*Bodhicaryavatara* 9.35–37). Benefit without a knower. Second, and decisive: where buddhas know and love, they love beings already there. They were once bound like us; they did not give anyone being. W3 asks for "a Person who said that word to each of us *first*," and W1(2) says why: "Our love answers to a worth already there; God's love gives it." A buddha's love answers. God's gives. For the Buddhist, love is the flower of the path; for the Catholic, it is also the root of the world.
*Verdict:* **Held with repair (Minor).** The clause is accurate for Theravada and for Indian Madhyamaka, contested for the Mahayana at large. One sentence should name the loving buddhas and say where they differ. Repair C-Dhamma.

**Exchange 6. W1(2): "the newborn who has done nothing."**
*Buddhist:* There is no newborn who has done nothing. The round has no first point that can be found (SN 15.1). So "an order of deeds ... cannot say why anyone matters before she has done anything" asks a question that, for us, has no case: no one is ever before doing. And karma was never our ground for regard; sentience is. You are attacking karma for a job we never gave it.
*Patrick's side:* Two parts granted: the phrase begs the question against rebirth, and the Buddhist ground for regard is sentience. Then put sentience in the argument. That she can suffer explains why harming her matters. It does not explain why she may never be spent for others, since others can suffer more; your own best modern reader, Charles Goodman, reads Santideva as a consequentialist for that reason, and Santideva allows even the forbidden to one who sees it will benefit others (*Bodhicaryavatara* 5.84). Theravada keeps the absolute, but grounds it in the doer's mind: killing always comes with hatred or delusion. That gets A6's verdict, and it is an honest rival. But it explains "I may never kill," not "her claim is her own and may never be spent," and A2 is about her.
*Verdict:* **Held with repair (Minor).** Change "has done nothing" to "has done nothing to earn it," and add one sentence on sentience. Repair W1(2).

**Exchange 7. The contemplatives found persons, and ranked them below the summit.**
*Buddhist:* W3 says our contemplatives "report a peace beyond the self rather than a Person." More exactly: our texts report meeting persons, gods among them, loving ones too, and finding them not final. Brahma Baka thought his realm eternal and was shown he was wrong (MN 49). And the Brahmajala tells how the first being reborn in a Brahma world, seeing others arrive after him, came to think he had made them (DN 1). That is our genealogy of belief in a Creator. Your sentence about your mystics' "darkness on the way to God" quietly makes our summit your foothill.
*Patrick's side:* By the fairness rule, neither of us gets to use a genealogy against the other. DN 1 explains how a belief could arise; it does not show the belief false, any more than an account of how Buddhism arose would. And each tradition can read the other's summit as a stage on its own road: you read our God as Brahma; we read your peace as a darkness on the way. Neither reading is evidence, and the book should say that plainly. v3 already marks its reading as the Church's *hope* (LG 16), not as a report of what Buddhists experienced.
*Verdict:* **Held with repair (Minor).** One sentence makes the symmetry explicit. Repair W3-para-2.

**Exchange 8. Love is not the last word.**
*Buddhist:* W3 calls love "the best thing in us." The Buddha did not. He taught that boundless love leads to companionship with Brahma (DN 13), and rebuked Sariputta for leaving a dying man there when more could be done (MN 97). Wisdom is beyond love. So "answered in the end by no one" is not a loss on our account. It is the summit.
*Patrick's side:* Then you have given the book its most exact sentence. The Buddha himself taught that boundless love leads toward a personal Highest. We agree that love leads to God. We part over whether anything lies beyond Love. Scripture says love never ends, and that of faith, hope and love the greatest is love (1 Corinthians 13:8, 13). W3 as written already says "that is where that account stops," which is the right weight: a standoff, honestly named.
*Verdict:* **Held at parity.** No defect; a gift for the book (section 7).

**Exchange 9. The hope line: "the Buddhist hopes for the end of all suffering."**
*Buddhist:* Accurate, but thin, and the contrast it sets up ("this child, herself") reads as if our hope were for suffering in the abstract. The bodhisattva vows to free every single being. Ksitigarbha vows not to attain buddhahood until the hells are empty, and in Japan, as Jizo, he is the guardian of children who have died. That is a hope for *this* child. And "herself, body and all" is, to us, the craving for existence once more.
*Patrick's side:* Granted on the first; the line should say the Buddhist hopes for every being, this child too. What remains is the true difference: that she, herself, body and all, is raised and made whole, and knows the One who never forgot her. You do not want that hope; we do. That is where the accounts part, and the line says so.
*Verdict:* **Held with repair (Minor).** Repair Evil-hope.

**Exchange 10. Why would one who lacks nothing create? (the C line "A God who is Love")**
*Buddhist (Santideva, *Bodhicaryavatara* 9.119–126; Dharmakirti):* A permanent cause either has the power at all times, and then all its effects would come at once, or it needs other conditions, and then the conditions do the work. And whoever acts for a purpose lacks something; if your God lacks nothing, as your evil section says, he has no reason to create.
*Patrick's side:* Creation is not a change in God (ST I q.45 a.2 ad 2); He wills from eternity that each thing be when it is (ST I q.46 a.1). And not every purpose is a lack. God alone is the perfectly generous giver, acting not for any gain but only to share His goodness (ST I q.44 a.4; CCC 293). Your own bodhisattva is the proof that purposeful action need not be grasping: he works for others without craving. If he can, so can the One who gave him being.
*Verdict:* **Held at parity.** The classical standoff; v3's "one mystery, and a mystery that explains" is the right modesty.

## 4. Ledger for v3 (every numbered item)

- **A heading (truths, not feelings; denials named; "the reader can ask whether he can pay it").** **Holds.** Buddhism keeps every item conventionally; Madhyamaka holds that conventional truth is fully binding for conduct (MMK 24.10, 24.36).
- **A1. Some things are really wrong, for everyone.** **Holds.** The precepts bind gods and humans alike.
- **A2. Guilty, not merely mistaken or flawed; the ashes; guilt faces a person.** **Holds.** The Vinaya's act of reconciliation (Sudhamma and Citta) is canonical support for "only guilt goes looking for her." Buddhist moral shame (hiri) is a wholesome guardian, not "I am less," but A2's line is about guilt and shame in the modern sense and does not misdescribe it.
- **A3. No one on earth can make the gravest wrongs right; the debt outlives her.** **Holds with Minor repair.** "Something we can no longer pay" should read "can no longer pay her ourselves": Buddhists dedicate merit to the dead and Catholics pray for them (Exchange 2).
- **A4. Equal dignity, whatever he can or cannot do.** **Holds.** Buddhism affirms equal compassion that reaches all four test cases. The real difference (the highest good closed to some births, AN 8.29) belongs in C, not here.
- **A5. What we are does not settle what we ought to do; reason sees the goods (q.94 a.2).** **Holds.** Buddhism agrees that craving is a desire we did not give ourselves and must not follow. Its denial that we are *for* anything given is fairly left to W.
- **A6. Real good asks something of the giver; some things must never be done.** **Holds.** Generosity (dana); the simile of the saw (MN 21); the first precept. Santideva's "even the forbidden is permitted" (BCA 5.84) softens the absolute for the bodhisattva, but no Buddhist text permits killing an innocent for others' organs.
- **A7. We are bound to the truth.** **Holds, strongly** (truthfulness a perfection; AN 3.65; Dhp 176).
- **W1 frame (Anscombe as question; the wrong is necessary; the test "owed or only ripening?").** **Holds with Minor repair.** Anscombe is now stated accurately. The test misaims: Buddhism also holds guilt owed to the one wronged (Exchange 1); the test bites only where no one on earth can receive. "The wrong is necessary" is stronger than Madhyamaka will say (empty, but unfailing); "not up to anyone" is safer.
- **W1(1) The debt still owed.** **Holds, at parity.** Fixed from Round 2. Rebirth takes the second horn without a Keeper, but not "in her own name," and the text's own wording marks that difference (Exchange 2). It weighs as a clue, and says so.
- **W1(2) A worth no deed confers and no order wants.** **Holds with Minor repair.** "The newborn who has done nothing" begs the question against a beginningless round; the Buddhist ground for regard is sentience, which the text does not yet address (Exchange 6). The core ("our love answers to a worth already there; God's love gives it") holds and is the decisive answer to the loving buddhas (Exchange 5).
- **W1 closing (not a commander; He chose to make creatures who can be wronged; the Judge lets Himself be asked).** **Holds.** The Buddhist objection that a purposeful creator must lack something is answered at parity (Exchange 10).
- **W2. The law and the mind that reads it.** **Holds, at parity.** Its residual question ("why, in a world of matter and chance, minds made to read an order") presupposes a naturalist world. The Buddhist holds mind beginningless and luminous by nature, so for him the question becomes why mind is so, and that is a stopping point like ours. Not aimed at this school, and its scope note says so.
- **W3, paragraph 1 (communion; craving and what is left; Pieper; the hope is not more years; "knows and loves no one").** **Holds.** Fixed from Round 2. The Buddhist rejoinder (wisdom is beyond love, DN 13, MN 97) is a standoff at parity, and the paragraph's last sentence already weighs it correctly (Exchange 8).
- **W3, paragraph 2 (hiddenness; the contemplatives; through persons; LG 16 as hope; the cost owned).** **Holds with Minor repair.** Make the symmetry of the two traditions' readings explicit (Exchange 7).
- **C opening (every account stops; accommodate vs explain; best defenders; the fairness rule).** **Holds.** Madhyamaka claims to stop nowhere (Nagarjuna: no thesis of his own); groundlessness is itself a stopping point, at parity, as in Rounds 1 and 2.
- **C: Nothing really binds.** **Holds.** Not this school.
- **C: Feeling, refined.** **Holds.** Not this school.
- **C: Reason's own law (Kant).** **Holds.** Not this school. Kant's postulate of a moral author of nature supports the Dhamma line's heart-reading argument (Exchange 3).
- **C: What we owe each other.** **Holds.** Not this school.
- **C: Well-being, counted impartially.** **Holds.** Not this school. (Goodman's consequentialist reading of Santideva is the nearest Buddhist relative.)
- **C: The flourishing of our kind.** **Holds.** Not this school.
- **C: Necessary truths that belong to no one.** **Holds.** The Theravada fixed order of things that stands whether or not Buddhas arise is a near relative; the line's gaps apply to it.
- **C: An impersonal sacred order.** **Holds.** Karma is now rightly out of this line.
- **C: The Buddha's Dhamma.** **Fails as written in one clause; Holds with repair.** Fixed in principle and much improved. But: (i) "It answers suffering, by desert ... that answer costs A4" misplaces the Buddha's answer to suffering and asserts a cost that does not follow (Exchange 4; **Serious**); (ii) "weighs ... judges hearts" builds the conclusion into the description and sits uneasily with "owed to no one" (Exchange 3; Minor); (iii) the quoted line about every being having been your mother is not a quotation (SN 15.14 says such a being is *not easy to find*), and in its sutta it is a ground for weariness with the round, not for compassion; Sn 1.8 is the better text (Minor); (iv) "no one can cancel a deed's fruit" overstates: the salt simile (AN 3.100) and Angulimala (MN 86) show the doer's own awakening can render most of it void; what no one can do is purify *another* (Dhp 165) (Minor); (v) "a peace that knows and loves no one" needs one sentence on the loving buddhas (Exchange 5; Minor); (vi) Madhyamaka's emptiness is not named (Minor).
- **C: Power and rank.** **Holds.** Not this school.
- **C: A God who is Love.** **Holds.** Optional: name the Buddhist parting here too (love as the last word).
- **Evil, the weight (children; animals; the wolf and the fawn; hiddenness; hell; CCC 306, 310, 309, 324, 312, 314).** **Holds with repair.** Honest and heavy. But it does not say that one rival bears this weight more easily, and why (Exchange 4). That omission is part of the Serious objection.
- **Evil, First (privation; no supreme Evil; a will that lacks nothing).** **Holds, at parity.** The Buddhist agrees that grasping arises from lack and that the one without craving does no evil. He denies that suffering is the loss of a prior good (aging and death are the nature of what is conditioned): the standoff over the goodness of being.
- **Evil, Second (not a trade; no one the price; limited skeptical theism).** **Holds** for this school. (The utilitarian's point about "no one is the price" and the Cross is not a Buddhist objection; the hungry tigress Jataka is self-gift, which supports the distinction the utilitarian asks for.)
- **Evil, Third (the God who suffers with us; not a proof).** **Holds.** The bodhisattva who enters hell also suffers with beings; the difference the text draws ("what the God being weighed actually is") is fair.
- **Evil, the hope line.** **Holds with Minor repair.** Accurate; widen the Buddhist hope to every being, this child too (Exchange 9).
- **Conclusion.** **Holds.** Modest and fair. Its closing appeal to the newborn does not touch the Buddhist, who does not give up A4; what it needs against him is in C.

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**As written: ONE SERIOUS. No Fatal.**

**S1. Serious: the Dhamma line's case on suffering misstates the rival and asserts a cost that does not follow, and the evil section omits the rival's real strength on the scale.** "It answers suffering, by desert: the child suffers for what she did before. But that answer costs A4." (a) The Buddha's answer to suffering is the four truths, not desert; karma explains only part of how suffering falls, as the line itself grants (SN 36.21). (b) No reason is given why desert costs A4, and the natural reason (desert lowers worth) is false of Buddhism: compassion and the first precept do not follow a being's karma (Vinaya, Mahavagga 8.26). (c) This rival carries the weight of natural evil more easily than theism, because it never claimed that being is good; the evil section weighs God only against a dark god, an indifferent source and a distant designer. Since the C opening promises each rival "as its best defenders state it," and the conclusion asks the reader to weigh "both sides," this is a fairness defect at the strongest non-theist rival's strongest point. It is fully answerable (the goodness of being, which the Buddhist denies and the Catholic affirms; and access to the highest good by insight versus by gift, AN 8.29 versus CCC 1250, 1261), but not as written. Closed by Repairs C-Dhamma and Evil-weight.

**Minor**
1. W1 frame: the test "owed or only ripening?" misaims; Buddhism also holds guilt owed to the one wronged (Vinaya, Cullavagga I; DN 2). Narrow it to the case where no one on earth can receive (Repair W1-frame). "The wrong is necessary" → "not up to anyone" is safer against Madhyamaka.
2. C-Dhamma, heart-reading verbs ("weighs," "judges") assume the conclusion; say the Buddhist denies a judge, then say why the fit looks like one (Repair C-Dhamma).
3. C-Dhamma, the "every being has been your mother" line is presented as a quotation and is not one; its sutta context is disenchantment, not compassion. Use Sn 1.8, paraphrased.
4. C-Dhamma, "no one can cancel a deed's fruit" → "no one can purify another" (Dhp 165; AN 3.100; MN 86).
5. C-Dhamma, "knows and loves no one": name the loving buddhas, and the difference (they love beings already there; they did not give being).
6. C-Dhamma: name emptiness for the Madhyamaka.
7. W1(2): "the newborn who has done nothing" begs the question against rebirth; add sentience (Repair W1(2)).
8. A3: "can no longer pay" → "can no longer pay her ourselves."
9. W3 paragraph 2: make the two traditions' readings of each other's summit explicitly symmetrical, and say neither is evidence.
10. Evil hope line: the Buddhist hopes for every being, this child too.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Whether persons are real at the ground of things (anatta and emptiness against the person), now drawn on the dead victim: rebirth keeps a stream; guilt seeks her by name (Exchange 2).
- Whether the fit of hearts and fortunes needs a mind, or is dependently arisen and empty (Exchange 3). Better than parity against traditional karma; parity against Madhyamaka.
- Whether it is good that we exist (Pieper and Genesis 1:31 against "birth is suffering"). This is the root of every other parting, and with Repair C-Dhamma the book names it.
- Whether anything lies beyond love: wisdom beyond the boundless states (DN 13; MN 97) against "love never ends" (1 Cor 13:8).
- Whether a cause that lacks nothing can act for a purpose, and whether an unchanging cause can produce a changing world (BCA 9.119–126; ST I q.44 a.4, q.45 a.2, q.46 a.1).
- Each tradition's inclusivist reading of the other's summit (Brahma and DN 1; the mystics' darkness and LG 16), neither of which is evidence.

**After the repairs in section 6: NONE below parity.** The repaired Dhamma line is more generous to the Buddhist (it concedes his advantage on evil) and more exact against him (it names the goodness of being and the gift that does not follow capacity). That makes v3 stronger, not weaker.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**Repair C-Dhamma. Replace the whole "Buddha's Dhamma" line with:**
> - **The Buddha's Dhamma** (karma, and beyond it the unconditioned; for the Madhyamaka, emptiness) is the most searching account of the moral life without a Creator. It explains why no one can purify another (A3), why intention matters most, why real good costs (A6), and why we must follow the truth (A7), and its ground for compassion is among the most beautiful anyone has offered: as a mother would guard her only child with her life, so one should hold every being in a boundless heart (Sn 1.8). It also bears the weight of suffering more easily than we do, because it never said that it is good that we exist. Birth itself, the Buddha taught, is suffering, as is all that is conditioned; craving keeps the round turning; and it can end. Karma explains some of how suffering falls, though the Buddha would not say that all of it comes from past deeds (SN 36.21). No one judges, the Buddhist says; the fruit simply ripens. But a world whose bodies and fortunes follow the moral quality of each heart, across lives, is a world with justice built into it, and justice built into a world looks like the work of a mind. A ripening debt is owed to no one, and it cannot forgive (A2). Its highest good is reached by insight, which some will never have in this life: the Buddha counted a birth without understanding among the unfortunate births (AN 8.29). The Catholic says the highest good is a gift, which God can give to the infant and to the one who will never think (CCC 1250, 1261). And where its buddhas know and love, they love beings already there; they did not give anyone being. For the Buddhist, love is the flower of the path; for the Catholic, it is also the root of the world. Here the two part honestly: both say grasping must die, and they part over what is left, and over whether it is good that we exist at all.

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **The Buddha taught that boundless love leads to the company of a god** (DN 13), and that there is something beyond it (MN 97). This is the most exact and respectful way to state where the two traditions part: not over whether love leads toward a personal Highest, but over whether anything lies beyond Love.
- **The honest trade on evil.** The Buddhist bears suffering lightly because he never said it is good that we exist; the Catholic bears it heavily because he does. Saying so costs the book nothing and earns it the trust of every Buddhist reader. It also shows why the problem of evil is the price of the very thing the book is about: "it is good that you exist."
- **The real cost to A4 is not desert but capacity.** The Buddhist highest good is reached by insight, and the Buddha counted a birth without understanding among the unfortunate births (AN 8.29). The Catholic highest good is a gift, given to the infant and the one who will never think. Grace first is the ground of equal worth: "worth follows the call, not the capacity."
- **Guilt owed to persons is shared ground.** The Vinaya's act of reconciliation (Sudhamma sent to ask Citta's pardon) shows Buddhism knows that guilt faces a person. The test "owed or ripening" separates the traditions only at the grave.
- **The heart-reading argument is Kant's moral argument applied to karma** (happiness in proportion to virtue needs a moral author of nature). Stated as fit, not as "reading," it is the strongest point in the lab against traditional karma, and it can be made without disrespect.
- **A buddha's love answers; God's love gives.** This answers the Mahayana's loving, omniscient buddhas (Lotus ch. 16, Amitabha) with the book's own W1(2) line.
- **Both traditions pay the dead.** Merit dedicated to the departed; Masses and prayers for the dead. Neither tradition thinks the debt simply dies. The Catholic pays through Someone.
- **The bodhisattva answers the Buddhist objection to creation.** Purposeful action without craving is the bodhisattva's own ideal, so "a purpose implies a lack" does not hold even on Buddhist terms.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "For the Buddhist, love is the flower of the path; for the Catholic, it is also the root of the world."
- "He never said it is good that we exist. We say it, and so we must answer for the child."
- "No one judges, the Buddhist says; the fruit simply ripens. But justice built into a world looks like the work of a mind."
- "A buddha's love answers. God's love gives."
- "Rebirth keeps a stream. Guilt seeks her by name."
- "We agree that love leads to God. We part over whether anything lies beyond Love."
