# Round 3: Moral Error Theory + Evolutionary Debunking + Cognitive Science of Religion vs. v3

## 1. School and voice

This is the same school as Rounds 1 and 2: the error theorists (J. L. Mackie, Richard Joyce, Jonas Olson, Bart Streumer, Richard Garner), the debunkers (Sharon Street, Michael Ruse, Joshua Greene, Buchanan and Powell), and the cognitive science of religion (Justin Barrett, Pascal Boyer, Jesse Bering, Ara Norenzayan, Lee Kirkpatrick, Nicholas Epley). Its stance has not changed. Morality *claims* authority that is categorical, aimed at persons, and impossible to cancel. A Lawgiver would be the natural home for such authority. Neither the authority nor the Lawgiver exists, and evolved minds in evolved cultures explain why we believe in both.

This round tests four new things in v3: its epistemic footing (the Moorean heading), its new aim for A7 (the norm of truth), its new argument from conscience correcting itself, and the psychological data it now leans on (guilt that "seeks a person," people who speak to their dead, "the newborn no instinct favors"). The fairness rule is applied in both directions.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

These are new cases and new pressure, not a repeat of Rounds 1 and 2.

**1. Starting point for justice.** No one is owed anything categorically. Justice is a set of conventions that let creatures with limited sympathy live together (Hume, Mackie). A fictionalist (Joyce) keeps the word "owed" because it steadies our commitments. An abolitionist (Garner) drops it and keeps compassion.
→ **Coherent; it borrows nothing.** New test: the kinless dead, whom no one remembers. On this account justice owes them nothing at all. No convention protects them, and the only thing they can receive is a sympathy no one living feels. The view is consistent, but it gives up A3 completely, and here no one is even left to notice the cost.

**2. A hard case (new): a mother with dementia asks every morning where her late husband is.** Telling her the truth makes her grieve fresh each day. "He's at work" calms her. The error theorist settles it by her welfare and by her earlier wishes, if she expressed any: tell the kind fib.
→ **Coherent, and it borrows none of the book's definitions.** The Catholic side has a better answer here than either "always tell" or "fib": redirect her without asserting anything false, because lying is never permitted but not every truth must be spoken (CCC 2482–2489). The error theorist's cost is that he cannot say she is *owed* respect as someone who can hear the truth. He can only say that the fib works. That is a real difference, but it is not a contradiction.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** It denies that equal dignity is true. The belief is valuable, historically late, and spread mainly by Christianity. It is worth keeping as a commitment or a fiction.
→ **An honest denial.** This round presses it from the other direction (Exchange 12b). If the Christian origin of equal dignity discounts it, the same reasoning discounts this school's own highest value: truthfulness at any cost.

**4. Ultimate good.** There is none. In practice the guiding ideal is lucid truthfulness, seeing the world without comforting illusions.
→ **Coherent within a life. It breaks at "ultimately."** It also leans on the one norm this school has the most trouble explaining. This round sharpens the point (Exchange 4). The school can treat *correct belief* as a standard built into what belief is. It cannot treat its own ideal of *inquiry*, keeping looking when looking hurts, the same way. That ideal is an "ought" laid on a person.

**Fairness note:** this school still answers the four challenges more cleanly than any rival that borrows. What it lacks is not consistency. What it pays is cost.

### 3b. Exchanges

**Exchange 1: The Moorean heading leaves out undercutting defeaters.**
*Opponent (Street, Joyce):* The heading's rule is: "what we cannot give up after honest reflection we have reason to trust until something stronger shows it false." I accept some rule like this, since every theory of knowledge has to trust how things seem somewhere. But as stated, the rule admits only one kind of defeater: evidence that A is *false*. My argument is not of that kind. I do not claim to show that torturing a child for fun is permissible. I claim that the process that gave you the conviction would have given it to you whether or not it was true, because selection rewarded cooperation, not moral accuracy. That is an *undercutting* defeater. It removes the reason to trust a belief without showing the belief false. A stopped clock that happens to show the right time gives you no reason to trust it, even though nothing shows its reading is wrong. As written, the rule never lets my kind of defeater in.
*Reply:* Fair on the wording. Any honest rule of trust has to admit undercutters. But v3 does meet yours, in three places. (a) Your undercutter is itself a claim to weigh against A1: "selection shaped this conviction in a way blind to its truth." That is a reconstruction of our deep past, and it is not more certain than "torturing a child for fun is wrong." (b) A4 and the conclusion point to judgments that run *against* what selection favored. An undercutter that explains a judgment by the very instinct the judgment overrides has not explained it. (c) W2 and the C row show that if your undercutter works, it also works on the norms of reasoning your own argument relies on.
*Rejoinder:* Then the heading should say so. Otherwise a careful reader assumes you never heard of undercutters.
**Result: Held with repair. Minor.** The case answers the undercutter as written. The heading should name it in one clause.

**Exchange 2: Moore's hand versus Moore's conscience.**
*Opponent:* Moore's "here is a hand" works because no rival explanation of the experience does better without the hand. Ethics has such a rival, and it is ordinary science: kin altruism, reciprocity, the punishment of cheaters, and the commitment devices Robert Frank describes. Moral Mooreanism borrows a prestige it has not earned.
*Reply:* Your rival explains our *starting points*: favor kin, return favors, punish cheats. It does much worse with the reflective judgments A actually lists, which run against those starting points: the stranger and the infant who can never repay are worth exactly what we are worth, and one person may not be killed to save five. Your science also explains the starting points of *reasoning*. Mercier and Sperber argue that reason evolved to win arguments, not to find truth. You keep trusting reasoning after hearing that genealogy. The fairness rule asks you to apply the same standard to conscience, or else to give up both.
*Rejoinder:* We can check reasoning's reliability by prediction and technology. Conscience has no such check.
*Reply:* That check is itself an inference, and it relies on norms of evidence that prediction cannot verify without arguing in a circle. Both sides stop at some seeming. Which seeming is more certain is the reader's question, and v3's heading leaves it to the reader.
**Result: Held at parity.** This is the honest standoff.

**Exchange 3: "Almost everyone finds, on reflection."**
*Opponent:* The heading makes an empirical claim. Among people who reflect for a living, it is false for A6. Many philosophers are consequentialists, or deontologists who allow a threshold, and they would accept a fatal experiment on one person to save a million. It is also false for part of A4. Singer, Tooley and others conclude after reflection that a newborn lacks an adult's right to life, and Dutch medicine has a protocol for ending the lives of some newborns. A4's own history sentence admits that the ancient world gave A4 up. So the heading's head count is weakest exactly where the conclusion leans hardest. One more point: "Mackie denies A1" understates him. Mackie denies that *any* of A1–A4 or A6 is objectively true.
*Reply:* Granted on the head count. The rule of trust works first-personally: what *you* cannot give up after honest reflection. "This is not a vote" already says so. Soften the count and correct the Mackie clause.
**Result: Held with repair. Minor.**

**Exchange 4: The norm of truth, pressed again. Belief answers to truth by its nature.**
*Opponent (Olson, drawing on Shah and Velleman on the aim of belief):* W2 says that whoever denies that anything binds "cannot fault the man who prefers a comfortable belief to a true one." I can fault him. Belief is, by its nature, the attitude that answers to truth. You cannot believe p while taking yourself to have no reason to think p true, and deciding whether to believe p simply *is* asking whether p is true. So a belief held for comfort is defective *as a belief*, the way a move that ignores the rules is not a chess move. That standard is not mysterious. It gives no one a categorical reason to do anything; it describes what belief is. My critique of religion is that its beliefs fail belief's own standard. That is not taste. So Round 2's claim that A7 beats me "in my own coin" fails.
*Reply:* Partly granted. Your account lets you call a comfortable belief incorrect. It does not let you fault the *man*. A7 has a second clause, and that clause concerns inquiry: "we may not stop looking because we fear what we will find." A standard built into belief says nothing about whether to look. Suppose a man arranges never to see the evidence, or makes himself believe by habit (Pascal's holy water). He breaks no rule internal to belief, and the belief he ends up with may even be true. To fault him you need a norm laid on the person: one ought to seek the truth. If that norm only means "if you care about truth," he can answer that he cares more about peace, and you have nothing left to say. Yet your school's own way of life, lucid truthfulness at cost, is exactly that norm on the person.
*Rejoinder:* I will pay that. I do not say anyone ought to inquire. I say inquiry belongs to a life I admire.
*Reply:* Then your case against religion comes down to two claims: its beliefs are false (which you still have to show), and its believers fall short of a life you admire. That is consistent. It is also a smaller case than the one your school usually makes.
**Result: Held at parity, with an edge on inquiry only. Minor** wording point. "Cannot fault the man" is literally true, but it reads as if the error theorist cannot even call a comfortable belief incorrect. This is an **honest correction of my Round 2 verdict**: A7 gives parity on belief and an edge on inquiry, not "better than parity" overall.

**Exchange 5: Going global.**
*Opponent (Streumer; Mercier and Sperber):* Then I go global. The felt "ought" of inquiry has the same genealogy as the moral "ought": a reputation for honesty, and the habit of checking what others tell us, paid off socially. All categorical normativity is in error, including the epistemic kind.
*Reply:* Then you offer me your debunking argument as a *cause* of belief, not a *reason* for it. "Suspend belief when its source is blind to truth" is one of the norms you have just declared false. Streumer is honest that no one can believe his view. And a view no one can believe cannot be the *best* account, because "best" is itself an epistemic ought. v3's C row makes this point.
*Rejoinder:* "No one can believe it" is a fact about us, not about the view.
*Reply:* Granted. It may be true. It cannot be argued for, and a converging case asks for nothing more than that.
**Result: Held.** The C row's wording should match Exchange 4 (Minor).

**Exchange 6: Conscience correcting itself. Consistency runs in both directions.**
*Opponent:* W2 says conscience corrects itself "against interest and toward those who can never repay," and that the engine is "reason holding itself to consistency: the law of truth again." Consistency is an engine with no steering wheel. It widens the circle (slaves are men too), and it narrows it. Michael Tooley and Peter Singer reached their conclusions about infants by holding themselves to consistency: if what makes killing wrong is the loss of a being with preferences about its own future, a newborn has no such preferences to lose. The same consistency carried Singer's concern to animals, who can never repay. So a correction made against interest, toward those who cannot repay, and driven by consistency, has produced a result that denies A4. The eugenicists also claimed only to be consistent with Darwin. The correction datum does not favor a law made for minds. It shows minds seeking coherence with whatever they started from.
*Reply:* Granted, and the correction improves W2. Consistency corrects *truly* only when it starts from what is true. The Church never claimed otherwise: reason reaches the secondary precepts by working out first principles it already grasps (ST I-II q.94 a.2, a.4). The engine is therefore consistency *plus* the first goods reason sees (A5). That puts the weight back on A, which is where the heading says it lies.
*Rejoinder:* And my story says those first goods were selected.
*Reply:* Then we are back to Exchanges 1 and 2. Parity.
**Result: Held at parity with repair. Minor.** This removes the "slight edge" Round 2 gave this move. What remains: both sides predict that conscience fails where interest pulls, and the theist can say why reason grasps first principles at all.

**Exchange 7: The pattern of failure is older than sin.**
*Opponent:* W2 says conscience fails where self-interest pulls, "which is what the Church expects, because disordered self-love is the root of sin (ST I-II q.77 a.4)." Question 77 concerns sinful *acts*. In-group bias, preference for kin and indifference to strangers are in the equipment before anyone chooses anything, and our primate cousins show them too. You are blaming a feature of the design on a choice.
*Reply:* The Church does not say every bias is a personal sin. She teaches that our nature as we have it is wounded, inclined to evil and pulled by disordered desire (CCC 405). Aquinas held that the lower powers' obedience to reason was a gift above nature (ST I q.95 a.1). Without that gift, the body's pull against reason is simply what a bodily creature has by nature. So the pattern of failure is expected from the start, not added afterward.
**Result: Held with repair. Minor.** Cite CCC 405 beside q.77 a.4, so the claim covers dispositions and not only acts. *(Verify ST I q.95 a.1 and CCC 405.)*

**Exchange 8: "Guilt seeks a person." A datum, or a bias?**
*Opponent (Baumeister, Stillwell and Heatherton on guilt as an emotion that repairs relationships; Bowlby and Parkes on the searching phase of grief; Bering and Bjorklund on intuitions about an afterlife; Boyer on ancestors):* W1(1) says "guilt seeks a person, and keeps seeking after everyone on earth who could answer is gone," and calls this a clue. Every piece of that is predicted without God.
- Guilt is a repair emotion. It aims at a person because its job is to mend a bond. That is why A2's line "only guilt goes looking for her" matches the research so well.
- Systems like this have no off-switch tied to death, because death is not something such a system perceives. The bereaved keep searching for the dead for months (Bowlby's "searching" phase), and no one takes that as evidence the dead are hiding.
- "People everywhere ... speak to their dead and ask their pardon" is among the best-mapped findings in my field. Children, and adults who reject any afterlife, still attribute ongoing thoughts and wishes to the dead (Bering and Bjorklund 2004), because we cannot imagine our way into a mind's absence. Ancestors are the textbook "full-access" watching agents (Boyer).

Under either hypothesis this pattern is close to certain. A clue that is equally expected either way weighs nothing.
*Reply:* On the feeling, granted. v3's C row already concedes that error theory "explains why we feel bound, guilty and watched." The psychology cannot be the clue. But W1(1)'s clue was never meant to be the feeling. It was the debt, which A3 takes as true: "we still owe her." If that is true, the question is what it is true *of*.
*Rejoinder:* Then say so. As written, W1(1) offers the practice of speaking to the dead as support ("People everywhere act as though she is"), and it ends on the shape of the emotion ("guilt is shaped for it"). By your own fairness rule, you may use that practice as evidence only if you answer the genealogy that explains it without anyone hearing. W1(1) does not answer it.
**Result: Failed as written; Held at parity after repair.** This is half of the Serious objection in §5.

**Exchange 9: The either/or is not exhaustive.**
*Opponent (with Joel Feinberg and George Pitcher):* Grant that the debt is real. W1(1) says: "Either the debt died with her, and what is left is only remorse ... or it did not, and she is still someone." There is a third reading. It is the one actually held by most secular philosophers who believe the dead can be wronged: the debt did not die, and it is owed to her *as she was*, the woman who lived and was made a promise. Pitcher argued that a living person can be wronged by what happens after her death. On this view "I still owe her" is true, and nothing requires her to exist now. v3's own A3 says it: "we still owe her something we can no longer pay." That is my view, word for word. Round 2 asked v3 to state this reading and its price. v3 dropped it, and its either/or now treats "the debt outlives her" and "she is still someone" as a single claim. That is the disputed premise, hidden inside the structure of the argument.
*Reply:* The third reading is consistent and has to be stated. Our side's answer is that guilt does more than acknowledge a debt. It asks to be forgiven, and forgiving is something a person does. On the third reading the debt can never be answered, by its very nature: no one can ever receive it, release it or forgive it, and the wrongdoer's guilt is a request no one will ever hear. That price is real, and the reader can test which reading fits the guilt he actually knows.
*Rejoinder:* I will pay that price. Some wounds never heal. That is tragedy, not evidence.
*Reply:* Agreed, and an honest text says so. On that reading the outcome is a cost, and the clue weighs only as much as the reader finds the third reading truer than the second to his own guilt. That is parity, not victory.
**Result: Failed as written; Held at parity after repair. Serious as written** (§5).

**Exchange 10: W1's test does not bite while the victim is alive.**
*Opponent:* The frame's test is: "is guilt owed to someone, or does it only ripen?" It sorts karma and impersonal orders well. But the godless realist, the contractualist, and the Aristotelian with corrective justice all answer "owed to someone: the victim." While she lives, all of them pass the test. Only W1(1)'s dead victim and W1(2)'s question about worth separate them from you. So "The test that does is this" claims more for the test than it delivers.
*Reply:* Granted. The test bites when she is gone, and when we ask what makes her worth what it is.
**Result: Held with repair. Minor.**

**Exchange 11: "May never be spent" as a commitment device.**
*Opponent (Frank; Greene):* W1(2)'s strongest point is that no total can make it right to kill one innocent for others. That absoluteness is exactly what a commitment device looks like: a rule that cannot be bargained over is believed, and being believed pays. The absoluteness is predicted, and it needs no Giver.
*Reply:* Your story explains why we would *feel* the rule as absolute. Whether it *is* absolute is A6 again, and A6 has held against Greene (Round 2, Exchange 10: Berker; Kahane and colleagues). W1(2) also claims only what it can: it connects the moral question to the question of creation, rather than proving creation from morality ("here the moral question and the question of creation meet").
**Result: Held at parity.** One Minor wording point. "She was wanted before anyone saw her" should be marked as the Church's answer, since many children are wanted by no one on earth.

**Exchange 12: "The newborn no instinct favors" (three presses).**
*(a) Opponent:* "No instinct favors" is too strong as an empirical claim. We have many instincts. Parents of severely disabled infants usually bond deeply, because attachment grows with closeness and care and not only with the baby-schema face. Instinct often *does* favor her, for the people who hold her. That makes the conclusion's move "past instinct" weaker than it looks.
*Reply:* The hillside is exactly the case where her parents' instinct did not save her. Keep the phrase tied to that infant, or say "the newborn instinct passed by." **Minor.**

*(b) Opponent (Henrich; Norenzayan):* Grant that the judgment runs against instinct. It does not run against culture. Equal worth for the deformed infant spread with a moralizing religion, and cultural evolution explains how universal norms spread when they help large societies cooperate. You have traded one genealogy for another, and v3's own A4 supplies the history.
*Reply:* The fairness rule requires this genealogy to be applied evenly. Nietzsche, whom C cites, argued that the will to truth at any cost is the last form of the Christian faith (*Genealogy* III.24, paraphrased). If the Christian origin of equal worth discounts A4, the same origin discounts the lucid truthfulness behind your own critique. And a truth can be learned from teachers, as arithmetic is. What matters is whether the reader, looking now, sees it.
*Rejoinder:* I accept the symmetry, and I keep both as commitments.
*Reply:* Then you hold both at the same price. That is the standoff C already names.
**Result: Held.**

*(c) Opponent:* The final comparison asks which you are surer of: "that she is worth exactly what you are worth, or any argument that says she is not." My argument does not say she is not. Street's argument says you cannot *know* that she is. As written, the Moorean comparison answers Mackie but not Street.
*Reply:* Granted. Add "or that you cannot know it." The answer still stands: you are surer of her worth than of a speculative claim that selection left you blind to it.
**Result: Held with repair. Minor.** Also, "What you feel then" sits awkwardly right after "no instinct favors." It could say "What rises in you then."

**Exchange 13: The God who is Love was fitted to the data.**
*Opponent (Feuerbach's projection, in the modern form of Epley and colleagues 2009, who found that believers' estimates of God's views track their own views more closely than their estimates of other people's views do):* The C row says theism "explains A1–A7 together." Of course it does. A God imagined as our moral convictions made perfect will explain our moral convictions. That is fit by construction, not explanation, and a hypothesis tailored to its data predicts nothing new.
*Reply:* Two answers. First, the God weighed here was not built out of A. The God of classical theism is reached by other routes (first cause, pure act, simplicity: ST I q.2–3), and the book identifies the Lawgiver with that God. A is evidence this God can be weighed against, not the mold He was cast from. Second, the Christian God's demands have repeatedly run *against* the moral convictions of the people who received them: love of enemies, the equal worth of slaves, the refusal to expose infants. A projection ought to flatter the people who make it. Epley's finding shows that individual believers project. It is about believers, not about whether God exists, and the same tendency shapes how people picture the moral facts.
**Result: Held at parity.** The point that God's demands ran against the culture is a modest edge.

**Exchange 14: Personal words under threat (C, sacred order), and a law that "looks like" a mind (C, Dhamma).**
*Opponent (Epley, Waytz and Cacioppo 2007 on anthropomorphism; Kelemen on children's readiness to see purpose everywhere; Banerjee and Bloom 2014):* The sacred-order row says the older teachers, "in danger and at the edge of grief, spoke of a Heaven that knows them." Those are exactly the conditions under which the research finds people of every belief reaching for a knowing agent. The Dhamma row says "a law that judges hearts looks like the work of a mind." Seeing minds behind patterns is the best-documented bias in my field, and atheists too report that life events happen for a reason.
*Reply:* Neither row offers these as proof. The first shows where the account's own teachers felt it fall short. The second raises a question. Still, the wording should not lean on "looks like," and the same research applies to Christian prayer under threat, so the point may be used only as a sign of strain, never as evidence.
**Result: Held with repair. Minor.**

**Exchange 15: W3 dropped the sentence about the science.**
*Opponent (Kirkpatrick on attachment and religion):* Round 2 asked W3 to face the science of this longing. v3 never mentions it. Attachment theory predicts a longing for a perfect attachment figure, and research on how people manage the fear of death predicts a wish to survive.
*Reply:* W3 now claims only fit ("a longing does not prove its answer"), and its closing line is presented as a cost, not as evidence. One sentence should still name the science. The mechanisms predict a longing for a protector who sees us and a wish not to die. They predict less well a longing to be known wholly and to be told "it is good that you exist."
**Result: Held with repair. Minor.**

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**As written: ONE SERIOUS. No Fatal.**

**Serious:**
1. **W1(1): its either/or is not exhaustive, and its evidence is the part this school explains best** (Exchanges 8–9). The text says "Either the debt died with her ... or it did not, and she is still someone." That leaves out the reading most secular defenders of wrongs to the dead actually hold, and the one A3 itself states: the debt outlives her and is owed to her as she was, unpayable forever. W1(1) then supports "she is still someone" with the universal practice of speaking to the dead, and it closes on the shape of guilt ("guilt is shaped for it"). The cognitive science of religion and attachment research predict both of these with near certainty whether or not anyone hears, and v3's own C row concedes that the rival explains the feeling. As written, the inference is invalid, and the clue's stated weight ("it weighs as one") has no support. *Answerable at parity:* the repair in §6 states all three readings, concedes the science, and rests the clue on the moral content of guilt (it asks to be forgiven by her) rather than on the psychology. Even repaired, W1(1) holds only at parity against this school. The rest of the case does not depend on it.

**Minor:**
2. The A heading's rule of trust admits only evidence of falsehood, not undercutters (Ex. 1). Its head count, "almost everyone ... on reflection," overstates for A4 and A6 (Ex. 3). "Mackie denies A1" understates him.
3. W1 frame: the "owed or only ripen" test does not separate person-directed rivals while the victim lives (Ex. 10).
4. W1(1): "or never knew" (carried over from Round 2).
5. W1(2): "she was wanted before anyone saw her" should be marked as the Church's answer (Ex. 11).
6. W2: "cannot fault the man" invites the reply that belief by its nature answers to truth (Ex. 4); "the engine of that correction is ... consistency" needs "with goods reason already sees" (Ex. 6); q.77 a.4 should be joined by CCC 405 (Ex. 7).
7. W3: the sentence about the science was dropped (Ex. 15).
8. C, error theory: "cannot say why anyone ought to accept its own case" should become "...ought to care whether it is true" (Ex. 4–5).
9. C, sacred order and Dhamma: "in danger and at the edge of grief" and "looks like the work of a mind" should be used as strain, not evidence (Ex. 14).
10. Evil, point 2: "a good for her, not a price paid with her" goes beyond the cited texts (doctrinal; flagged for the censor).
11. Conclusion: "no instinct favors" overstates; add "or that you cannot know it" (Ex. 12a, 12c).

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**
- **The error theorist's "nothing binds."** It is coherent and borrows nothing. The Moorean reply makes him pay in cost. The C row states that cost fairly.
- **Moorean trust against the evolutionary undercutter.** Which is more certain: A1, or a reconstruction of how selection shaped moral belief? Both sides stop at a seeming (Ex. 2).
- **The norm of truth.** Parity on *belief*, since belief answers to truth by its nature. An edge for our side on *inquiry*, where the error theorist must reduce his own ideal of lucid truthfulness to an admired way of life (Ex. 4). Global error theory may be true but cannot be argued for (Ex. 5). *This revises my Round 2 verdict of "better than parity."*
- **The debt to the dead.** "Owed to her as she was, and never answerable" against "she is still someone." Each side pays a price, and the reader tests the readings against the guilt he knows (Ex. 9).
- **Genealogy of equal worth.** Its Christian origin is matched by the Christian origin of the will to truth. Either both are discounted or neither is (Ex. 12b).
- **Projection.** Theism fits A1–A7 well. Whether that is explanation or construction is at parity, with a modest edge from demands that ran against the culture (Ex. 13).
- **Psychological clues.** Guilt that seeks, love that longs, and personal words under threat are predicted by both accounts. Only their *moral content, taken as true*, can carry weight. v3 mostly observes this; W1(1) does not yet.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**A heading.** Replace "Almost everyone finds, on reflection, that he cannot give them up, and what we cannot give up after honest reflection we have reason to trust until something stronger shows it false." with:
> Most people find, on reflection, that they cannot give them up, and what we cannot give up after honest reflection we have reason to trust, until something more certain shows it false, or shows that we would believe it whether or not it were true. (W2 and the conclusion take up the claim that evolution has shown this.)

Replace "(Mackie denies A1, Nietzsche A2–A4, Singer parts of A4 and A6)" with:
> (Mackie denies that any of them is objectively true; Nietzsche gives up A2–A4; Singer, parts of A4 and A6)

**A2 (optional).** After the ashes sentence, add:
> Or I tell a stranger she will never meet the secret she trusted to me alone: nothing in her life is different, and still she was wronged.

**W1 frame.** After "None of them is anyone the guilt could be owed to.", replace "Two witnesses say guilt is owed." with:
> While the one we wronged is alive, the best rivals answer rightly: it is owed to her. The test bites when she is gone, and when we ask what makes her worth what it is. Two witnesses say guilt is owed to Someone as well.

**W1(1).** Replace everything from "A wronged person needs forgiving" through "the wrongdoer does not sit at the final banquet beside his victim as though nothing had happened (*Spe Salvi* 44)." with:
> A wronged person needs forgiving, and only she can forgive the wrong done to her. When she has died, no one on earth can forgive in her place, and still we owe her (A3). There are three ways to read that. The debt died with her, and what is left is only remorse. Or the debt outlives her but is owed only to her as she was: real, and never to be received, released or forgiven by anyone. Or it outlives her because she is still someone. The second is consistent, and serious thinkers hold it; its price is that the guilt we carry toward her asks for something no one will ever give. The third is what guilt itself asks for: it does not only grieve a fact; it asks to be forgiven, and only someone can forgive. People everywhere speak to their dead and ask their pardon. Science can show why minds like ours would do that whether or not anyone hears, so that alone settles nothing; it cannot show that no one does. Many of the dead have no one left to speak to them. The Catholic answer is not that God forgives in her place. He does not. Every wrong against a person is also a wrong against the One who made her precious, and that part He can forgive (CCC 1850). What was done to her, He keeps for her: He forgets no one (Isaiah 49:15), and He can raise her, so that she is there to be answered to, to be healed, and to forgive in her own name. Grace "does not make wrong into right," and the wrongdoer does not sit at the final banquet beside his victim as though nothing had happened (*Spe Salvi* 44).

Then replace "Reason cannot prove that hope. It can see that guilt is shaped for it: guilt seeks a person, and keeps seeking after everyone on earth who could answer is gone. Like the longing in W3, this is a clue, and it weighs as one." with:
> Reason cannot prove that hope. It can see that guilt asks for what only that hope could give: to be forgiven by her. Like the longing in W3, this is a clue, and it weighs as much as the reader finds the third reading truer than the second to the guilt he actually knows.

Keep "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."

**W1(2), last sentence.** Replace "Everyone is someone's child; she was wanted before anyone saw her." with:
> Not every child is wanted by anyone on earth; the Church says each one was wanted before anyone saw her (Jeremiah 1:5).

**W2, three edits.**
(i) Replace "whoever denies that anything binds must deny that too, and then he cannot fault the man who prefers a comfortable belief to a true one." with:
> whoever denies that anything binds must deny that too. He can still call a comforting belief false, since belief by its nature answers to truth; what he cannot say is that anyone ought to look, or keep looking, when not looking would serve him better (A7).

(ii) Replace "which is what the Church expects, because disordered self-love is the root of sin (ST I-II q.77 a.4)." with:
> which is what the Church expects: our nature as we have it is wounded and pulled by disordered desire (CCC 405), and disordered self-love is the root of sin (ST I-II q.77 a.4).

(iii) Replace "Yet conscience also corrects itself, against interest and toward those who can never repay, and the engine of that correction is reason holding itself to consistency: the law of truth again." with:
> Yet conscience also corrects itself, against interest and toward those who can never repay. What drives that correction is reason holding itself to consistency with goods it already sees (A5): the law of truth again. Consistency alone can run either way; it has been used to shut the newborn out as well as to let the slave in. It corrects truly only when it starts from what is true.

**W3.** After "...by someone who will not die.", insert:
> Science can trace the paths this longing travels in us, as it traces the paths of sight: attachment, the wish not to die, a mind that pictures being seen. That tells us how we long, not whether there is Someone to long for; and those paths explain a longing for a protector more easily than a longing to be known wholly and told that it is good that we exist.

**C, Nothing really binds.** Replace "so it cannot say why anyone *ought* to accept its own case." with:
> so it can say its own case is true, but not why anyone ought to care whether it is.

**C, Impersonal sacred order.** After "(*Analects* 14.35)", add:
> Minds under threat reach for a knowing Heaven, ours as well; this shows where the account strains for its own teachers, not that it fails.

**C, The Buddha's Dhamma.** Replace "gravity does not read intentions, and a law that judges hearts looks like the work of a mind." with:
> gravity does not read intentions, and a law that weighs hearts raises the question whether a mind is at work; the Buddhist answers that the intention ripens in the one who intends.

**Evil, point 2 (for the doctrinal censor).** Replace "the good the Church hopes for is a good for her, not a price paid with her for someone else. No one is the price of someone else's good, not even in God's hands." with:
> the good the Church hopes for includes a good for her; she is never only a price paid for someone else. No one is merely the price of someone else's good, not even in God's hands.

**Conclusion.** Replace from "look at the newborn no instinct favors" to the end with:

What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it.

## 7. New insights and lines worth keeping

**Insights:**
- **A7's edge lies in inquiry, not in belief.** The error theorist can call a comfortable belief incorrect, because belief by its nature answers to truth. He cannot say anyone *ought* to keep looking. Aim A7 at the second clause ("we may not stop looking because we fear what we will find"). That clause is where his own ideal of lucid truthfulness becomes a debt he cannot explain.
- **Consistency has no steering wheel.** It let the slave in and shut the newborn out. Moral progress shows truth winning only when consistency starts from goods reason already sees, which ties W2 back to A5 and to the Moorean heading. One argument, not three.
- **The debt to the dead has three readings, not two.** State the middle one ("owed to her as she was, never answerable") and its price, and let the reader test which fits his guilt. Its price is real, and an honest statement of it persuades more than a hidden premise does.
- **One rule for every psychological clue.** The science predicts the *feeling* in every case: guilt that seeks, love that longs, speaking to the dead, personal words under threat. The case should never rest on the shape of a feeling, only on its moral content taken as true (A). v3 follows this rule almost everywhere; W1(1) is the exception.
- **The fairness rule now cuts our way on A4.** If the Christian origin of equal worth discounts it, the Christian origin of the will to truth (Nietzsche's own point) discounts the debunker equally.
- **Projection predicts flattery.** The Christian God's demands repeatedly ran against what his hearers wanted. That is modest but real evidence against "fitted by construction."

**Lines worth keeping:**
- "Science can show why we speak to our dead whether or not anyone hears. It cannot show that no one does."
- "Guilt does not only grieve a fact; it asks to be forgiven, and only someone can forgive."
- "Consistency alone can run either way. It corrects truly only when it starts from what is true."
- "He can still call a comforting belief false; what he cannot say is that anyone ought to keep looking."
- "The question is which reading fits the guilt you actually know."
- "Not every child is wanted by anyone on earth; each one was wanted before anyone saw her."
