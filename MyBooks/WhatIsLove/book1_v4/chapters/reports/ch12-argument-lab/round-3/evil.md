## 1. School and voice

This is the staunch "a good God would never allow this" atheist at full strength. J. L. Mackie brings the logical problem and the question of why God did not simply begin with heaven. William Rowe brings the fawn and the inference from evil we can see no reason for to evil that has none. Paul Draper brings indifference: pain falls where survival would put it. J. L. Schellenberg brings hiddenness, built on what a loving parent would do. Stephen Maitzen brings demographics and, new this round, his argument that ordinary morality and theodicy pull against each other. Stephen Law brings the evil god, Marilyn McCord Adams the problem of hell as an undefeated horror, and Ivan Karamazov the refusal.

His charge in Round 3: v3 took nearly every Round 2 repair, and the evil section is now honest. So he stops attacking the honesty and attacks the joints. Does "a good for her" survive a dilemma? Does it reach the fawn? Is "permitting" the right word for what the Creator does in nature? Can the book call God a mother (Isaiah 49:15) and then defend Him with a line that would not clear a mother? Does "no one is the price" survive hell? And does the hiddenness answer give a reason, or only describe the way God works?

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.**
*His answer:* The school is split. Rowe and the evidential camp are secular realists: agony is bad in itself, and no one may be made to suffer for nothing. Mackie denies objective value and treats justice as a human invention that checks our limited sympathies. Maitzen holds an ordinary, intuition-based morality: prevent the suffering of children when you can.
*Verdict: Holds as a position. It stops at a brute fact (Rowe) or at invention (Mackie).* The school runs its argument as an internal test of theism, so it needs none of its own premises. But Mackie's form can only charge theism with inconsistency, not cruelty. Rowe's form keeps its moral force by borrowing A1.

**2. A hard case.** A newborn whose pain cannot be relieved.
*His answer:* Many in the school would permit ending her life: whoever can end pointless suffering should, even by ending the sufferer. Others stop at the fullest palliation.
*Verdict: Coherent, borrows nothing, and it shows something.* His case against God and his ethics share a premise the book denies (A4, A6). So the God of A4 does not "solve" suffering by ending sufferers. He bears with them and brings them through. That is an observation, not a refutation.

**3. Ground of equal, unconditional dignity.**
*His answer:* Invented (Mackie), brute (Rowe), or equal consideration of interests that come in degrees (the sentientist majority).
*Verdict: Breaks or stops at a brute fact.* The irony stands from Rounds 1 and 2. The problem of evil weighs most when the suffering child has unconditional worth, and that is our premise. The conclusion's infant on the hillside is at once the book's clearest witness and its hardest question (Exchange 6).

**4. Ultimate human flourishing.**
*His answer:* Love, knowledge and the relief of suffering, inside mortality. Schellenberg remains open to some ultimate reality in deep time, but not to a personal, loving God.
*Verdict: Coherent and bounded.* On his view the victims of history are never answered. He calls that the tragic truth, and v3 rightly declines to count it as evidence (A7).

### Exchange 1. Maitzen's dilemma against "a good for her"

*Attack:* v3 says faith is certain that God permits no evil unless He brings a good from that very evil (CCC 324), and that for a person who suffers, that good is a good *for her*. Now take a dilemma. Either the good *requires* the evil, or it does not. If it requires it, then whoever stops the abuse of a child robs that child of a good that God would have drawn from it, and ordinary morality (stop it, now) conflicts with theism. If the good does not require the evil, God could have given her the good without the evil, so the evil was gratuitous, and Rowe wins.

*Reply:* The dilemma treats *why He permits* and *what He brings from it* as one answer. The Church gives two, and v3 already contains both, though it does not keep them apart. First, why He permits: a world of real causes, in which creatures have "the dignity of acting on their own, of being causes" for each other (CCC 306, 310). We are among those causes. The hands that pull a child from the fire are part of what He made real. His permission is a policy toward a world of real agents, and it never excuses ours: evil never becomes good (CCC 312), and our will must match His in what He wants us to want (the good of each person), not in each thing He permits (ST I-II q.19 a.10). So the first horn fails: stopping the abuse robs her of nothing. God can bring good from whatever happens, including from her rescue. Second, what He brings: the good from that very evil (CCC 324) does not justify the evil. It answers it, and defeats it in her life. So the second horn says only that this good did not require this evil, which is true. What required the permission was the good of a world of real causes, not the particular good drawn afterward.

*Rejoinder (Rowe):* Then the permission rests on a policy, and a policy can make exceptions. God could stop this one beating, unseen, and the world of real causes would be just as real.

*Second reply:* That is the honest place where the debate stands. The tradition answers that there is no line short of constant hidden rescue: if the thousandth case is stopped, why not the thousand-and-first? A world of real causes where God stops every cause whenever it would hurt would not have real causes at all. Rowe answers that a good God would accept some arbitrariness rather than this much horror. Neither side can prove its case here. v3 already says so: "It is not no evidence."

**Held with repair (Minor).** v3 has both answers in its text (306/310 in paragraph 1, 324 and "for her" in paragraph 2), but as written a reader can take "a good for her" as the reason she was made to suffer. Keep the two answers apart in one sentence (Section 6, R1). The policy question is an honest standoff.

### Exchange 2. The fawn falls outside "for her" and "eternal life"

*Attack:* v3 names the fawn in the forest fire as the paradigm case that free will cannot answer. Then its second reason is built on two things that do not reach her: a good *for her*, and a good *finished only in eternal life*. The Church does not teach that the fawn has eternal life. So for the paradigm case, "we would not expect to see how each particular evil is answered" has no stated ground. And the Church's own ethics makes it worse. God "surrounds them with his providential care" (CCC 2416), and "It is contrary to human dignity to cause animals to suffer or die needlessly" (CCC 2418). A fawn burning for days, unseen, is as needless as suffering gets.

*Reply:* Three points. (a) For animals, A6 is not at stake. The Church permits using animals within reasonable limits (CCC 2417), so ordering one creature's loss to the good of a whole (the wolf lives by the fawn; ST I q.19 a.9) is not the trade A6 forbids. v3's "No one is the price" concerns persons, and it should say so. (b) The Church's hope does reach creation: creation "waits with eager longing" to be set free (Romans 8:19–22), and the visible universe is destined to be transformed (CCC 1046–1047). CCC 324 is not limited to persons: no evil is permitted without a good from that very evil. So the eschatological point covers the fawn, though more thinly. (c) CCC 2418 governs *us*, as stewards who need not cause animal pain. It does not settle what is needless in a whole creation on its way to perfection (CCC 310). But that is exactly what Rowe asks, and I cannot show the fawn's agony is not needless.

*Rejoinder:* So for the fawn your answer is "a world of real causes, a hope for creation in general, and the rest of the case."

*Second reply:* Yes. That is what v3 already says it is: "the pattern of pain in nature still weighs." The book does not claim to answer the fawn. It claims that the fawn does not overturn the case.

**Held with repair (Minor).** As written v3 owns the fawn as weight, so there is no overclaim in its verdict. But the stated reason for not expecting to see God's answer ("eternal life") does not reach the case v3 names first. Add creation's hope, and say plainly that it is thinner for the fawn than for the child (R2). The fawn remains an honest standoff.

### Exchange 3. "Permitting" is not what the Creator does in nature

*Attack:* v3 says permitting "what a world of real causes brings about" is not doing it (Chapter 11). Chapter 11's distinction is for agents among other agents, like a bystander or a surgeon. Your own Catechism says God "is the first cause who operates in and through secondary causes" (CCC 308). Your own Aquinas says more. By causing the good of the order of the universe, God "consequently and as it were by accident, causes the corruptions of things" (ST I q.49 a.2). In nature, the Creator is not a bystander. On your own sources, He is the cause of the fire's burning, per accidens.

*Reply:* Granted, and the wording should change. But the conclusion stands. CCC 324 itself says God "permits physical and even moral evil," so the word is the Church's. In nature, though, its sense is *not intending*, not *not doing*. Aquinas says the same in the same article: God does not will death for its own sake. This is the structure of double effect, which A6's ethics already uses. The harm is neither the end nor the means. It is what follows in a world whose real causes can fail. So God is still not the surgeon, who intends a death as his means.

*Rejoinder:* Double effect has a fourth condition, proportion. Is the good of a world of real causes proportionate to a hundred million years of agony? That is the problem of evil restated.

*Second reply:* It is, and v3 does not claim to have shown the proportion. It says the weight is heavy and is outweighed only by the whole case, in this book's judgment.

**Held with repair (Minor).** "Permitting ... is not doing it" is accurate for free creatures' acts. For natural evil it is loose, and an informed reader will cite q.49 a.2 against it. Say "does not intend, either as an end or as a means" (R1). The question of proportion is the owned weight, not a new defect.

### Exchange 4. The mother who permits: "He keeps her" and the victims before death

*Attack:* v3 calls God a mother who does not forget her child (Isaiah 49:15). It says the dead victim is "kept": "What was done to her, He keeps for her." It says "a child is loved long before he can say who loves him." Then it defends God's permission with "permitting is not doing." That line would never clear a mother. A mother who stands by while her child is tortured, when she could stop it, did not torture the child, and is still guilty. Schellenberg's whole argument is that a loving parent makes herself present to her child. And "He keeps her" is a word the Psalms use for protection ("The Lord will keep you from all evil," Psalm 121:7). The child tortured to death was not kept *from* it. W1(1)'s most beautiful line describes a keeping that arrives only after the keeping she needed. And Ivan: "so that she is there ... to forgive in her own name." Is her forgiveness the point of raising her? I will not have the harmony require the tortured child to embrace her torturer.

*Reply:* (a) The parent analogy is the right test, and doing versus permitting is not where the difference lies. The difference is that no human mother can raise her child, heal her, and bring a good *for her* from that very evil (CCC 324). God can. That is why permitting can be innocent for God when it would not be for us, and it is why Exchange 1's two answers matter. A mother who allows surgery is not guilty. A mother who allows torture is guilty because she can do nothing for her child afterward. (b) On "kept": W1(1) does not claim she was kept from the harm. It claims she is kept *for* justice: she is there to be answered to and healed. Whether she should have been kept *from* it is the question the evil section weighs, and the book weighs it openly. W1(1) should point there in a single clause, or it reads as a consolation laid over the accusation. (c) On Ivan: the order in v3 is right. She is there first to be answered to (justice; *Spe Salvi* 44: grace does not make wrong into right), then to be healed, then to forgive. Forgiveness is a gift, and a gift forced is no gift (Chapter 4). She is raised so that she *can* forgive, not so that she must.

*Rejoinder:* So "He keeps her" survives the victims who suffer before death only as a hope about afterward, and the accusation about before is moved to the other side of the scale.

*Final reply:* Yes. That is where it belongs. W1(1) is a witness about guilt and to whom it is owed, and the evil section is the weighing of why He allowed it. Mixing them would weaken both.

**Held with repair (Minor).** "He keeps her" survives, but only as justice and hope after the harm. It must not read as a claim that she was kept from it. Add one clause in W1(1), and write "free to forgive" (R3). The parent analogy is answered at parity by the ability to defeat the evil in her life, not by doing versus permitting.

### Exchange 5. Hell and "no one is the price, not even in God's hands"

*Attack:* v3 names hell (CCC 1033). It also says that for a person who suffers, the good is a good for her, and that no one is the price of someone else's good, "not even in God's hands." Take a girl abused for years who becomes cruel and dies refusing God. Her suffering helped shape the will that refused. Whatever good God drew from that very evil (CCC 324) did not go to her. So she was the price. Adams went further, since she was a Christian: hell is a horror never defeated within the life of the one who suffers it, so a God who allows it is not good *to her*. And your Aquinas holds that God shows His justice in the reprobate (ST I q.23 a.5 ad 3), which sounds very much like the order of the whole being completed with them.

*Reply:* (a) "Price" concerns God's intention, not the result. The good is *offered* to her, and the Church's hope is for her. If she refuses it, she is not spent for others. She has refused what was given (Chapter 4). (b) The objection assumes her suffering counts against her. The Church teaches the opposite: responsibility for an act "can be diminished or even nullified" by duress, fear, habit and psychological or social factors (CCC 1735, 1860). The God who judges hearts holds no one to account for what suffering did to her will beyond what she knowingly chose. (c) CCC 1037 binds: God predestines no one to hell. Aquinas's account of reprobation is one theological school's account of fittingness, and he himself says the fault comes from the creature's own will (q.23 a.3 ad 2). The Church names no one as lost and prays for all.

*Rejoinder (Adams):* Self-exclusion that is permanent is still a horror God permits forever.

*Second reply:* Yes, and v3 puts it on the scale as a weight of the faith's own. The disagreement between Adams's universalism and the Church's teaching that freedom can be final is a real one. It is not a contradiction inside v3.

**Held at parity; Minor wording.** Write "a good offered to her," and add the clause on diminished responsibility to the hell sentence (R1, R6). Whether final self-exclusion can be consistent with a love that keeps every victim is an honest standoff.

### Exchange 6. Hiddenness: the way God works is not a reason for it, and the infant on the hillside

*Attack:* (a) v3 says God makes Himself known "mostly through persons," entrusts the work to us, and calls the lateness "a summons to mission." That describes the way He works. It does not say why a perfectly loving God would choose a way that leaves non-resisting seekers without a conscious relationship for a lifetime. Then v3 calls this "a real answer." It answers "why do they not find Him?" It does not answer "why would Love arrange it so?" (b) Through persons, God's self-disclosure is hostage to sinners. For the child abused by a minister of the Church, the person through whom God was to be known became the reason He cannot be believed. (c) Take the infant your own conclusion invokes, left on the hillside, whom no instinct favors. W3's answer is a mother's love before a creed and a neighbor's mercy before a name. She has neither. "A child is loved long before he can say who loves him." She was not loved by anyone on earth. For her the hiddenness answer gives nothing at all.

*Reply:* (a) Granted in part. The reason the tradition gives is the same one v3 already gives for natural evil. God gives His creatures the dignity of being causes for one another (CCC 306), and that includes being the way He is known. Love is passed on through faces, which is how He came Himself. That is a reason at the level of a setting, as CCC 306 and 310 are for natural evil. It is as strong as the free-will answer and no stronger. Schellenberg answers that a loving God would add direct presence wherever human mediation fails without fault. The Church answers that He does, in conscience and grace (LG 16), but not as conscious recognition until the end. That is an honest standoff, and v3 should call its answer "real but not full." (b) The Church names scandal by those who teach as an especially grave evil (CCC 2285), and she holds no one guilty for what he could not know (CCC 1860; LG 16). The damage is real, and it falls on the weight side with every other evil done by free creatures. (c) Granted, and this is the sharpest point. For the infant whom no one holds, the knowing comes only at the end, and the Church can only entrust such infants to God's mercy (CCC 1261). v3 already says "or only at the end." It should say so for her by name, because the conclusion invokes her.

*Rejoinder (Maitzen):* The spread of belief follows birthplace.

*Second reply:* "Through persons" *predicts* that it would. Belief spreads where persons carry it. Naturalism predicts the same thing. So the demographic fact is now neutral between the two accounts, not evidence against this one. What remains is (a), the choice of this way, which is the owned cost.

**Held with repair (Minor).** As written, "That is a real answer" slightly overclaims, and the answer gives the way God works without its ground. Link it to CCC 306, call it real but not full, and name the infant (R4). Lifelong non-resistant non-belief remains owned weight.

### Exchange 7. Mill: the law and the mind fit, but the law and the world do not

*Attack:* W2 says a law and a mind made for each other are best explained by one Source who made both. But the same Source made nature, and nature does daily what the law condemns: it kills the innocent with the guilty, without regard to desert (Mill's essay "Nature"). If one Source made the law and the world, the Source's world breaks the Source's law. That is less unity, not more.

*Reply:* The moral law binds persons, and nature is not a person. An earthquake does not murder. A world on its way to perfection, with constructive and destructive forces (CCC 310), does not break the law. It is the setting in which persons keep or break it. And Christ refused to read disaster as desert: the eighteen killed when the tower of Siloam fell were not worse sinners (Luke 13:4), and the man born blind was not blind for his own or his parents' sin (John 9:3). So the Catholic carries the full weight that the doctrine of karma avoids by desert. That is a cost, but not an inconsistency.

*Rejoinder:* Then the fit between law and world is simply missing, and it weighs against you.

*Final reply:* It weighs. It sits in the evil section, where v3 puts it.

**Held.** W2's claim is about law and mind, and v3 does not overstate it. Mill's point is the evil weight, and it is owned.

### Exchange 8. Mackie: why not begin with heaven?

*Attack:* v3 says free will answers much of the evil we do to one another. But the saints in heaven are free and cannot sin. So freedom does not require the possibility of evil, and God could have created us there. Your free-will answer fails at its root. v3 still does not answer this, though Round 2 flagged it.

*Reply:* The blessed cannot sin because they freely turned to God and now see Him. Their sinlessness is the fruit of a choice, not a starting condition. Love has to be given freely before it can be confirmed forever (Chapter 1; Chapter 4). Aquinas holds that even the angels were not created in final beatitude but reached it by their own act (ST I q.62 a.1, to verify).

*Rejoinder:* Coherent, but it belongs in the text, since lay readers ask it first.

**Held with repair (Minor).** One cross-reference clause (R5).

### Exchange 9. Your own method licenses Rowe's premise

*Attack:* The A heading says that what we cannot give up after honest reflection we have reason to trust. Rowe's key premise, that a wholly good and all-powerful being would prevent intense suffering unless it had a reason, is something almost no one can give up on reflection either. So your method hands me my premise.

*Reply:* It does, and v3 grants it in so many words: "We know enough of goodness to know what a good God would value, which is why this weight is real." The book does not use skepticism about goodness to defend God. It uses only skepticism about which particular answer is given, and when. That is the right line.

**Held** (credit to v3). The method and the argument from evil share a root, and v3 is consistent about it.

### Exchange 10. Law: malice as an end, not a grasping

*Attack:* "A will that lacks nothing has nothing to grasp at." The evil god does not grasp. It delights in suffering as its good, just as your God delights in goodness. You assume that the good comes first. The mirror assumes that evil does.

*Reply:* The mirror fails at a point that is not assumed. A world of only good is possible, but a world of only evil is not, because malice needs a victim with goods to destroy, and even cruel delight is a pleasure, which is a good misused. Evil depends on the good, and the good does not depend on evil. That asymmetry is in A itself: A2's guilt and A4's worth are a wronged good, not a thing in their own right. And v3 claims only that the dark god is answered this way. The indifferent source is answered by the whole case.

**Held.**

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**As written: NONE.**

No Fatal or Serious objection from this school stands against v3 as written. Every Round 2 Serious finding is fixed, and the evil section now claims only what it can carry: a setting rather than an explanation, a weight called heavy, a judgment called the book's own, and the pattern of pain conceded as evidence. Each new objection this round found a joint that needs a clearer sentence. In each case the answer at parity was already in v3's own text (CCC 306/310 with 324; "the pattern of pain in nature still weighs"; "or only at the end"; CCC 1037), so none of them overturns a claim the version makes.

**Minor (wording and scope; none changes a conclusion):**

1. **Second reason: two answers run together.** As written, "a good for her" can be read as the reason she was made to suffer, which exposes it to Maitzen's dilemma (paralysis or gratuity). Keep the reason for permitting (a world of real causes, including the rescuers) separate from the good brought from the evil (Exchange 1).
2. **Second reason: "permitting is not doing" in nature.** On CCC 308 and ST I q.49 a.2, the Creator is a cause of natural corruption per accidens. The right word is "does not intend" (double effect). The question of proportion is the owned weight (Exchange 3).
3. **Second reason: "No one is the price" needs its scope (persons) and its ground (SCG III.112),** and should read "a good offered to her" so that it holds against the case of a victim who is finally lost (Exchanges 2, 5).
4. **The skeptical-theism sentence grounds itself in eternal life, which does not reach the fawn.** Add creation's hope, and own that it is thinner (Exchange 2).
5. **W1(1): "He keeps her" can read as consolation laid over the accusation.** Add one clause saying she was not kept *from* it, and say "free to forgive" (Exchange 4).
6. **W3 hiddenness: "a real answer" gives the way God works without its ground,** and leaves out the infant no one holds, whom the conclusion invokes (Exchange 6).
7. **W1(2): "she was wanted before anyone saw her"** states the hypothesis as fact, over the unwanted child.
8. **"Heaven first" (Mackie) is still unanswered in the text.** It needs one cross-reference to Chapter 1 (Exchange 8).
9. **Hell: add the clause on diminished responsibility** (CCC 1735, 1860), so that the reader does not suppose suffering that deforms a will counts against the sufferer (Exchange 5).

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**
- **Draper's pattern.** Pain falls where survival puts it. CCC 306 and 310 make the pattern compatible with God, not expected. Owned weight.
- **The policy question (Rowe against van Inwagen).** Could God stop *this one* evil without unmaking a world of real causes? Theism says there is no line short of constant hidden rescue; Rowe says a good God would accept some arbitrariness. Neither can prove its case.
- **Proportion.** Whether the good of a world of real causes and free love is proportionate to deep-time agony. The book offers a judgment, not a demonstration, and says so.
- **The fawn.** The Church's hope for creation (Romans 8:21) is real but does not name this fawn.
- **Lifelong non-resistant non-belief (Schellenberg).** "Through persons" grounded in CCC 306 is as strong as the free-will answer and no stronger. Schellenberg holds that Love would supplement wherever mediation fails without fault; the Church says it does, in conscience and grace, without conscious recognition until the end.
- **Hell (Adams).** Whether final self-exclusion can be consistent with a love that keeps every victim, or whether only universal salvation defeats every horror. Real disagreement, owned as weight of the faith's own.
- **Ivan's refusal.** It attacks no premise. It is love's protest, and the book honors it.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**R1. Evil, second reason.** Replace from "Permitting what free creatures do" through "not even in God's hands." with:
> What a free creature does is his own act, not God's (Chapter 11); and the harms that follow in a world of real causes God does not intend, either as an end or as a means (ST I q.19 a.9). Two answers must be kept apart here. *Why* He permits is the setting above: a world of real causes, and we are among them. The hands that pull a child from the fire are part of what He made real, so His permission never excuses ours (CCC 312). *What* He brings from the evil is a further thing: for a person who suffers, the good the Church hopes for is a good offered to her, not a price paid with her for someone else, because God governs each person for her own sake (St. Thomas, *Summa contra Gentiles* III.112). No one is the price of someone else's good, not even in God's hands.

**R2. Evil, skeptical-theism sentence.** Replace "what we would not expect to see, if the good is finished only in eternal life (CCC 314, 324), is how each particular evil is answered." with:
> what we would not expect to see, if the good is finished only in eternal life and in a creation set free (Romans 8:21; CCC 314, 324, 1046–1047), is how each particular evil is answered.

and after "the pattern of pain in nature still weighs." add:
> The Church does not say whether the fawn will live again; she says creation itself waits to be set free (Romans 8:19–22). For the fawn that hope is thinner than it is for the child, and this book does not pretend otherwise.

**R3. W1(1).** Replace "and He can raise her, so that she is there to be answered to, to be healed, and to forgive in her own name." with:
> and He can raise her, so that she is there to be answered to, to be healed, and free to forgive in her own name. He did not keep her from what was done to her; that weight is on the other side of the scale, and this book weighs it there. What He keeps is her claim, so that what was done to her is not the last word over her.

**R4. W3, hiddenness paragraph.** Replace "and that He has entrusted us with making Him known to one another (CCC 848);" with:
> and that He has entrusted us with making Him known to one another (CCC 848), as He gives His creatures the dignity of being causes for one another in everything else (CCC 306);

and replace "That is a real answer, and it has a real cost: for many honest seekers the conscious knowing comes late, or only at the end." with:
> That is a real answer to why many do not find Him, though not a full answer to why Love would choose this way, and it has a real cost: for many honest seekers the conscious knowing comes late, or only at the end. For the infant no one holds, there is not even a mother's love to carry Him, and the Church can only entrust her to His mercy (CCC 1261).

**R5. Evil, the weight.** After "Free will (Chapter 1) answers much of the evil we do to one another;" insert:
> it also answers why God did not simply begin with heaven, where the saints are free and cannot sin: love must first be freely given before it can be confirmed forever (Chapter 1);

**R6. Evil, hell.** Replace "though God predestines no one to that refusal (CCC 1037)." with:
> though God predestines no one to that refusal (CCC 1037), and holds no one to account beyond what he knew and freely chose; fear, wounds and what others did to him can lessen or even remove his responsibility (CCC 1735, 1860).

**R7. W1(2), last line.** Replace "Everyone is someone's child; she was wanted before anyone saw her." with:
> Everyone is someone's child. If this witness is right, she was wanted before anyone saw her, even when no one on earth wanted her.

**Chapter text (for the redline; outside v3):** line 131's "not because the evidence is insufficient" and "far more faith" still contradict v3 (CCC 37; "converging and convincing"). Line 109 ("Every time an atheist says 'That's wrong!'") must be limited to the one who denies that anything is really wrong, and must never stand as a reply to the problem of evil.

## 7. New insights and lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **Why He permits and what He brings are two answers.** The first is a world of real causes (CCC 306, 310). The second is a good from that very evil, offered to the one who suffered (CCC 324). Run together, they fall to Maitzen's dilemma. Kept apart, the first explains why our duty to rescue is untouched, and the second explains why the rescue costs her nothing God cannot give.
- **We are among the causes He made real.** The strongest Catholic answer to "why does He not intervene?" also tells us what we are for in the face of evil. His permission is never ours.
- **In nature the word is "not intended," not "not done."** Aquinas holds that God causes natural corruption per accidens. The book should use double effect, which it already owns, rather than a doing-versus-allowing distinction made for agents among agents.
- **The parent test is passed by defeat, not by permission.** A mother who allows torture is guilty because she can do nothing for her child afterward. God's innocence, if He is innocent, rests on what He can do for her: raise, heal, and answer.
- **"Through persons" predicts the demographics.** Maitzen's datum becomes neutral between the two accounts. What remains is the cost of the way God works, which is owned.
- **The case's clues are what keep its answer to Rowe from being ad hoc.** "We would not expect to see it now" rests on the good being finished at the end, and W1(1) and W3 give reason's own clues for that end. The answer is only as strong as those clues, and the book should not ask more of it.
- **Christ refused to read disaster as desert** (Luke 13:4; John 9:3). So the Catholic carries the full weight that the doctrine of karma avoids by desert. That is part of the honesty of the case.
- **The infant on the hillside is the book's clearest witness and its hardest question.** Both are true, and the conclusion is stronger if the book knows it.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "The hands that pull a child from the fire are part of what He made real."
- "His permission never excuses ours."
- "He did not keep her from what was done to her. What He keeps is her claim."
- "Free to forgive in her own name."
- "A real answer to why many do not find Him, though not a full answer to why Love would choose this way."
- "For the fawn that hope is thinner than it is for the child, and this book does not pretend otherwise."
- Kept from v3, and good: "No one is the price of someone else's good, not even in God's hands." "A will that lacks nothing has nothing to grasp at." "That does not explain why. It means that, if He is, the last word over the suffering child is not the suffering."
