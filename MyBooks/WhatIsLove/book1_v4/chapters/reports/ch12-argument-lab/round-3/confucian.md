# Argument Lab, Round 3: Confucian opponent vs. v3

## 1. School and voice

The opponent speaks for the Confucian tradition at full strength: the *Analects*, Mencius (the nature is good; the four sprouts), Xunzi (the nature must be shaped; Heaven is the constant course of nature), the *Doctrine of the Mean* and the *Great Learning*, the Neo-Confucian school of principle (*li* 理: Zhang Zai, the Cheng brothers, Zhu Xi), and Mou Zongsan's "immanent transcendence," in which the moral heart-mind is itself the creative infinite. He grants nearly everything the book says about love. He denies that its Source is Someone. This round he presses the parts of v3 that were rewritten to answer him: the sacred-order row, the line "Heaven and Earth give life to things; God wanted *you*," and W1(1)'s appeal to people who speak to their dead.

*Accuracy notes.* *Analects* numbering follows Slingerland (Legge is often one off). Every passage below is paraphrased. Nothing is a direct quotation of a modern author. The classical Chinese terms are given only so the passages can be checked. Whether Heaven (*Tian*) is personal is honestly contested inside the tradition. Roughly, it runs from personal (Zhou religion, *Shangdi*, much of the *Analects*), through ambiguous (Mencius), to naturalized (Xunzi) and principle-centered (Zhu Xi).

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges (Round 3: new material only)

**Challenge 1. Starting point for justice.**
*Answer.* Justice starts with repayment (*bao* 報). What I owe is fixed first by what I have received: life and body from my parents, culture from the sages, nourishment from Heaven and Earth. The *Book of Rites* speaks of "repaying the root and returning to the beginning" (*bao ben fan shi*). Rightness (*yi*) grows from the sprout of shame and disdain (*Mencius* 2A6). Reciprocity measures it (*Analects* 15.24). One principle with many expressions extends it to the stranger (Cheng Yi on Zhang Zai).
*Verdict: Holds, and it is close to the book.* A justice that begins in repaying a gift is very near Chapter 4's "gift of covenant." It borrows nothing. But it raises the book's question by its own logic. Repayment needs someone who receives it. Parents can receive what we repay them, but "Heaven and Earth" receive nothing, on Zhu Xi's own account.

**Challenge 2. A hard case, resolved (new).**
*Case (Mencius 7A35).* Shun is emperor. His father kills a man. What should Shun do?
*Answer.* Mencius says Shun would not stop the judge Gao Yao from arresting his father, because the law is not his to set aside. He would give up the empire "like a worn-out sandal," carry his father on his back, flee to the edge of the sea, and live there gladly for the rest of his life. Shun does not corrupt the public law. He does not abandon his father. He pays for both with everything he has.
*Verdict: Coherent, and honest about the cost. It strains A4 at exactly one point.* It reaches self-gift (A6) without borrowing the book's terms: the emperor gives up the throne. But the murdered man's claim yields to the son's devotion, since the killer is never punished. Modern Chinese scholars argue fiercely over whether this is nepotism. That is the place where role ethics, at its best, still lets the stranger count for a little less. The book's A4 ("equal worth is not equal duty, but no one is worth less") draws the line exactly there.

**Challenge 3. What grounds equal, unconditional dignity?**
*Answer.* Zhu Xi's image: the one moon shines whole in ten thousand rivers. Every person carries the whole of principle, the Supreme Ultimate (*taiji*), and not a share of it. People differ only in how clear or clouded their material endowment (*qi*) is. So the newborn, the man in a coma and the severely disabled child carry exactly the nature the sage carries. Worth follows nature, not capacity. And because each one holds the whole, no sum of others holds more. Mencius's rule that one may not kill one innocent person even to gain the whole world (2A2) follows directly.
*Verdict: Holds for equality. It breaks at "for her own sake" (Exchange 4).* It is still the best capacity-free ground any non-theist school has given this lab. But if one river is drained, the moon has lost nothing. The image explains why everyone is equal. It does not explain what is lost when *this one* is gone.

**Challenge 4. The ultimate human good.**
*Answer.* Sagehood: to form a triad with Heaven and Earth (*Mean* 22), to form one body with all things (Wang Yangming), and to meet death in peace. Zhang Zai, paraphrased: while I live I follow and serve; when I die, I rest. For the world, the hope is the Great Unity (*Book of Rites*, "Liyun"), in which widowers, widows, orphans, the childless, the disabled and the sick are all cared for.
*Verdict: Coherent, and noble. It stops short at communion and at the dead.* Union is not communion. The Great Unity is a real hope for future victims without God. It is not a hope for *this* victim, who died before it came.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1: did v3 fix Round 2?**
*Audit against my Round 2 §5:*
1. W1(1) contradicted A3: **fixed.** "The Catholic answer is not that God forgives in her place. He does not." CCC 1850 and *Spe Salvi* 44 are used correctly.
2. The sacred-order row was wrong three ways: **mostly fixed.** "No problem of evil" is now conditional. "Indifferent" is gone. "Reach for personal words" is gone. The capacity-free A4 is credited. Mou is named. The Stoics are moved into a parenthesis. A few smaller accuracy faults remain, and one of them came from my own Round 2 wording (Exchanges 2, 3, 7).
3. W1(2)'s "received" slid to "Someone": **fixed.** The step now runs through being as gift (ST I q.20 a.2) and being wanted (GS 24). It no longer runs through "unconferred."
4. Mou Zongsan was omitted: **fixed.**
5. Anscombe's fork was presented as exhaustive: **fixed.** It is now posed as a question.
6. The God row's "one stopping point instead of many": **fixed.** Against the sacred order it now says "the one that knows and loves."
7. Minor items (the A4 citation placement, Pieper uncredited, "not less than personal," the relational-self clause in W3): **all fixed.** *Veritatis Splendor* 41 (participated theonomy) now answers Mou's heteronomy charge in the W1 closing, a repair I had not asked for and a good one.
*Result: Held.* v3 is a real improvement against this school. What follows are new objections.

**Exchange 2: "Heaven as Xunzi and Zhu Xi understood it" conflates two opposite Heavens.**
*Opponent.* "Your row says that Heaven as Xunzi understood it explains A1, A3, A5, A6 and W2. It does not. Xunzi's essay on Heaven (ch. 17) says that Heaven's course is constant, that it did not keep Yao alive or destroy Jie, and that it answers no one's conduct. For Xunzi the moral Way, ritual and rightness, was *made* by the sage-kings through deliberate effort. Our nature is bad, and goodness is artifice. Xunzi's Heaven explains none of A, and it is not sacred in your sense. And the row's next sentences (every person carries the whole of principle; the heart of Heaven and Earth is to give life) are things Xunzi would deny. Your own Round 2 repair put his name there. It was wrong then too."
*Defense.* "Conceded, and the error came from the Confucian side's own Round 2 wording. Xunzi does belong in the row in one place: his is the Heaven that is free of the problem of evil, because it cares for no one. That is the fork's first horn. But his ground for the moral law is the sages' making, and that puts him nearer the flourishing row, and in A3's trouble (what sages made, later sages could remake). He answers that trouble by saying the core of the Way did not change across all the former kings."
*Result: Held with repair (Minor).* Name the Neo-Confucians in the heading. Mention Xunzi only as the limit case, the Heaven free of evil because it cares for no one, and send his moral Way to the flourishing row. See §6.

**Exchange 3: "It gives life to kinds" is not what Zhu Xi said, and "God wanted *you*" overreaches Aquinas.**
*Opponent, first part.* "Zhu Xi's essay on humaneness says that Heaven and Earth take giving life to things (*sheng wu* 生物) as their heart. It also says that people and things, in coming to be, each receive the heart of Heaven and Earth as their own heart. Things, not kinds. Each one receives it. Your row's 'gives life to kinds' is a gloss that makes our Heaven look colder than it is."
*Defense.* "Half conceded. Each thing receives the life-giving heart. But look at the passage where Zhu Xi is asked whether Heaven and Earth really have a mind (*Classified Conversations*, book 1). He answers: if they truly had none, cows would give birth to horses and peach trees would bear plum blossoms. The mind he grants Heaven shows itself in the *constancy of kinds*. In the same book he says that principle has no feeling or intention, no calculation and no making. So the accurate claim is this: it gives life to each thing, keeps each kind true to itself, and intends no one. That is stronger than 'gives life to kinds,' and it is true to the text."
*Opponent, second part.* "Now your line. 'God wanted *you*.' Your own Aquinas says that God wills Himself as the end and wills other things as ordered to His goodness (ST I q.19 a.2). Then God does not want you *for your own sake*. He wants you for the sake of His goodness, just as Heaven gives life out of its own life-giving nature. Both sources overflow without need. 'Wanted' also sounds like a lack being filled. Your 'for her own sake' is rhetoric that your own metaphysics takes back."
*Defense.* "Aquinas answers this himself. God wills His goodness, and His goodness is to give. To will His goodness *is* to will her good. Among creatures, the rational ones are willed and governed for their own sake, and the rest for theirs (*Summa contra Gentiles* III.112). That is what *Gaudium et Spes* 24 means by 'willed for itself': an end, never a means to other creatures. 'Wanted' is meant as a parent wants a child, not as a hunger. The real asymmetry is not whether there is overflow, since both have it. It is whether the singular is known and willed. God knows each singular thing (ST I q.14 a.11), and His love does not find goodness in her but pours it into her (I q.20 a.2). Zhu Xi's principle has no intention. One gives life *to* her; the other gives life to things, and she is one of them."
*Opponent rejoinder.* "Then say 'intends none,' not 'kinds,' and let the reader see that we too speak of a heart."
*Result: Held with repair (Minor).* Keep the line "Heaven and Earth give life to things; God wanted *you*." Its first half is literally Zhu Xi's phrase. Replace "gives life to kinds and lets each one go" with "gives life to each thing and intends none: it keeps each kind true to itself and lets each one go." See §6.

**Exchange 4: the moon in ten thousand rivers. Can "never spent" stand without being wanted?**
*Opponent.* "W1(2) says that being human explains why the newborn belongs, but not why what belongs may never be spent. The row says we can say each one shares the nature, but not that this one is wanted. Here is our answer to both. Each one carries the *whole* principle, as each river holds the whole moon. Wholes do not add up. Five rivers do not hold five moons. So no total of others can outweigh one, and Mencius forbids killing one innocent person to gain the whole world (2A2). We explain 'never spent' without anyone wanting anyone."
*Defense.* "It is a beautiful image, and it proves too much. If five rivers do not hold more moon than one, then one river does not hold less moon than five. Drain one, and the moon has lost nothing. Drain all five, and the moon in the sky is untouched. The image does not forbid spending her. It makes the spending cost nothing at the level that matters. So Mencius's ban has to rest somewhere else, and in Mencius it does: on the doer's rightness. The sage *would not do* one unrighteous act, even for the world. That is a real and absolute prohibition. But it sits in the agent's heart. A4 puts the 'never' in *her*. v3's W1 opening says the wrong is wrong first because of what it does to the child."
*Opponent rejoinder.* "Your God also loses nothing when she dies. And your Church says her soul does not perish. You are no better off."
*Defense.* "He loses nothing. *She* does: her life, her years, her body. And He does not shrug at that, as the moon shrugs at a drained river. He keeps her and will raise her (Isaiah 49:15; W1(1)). The difference is not whether the source is diminished. It is whether the one lost is kept."
*Result: Held.* No repair to the argument is needed. The exchange gives the book its clearest image of the difference (§7).

**Exchange 5: "an equal call explains an equal worth." We have an equal call too.**
*Opponent.* "W1(2) says that worth follows the call, not the capacity. The *Doctrine of the Mean* opens by saying that what Heaven ordains (*ming* 命) is called the nature. Zhu Xi glosses *ming* as 'like a command.' Mencius says that anyone can become a Yao or a Shun (6B2). Every person is equally ordained and equally called to sagehood. We have your equal call, and no caller is needed."
*Defense.* "Two answers. First, Zhu Xi's own word is 'like' (*you* 猶): like a command. He knew it was a figure. The book should *not* argue from that 'like' to a Commander. That would be the old 'laid by Someone' step, and the book has rightly dropped it. Second, and decisive: look at what the call is *to*, and who can answer it. Mencius's call is to sagehood, reached by effort in this life. The child who will never speak cannot become Yao or Shun. Zhu Xi would say her *qi* is too clouded for it in this life. The call the Church means is to communion with God (GS 29), given by grace and finished beyond death (CCC 1024). It does not wait on effort or on capacity. So the Mencian call is equal *in principle* and unequal in who can ever answer it, while the Christian call is equal in both. That is exactly the case A4 cares about most."
*Opponent rejoinder.* "Zhu Xi would say her nature is complete even if it is never realized. Worth does not need realization."
*Defense.* "Then her worth rests on the nature, not on the call, and we are back at the moon (Exchange 4). On equality, parity. On 'for good' and 'for her own sake,' theism is ahead."
*Result: Held.* W1(2) stands. One small wording fault in its last sentence: see Exchange 9.

**Exchange 6: W1(1) and the ancestral rite. The dilemma leaves out the road our tradition actually walks.**
*Opponent.* "W1(1) says: 'Either the debt died with her, and what is left is only remorse ...; or it did not, and she is still someone. People everywhere act as though she is.' No civilization has organized itself more thoroughly around debts to the dead than ours. Three years of mourning, because for three years the parents carried us (17.21). Keeping the father's way for three years after he dies (1.11). The ancestral temple. So we know the practice from the inside, and it refutes your dilemma in two ways.
(a) *The 'as though.'* The Master sacrificed 'as though they were present' (3.12). He would not discuss spirits (7.21) and said: you cannot yet serve the living, so how can you serve the dead (11.12)? Xunzi says plainly that the cultivated man keeps the rites for the dead as the human way, and only the common people take them as serving ghosts (ch. 19). Our most reflective practitioners speak to the dead and decline the inference you draw from their speaking. 'People everywhere act as though she is' is true, but the most careful of those people do not believe that she is.
(b) *The third road.* For us the debt did not die with her, and she is not still someone. The debt is real, it is owed to her as she was, and the living *repay* it (*bao*): by rite, by remembrance, by keeping her wishes, by caring for her children. That is not 'only remorse.' It is the completion of a relationship, carried out by the living. Your dilemma's first horn insults two thousand years of filial piety, and its second horn does not follow from the denial of the first."
*Defense.* "The third road is real. As written, the either/or leaves it out, and that is a fault, not a matter of style. The dilemma is the hinge of W1(1). (The debunker reaches the same point this round from another direction: 'owed to her as she was.') But look at what the third road can and cannot do.
First, it can repay. It cannot *receive*, and it cannot *forgive*. What the living pay the dead, no one receives, if no one is there. And what cannot be repaid (the ashes buried in the field, the child killed) no one ever releases. Your own tradition felt this. Mozi mocked the Confucians of his day: to hold that there are no spirits and still study the sacrificial rites, he said, is like learning the ceremonies for guests when there are no guests, or making fishnets where there are no fish (*Mozi* 48, paraphrased). That is an argument, not a genealogy, and it is the book's argument. A rite of repayment is shaped for a receiver.
Second, your own canon keeps a moment where guilt is confessed to Someone and pardon is placed in His heart. In *Analects* 20.1 the founder of the Shang, Tang, offers a black bull and announces to the august Lord on High that he dares not pardon the guilty himself. The judgment lies in the Lord's heart, and if he himself has sinned, let the guilt not fall on the people. I do not use that as a genealogy: the later tradition read it otherwise, and the fairness rule binds us. I use it as one more datum of the shape W1(1) describes. Guilt that cannot be settled on earth looks for Someone with whom the judgment lies.
Third, the gap that Round 2 found is still there. Many of the dead have no descendants to repay them, and your own dynasties had to build public altars for the unattended dead. The third road is closed exactly where the case is hardest."
*Opponent rejoinder.* "Fine. But then W1(1) must *say* there are three roads, and must stop calling ours 'only remorse.' And 'ask their pardon' is not what most of the world does at its graves; most of it reports, feeds and honors. Some ask pardon."
*Defense.* "Agreed on both."
*Result: Failed as written (Serious); Held with repair.* As written, the dilemma is not exhaustive. It mislabels the strongest alternative as "only remorse," and its evidence ("people everywhere act as though she is") is explicitly read "as though" by the tradition that practices it most. Repaired (§6), W1(1) names three roads, honors the second, and rests the clue on what the second road cannot do: no one receives the repayment, no one releases what cannot be repaid, and the kinless dead get nothing. Repaired, W1(1) holds as a clue, at parity or slightly ahead against this school. The rest of the case does not depend on it.

**Exchange 7: Mou Zongsan is not "impersonal," and his world is not loveless.**
*Opponent.* "You file Mou under 'an impersonal sacred order.' His whole charge against Zhu Xi is that Zhu's principle 'merely is and does not act.' For Mou the ground is a living heart, creative activity, the original heart-mind that is principle. He would reject your heading. And you write that 'no one is there to forgive me or love me back.' Mou defines humaneness (*ren*) as sympathetic communication (*gantong*): the heart reaching every other heart and being reached by it. In his world everyone loves back: parents, friends, the sage, and the myriad things in resonance. As for forgiving: Wang Yangming's innate knowing (*liangzhi*) knows its fault in the very act of seeing it and corrects it at once. We do not need pardon. We need awakening."
*Defense.* "On the label: fair. Mou is neither an order nor a Person. The row should say that he would refuse the word 'impersonal,' and why. On 'love me back': 'there' in v3 means at the deepest ground, but read flatly the sentence says something false about Confucian life, and it should be narrowed. On forgiving: awakening and correction are real, and the Church prizes them (conversion, CCC 1430–1431). But correcting myself is not being released by the one I wronged. My original heart can correct what I will do. It cannot answer for what I did to her. And the heart Mou calls infinite is the same in all of us. It is no one over against me who knows me as myself, and no one who keeps the one I lost."
*Result: Held with repair (Minor).* See §6.

**Exchange 8: the problem-of-evil fork. Zhu Xi's answer is your answer.**
*Opponent.* "Your row says such an order is free of the problem of evil only if it cares for no one in particular. Zhu Xi's Heaven *cares*: its heart is to give life. It still carries a lighter problem than yours, and it has an answer. Principle is wholly good. The unevenness (Bo Yi starving, the robber living long) lies in the material force, which principle does not steer step by step. That is your own CCC 310 and ST I q.19 a.9: a world of real causes, an order willed as a whole, no loss willed for its own sake. And Sima Qian did not 'watch' good men starve. Bo Yi died a thousand years before him. He set the histories side by side, and the saying he was testing, that the Way of Heaven has no favorites and is always with the good man, comes from the *Laozi* (ch. 79)."
*Defense.* "The parallel is real and should be credited. But the two answers differ at the end. A principle that does not steer and intends no one has a lighter problem, because no one chose to permit anything. It also has nothing to promise: it cannot say that Bo Yi, himself, will be answered. The Church carries the heavier problem *because* she claims a God who knows each one, and she pays for it with the weight the evil section owns. The fork in v3 is accurate as stated: free of the question only by intending no one, and facing it the more it cares. The 'watched' wording is a small inaccuracy."
*Result: Held with minor repair.* Add one clause crediting Zhu Xi's answer, and replace "watched." See §6.

**Exchange 9: smaller presses.**
(a) *A2, "Shame says I am less."* "Mencian shame (the shame and disdain that sprout into rightness, 2A6) is not 'I am less.' It is shame *at the wrong*, and it turns toward the one I failed. The guilt-culture versus shame-culture contrast is old anthropology, and most scholars no longer accept it." *Defense:* "The line describes two feelings, not two civilizations, and A2 already says that almost no tradition treats wrongdoing as a mere mistake. But one word avoids the misreading: 'Shame, left to itself, says I am less.'" *Held with minor repair.*
(b) *W1 frame, "The test that does is this: is guilt owed to someone, or does it only ripen?"* "Confucian role ethics passes your test on the 'owed' side without God. Guilt is owed to the one wronged, and to her parents and her line. So your test separates you from karma and from bare principle, not from us. The deciding case is the dead victim, which is W1(1)." *Defense:* "Granted. The frame should add the second half of the test." *Held with minor repair.*
(c) *The row's citation for "in danger and at the edge of grief."* "14.35 ('it is Heaven that knows me') is spoken when no one understands him. It is neither danger nor grief. The danger passages are 7.23 and 9.5. The grief passage is 11.9 ('Heaven has bereft me!'). Cite them." *Held with minor repair.*
(d) *"Its most careful teacher, Zhu Xi."* "Mou called Zhu Xi a side branch that became the main line. 'Most careful' is a ranking the tradition disputes. 'Most systematic' is not." *Minor.*
(e) *W1(2), last sentence: "Everyone is someone's child; she was wanted before anyone saw her."* "Read plainly, that says every child was wanted by her parents. Round 2 caught the same false universal in 'every child is loved.' Many children are not wanted." *Defense:* "Say what is meant: even the child no one wanted was wanted." *Held with minor repair.*
(f) *W2, "one Source who made both."* "Zhu Xi's principle does no making. The *who* and the *made* settle in advance what you then say W1 and W3 must show." *Defense:* "Fair. Say 'one Source of both' here and let W1 and W3 do their work." *Minor.*
(g) *The Stoic parenthesis.* "Accurate. Cleanthes's *Hymn to Zeus* is a prayer, Epictetus prays it (*Handbook* 53), and Seneca's *On Providence* and Chrysippus answer for evil. But it leaves out the point that matters most to your book. Chrysippus said that if the foot had a mind it would *want* to get muddy for the sake of the whole (reported by Epictetus, *Discourses* 2.6). Stoic Providence spends the part for the whole. And it does not keep the dead for good: souls last at most until the world-fire." *Defense:* "That sharpens the row in our favor, and A6's 'never spent' is exactly what Stoic Providence lacks. Add it." *Held with optional repair.*

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**As written: ONE Serious objection stands. No Fatal objection.**

1. **W1(1): its either/or leaves out the third road, and its evidence is read "as though" by the tradition that practices it most.** **Serious.** "Either the debt died with her, and what is left is only remorse ...; or it did not, and she is still someone" omits a debt owed to the dead as she was and repaid by the living (*bao*: rite, remembrance, keeping her wishes, caring for her children). For two millennia the Confucian tradition lived that road with full seriousness and without holding that the dead hear. "Sacrifice as though present" (*Analects* 3.12); Xunzi ch. 19. As written, W1(1) labels that road "only remorse," and it cites "people everywhere act as though she is" as support for the second horn. The inference is invalid at the hinge of the witness. This converges with the debunker's Round 3 finding from a different direction. *Answerable at parity:* the repair in §6 names the third road and honors it. It then rests the clue on what that road cannot do. No one receives the repayment. No one releases what cannot be repaid. The kinless dead get nothing (Mozi's "rites without guests"; *Analects* 20.1). Repaired, W1(1) is a clue at parity or slightly ahead. The rest of the case does not rest on it.

**Minor (wording and accuracy; none changes the argument):**
2. The sacred-order row names Xunzi alongside Zhu Xi as one Heaven. Xunzi's Heaven explains none of A, and his moral Way is the sages' making.
3. The row says "gives life to kinds." Zhu Xi says it gives life to things, and each receives Heaven's heart. Accurate: "gives life to each thing and intends none."
4. Mou is filed under "impersonal," which he would reject. "No one is there to ... love me back" reads too flatly against a tradition built on *ren* as mutual response.
5. The citation for "in danger and at the edge of grief" should be 7.23, 9.5, 11.9 and 14.35, not 14.35 alone. "Most careful" should be "most systematic." "Watched" should be "set side by side."
6. Zhu Xi's own answer to evil (principle good, material force uneven) is close to CCC 310 and should be credited.
7. A2: "Shame says I am less" misreads Mencian shame. Say "Shame, left to itself."
8. W1 frame: the ripening test does not separate theism from person-centered secular ethics. Add the dead-victim half.
9. W1(2): "she was wanted before anyone saw her," after "everyone is someone's child," reads as a false universal about parents.
10. W2: "one Source who made both" assumes agency before W1 and W3 show it.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**
- *Equality without capacity tests (A4).* The Neo-Confucian ground (each carries the whole principle) matches the theist ground on equality. Theism is ahead only on "for her own sake" and "for good" (Exs. 4–5).
- *The problem of evil.* A principle that intends no one carries a lighter problem and has nothing to promise. A God who knows each one carries the heavier problem and can promise this child. Each pays for what it gets (Ex. 8).
- *Autonomy.* Mou's charge that received worth is lesser worth is answered by Chapter 4 (a gift becomes truly the beloved's own), CCC 460 and VS 41. He will not grant it, and neither side can force the other.
- *Union versus communion (W3).* Forming one body with all things against being loved by Someone besides oneself. Each side's summit is coherent, and they want different things.
- *Debts to the dead (W1(1), repaired).* Repayment by the living (*bao*) against a debt that waits for its creditor. Theism is ahead at the kinless dead and the unrepayable wrong, at parity elsewhere.
- *Ricci's causal principle* (what lacks understanding cannot give rise to beings that understand). It stays at parity and is not relied on.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**W1(1), replace from "Either the debt died with her" through "Many of the dead have no one left to speak to them." with:**
> Then there are three roads. The debt died with her, and what is left is remorse, a wound that will never be healed by the one it was done to. Or the debt outlives her, owed to her as she was, and the living repay what they can: they keep her memory, honor her wishes, and care for those she loved. That is the road of the great ancestral traditions, and their wisest teachers walked it honestly: Confucius sacrificed to the dead as though they were present, and Xunzi taught that the cultivated man keeps those rites as the human way of completing a bond, without holding that the dead hear. Or she is still someone, and the debt can one day be received by her. The second road is noble, and the Church walks much of it; she too honors and remembers her dead. But on that road, what the living pay, no one receives, and what can never be repaid, no one ever releases. And many of the dead have no one left to remember them at all. In every age and land people speak to their dead, and some ask their pardon; even those who say the dead do not hear keep the rite, as though it were shaped for someone who does.

> The test that does is this: **is guilt owed to someone, or does it only ripen?** And if it is owed to her, who is there to receive it when no one on earth is left?

**W1(2), last sentence, replace with:**

**A2, replace "Shame says I am less;" with:**

**W2, replace "are best explained by one Source who made both." with:**
> are best explained by one Source of both.

**C, replace the "impersonal sacred order" row with:**
> - **An impersonal sacred order** (Heaven's principle as the Neo-Confucians, above all Zhu Xi, understood it; the Stoic Logos) is among the strongest rivals a book about love can face. It explains a great deal (A1, A3, A5, A6, W2). It holds that every person, however clouded in body or mind, carries the whole of Heaven's principle, as the one moon shines whole in ten thousand rivers, so that worth follows nature, not capacity (A4). Its sages say the heart of Heaven and Earth is to give life. But it gives life to each thing and intends none: it keeps each kind true to itself and lets each one go. It can say that each one holds the whole nature, not that *this* one is wanted for her own sake; when one river is drained, the moon has lost nothing. Heaven and Earth give life to things; God wanted *you*. An order cannot be wronged or forgive (A2), and it cannot know or love anyone (W3). Its most systematic teacher, Zhu Xi, said that principle has no intention, and that Heaven both has a mind and has none; Confucius, in danger, in grief, and when no one understood him, spoke of a Heaven that knows him (*Analects* 7.23, 9.5, 11.9, 14.35). The modern Confucian Mou Zongsan would refuse the word "impersonal": for him the ground is a living moral heart, the same in all of us, that is itself the infinite. Then the law is mine at my deepest, and others love me back; but no one at the ground of things knows me as myself, releases what I did to another, or keeps the one I lost. Such an order is free of the problem of evil only if it intends no one in particular. Zhu Xi's answer, that principle is wholly good and the unevenness lies in the material force it does not steer, is close to the Church's own (CCC 310); but a principle that does not steer can promise no one anything. The more such an order cares, the more it faces our question, as the historian Sima Qian did when he set the good men who starved beside the robber who lived long and asked whether the Way of Heaven is just. (Xunzi's Heaven, the bare constant course of nature, is free of that question, because it cares for no one; but for him the moral Way was made by the sages, not given by Heaven, and he belongs nearer the flourishing of our kind. The Stoic Logos sits nearer a Person: it was prayed to as Providence, and the Stoics, like us, had to answer for evil. But their Providence cares for the whole and spends the part for it, and it does not keep the dead for good.)

**Evil section, optional fairness, replace "Not every account without God is without hope: the Buddhist hopes for the end of all suffering." with:**
> Not every account without God is without hope: the Buddhist hopes for the end of all suffering, and the Confucian for a world in which the widow, the orphan and the disabled are all cared for.

## 7. New insights for the book

1. **The moon in ten thousand rivers.** This is Zhu Xi's own image for why everyone is equal, and it shows the difference between a life-giving order and a loving God more clearly than any argument. The moon is whole in every river, so every person is equal. Drain one river, and the moon has lost nothing. God is not the moon and she is not His reflection. She is His wanted child, and He keeps her. (Ch. 12; Ch. 4 on gift.)
2. **A call that waits on effort versus a call that does not.** Mencius said anyone can become a Yao or a Shun (6B2). The child who will never speak cannot. The Christian call (GS 29; CCC 1024) is given by grace and finished beyond death, so it reaches exactly the child A4 cares about most. (Ch. 12, A4/W1(2).)
3. **Mozi's "rites without guests."** A Chinese philosopher made W1(1)'s point twenty-four centuries ago, against Confucians who kept the sacrifices while doubting the spirits: like learning the ceremonies of hospitality with no guest, or making nets where there are no fish (*Mozi* 48, paraphrased). It is an argument, not a genealogy, so it is fair to use. (Ch. 12.)
4. **Pardon placed in the Lord's heart, inside the Confucian canon.** In *Analects* 20.1, Tang dares not pardon the guilty himself. He says the judgment lies in the heart of the Lord on High, and he asks that his own guilt not fall on the people. Use it as a datum of guilt's shape, not as proof that the tradition was "really" theist. The later tradition read it otherwise. (Ch. 12, Ch. 8 on mercy.)
5. **Justice begins in repayment (*bao*).** "Repaying the root" is a non-Christian witness that justice starts from a gift received. That is Chapter 4's covenant logic. Its limit is the book's question: repayment needs someone to receive it. (Ch. 7 on justice; Ch. 4.)
6. **Shun carrying his father to the sea (Mencius 7A35).** This is the most honest hard case a role ethic offers. It reaches self-gift without the book's words, and it shows where the stranger's claim still yields to one's own. A4's "equal worth is not equal duty, but no one is worth less" is the Catholic line through it. (Ch. 7 or Ch. 11 on prudence.)
7. **Mencius's extension (1A7) is W2's engine of correction.** The king who spared the ox is told to extend that same heart to his people. Conscience corrects itself by consistency. That is the book's own point, made by a pagan sage. (Ch. 12, W2.)
8. **Zhu Xi's theodicy parallels CCC 310.** Principle is good, and the material force is uneven and not steered step by step. Crediting this makes the evil section fairer, and it makes the real difference stand out: a principle that does not steer can promise no one anything.
9. **Sima Qian's question was a test of the *Laozi*'s saying** that the Way of Heaven has no favorites and is always with the good man. Abraham's question (Gen 18:25) is the biblical counterpart, and the Judge answers by letting Himself be asked.
10. **Verify before anything enters the book:** Zhu Xi, "Treatise on Humaneness" (*Ren shuo*); *Zhuzi yulei* book 1 (principle without intention, calculation or making; Heaven's mind; cows and horses, peach and plum) and book 94 (the moon in the rivers); Zhu Xi's commentary on *Mean* 1 ("*ming* is like a command"); *Xunzi* chs. 17, 19; *Analects* 1.11, 3.12, 3.13, 7.21, 7.23, 9.5, 11.9–12, 14.35, 17.21, 20.1; *Mencius* 1A7, 2A2, 2A6, 6A7, 6A8, 6A10, 6B2, 7A1, 7A2, 7A35, 7B24; *Mozi* 48; *Shiji* 61; *Laozi* 79; *Liji* "Liyun" and "Jiaotesheng"; Epictetus, *Discourses* 2.6 and *Handbook* 53; Cleanthes, *Hymn to Zeus*; *Summa contra Gentiles* III.112; ST I q.14 a.11, q.19 a.2, q.20 a.2; GS 24, 29; VS 41; CCC 310, 1024, 1430–1431. For Mou: *Xinti yu xingti* and *Zhi de zhijue yu Zhongguo zhexue* (paraphrase only).
