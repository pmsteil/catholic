# Round 3: Doctrinal Censor's Read of v3 (pre-imprimatur)

**Scope.** v3 only (EVOLUTION.md lines 101–141). Read as a theologian preparing a text for a bishop's *nihil obstat* / *imprimatur* review: every theological statement and citation checked against Scripture (RSVCE), the Catechism, Vatican II, *Veritatis Splendor*, *Spe Salvi*, *Dignitas Infinita*, *Salvifici Doloris*, *Dominus Iesus*, Vatican I and Aquinas. This is not an opponent's score.

**Bottom line.** v3 is sound in its main lines: it rejects voluntarism, keeps reason's witness distinct from faith, treats non-believers without presuming guilt, and handles natural evil with the right Catechism texts. **Five items are Errors** that a censor would have to require changes to: (1) the ashes example; (2) "the enemy … never to be killed"; (3) "experimented on to death *without consent*"; (4) the W1(1) treatment of forgiveness and the dead (it collides with CCC 1441, splits God's forgiveness into a "part", and reads as if the dead victim is not "there" until the resurrection); (5) "No one is the price of someone else's good, not even in God's hands," which as written cuts against the Cross and against redemptive suffering. Most of the rest is "Needs care": wording a reader could misread, or Church teaching stated more weakly than the Church states it (the knowability of God, hell, the resurrection, the communion of saints).

### Protestant-sounding or non-Catholic framing, gathered in one place
1. Forgiveness described with no Church and no Sacrament (#10).
2. The dead victim seemingly not "there" until the resurrection, which reads as soul-sleep (#11).
3. "A God who suffers," without the impassibility distinction (#44).
4. The resurrection presented as something faith "hopes" for rather than professes (#45), and God "can" raise rather than will raise (#11).
5. "Grasping love must die," which sounds like renouncing desire rather than healing it (#25).

None of these is deliberate. Each is fixed by a clause.

## 3. Replacement wording (every Error and Needs-care item)

**R1 (A2, replaces the ashes sentence).**
> If I promise my dying friend to keep a secret she trusted to me, and after her death tell it for a laugh to a stranger in a far city who never knew her and will never repeat it, nothing in anyone's experience is worse, and still she was wronged.

**R2 (A4, replaces the first sentence after the bolded claim).**
> The newborn, the dying, the severely disabled, the enemy: equal, prior to anyone's recognition, never to be used as a means for better totals, never, if innocent, to be deliberately killed, and not lost when life grows hard, nor even by the worst of crimes (*Dignitas Infinita* 1, 15; CCC 2261, 2267).

**R3 (A6).**
> … a city may not secretly hang an innocent man to calm a riot; no one may be experimented on to death, however many would be saved, and not even his own consent could make it right (CCC 2295).

**R4 (A5).**
> Reason sees certain goods (life, the union of man and woman and the raising of children, truth, including the truth about God, and a just life in common with others) as good and to be pursued, and sees that good is to be done and evil avoided (ST I-II q.94 a.2).

**R5 (W1 coda, replaces "The wrong is necessary given what we are; what we are is His gift.").**
> Its wrongness is necessary, given what we are; and what we are, as He made us, is His gift. The disorder in our desires is not His work but the wound of sin (CCC 405).

**R6 (W1, replaces from "On one point every account agrees" to "He is the Good (Chapter 3).").**
> On one point every account agrees: the wrong is necessarily wrong. We see it first in what it does to the child, and anyone can see it, with or without a revealed law (Romans 2:14–15): it is not wrong merely because it is forbidden. Not even God makes cruelty right; He cannot, because He is the Good (Chapter 3). And because the child is His, every such wrong is also, and before all else, an offense against Him (CCC 1440).

**R7 (W1(1), replaces from "A wronged person needs forgiving" through "as though nothing had happened (*Spe Salvi* 44).").** The closing sentences of v3 ("Reason cannot prove that hope … only a person can forgive.") stay as they are.
> A wronged person needs forgiving, and the pardon that heals the bond between us is hers to give. When she has died, or never knew, no one on earth can give it in her place, and still we owe her (A3). Either the debt died with her, and what is left is only remorse, a wound that will never be healed by the one it was done to; or it did not, and she is still someone. People everywhere act as though she is: they speak to their dead and ask their pardon.
>
> The Catholic answer begins with God. Every wrong against a person is also, and before all else, an offense against the One who made her precious (CCC 1440, 1850), and only God forgives sins (CCC 1441). He does so through His Church, in the Sacrament of Reconciliation (CCC 1442), and His forgiveness is whole. Yet it does not cancel what we owe her: absolution "does not remedy all the disorders sin has caused" (CCC 1459), so we must still repair what we can, and God does not take her place in giving the pardon that is hers to give. Nor has she ceased to be. The soul does not perish at death (CCC 366); she lives to God (Luke 20:38), who forgets no one (Isaiah 49:15); and on the last day He will raise her body too (CCC 998). Death does not cut the bond. The living can still reach the dead in love, with prayer, with a sign of gratitude, even with a request for pardon, and it is never in vain (*Spe Salvi* 48; CCC 958, 1032). And no one dies with no one left to speak for her: at every Mass the Church prays for the dead, even those whose faith God alone has known. How the last reconciliation between a victim and the one who wronged her is completed, the Church does not spell out. She says that grace "does not make wrong into right," and that the wrongdoer does not sit at the eternal banquet beside his victim as though nothing had happened (*Spe Salvi* 44).

*(Note for the author: "as though nothing had happened" is a direct phrase from* Spe Salvi *44 and may stay in quotation marks if preferred. The Eucharistic Prayer reference is deliberately paraphrased, not quoted, until the Missal wording is checked.)*

**R8 (W1(2), replaces from "Worth follows the call" to the end of (2)).**
> Her worth rests on what she is, a person made in God's image, and on the call He gives with her being: the call to communion with Him, which the Church calls the root reason for human dignity (GS 19; CCC 1700). It does not follow capacity, and an equal call explains an equal worth. Every child is someone's child; and wanted or not by anyone else, she was wanted by God before anyone saw her (Jeremiah 1:5).

**R9 (W1 coda).**
> The law binds because He is good; a breach of it can be forgiven because He is Someone.

**R10 (W2, after "a creature that misreads cliffs and lions does not last.").**
> (The Church has no quarrel with that, so long as it is not taken to explain the mind itself: the spiritual soul is not produced by matter but created by God for each one of us; CCC 366.)

**R11 (W3).**
> Part of that longing grasps, and both the Buddhist and the Catholic say grasping love must die. The Catholic adds that desire itself is not to be killed but healed (*Deus Caritas Est* 5). We part over what is left.

**R12 (W3, the hiddenness paragraph; replaces from "Some of the most disciplined seekers" to "…a summons to mission.").**
> Some of the most disciplined seekers in history, the Buddhist contemplatives, looked inward for a lifetime and report a peace beyond the self rather than a Person. The Church rejects nothing that is true and holy in their way, even as she proclaims Christ, in whom all may find the fullness of religious life (*Nostra Aetate* 2). Her own mystics speak of a darkness on the way to God too, though theirs is the night of faith before Someone, not the absence of anyone. The Church does not presume that such seekers are hiding from Him (CCC 847), though she knows that any of us can (CCC 29). She teaches that God can truly be known with certainty by the light of reason (CCC 36), yet that knowing Him by reason alone is hard for us as we are (CCC 37). He makes Himself known through the world He made and the conscience He gave (CCC 32–33), fully in Christ (*Dei Verbum* 4), and, for most of us first, through persons: a mother's love before a creed, a neighbor's mercy before a name (Matthew 25:37–40). He has entrusted us with making Him known to one another (CCC 848). And He does not withhold what is needed for salvation from anyone who, through no fault of his own, has not yet come to an explicit knowledge of Him and, by grace, tries to live a good life (*Lumen Gentium* 16). Whatever good and truth such a person has found, the Church receives as a preparation for the Gospel (LG 16). She hopes that he is already following a light without yet knowing its name, and that when he turns from a wrong out of love for the good, he is already turning toward the One who forgives. A child is loved long before he can say who loves him. That is a real answer, and it has a real cost: for many honest seekers the conscious knowing comes late, or not in this life. This life is where the choice is made (CCC 1021), and the Spirit, in ways known to God, offers every person the possibility of sharing in Christ's Paschal Mystery (GS 22). The Church does not hide that cost; she calls it a summons to mission.

**R13 (C, Buddha's Dhamma).**
> … and its ground for compassion toward every being, the Buddha's teaching that it is hard to find a being who has not at some time been one's mother (Saṃyutta Nikāya 15.14–19), is among the most beautiful anyone has offered. In its classical form it answers suffering by desert: what one suffers ripens from what one did before, in this life or an earlier one. Applied to the child, that answer costs A4, and the Buddha himself would not say that every pain comes from past deeds.

**R14 (C, A God who is Love, first sentence).**
> A God who is Love is also a stopping point, but not a brute one: He is the One whose very being is to be (Exodus 3:14; ST I q.3 a.4), who needs nothing outside Himself to explain Him.

**R15 (Evil, replaces the hell sentence).**
> And the Catholic faith adds a weight of its own. It teaches that hell is real and eternal (CCC 1035): a person can refuse God forever by his own free choice (CCC 1033). God predestines no one to that refusal (CCC 1037), and He desires all to be saved (1 Timothy 2:4). The Church sees in this the seriousness of freedom: a love that could never be refused would not be a gift (Chapter 4).

**R16 (Evil, *Second*).**
> Permitting what free creatures do is not doing it (Chapter 11); and the losses of a world of real causes God wills only as bound up with the good of the whole, never for their own sake (ST I q.19 a.9; q.49 a.2).

**R17 (Evil, replaces "and for a person who suffers, the good the Church hopes for is a good for her, not a price paid with her for someone else. No one is the price of someone else's good, not even in God's hands.").**
> And for a person who suffers, the good the Church hopes for includes a good for her: God never spends anyone as a mere means for others. That is not to deny that suffering can bear fruit for others. Christ's did. He laid down His life of His own accord (John 10:18), as a ransom for many (Mark 10:45), and the Father raised Him. Whoever joins her suffering to His shares in that fruitfulness (Colossians 1:24; *Salvifici Doloris* 19, 24). But what Christ gave was given, not taken, and it was not lost.

**R18 (Evil, end of *Second* and *Third*).**
> … For one weighing by reason alone, then, not seeing God's reasons is weak evidence that He has none. It is not no evidence: the pattern of pain in nature still weighs. *Third*, the God weighed here is not a designer watching from a distance. He entered suffering Himself: the eternal Son became man and suffered in His own flesh on the Cross. God, St. Bernard said, cannot suffer, but He can suffer with (*Spe Salvi* 39). Christian faith professes that He will raise the dead (CCC 998), and hopes in Him to wipe away every tear (Revelation 21:4; *Salvifici Doloris* 15, 25). … The question is not whether a distant designer fits this world, but whether a God who suffers with it and will not leave it in the ground does.

**R19 (Conclusion, insert after "converging and convincing (CCC 31)").**
> The Church teaches that arguments of this kind can bring a person to certainty (CCC 31), and that God can be known with certainty from the things He has made by the natural light of reason (CCC 36; First Vatican Council, *Dei Filius* ch. 2). This book offers the case for weighing, not because certainty is out of reach, but because each reader must reach it with his own reason, and many of us find the way hard (CCC 37).
