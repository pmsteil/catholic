# Argument Lab, Round 7 (final): Non-Naturalist Secular Moral Realism vs. v7

## 1. School and voice

Godless normative realism: Parfit, Enoch, Shafer-Landau, Wielenberg, Huemer, with Fine on essence and necessity, directed duties (Darwall, Wallace, Thompson), Kamm and Nagel on the shape of constraints, and McMahan as this school's voice on infants' moral status. Moral truths are objective, necessary, known by reflection, and need no God. This round the school checked whether v7 took its one Round 6 repair (R1, for S1-R6), then tested every sentence that changed from v6, above all the new conclusion, the A6 Christ and Kolbe sentence, and W1(2)'s narrowed right.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

1. **Justice.** What each person is owed, much of it owed *to* her, fixed by necessary truths about persons. *Holds; it posits its ground.*
2. **A hard case.** Re A (the conjoined twins): Mary may not be killed as a means; Jodie is not bound to keep sustaining her. *Coherent; it borrows justice and mercy in all but name (Rom 2:14–15).*
3. **Equal dignity.** Kind-based essentialism (worth follows the rational kind), or, for McMahan's wing, denied: infants are wronged, but less than persons. *The first holds but posits the measure; the second is consistent and pays A4, and after v7 it must say so openly.*
4. **Ultimate good.** An objective list lived with integrity; death ends it. *Coherent for a life; breaks on unity, and on the child who died before any of it reached her.*

### v7's new wording under pressure

**Exchange 1: does the grown man now reach McMahan's reader? (Held)**
*Realist (McMahan's voice):* I grant that the weak newborn was wronged no less than the strong one. I deny that either was wronged as gravely as the grown man, because he falls under the morality of respect and they fall under the morality of interests, and their time-relative interest in going on is weak.
*Defense:* Then you have chosen the text's second option: "or that it was a lesser wrong." The question the conclusion asks is which you are surer of: that the infant on the hillside was wronged no less than the man beside her, or the argument that says she was wronged less. The text no longer pretends you must grant the first; it asks you to choose, in front of the case.
*Realist rejoinder:* That is fair. McMahan himself calls the conclusion that killing an infant is less wrong than killing an adult a hard one, and accepts it as the cost of his account (paraphrase; verify, *The Ethics of Killing*, 2002). The comparison now puts that cost where it belongs. The "If ... then" is valid: the grown man has more of every ability she lacks, so if she was wronged no less, the gravity of the wrong did not track strength or mind.
*Result:* **Held.** S1-R6 is closed. The argument by elimination now has a contrast for each ground it eliminates.

**Exchange 2: the future-length confound. Does the grown man vary more than mind? (Held)**
*Realist:* The grown man differs from her not only in mind but in how much future he has to lose. A deprivation account (Marquis's kind; paraphrase) says the infant loses more. Then a reader could hold that her worth is graded by mind and that a larger loss evens the wrong out, so "wronged no less" would not show that her worth "never followed ... her mind."
*Defense:* Three replies. (a) No one the paragraph addresses holds that view. "The one who gives up A4" is Singer's or McMahan's reader, and both grade the wrong *down* for the infant; the reader who grants "wronged no less" on deprivation grounds, like Marquis, keeps A4. (b) The first consequent rests on the plain premise that how gravely someone is wronged by being left to die answers to what she is owed, and the reader is free to deny it, which is the third option the text offers ("you cannot know it"). (c) Where a reader puts the ground somewhere else, in her future rather than her nature, he is disputing the second step, and v7 now marks that step as "this book answers," not as what follows.
*Realist rejoinder:* Granted. The view that would break the first step (mind-graded worth, multiplied by loss, coming out exactly even) is built for the objection; no one holds it. And "her future" is close to the book's own answer, since a future like ours is what a nature of her kind carries.
*Result:* **Held.** No repair. "This book answers" is doing real work here.

**Exchange 3: the threshold view. Worth follows having a mind at all. (Held, at parity)**
*Realist:* A range-property view (Rawls's word; Carter's "opacity respect"; paraphrase, verify) says worth is equal above a threshold. If the threshold is sentience, or Regan's "subject of a life," the newborn is above it, she was wronged no less than the man, and her worth *does* follow her mind: the having of one, not its degree.
*Defense:* "Followed" in the conclusion means tracked, as the whole of A4 uses it ("whatever he can or cannot do"); a threshold view grants that her worth does not rise or fall with her mind, which is all the first consequent says. Where the ground lies (kind, or a low threshold of mind) is the second step, now labeled "this book answers," and argued in W1(2): "That she can suffer explains why harming her matters; it does not explain this either, for others can always suffer more." The godless realism row already records the standoff: "why worth follows kind and not ability, which it must take as given."
*Realist rejoinder:* Fair. The threshold reader is also not the one who "gives up A4": he holds it and extends it. His dispute is with "it follows what she is," and the text owns that as its answer. That is the recorded standoff on the measure, not a fault in the inference.
*Result:* **Held, at parity.**

**Exchange 4: Kolbe and the closeness problem. (Held)**
*Realist (Kamm's and Bennett's line, paraphrased):* v7 says a man "may not aim at his own death, by his own hand or another's," yet praises Kolbe, who "offered himself in a condemned man's place." He intended that he, not Gajowniczek, be the one they killed. Intending to be the one killed looks like aiming at one's death by another's hand. Double effect has a well-known closeness problem; this is it.
*Defense:* Apply the test the tradition itself uses (Anscombe, Finnis; paraphrase): would his purpose have failed if the death had not come? Kolbe's purpose was that the other man live. Had the guards taken Kolbe in his place and then spared both, nothing Kolbe aimed at would have failed. The death served his purpose only through the captors' own wicked will, not as his means. The man who asks to be killed to end his suffering fails in his purpose if he is not killed: there the death *is* the means. And the second test in the sentence stands on its own: "his asking never makes it right for anyone to kill him." That is true of Kolbe's killers, who did a grave wrong, and it no longer reads, as v6's "no one may kill him because he asks" could, as though Kolbe's asking were what the killers acted on.
*Realist rejoinder:* The counterfactual test is the right one and Kolbe passes it. The closeness problem bites on cases where the effect is constitutive of the means (the classic hysterotomy and craniotomy debates), and A6 does not rest on any of those.
*Result:* **Held.** The censor's rewording is an improvement against this school too.

**Exchange 5: "so Christ, who laid down His life of His own accord." Does Christ aim at His death? (Held, at parity)**
*Realist:* Christ is offered as an instance of the rule that a man may lay down his life but "may not aim at his own death." Yet the book says His death is "a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45). If the death is the means of redemption, He aimed at it as a means, and the example breaks the rule it illustrates.
*Defense:* The text says what CCC 609 says: He "freely accepted" His Passion and death, and the sentence's verb is "accept the death that comes, even at the hands of those who wrongly kill him." On the Church's account what redeems is His love and obedience, offered unto death and shown in it, not the death taken as a thing aimed at in itself; Aquinas says the satisfaction lay in the love with which He suffered, and that He was the cause of His death by not hindering what His persecutors did, though He could have (ST III q.47 a.1; q.48 a.2; paraphrase, verify). And Christ alone falls also under the line W1(2) already draws: "He who gives life, and can give it back, may call it home." He said, "I have power to lay it down, and I have power to take it again" (John 10:18, RSV).
*Realist rejoinder:* So Christ fits the rule by the will's aim, and exceeds it by a power no man has. That is coherent, and it is the book's claim, labeled as such. Whether "love unto death" can be separated from "death as means" is the old dispute about double effect, at parity.
*Result:* **Held, at parity.** No repair.

**Exchange 6: "no number of others has any right to take her life." (Held, better than v6)**
*Realist:* In Round 6 I tested "any right over her life" against weighed side effects and a city's call to arms (CCC 2310). Does the narrower "right to take her life" still carry the argument?
*Defense:* It carries it better. Side effects and a duty to defend the nation involve risk and loss, not a taking. "No one may take what is not his to take, even to keep others from taking more" is Nagel's and Kamm's agent-centered constraint, with a ground given instead of a status posited. "Her" is the innocent; the guilty aggressor and legitimate defense (CCC 2263–2265) are not in view.
*Result:* **Held.** The standoff (posited standing against given standing) is unchanged.

**Exchange 7: "whatever voice he claims to have heard in His name, may claim it for himself." (Held)**
*Realist (Wielenberg's worry):* Abraham claimed to have heard God's voice. Does the sentence say he did wrong, against Aquinas, who holds that one who kills at God's command does not sin (ST I-II q.100 a.8 ad 3)?
*Defense:* It says no one may claim the right *for himself*, which is CCC 2258's shape, and that "that right is never ours." On Aquinas's account the one who acts at a true command claims nothing for himself; the death is God's sentence and he is its instrument. "Claims to have heard" aims at pretenders, which is all the text needs.
*Result:* **Held.** The Round 6 note for the censor stands: if the book ever discusses Abraham, give Aquinas's account.

**Exchange 8: "the ordinary care owed to the sick, food and water among it, may not be withdrawn." (Minor)**
*Realist:* Take the dying patient whose body can no longer take in food or water. Is it wrong to stop? As written, the sentence says so, without qualification. If the Church says otherwise, the text has made the trust stricter than the Church does, and a realist who accepts the trust can still reject the stricter rule.
*Defense:* *Samaritanus Bonus* V.3 itself, cited in the same parenthesis, holds that nutrition and hydration are obligatory to the extent and for as long as they serve their own end, nourishing the patient (paraphrase; verify wording on vatican.va), and the CDF's 2007 commentary on its responses names the exceptions (the body cannot assimilate them, or they cause significant harm; verify). "The ordinary care owed" can be read as already carrying that limit, since care that no longer nourishes is no longer care that is owed.
*Result:* **Holds on a careful reading; Minor wording point (M2).** It does not touch the argument: withdrawing what no longer nourishes does not aim at death.

**Exchange 9: the A heading's "the line." (Minor)**
*Realist:* The heading now says many "deny the Church's teaching that the line holds at every number and against a man's own request." No line has been named yet; the reader meets it in A4 and A6.
*Result:* **Minor read-aloud point (M1).** The meaning is recoverable; one phrase fixes it.

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**Fatal against v7 as written:** none.

**Serious against v7 as written:** none. S1-R6 is closed by the new conclusion: the strong newborn holds kind and mind fixed and varies strength; the grown man holds kind fixed and has more of every ability she lacks; so if she was wronged no less than either, the gravity of the wrong did not follow strength or mind, and the step from there to "what she is" is labeled as this book's answer. The two strongest attempts to reopen it (Exchanges 2 and 3) fail against the text as written: the first relies on a view no one holds, and the second disputes the labeled second step, not the inference.

**As written: NONE.**

**Minor:**
- **M1. A heading, "the line."** Forward reference with no antecedent; recoverable, but a read-aloud stumble.
- **M2. W1(2), food and water.** "May not be withdrawn" lacks the limit *Samaritanus Bonus* V.3 itself gives (for as long as they nourish). Read with "the ordinary care owed," it holds; said plainly, it would be exact. (Flag for the censor.)
- For the censor, not this school, carried from Round 6: if Abraham is ever discussed, give Aquinas's "God's sentence" account; SCG III.122 still needs a critical text and its chapter's subject checked.

**Misstatements of this school:** none. The godless realism row, W2's first rival answer, and the W1 frame all state the school as its best defenders would.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**
- *The dead:* the tragic view (a real debt, forever unanswered) against "she is still someone."
- *Where necessity lives:* in essences, nowhere at all (Parfit), or first in a Mind. Our side's edge is breadth, not fewer axioms, and v7 says so.
- *Reach:* reliable general reasoning against the access problem shared with mathematics.
- *Why the line cannot be waived:* the realist allows waiver; the Church does not. "Keeping, not disposal" explains it only for one who grants the datum.
- *Posited standing against given standing:* Kamm's inviolability as a status, or "no number of others has any right to take her life" because her life is a gift.
- *The measure of equal worth:* kind, or a low threshold of mind, or a future like ours. After v7 this is a dispute over the labeled answer, not a gap in the inference. Our side leads narrowly: kind explains why the threshold falls where it does; a threshold of mind must posit it.
- *McMahan's choice:* the comparison forces it; it cannot make it for him. Most readers are surer of the infant than of the account.
- *Love unto death and death as means* (Christ, Kolbe): the double-effect dispute, with the counterfactual test on our side.
- *Whether wrongness binds of itself* or needs a Lawgiver: Anscombe's question, stated openly.
- *Natural evil:* the fawn and the parasite, carried as weight. Parity only by concession.

## 7. New insights; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
1. **The elimination now works because the second contrast dominates.** The grown man has more of every ability the infant lacks, so "wronged no less than him" rules out strength and mind together; the strong newborn isolates strength for the reader who wants it isolated. Run the same check on any other "her worth never followed X" sentence in the chapter.
2. **"This book answers" is what lets the conclusion survive rival grounds.** Everything that follows "and then" is our answer, open to the threshold theorist and the deprivation theorist; everything before it is an inference they must grant once they grant "wronged no less." Keep that seam visible when the case is condensed for the chapter.
3. **The chosen-death line now has two tests, each stated in the will's terms:** what the will aims at ("may not aim at his own death") and what asking can do ("never makes it right for anyone to kill him"). Kolbe passes the counterfactual test (his purpose would not fail if he were spared); the requester does not.
4. **Christ fits the rule by "accept" (CCC 609) and exceeds it by John 10:18.** The book can hold both without strain, because W1(2) already says who may "call it home."
5. **"Right to take her life" is the right width.** It lets the book weigh side effects and a nation's call to defense (CCC 2310) without bending the absolute.
6. **Chapter text (open since Round 2):** chapter_12_current.md still has "something only a person can do" and "Only a Someone can." Directed-duty realism grants that persons lay claims, namely the victim. Rework both along the W1 frame when the chapter is redlined.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "Wronged no less than a strong newborn left beside her, or a grown man left there to die."
- "If she was wronged no less than either, her worth never followed her strength or her mind."
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it."
- "He may not aim at his own death, by his own hand or another's, and his asking never makes it right for anyone to kill him."
- "No number of others has any right to take her life."
- "God says no to the wound and yes to her."
- "A wrong done to a person needs forgiving."

**To verify before use:** *Samaritanus Bonus* V.3 on nutrition and hydration (vatican.va); CDF Responses and Commentary (2007); ST III q.47 a.1 and q.48 a.2 (newadvent) if the Christ line is defended in the chapter; McMahan, *The Ethics of Killing* (2002), on the time-relative interest account and his acceptance of the infant conclusion; SCG III.122 against a critical text. All non-magisterial material above is paraphrased.
