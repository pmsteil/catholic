# Round 7 (final): Kantian Constructivism vs. v7

## 1. School and voice

The opponent speaks for Kant (*Groundwork*, the second and third *Critiques*, *Metaphysics of Morals*, *Religion*, the Collins lectures) and his best modern readers: Korsgaard, Wood, Herman, O'Neill and Hill. The moral law is the law of a free will as such. Humanity is an end in itself, with dignity and not price. God is a postulate of practical reason, never the ground of obligation. He logged "As written: NONE" against v4, v5 and v6. In this final round he checks his one Round 6 repair and tests every sentence v7 changed where it touches Kant. That means the Kant row's new impunity clause, the W1(2) parenthesis and Abraham clause, A4's new parenthesis on asking, A6's Christ and Kolbe wording, "a mere means against her own good," and the conclusion's two contrasts.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

1. **Justice.** He starts from one innate right: freedom from being forced by another's choice, so far as that freedom fits everyone's freedom under universal law (*Doctrine of Right* 6:237). *This holds and borrows nothing.*
2. **Hard case.** The murderer. Kant requires execution (6:333) and allows clemency only for crimes against the sovereign (6:337). *This is coherent, but it is justice with mercy torn away. His heirs bring mercy back as respect for the convict, and his own placability (6:460–461) puts the pardon in the victim's hands.*
3. **Equal dignity.** Humanity, as capable of morality, has a worth above all price (4:434–435), and the vicious keep it (6:463). *This holds for the enemy. It reaches the newborn only through a practical idea of personhood (6:280–281), which is the "stretch." Kant himself failed the newborn at 6:336.*
4. **Final end.** The highest good is virtue crowned with proportionate happiness. *Kant borrows openly by postulating God and immortality. His secular heirs drop the postulates and lose the dead.*

### Exchange 1: Is "regarding her in two ways" now accurate to Kant?

- *Kantian:* I check my own wording against the text. At 6:417–418 Kant says that the human being can recognize a duty to himself without contradiction only because he is thought of "in two senses": as a natural being with reason (*homo phaenomenon*), and as a personality endowed with inner freedom (*homo noumenon*), who is the one able to bind. The Doctrine of Right says the same: the humanity in one's own person gives the law (6:239; see 6:335). "Her free reason binding her as a natural being" names the binder and the bound correctly. "Two ways" keeps the one person that Wood and Korsgaard insist on.
- *Patrick's side:* Then the parenthesis is fair to Kant and still marks the place where the book parts from him. Kant answers the paradox of the self-release from within the person. The book answers it from the Giver: her life is given "to her keeping and not to her disposal."
- *Kantian:* Agreed. One note for the read-aloud pass: the run of commas ("and, so that she cannot release herself, regarding her") is stiff when spoken. That is a matter of style, not of substance, and I log nothing.
- **Verdict: Held.** The only Kantian wording point left from Round 6 is closed.

### Exchange 2: The Kant row's new clause, "yet not as impunity: the one forgiven must still repair what he can (CCC 1459)"

- *Kantian:* "Impunity" is my word (*impunitas criminis*, 6:337). In my sense it means that the *punishment* goes unexacted. Restitution is a different thing. It belongs to what the wrongdoer owes the one he wronged, while punishment is what public justice owes the crime itself (paraphrase of the Doctrine of Right's division between private right and the right to punish; verify). A thief who returns the horse has made restitution, and he still goes unpunished if the court remits his sentence. Your colon gives restitution as the reason God's forgiveness is "not as impunity." By my definition that reason does not reach the point. The clause asserts more than its stated ground supports.
- *Patrick's side:* The claim is true, and the paragraph cited already holds the stronger ground. CCC 1459 says more than "repair the harm." It goes on: absolution "does not remedy all the disorders sin has caused." The sinner must still "make satisfaction for" or "expiate" his sins, and this satisfaction is called penance. That is the penal side. Behind it stands the Church's teaching that forgiveness takes away the eternal punishment of sin while the temporal punishment remains (CCC 1472–1473). So the Church denies impunity in your sense too, not only in the sense of restitution.
- *Kantian:* Then say so, and in the same paragraph. Name satisfaction beside repair and my objection is answered in my own terms. It also fits my *Religion* better than you might expect. There I treat the change of heart as itself bearing the suffering the old man deserved, so that justice is not simply waived (6:72–74; paraphrase, verify). Satisfaction is the Church's name for punishment that is not waived.
- **Verdict: Held with repair (Minor).** The clause is true. Its stated ground, restitution alone, does not meet Kant's meaning of impunity. Adding "and make satisfaction for his sins" closes the gap from the paragraph already cited (§6, repair 1).

### Exchange 3: Does God really do "what Kant's ruler may not"?

- *Kantian:* A sharper question. My ruler *may* remit punishment for crimes against himself. Your W1 says that every wrong is "also, and before all else, an offense against Him" (CCC 1440). Then every sin falls under the half of my rule that *permits* remission. On that reading God does what my ruler may do, not what he may not.
- *Patrick's side:* Both descriptions are true, at different depths. At the surface the reader sees, God forgives murder, and your ruler may never pardon a murderer. At the depth, what God remits is the offense against Himself and its eternal punishment, and He remits nothing that is hers: not her pardon, not the repair, not the satisfaction. The sentence's last clause, "God does not take her place," says exactly that.
- *Kantian:* Then your God keeps my rule whole, both halves, and the row could say so. That is a gain for you, not a defect. The row as written is true and not misleading, and "half of W1(1)" remains the accurate claim about my *ruler*. I leave it as an insight (§7).
- **Verdict: Held.** No repair is needed.

### Exchange 4: W1(2)'s new Abraham wording: "no one, whatever voice he claims to have heard in His name, may claim it for himself (CCC 2258)"

- *Kantian:* v6 said that no voice "can make it ours to take." v7 borrows CCC 2258's phrase "claim ... for himself." The fanatic will slip through that phrase. He never claims the right *for himself*; he says God acts through him as an instrument. Read alone, the new clause could leave open the door that v6 closed.
- *Patrick's side:* It is not read alone. The two sentences before it say: "we, who can do neither, may not take it. That right is never ours." That is unconditional; it forbids the act, whatever the agent calls himself. The new clause adds only that a claimed voice changes nothing. "For himself" is the Catechism's own wording, and it keeps the harmony with Aquinas that this school granted in Round 6. A death God commands is His own taking, and it never becomes a creature's right.
- *Kantian:* Granted. "May not take it" closes the practical door. The rest is our old theoretical standoff: you leave the Giver free, and I deny that such a command could ever be known as His (*Conflict of the Faculties* 7:63). Neither of us draws the knife.
- **Verdict: Held (parity).**

### Exchange 5: The conclusion's two contrasts, and A4's own "equal worth is not equal duty"

- *Kantian:* The new contrasts are an improvement. The strong newborn holds age and relation fixed and varies strength, and the grown man varies mind. But your A4 teaches that "equal worth is not equal duty." My Doctrine of Right gives the newborn a strict claim on her parents' care (6:280–281). It gives a grown stranger no juridical claim to be rescued; beneficence is a wide duty of virtue. So a reader might judge the infant wronged "no less" than the grown man, even more, because of what her parents owed her, not because of her worth. Then "no less than either" would not yet show that "her worth never followed her mind." The duty would be doing the work, and A4 says duty is not worth.
- *Patrick's side:* The wrong in view is not a failure of care. It is exposure: the ancient world put infants on the hillside to be rid of them, a killing done by leaving. "A grown man left there to die" is put out there by the same hands. That is a breach of the duty every person owes every other, not to kill the innocent, and it does not wait on kinship or on any positive duty. With the duty held equal, the contrast isolates worth. The strong newborn "left beside her" removes even the question of who owed her what.
- *Kantian:* On that reading, which the hillside and "left there to die" carry, the elimination is sound. It also convicts Kant once more. At 6:336 he let the mother's shame lessen the crime of killing her newborn, and by his own principle that dignity has no degrees, he was wrong. My best heirs answer the question as you do: Wood speaks of respecting rational nature even where it is undeveloped, and Kain argues that a being of rational kind has full moral status (paraphrase; verify). They then say that "what she is" is a being of rational nature. "This book answers" leaves room for that, so the reply is honestly scoped.
- **Verdict: Held.** The contrasts work as written. A reader who takes "left there to die" as a failure to rescue would reopen the duty question, so "put out there to die" is an optional sharpening and not a needed repair (§6, optional).

### Exchange 6: "Never spends her as a mere means against her own good": a narrowed Formula of Humanity?

- *Kantian:* My Formula of Humanity forbids using anyone *merely* as a means, and O'Neill and Korsgaard read it as forbidding a use the other could not possibly consent to, *including* manipulation "for her own good." W1(2) now says God never spends her "as a mere means against her own good." Read as two conditions, it seems to allow a use as a mere means that serves her good, which is exactly the paternalism my heirs condemn.
- *Patrick's side:* The floor for us is unchanged and unqualified. A4 says she is "never to be used as a mere means," full stop. The qualifier appears only where God is the subject, and there it explains why He never treats her as a mere means: He "willed her for her own sake" (GS 24), so whatever He does with her is ordered to her. It also had to be said, because v6's "never spends her as a means" denied what the book itself affirms in the Evil section, that a person's suffering joined to Christ's bears fruit for others (Col 1:24).
- *Kantian:* That is my own picture of the head of the kingdom of ends, who has no need or interest set against any member (4:433–434). The qualifier does not lower the bar for creatures. It states God's case.
- **Verdict: Held.**

### Exchange 7: A4's new parenthesis, the A heading, and A6 on asking

- *Kantian:* A4 now says plainly that the Church denies a man's asking could ever make it right to kill him (CCC 2277; EV 65–66), and the A heading names "every number and against a man's own request" as the Church's teaching. I hold both lines. Justice has no price in lives (6:332; "fiat iustitia," 8:378–379), and one may not dispose of the humanity in oneself to escape pain (6:422–423). A6's new dissenters' question, "why may he not ask for death?", is the one my heirs actually ask (Hill and Cholbi are more permissive; Velleman argues the Kantian case against). The answer A6 gives, "may not aim at his own death," is close to my test of whether one's death is made the means. Kolbe still falls among my open casuistical questions (Curtius, chosen martyrdom, 6:423–424), and A6 decides it as "this book's claims."
- *Patrick's side:* The parenthesis now carries the Church's denial, so "without his asking" no longer reads as if asking could make it right. "Accept the death that comes, even at the hands of those who wrongly kill him: so Christ, who laid down His life of His own accord" fits John 10:18 and CCC 609.
- *Kantian:* Nothing here misstates Kant. The heading puts the dissenters where they are, and Kant is not among them.
- **Verdict: Held.**

### Exchange 8: Smaller checks on v7's other new wording

- **A2 and W1(1), "a wrong done to a person needs forgiving."** This is better than "a wronged person needs forgiving," and it matches placability (6:460–461). *Held.*
- **W1(2), CCC 2278–2279 with ordinary care and *Samaritanus Bonus* V.3.** Kant would accept the line between intending and foreseeing here, and the line about ordinary care is not about him. *Held.*
- **"No number of others has any right to take her life."** It is narrower and correct. Kant's state may execute the murderer (6:333), but she is innocent. *Held.*
- **W3, Smith (TMS III.2.33), "the longing alone settles nothing."** Kant's court of conscience before an ideal Judge (6:438–440) remains an optional companion. *Held.*
- **Flourishing row, "rather than holding it in trust."** Kant agrees against owning one's life (Collins, paraphrase; verify). *Held.*
- **Kant row, the unchanged sentences.** No author of the law, not even God (Collins 27:283; 6:227); 4:459; *Religion* 6:98–99; rational faith counted as a clue. These were all accurate in Round 6 and are unchanged. *Held.*

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**As written: NONE.**

No Fatal or Serious objection from this school stands against v7 as written. v7 took this school's one Round 6 repair word for word, and it is accurate to 6:417–418 and 6:239. Its new wording breaks nothing for Kant:
- The impunity clause is true. Its stated reason is incomplete in Kant's terms, but the same cited paragraph (CCC 1459) answers him. That is a Minor wording point (below).
- "May claim it for himself" does not reopen the fanatic's door, because "we ... may not take it. That right is never ours" stands just before it.
- "A mere means against her own good" applies only to God and leaves A4's floor for us unqualified.
- The conclusion's two contrasts isolate worth once "left there to die" is read, as the hillside invites, as killing by exposure.

**Minor (wording only; the argument stands without it):**
1. **Kant row, "yet not as impunity: the one forgiven must still repair what he can (CCC 1459)."** Kant's *impunitas criminis* (6:337) concerns *punishment*, not restitution. Restitution alone does not show that the crime is not left unpunished. The Church's stronger ground is in the same paragraph: satisfaction or expiation (CCC 1459; see 1472–1473). Name it (§6, repair 1).

**Optional (not a defect):** In the conclusion, "a grown man put out there to die" would make plain that the wrong compared is a killing by exposure, the duty every person owes every other, and not a failure to rescue. That keeps A4's "equal worth is not equal duty" from intruding (Exchange 5).

**Misstatements of this school:** None. The parenthesis "regarding her in two ways" is now accurate.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**
- **Postulate vs clue.** Kant holds the moral Ruler as rational faith; the book counts Him as a clue. The Kant row states this.
- **Self-standing dignity vs dignity given with being.** Both are stopping points.
- **Duties to oneself.** Kant answers with one person regarded in two ways; the book answers with a life given "to her keeping and not to her disposal."
- **Abraham.** Both refuse in practice. Only the book leaves the Giver free to call a life home, and never as a creature's right.
- **Remission by God.** The book says God remits what is His and keeps what is hers. Kant's *Religion* hopes for this remission; his *Doctrine of Right* forbids it to a human ruler.
- **Duties to God; form vs end; where necessity lives; certainty.** These are unchanged from Rounds 4–6.

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **God keeps Kant's clemency rule whole, not half.** Kant's ruler may remit what was done against himself and may never remit what was done to a subject. The Church says that every wrong is, before all else, an offense against God (CCC 1440). God forgives that offense whole, with its eternal punishment, and leaves untouched what is hers: her pardon, the repair, the satisfaction. Read this way, God does at full depth what Kant's ruler is *permitted* to do, and never what he is forbidden. The row's present wording ("does what Kant's ruler may not ... and does not take her place") is also true; this is a possible line for the chapter, not a repair.
- **Satisfaction is the Church's answer to Kant's word "impunity."** Kant separates restitution from punishment (verify). The Church keeps both after absolution: repair *and* satisfaction (CCC 1459), and temporal punishment remains after the eternal punishment is remitted (CCC 1472–1473). Kant's *Religion* has its own version: the converted man bears, in the pain of his change of heart, the suffering the old man deserved (6:72–74; paraphrase, verify).
- **Exposure is a killing, not a failure of care.** The conclusion is strongest when the reader sees the hillside as a deed aimed at death. Every account then owes the infant and the grown man the same duty, and the only remaining difference is worth.
- **Kant's best heirs can join the conclusion.** Wood's respect for undeveloped rational nature and Kain's case for the full moral status of beings of rational kind (paraphrase; verify) both answer the hillside with "what she is." They part from the book only over whether "what she is" needs a Giver.
- **Still to verify before the chapter:** 6:417–418 and 6:239 for the "two senses" (the wording now in the text); 6:337 for *impunitas criminis*; the Doctrine of Right's division of restitution from punishment; *Religion* 6:72–74; SCG III.122.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "Regarding her in two ways, her free reason binding her as a natural being." (v7; accurate to Kant)
- "That right is never ours." (v7)
- "God says no to the wound and yes to her." (v7)
- "Gave her life to her keeping and not to her disposal." (v6–v7)
- "Wronged no less than a strong newborn left beside her, or a grown man left there to die." (v7)
- "God forgives what is His to forgive, and never what is hers."

## 8. Score

**9.5/10 as written; 9.6 with repair 1.** v7 took this school's last repair word for word, and it now states Kant's answer to duties to oneself accurately. None of its new wording misstates Kant or opens a door he could walk through. What remains is one Minor clause, where restitution is given as the reason for "not as impunity" when Kant means punishment, and the same paragraph of the Catechism fixes it. Beyond that there is a set of honest standoffs that the text states fairly.
