# Round 7 (final): Expressivism / Quasi-Realism vs. v7

## 1. School and voice

The voices are Hume (*Treatise* Book 3; *Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals*, EPM; *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, EHU; *Dialogues*; *Natural History of Religion*; "Of Suicide"), Adam Smith (*Theory of Moral Sentiments*, TMS), the emotivists Ayer and Stevenson, and the quasi-realists Blackburn and Gibbard. Moral judgment expresses attitudes and plans, refined by information, consistency and sympathy; the quasi-realist then earns "true," "wrong of itself" and "owed to her" at first order. This round checks whether v7 took my Round 6 repair (the W3 Smith sentence), then tests v7's new wording sentence by sentence against v6: W3, the Feeling-refined row, A4 and A6, W1(2), the evil section's new lines, and the conclusion's two contrasts.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Justice.** Hume: conventions approved through sympathy with public utility; Smith: fitting resentment of injury done to the individual. *Coherent; breaks at the edge Hume admits* (the weak are owed gentle usage, not justice, EPM §3 pt 1). v7's conclusion now tests that edge directly (Ex. 3).

**2. A hard case.** The secret told after the friend's death (A2). Hume's account of promising misses the wrong; the quasi-realist finds it at first order by commitment. *Holds in result; the founding theory breaks on the reason.*

**3. Equal dignity.** A stance of equal regard, held as not depending on our attitudes (Blackburn's rigidified attitudes). *Coherent; declines to ground.* At parity on proof; parts from the book on the further why.

**4. The ultimate good.** Plural happiness and a life one can review with approval. *Coherent and smaller.* Hume's calm death shows W3's longing can be borne, not that it is answered.

### Exchange 1. W3's Smith sentence: is it fair to Smith now?

*Expressivist:* The sentence says Smith's appeal to the all-seeing Judge could explain "a longing to be judged truly," and then "It does not as easily explain a longing to be told, before we have earned anything, that it is good that we exist." Smith opens Part III by saying we desire not only to be loved but to be lovely (TMS III.2.1, paraphrase, verify). A desire to be loved is in his psychology from the start. Does "not as easily" undersell him?

*Patrick's side:* Smith's desire to be loved runs, in his own pairing, toward being *lovely*, the fit object of love: approval for what we are and do. The longing W3 names is to be told it is good that we exist *before* we have earned anything. That is not approbation of conduct or character, which is Smith's whole field. And the paths that do trace it (attachment, the wish not to die) are already granted in the sentence before, with "That tells us how we long, not whether there is Someone to long for, and the longing alone settles nothing." "It" in "It does not as easily explain" refers to "that way," Smith's appeal, not to every natural path.

*Expressivist:* Then the sentence claims less than I feared, and what it claims of Smith is true. Smith himself goes further toward you than you say: in the same chapter he writes (paraphrase, verify) that the virtuous man who doubts a future state cannot help wishing earnestly to believe it. v7 is right not to use that, since A7 forbids believing because it comforts.

*Verdict: HOLDS.* Insight in §7: Smith's own wish passage sits exactly where A7 puts it.

### Exchange 2. The Feeling-refined row, read beside the new W3

*Expressivist:* The row is unchanged from v6. W3 now cites TMS III.2.33 too. Do the two now say different things about Smith? And is "equal worth wins only by being ours" still fair, given Blackburn's reply that the wrongness of infanticide does not depend on our approving of it?

*Patrick's side:* The two places agree. W3 says Smith's appeal could explain a longing to be judged truly; the row says "On the account that keeps only feeling, both judges are no one," scoped to the account, not to Smith's private hope. On "only by being ours": the row has already granted that this school "can say nearly everything in A in its own voice, even 'her worth does not depend on us.'" The sentence is at the level of explanation ("in the end"), where the quasi-realist himself says the commitment is ours, rigidified, and that from within it the other heart is mistaken. "It can say which one sees truly only from within the heart we share" states Blackburn's own view of where judgment stands.

*Expressivist:* Agreed; this was settled in Round 4 and nothing in v7 disturbs it. The repetition of III.2.33 is a matter for the condensed chapter, not a defect.

*Verdict: HOLDS.* The further-why standoff stands as before.

### Exchange 3. The conclusion's two contrasts, and Hume's line between weak and strong

*Expressivist:* v6 compared the infant only with "a strong child," who is also a newborn. That comparison could not even reach Hume: in EPM §3 pt 1 justice fails toward those who cannot make us feel their resentment, and *every* newborn is in that state. v7 adds "a grown man left there to die." Now press it. The grown man knows what is being done to him: he dreads, he despairs, he knows he was betrayed. Anyone, even one who holds equal worth, will say his case is worse. So "wronged no less than ... a grown man" is false, and the inference "If she was wronged no less than either, her worth never followed ... her mind" has a false antecedent.

*Patrick's side:* The question is "wronged," not "harmed" or "worsened," and A2 was built for exactly this: "Making the world worse and wronging someone are not the same." In the letter case the dead woman suffers nothing and is still wronged; the stranger who finds the letter frustrates her wish just as much and wrongs no one. The grown man's dread is a further harm, and it makes his case worse. It does not make the wrong done to him in being abandoned to die a greater wrong, unless one holds that a person's claim not to be killed grows with what he can feel or foresee. That is the McMahan view the conclusion names as one of the arguments "that says ... it was a lesser wrong," and the reader is asked which he is surer of.

*Expressivist:* Fair. With A2 in place, the grown man varies mind and strength together while holding the wrong fixed, and he now meets Hume where Hume actually drew the line: the man who could hold us to account, set beside the infant who never can. Hume's answer, that she is owed gentle usage under the laws of humanity but not justice, is precisely "a lesser wrong," and it is named and left to the reader. The quasi-realist who says in his own voice that she was wronged no less can then say "it follows what she is" at first order, and the text labels that step "this book answers," which is honest.

*Verdict: HOLDS, and stronger than v6.* The Realist's Serious item is repaired, and the repair brings this school's founder into the comparison for the first time.

### Exchange 4. Christ "of His own accord" and "Of Suicide"

*Expressivist:* A6 now says a man may "accept the death that comes, even at the hands of those who wrongly kill him: so Christ, who laid down His life of His own accord (John 10:18; CCC 609)." Hume's argument in "Of Suicide" was that a man who lays his life down of his own accord does no more than turn nature's course. If Christ is the exemplar and He laid it down of His own accord, why is the man who asks for death not doing the same?

*Patrick's side:* Read John 10:18 whole (RSV): "No one takes it from me, but I lay it down of my own accord. I have power to lay it down, and I have power to take it again." That is the very power W1(2) reserves to God: "He who gives life, and can give it back, may call it home; we, who can do neither, may not take it." Christ's "own accord" is the Lord of life acting; what A6 asks of a man is the other half of the sentence, to "accept the death that comes," and the guards and executioners "wrongly kill him." Christ did not kill Himself, and those who killed Him were not made right by His freedom (CCC 609: He freely accepted His Passion and death).

*Expressivist:* Then the passage is consistent with itself. That a man's life is held in trust and not owned is still exactly where Hume and the Church part; it is not a new gap.

*Verdict: HELD at parity.* Insight in §7: John 10:18 and the trust sentence in W1(2) now explain each other.

### Exchange 5. Kolbe and "his asking never makes it right"

*Expressivist:* In Round 6 the censor found that "no one may kill him because he asks" collided with Kolbe, who asked to take a condemned man's place and was then killed. v7: "he may not aim at his own death, by his own hand or another's, and his asking never makes it right for anyone to kill him." Sidgwick's heirs still deny that aim against foresight carries moral weight; Kolbe foresaw his death with certainty.

*Patrick's side:* The collision is gone. Kolbe's offer did not make the killing right; the guards wrongly killed him; and he aimed at another man's life, not at his own death. Whether the aim carries the weight the book gives it is a dispute v7 places openly: "Those are this book's claims, argued in W1(2), not shared ones." And A6 now gives the dissenter his real reason in his own words: "if he may lay down his life for others, as a firefighter does (John 15:13), why may he not ask for death?"

*Expressivist:* That question is Hume's, put fairly. The answer it gets (the aim, and the one who kills) is the book's, and labeled as such.

*Verdict: HOLDS.* Aim against foresight stays an honest standoff.

### Exchange 6. W1(2) and ordinary care: Hume's reductio comes back

*Expressivist:* v7 adds: "but the ordinary care owed to the sick, food and water among it, may not be withdrawn (CCC 2278–2279; *Samaritanus Bonus* V.3); what the trust forbids is that her death be the thing aimed at." My Round 6 concession rested on the trust forbidding one aim only, not every turn given to nature's course. But "food and water ... may not be withdrawn" with no condition forbids a turn given to nature's course whatever the aim: it would oblige feeding a dying body that can no longer take food in. That is the form of the trust that "Of Suicide" refutes, and it no longer follows from "what the trust forbids is that her death be the thing aimed at."

*Patrick's side:* The Church does not hold the unconditional form. *Samaritanus Bonus* V.3 (checked on vatican.va this round) says nutrition and hydration are basic care, owed insofar as and until they demonstrably attain their purpose, and that they should be suspended when the patient's body cannot absorb or metabolize them; artificial provision is owed where it does not cause harm or intolerable suffering. Withdrawing food from someone whose body can still take it, without burden, is an omission that "of itself" causes death (CCC 2277), and with no burden to relieve, nothing is left to aim at but the death. So the doctrine coheres with "what the trust forbids is that her death be the thing aimed at." And the word "owed" in v7 already restricts the clause to care that is owed.

*Expressivist:* Then the doctrine meets the reductio and the sentence nearly does. But a reader who sees "food and water among it, may not be withdrawn" will not hear "owed" as carrying SB's condition, and the summary clause that follows ("what the trust forbids is ... aimed at") will look contradicted. Add the condition in plain words.

*Verdict: HELD WITH REPAIR (Minor, wording and doctrinal precision).* §6, R1. The Church's position answers the reductio at parity; only the compression loses it.

### Exchange 7. "Whatever voice he claims to have heard in His name" and Abraham

*Expressivist:* v7 recast the clause on CCC 2258: "That right is never ours, and no one, whatever voice he claims to have heard in His name, may claim it for himself." The *Natural History of Religion* (paraphrase, verify) charges that religion puts the word of a deity in the place of humanity. And Genesis 22 praises a man for being ready to kill an innocent at a voice. Aquinas even says Abraham did not consent to murder, because his son was to be slain at the command of the Lord of life and death (ST I-II q.100 a.8 ad 3, paraphrase, verify). So the book's own tradition allows a voice that makes killing the innocent not murder.

*Patrick's side:* The sentence keeps that line where Aquinas keeps it. "He who gives life, and can give it back, may call it home": the Lord of life is not under the trust. What v7 denies is that any man may act on a voice *he claims* to have heard: "may claim it for himself" is CCC 2258's own wording. Abraham was stopped (Genesis 22:12), and the Church reads the story as a test of faith and a figure of the Father who did not spare His own Son (CCC 2572, verify paragraph). And W1's closing paragraph adds the test the *Natural History* asks for: "He did not choose whether cruelty is evil." A voice that commands cruelty refutes itself on this account. The fairness rule holds too: secular authorities have licensed killing in the name of the nation and the future.

*Expressivist:* Whether God *could* command what Aquinas says He could is a question about the conquest texts that other schools have already logged as a standoff. For any human claimant, the clause is now as strict as Hume could ask.

*Verdict: HELD at parity.* (Standoff already on the list: the conquest texts.)

### Exchange 8. The evil section's new lines: "God says no to the wound and yes to her," and "faith adds"

*Expressivist (as Philo):* "God says no to the wound." But the next paragraph says that in willing the order of the whole He wills some things to be naturally corrupted (ST I q.19 a.9), and that He permits moral evil. A "no" that does not prevent is a word, not a deed. And "faith adds that He permits no evil from which He will not draw a good" is a premise reason does not grant.

*Patrick's side:* The "no" is said where the text puts it, against "a yes said to whatever happens," the yes of an indifferent source or of *amor fati* that must affirm the wound along with her. The book does not say the "no" prevents: "He did not keep her from what was done to her; that weight is on the other side of the scale." And "faith adds" is labeled as faith, set after "so far as reason can see," and cited (CCC 311, 312, 324, 412). Reason is not asked to grant it; it is told what the Church holds.

*Expressivist:* Then both lines are honest. One read-aloud point: "though that good is never the evil's wage" is hard to hear. Some readers will hear Romans 6:23 ("the wages of sin is death") and take it to mean the good is not what the evil earns; others will take "wage" as the price paid. Both readings are true, so nothing is misstated, but the image costs a second reading.

*Verdict: HOLDS.* Optional wording in §6, R2.

### Exchange 9. A4's parenthesis and the A heading

*Expressivist:* A4 now names the asking: "and whether a man's own asking could ever make it right to kill him, which the Church denies (CCC 2277; *Evangelium Vitae* 65–66), A6 names as this book's claims." And the A heading now lists "the Church's teaching that the line holds at every number and against a man's own request" as what many deny. Hume denies both.

*Patrick's side:* That is why both are now scoped by name. The clause in A4 that the shared reading carries ("nor 'for his own good' without his asking") is one Hume shares: "Of Suicide" defends a man's own choice, never another's choice for him.

*Verdict: HOLDS.* v6's implication that asking might change things is now cancelled in so many words.

### Exchange 10 (our side presses). Can this school sign the conclusion?

*Patrick's side:* Blackburn will say "she was wronged no less than a grown man" in his own voice. Then the conclusion has done nothing against you.

*Expressivist:* Correct, and it does not claim to: it is addressed to "the one who gives up A4." I can also say "it follows what she is" and "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it." What divides us is the C row's question, whether a further why exists, and the case's claim against me is to explain more, not to compel. The text has said that since Round 4.

*Verdict: Not a defect.* The standoff on the further why is honest.

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**As written: NONE.**

No Fatal or Serious objection from this school stands against v7 as written. My Round 6 repair was taken and improved. The new wording this school can press hardest (Christ "of His own accord," Kolbe and the asking, the CCC 2258 clause, the evil section's "no to the wound" and "faith adds," and the conclusion's grown man) either holds or meets this school at a parity it already concedes. The one new fault is a compression in W1(2), and the Church's own text answers it.

**Minor (wording; changes no conclusion)**
1. **W1(2), ordinary care:** "food and water among it, may not be withdrawn," stated without *Samaritanus Bonus* V.3's own condition (owed while the body can take them in and they do not cause harm or intolerable suffering), reads as a rule that holds whatever the aim, and so seems to contradict "what the trust forbids is that her death be the thing aimed at" (Ex. 6). The doctrine coheres; the sentence should show it.
2. **Evil ¶2, second point (optional, read-aloud):** "though that good is never the evil's wage" needs a second reading (Ex. 8).

**Misstatements of this school:** none. (Hume's EPM §3 pt 1, the sensible knave, TMS III.2.33 and the quasi-realists' first-order voice are all stated fairly.)

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- **Is there a further "why"?** The quasi-realist's first-order answer against the book's demand for a ground (C row; W1 closing; Ex. 10).
- **Whose life is it?** "Of Suicide" against life held "to her keeping and not to her disposal," drawn at the aim alone.
- **Aim against foresight.** Sidgwick and Hume's heirs deny it carries weight; the book holds it does and scopes it as its claim (A6; Ex. 5).
- **God's causing and the creature's defect.** EHU §8's dilemma against being/defect; Hume called the second horn beyond philosophy.
- **What God could command.** Aquinas on Abraham against Hume's *Natural History*; for every human claimant the book's line is absolute (Ex. 7; the conquest texts).
- **The debt to the dead.** Road 2 against road 3.
- **The coherent dissenter** (the knave). Neither side compels him; they differ on what he is missing.
- **The indifferent source** (*Dialogues* XI). Conceded to fit the pattern of pain; answered only by the whole case.
- **The longing.** Hume's calm death against W3: a longing that can be borne is not thereby answered; Smith's own wish to believe is no evidence either (A7).

## 7. New insights and lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **The grown man brings Hume into the conclusion.** v6's strong child could not separate Hume's weak from Hume's strong, since every newborn is powerless. The grown man is the one who could hold us to account; set beside the infant who never can, he puts Hume's EPM middle position (gentle usage, not justice) in front of the reader in Hume's own terms.
- **A2 is what makes the grown-man contrast fair.** The grown man dreads and knows; his case is *worse*. The conclusion asks whether she was *wronged* less, and A2's letter case has already shown that wronging does not follow what the victim feels. Without A2 the contrast would be confounded; with it, it is exact. In the condensed chapter, keep A2's letter case before the conclusion.
- **John 10:18 and the trust explain each other.** "I have power to lay it down, and I have power to take it again" (RSV) is the power W1(2) reserves to God: "He who gives life, and can give it back, may call it home." Christ's "own accord" is the Lord of life's; what a man may do is accept the death others wrongly give. That closes the cheapest form of "Of Suicide" against A6.
- **Ordinary care and the aim.** The Church's own condition on food and water (owed while the body can take them in) is what keeps the obligation from becoming the "turn given to nature's course" that Hume's reductio needs. With the condition, withdrawing food that still nourishes and does not burden leaves nothing to aim at but the death (CCC 2277, "of itself or by intention").
- **Smith wished to believe, and v7 is right not to use it.** In TMS III.2 (paraphrase, verify) Smith says the virtuous doubter cannot help wishing a future state were true. That is a fine witness to W3's longing and exactly what A7 forbids counting as evidence. v7 counts the longing as a clue to fit, which is all Smith's wish can bear.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "That tells us how we long, not whether there is Someone to long for, and the longing alone settles nothing." (v7)
- "His asking never makes it right for anyone to kill him." (v7)
- "If he may lay down his life for others, why may he not ask for death?" (v7; the dissent in its own voice, and why the answer is fair)
- "God says no to the wound and yes to her." (v7)
- "Wronged no less than a strong newborn left beside her, or a grown man left there to die." (v7)
- "Given to her keeping, not to her disposal." (v6–v7)
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it." (v5–v7; a quasi-realist can say it too, which is why it is fair)
