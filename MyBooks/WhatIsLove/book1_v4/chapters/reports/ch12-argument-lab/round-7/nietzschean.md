# Argument Lab, Round 7 (final): Nietzschean opponent vs. v7

## 1. School and voice

This is Nietzsche of the mature works (*Beyond Good and Evil*, *Genealogy*, *Gay Science*, *Twilight*, *Antichrist*, *Ecce Homo*), argued as Leiter, Reginster, Richardson and Clark argue him: anti-realist about moral value, a perfectionist about rank, and a critic of morality by genealogy. Round 7 first checks what v7 did with my two Round 6 Minors. It then tests v7's new wording: the conclusion's second contrast ("or a grown man left there to die"), "this book answers," "God says no to the wound and yes to her," W1(2)'s CCC 2258 wording, A6's recast Christ and Kolbe sentence, and Evil, Second's "never the evil's wage." All Nietzsche material is paraphrased, and the fairness rule applies both ways.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges (short)

1. **Justice:** exchange between parties of roughly equal power (*GM* II §8). At its height it overcomes itself in mercy, which only the strong can afford (*GM* II §10). *Coherent, and it borrows nothing. It denies A4.*
2. **Dilemma:** one ventilator, the same prognosis, a composer of genius and a man with profound disability. It goes to the composer, without remorse (*BGE* §126, §258). *It decides the case. Nearly every reader rejects it once it is said about a named patient.*
3. **Equal dignity:** denied. There is an order of rank. The lower have real claims, including gentleness from the higher (*Antichrist* §57), but lesser ones (*BGE* §257). *A consistent graded view. v7's conclusion makes it pay its price twice, once for each contrast.*
4. **Flourishing:** become who you are; self-overcoming; *amor fati*. *It is good for a few, with the many as their base (BGE §258). It does not answer for humans as such.*

### 3b. Exchanges

**Exchange 1. Rank against the strong newborn (re-test).**
*Nietzsche:* The first contrast is the one you had in Round 6. I say again that she was wronged less than the strong newborn beside her.
*Patrick's side:* Then you say "a lesser wrong" about her, by name, which is the answer the question names. "Left beside her" now states in the text what Round 6 had to argue: same hillside, same helplessness, and only strength differs. The baseness-of-the-doer move from Round 6 still answers a different question (the doer's ethics, not the wrong done to her).
**Held.**

**Exchange 2. "A grown man": which man?**
*Nietzsche:* Your second contrast is unranked. Of course she was wronged no less than a grown man of the herd; both are low. She was wronged less than a grown man of high type. So I can say "no less than a grown man" and keep rank.
*Patrick's side:* The indefinite article here reads as any grown man, as "a strong newborn" does in the same sentence; a reader who answers "it depends which man" is saying "a lesser wrong" about her beside some men. And the first contrast has already caught you: you cannot grant "no less than a strong newborn" without giving up worth by strength and health, which is the physiological core of your rank. The second contrast was written for the reader who grounds worth in mind (McMahan, Singer), not for you.
*Rejoinder:* Still, the sentence leaves my reading open to a careless reader. Rank in my sense is rank among grown men above all.
*Reply:* Granted as wording. "A grown man, however gifted" would shut it, and would echo W1(2)'s "the genius and the newborn are for the same thing."
**Held.** Optional Minor wording (section 5, Minor 2).

**Exchange 3. Defenselessness in the second contrast.**
*Nietzsche:* In Round 6 you cancelled my aggravation move because both infants were equally helpless. A grown man is not equally helpless. So "no less than a grown man" can be bought by adding up two things: her worth is less, but her greater helplessness makes leaving her more base, and the two balance. Your Church itself counts oppressing the defenseless as an aggravating fault.
*Patrick's side:* Two answers. First, the question asks whether *she* was wronged no less, a wrong done to her; aggravation by helplessness belongs to the doer's guilt, and the two are kept apart in A2 (wronging is not the same as worsening, and guilt faces a person). You are still answering the doer-centered question. Second, "left there to die" already implies he cannot save himself: a man who could walk off the hillside is not left to die. The extra thing she lacks is understanding, and that is exactly the ground this contrast is meant to test, so it cannot also be counted as a separate aggravation without counting it twice.
*Rejoinder:* The text implies his helplessness but does not say it. On a hillside a reader pictures a man who could walk away.
*Reply:* Fair as wording. "Left there helpless to die" restores, in the second contrast, the one-difference structure that made Round 6's Exchange 1 work.
**Held.** Optional Minor wording (section 5, Minor 2).

**Exchange 4. "God says no to the wound and yes to her."**
*Nietzsche:* Three tu quoques. (a) Your risen Christ kept His wounds (W3), so you say yes to the wound for eternity, as I do. (b) Your God wills the order of the whole, and in willing it, by your own Aquinas, wills some things to be naturally corrupted (Evil, Second; ST I q.19 a.9). He says yes to the order that brings the wound; a "no" that does not prevent is only a sentiment. (c) He draws good from that very evil (CCC 324), so His no turns into a yes after all.
*Patrick's side:* (a) What is kept in Christ is the wound healed and glorified, as the sign of a love given, not the injury as injury; the Church says His risen body still bears the traces of His Passion (CCC 645; ST III q.54 a.4, verify), and W3 says "not erased but kept, and healed." Eternal return keeps the same wound unhealed, again, forever. That is the difference v7 is pointing to. (b) The text already says He wills the order "never the loss for its own sake," that a person the order "never gives away," and that "He did not keep her from what was done to her; that weight is on the other side of the scale." So the "no" is not offered as prevention. It is a judgment that the wound ought not to be, and a promise that it will not be the last word ("will wipe away every tear," Revelation 21:4; Evil, Third). The text does not overclaim. (c) The good drawn from the evil "is never the evil's wage," and "the evil never thereby becomes good" (CCC 312). The Christian can say what was done to her ought never to have been, and still hope in what God brings. *Amor fati* cannot say "ought never to have been" without taking back its yes.
*Rejoinder:* "The wound" is loose. W1 uses "the wound of sin" as a term, and W3 uses "wounds" for Christ's glorified ones. Your new line borrows a word the case already uses twice in other senses.
*Reply:* That is true and harmless in sense, since in context the wound is plainly what was done to her. But the preceding sentence says *amor fati* "must say yes to what was done to her as well as to her." Answering in the same words makes the contrast exact: God separates the deed from the person, which is what the yes said to whatever happens cannot do.
**Held.** Optional polish (section 5, Minor 3).

**Exchange 5. Meaning-giving: the ascetic priest's gift.**
*Nietzsche:* Your Evil section is exactly what I diagnosed. Man does not refuse suffering; he refuses meaningless suffering, and the ascetic ideal gave it a meaning (*GM* III §28, paraphrase, verify). "He permits no evil from which He will not draw a good" is that meaning-giving with a doctrine attached.
*Patrick's side:* The fairness rule answers first. Your own philosophy gives suffering a meaning too: the discipline of great suffering that made every elevation of man (*BGE* §225, paraphrase, verify), and the yes that wants it again. If the need for meaning undercuts a view, it undercuts both or neither. The question is whether the meaning is true, and the case weighs that. Second, the text refuses the move you describe at the points where it would be a cheat: the good "is never the evil's wage"; for natural evil the Church gives "a setting, not an explanation"; for the fawn "that hope is thinner ... and this book does not pretend otherwise"; and evil "weighs against that conclusion, heavily." A priest selling meaning would not leave the price on the scale.
**Held at parity.**

**Exchange 6. W1(2)'s new CCC 2258 wording and the priest.**
*Nietzsche:* You changed "no voice that claims His name can make it ours to take" to "no one ... may claim it for himself." The priest never claims it for himself. He claims to be God's instrument (*Antichrist* §26). And Abraham heard a voice.
*Patrick's side:* The sentence has two clauses. "That right is never ours" comes first, and an authorization to kill the innocent, if it were given to us, would make the right ours. So the instrument is covered, "whatever voice he claims to have heard in His name." On Abraham, the text's own clause fits the Scripture closely: "He who gives life, and can give it back, may call it home." Hebrews reads Abraham's faith the same way: "He considered that God was able to raise men even from the dead; hence, figuratively speaking, he did receive him back" (Hebrews 11:19, RSV). And Genesis 22 ends with the ram, not with Isaac's death. The Church teaches that the ban on deliberately killing the innocent binds always and everywhere (VS 52, 80; CCC 2261). How St. Thomas's reading of Abraham fits with that is still a question for the censor, not a gap against my school.
**Held.** The anti-priestly line survives the recast.

**Exchange 7. Christ "of His own accord," and the free death.**
*Nietzsche:* A6 now says Christ "laid down His life of His own accord." He "set his face to go to Jerusalem" (Luke 9:51, RSV), knowing what waited. That is my free death, at the right time. And A6 still forbids a man to "aim at his own death."
*Patrick's side:* The recast states the line by the will's aim, as Round 6 asked. "No one takes it from me, but I lay it down of my own accord" (John 10:18, RSV): He gives Himself, and the killing is done by others who "wrongly kill him." He aims at the Father's will and at our redemption, and He accepts the death that comes; He does not seek death for its own sake. Your own portrait of Jesus agrees on the one point that matters here: on your account he did not resist and did not defend himself, and died as he had lived (*Antichrist* §§33–35, paraphrase, verify). That is accepting a death, not aiming at it. A6 also states the whole line as "this book's claims ... not shared ones," so it asks nothing of you as a premise.
**Held at parity.** This is the named standoff over free death, honestly placed.

**Exchange 8. The saints' rank is my rank with a halo.**
*Nietzsche:* A4 grants "the saints are higher than I am in how well they love," and W1(2) grants that "the dignity of a good life" can be lost. So you have an order of rank too. You have only renamed it.
*Patrick's side:* A4 draws the line exactly where we part: higher "in how well they love, never in what they are worth." Your rank is a rank of worth, and it decides who may be spent (the ventilator). Ours is a rank in love, and it runs the other way: "whoever would be great among you must be your servant, and whoever would be first among you must be slave of all" (Mark 10:43–44, RSV). A rank in which the highest are those who serve the lowest best cannot be used to rank the newborn below the composer.
*Rejoinder:* That inversion is the slave revolt itself (*GM* I §§7, 10).
*Reply:* Then the genealogy is doing the work, and the fairness rule applies as in Round 6: your account gives the will to truth the same ancestry (C row), so ancestry undercuts both or neither.
**Held at parity.**

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**As written: NONE.**

Against my school, the conclusion's step is valid in v7 as it was in v6, and v7 states in the text ("a strong newborn left beside her") the one-difference structure that Round 6 had to argue for. The rank theorist cannot say "no less than a strong newborn" and keep worth by strength and health. He must say "a lesser wrong" about her, by name, and that is a price, not a defect. The second contrast is aimed at the mind-based reader, and the two ways I tried to get around it (an unranked "grown man," Ex. 2; adding up lesser worth and greater helplessness, Ex. 3) both fail. The first fails because the indefinite article reads generically and the first contrast has already caught rank. The second fails because it answers the doer-centered question, not the wrong done to her. Neither opens a logical gap. They are wording points only. The new line "God says no to the wound and yes to her" holds under the *felix culpa*, providence and glorified-wounds tu quoques (Ex. 4). The CCC 2258 recast still disarms the priest (Ex. 6), and the A6 recast states the free-death standoff correctly (Ex. 7).

**Minor (as written)**
1. **C, Power and rank, "Then either" (carried from Round 6, not taken).** "He denied that it binds every mind. Then either ... or it binds only those who hold it. Then his unmasking ..." The first "Then" makes the dilemma sound as if it followed from his denial. It is the case that puts the choice. And two "Then"s come one after the other. Wording only.
2. **Conclusion, second contrast (optional).** "A grown man left there to die" does not say he is helpless, and does not say "any" man. A careless reader could picture a man who could walk away (Ex. 3), or a low man (Ex. 2). The sentence is valid as written, read naturally. Wording only.
3. **Evil, First, "the wound" (optional, very low).** The case already uses "wound" in two other senses (the wound of sin in W1, Christ's glorified wounds in W3). Answering the previous sentence in its own words ("what was done to her") would make the contrast exact.

**Misstatements of my school (very low; verify)**
- **C, Power and rank, "which he called the one virtue his kind could not get away from."** In *BGE* §227, as I read it, he calls honesty "our virtue," the one we free spirits cannot get away from, and elsewhere the last or youngest virtue. "The one virtue" may slightly overstate "our virtue." Check the text. If it does not say "the one," write "which he called the virtue his kind could not get away from."
- "He said we need art to bear the truth": supported in paraphrase by *GS* §107. The sharper form ("art, lest we perish of the truth") is from the *Nachlass*, so the chapter should cite *GS* §107 rather than *The Will to Power*. Verify.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- **Rank vs. equal worth.** He is surer of rank. The case makes him say "a lesser wrong" about the weak infant beside a strong newborn, by name.
- **Ressentiment genealogy vs. A4 and vs. the inverted rank of service** (Ex. 8). Ancestry undercuts both or neither.
- **Immanent critique.** Whether honest inquiry ends at "God is dead" or at the Lawgiver. The whole case decides it, as the C row says.
- **Honesty as fate vs. honesty as binding.** On his account the virtue needs no ought. On ours, without one it binds only those who already have it.
- **Meaning-giving** (Ex. 5). Both views give suffering a meaning. The question is which meaning is true, and the case leaves the weight on the scale.
- **"Finally."** One Source, or art to bear the truth.
- **Free death vs. "may not aim at his own death"** (Ex. 7). This is named as the book's claim.
- **Dionysus vs. the Crucified** on the evidence of nature alone. The book owns this as weight.

> but he denied that it binds every mind. Then either truthfulness binds every reasoning mind, and something binds beyond power (A7); or it binds only those who hold it. Then his unmasking can turn our own honesty against us,

> but he denied that it binds every mind. So the case puts a choice. Either truthfulness binds every reasoning mind, and something binds beyond power (A7); or it binds only those who hold it, and then his unmasking can turn our own honesty against us,

## 7. New insights and lines worth keeping

1. **One difference per contrast.** "Left beside her" now puts the Round 6 argument into the sentence itself: same hillside, same helplessness, only strength differs. If the second contrast also says "helpless," each contrast changes exactly one thing, and neither the rank theorist nor the mind-based reader can buy "no less" with something else.
2. **Kept and healed vs. kept and repeated.** Eternal return keeps the wound as it was, forever. The resurrection keeps it healed, as the risen Christ's wounds are kept (W3; CCC 645, verify). This is the cleanest answer to "you too say yes to the wound."
3. **Abraham and "can give it back."** W1(2)'s own clause, "He who gives life, and can give it back, may call it home," matches Hebrews 11:19's reading of Abraham. The chapter could cite it there. This is a note for the censor, not a repair.
4. **Nietzsche's own Jesus accepts death; he does not aim at it** (*Antichrist* §§33–35, verify). That supports A6's line on Christ against the free-death reading.
5. **The rank of service.** The saints are higher in love, never in worth, and in that rank the first are servants of all (Mark 10:43–44). This answers "your rank with a halo."

**Lines worth keeping**
- "Wronged no less than a strong newborn left beside her." (v7)
- "A yes said to whatever happens is said to no one; ... it cannot take her side." (v6–v7)
- "God says no to the wound and yes to her." (v7)
- "That right is never ours." (v7)
- "Honesty may cost a life but can never finally be the enemy of life." (v6–v7)
- "His yes wants the deed again; ours wants her back."
- "Not every child is wanted by those who made her; every child was wanted by the One who did." (v5–v7)

## 8. Score

The conclusion's step is valid against rank, and the new wording ("left beside her," the grown man, "this book answers," "God says no to the wound and yes to her," the CCC 2258 recast) opens no gap against this school.
