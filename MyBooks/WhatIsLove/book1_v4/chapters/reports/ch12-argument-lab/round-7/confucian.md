# Argument Lab, Round 7 (final): Confucian opponent vs. v7

## 1. School and voice

The opponent speaks for the Confucian tradition at full strength: the *Analects* (Slingerland numbering), Mencius and Xunzi, the *Odes*, the *Mean*, the *Great Learning* and the *Classic of Filial Piety*, the school of principle (the Cheng brothers, Zhang Zai, Zhu Xi), Wang Yangming's learning of the heart, and Mou Zongsan's "immanent transcendence," read with Ivanhoe, Angle and Tu Weiming. He grants nearly everything the book says about love and denies only that its Source is Someone. This round he checks whether v7 took his Round 6 repairs (R-f, R-g), then tests v7's new wording sentence by sentence against v6. Everything is paraphrased; nothing is quoted from a real thinker.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges (unchanged in substance)

1. **Justice.** It begins in repayment (*bao*): we owe first what we received, from parents, from the sages, and from Heaven and Earth; reciprocity is its measure (15.24). *Holds, close to Chapter 4.* But repayment needs a receiver.
2. **A hard case.** Shun carries his murderer father to the sea (Mencius 7A35): he neither bends the law nor abandons his father. *Coherent and honest about the cost.* It strains A4 at one point: the victim's claim yields.
3. **Equal dignity.** Each person carries the whole of principle, as the one moon is whole in ten thousand rivers; every man in the street can become a Yu (Xunzi 23). *Holds on equality.* It breaks at "for her own sake": drain one river and the moon has lost nothing.
4. **Ultimate good.** Sagehood, forming one body with all things; for the world, the Great Unity in which widow, orphan and disabled are all cared for. *Coherent and noble.* It stops short of communion, and of the dead.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1: did v7 take R-f and R-g?**
*Audit.*
- **R-f (9.5, "the culture he carried"):** **taken word for word.** The sacred-order row now says Heaven "would not let the culture he carried perish." That is what 9.5 says: King Wen is gone, the culture (*wen*) is here, and Heaven will not let it be lost. The row now speaks of Heaven keeping something for others' sake, which is closer to Providence than "his work" was, and still accurate.
- **R-g (W3, "and the Neo-Confucians ... found"):** **taken word for word.** "So did" is gone. The Buddhist contemplatives "report a peace beyond the self"; the Neo-Confucians "found a principle rather than a Person." Zhu Xi's lifelong refusal of the Chan "empty mind" is no longer blurred.
- **R10 (optional, Ricci and the Lord on High):** not taken. It was offered for richness, not for the stop test, and its absence breaks nothing.
*Result: Held.* Every point this school raised in Round 6 is closed.

**Exchange 2: is R-g, now in place, fair to the Neo-Confucians as "seekers who do not find Him"?**
*Opponent.* "The paragraph asks why honest seekers do not find Him. Zhu Xi was not looking for a Person and would say he lacked nothing. And Wang Yangming at Longchang did not find a principle out in things; he found that his own heart is principle. 'Found a principle rather than a Person' fits Zhu Xi better than Wang."
*Defense.* "The sentence names both searches: they 'searched things and their own hearts.' That is Zhu Xi's investigation of things and Wang's turn to the heart, in eight words. And Wang himself called innate knowing Heaven's principle, so 'found a principle' is his own account of what he found. As to whether they were seeking: the paragraph's question is why many who seek the highest good with discipline and honesty do not meet a Person there. Your teachers sought the highest good for centuries, with more discipline than most of us. The paragraph does not say they sought a Person and failed; it says what they found, and it does not count their finding as evidence either way ('neither reading is evidence')."
*Result: Held.* No repair.

**Exchange 3: W1(1), road two. Does Xunzi's rite release what the text says no one releases?**
*Opponent.* "Road two says: 'what can never be repaid, no one ever releases.' Xunzi disagrees. The sages set the three-year mourning to end at the twenty-fifth month (Xunzi, 'Discourse on Rites'; verify): the rite gives grief its measure and then sets the mourner free. The form itself completes the bond. That is a release, and it needs no Keeper."
*Defense.* "The rite ends the mourning; it does not end the debt. Even on Xunzi's account the seasonal sacrifices go on after the twenty-fifth month, because the gratitude is never paid off. Your own *Odes* say the debt to parents is as boundless as Heaven. And the text's subject is narrower than grief: it is a *wrong* done to her. A rite that measures grief can tell me when to stop weeping; it cannot tell me she has pardoned what I did to her. Xunzi would not claim it can: for him the rites ornament and complete feeling; they do not make a wrong not wrong."
*Opponent rejoinder.* "Then the community releases. The sages' form is the people's verdict: you have done what can be done."
*Defense.* "Then a people gives the pardon in her place, which A3 says no one on earth can do: 'not her friends, not a nation by vote, not the whole human race.' Xunzi does not say the rite pardons; he says it is the human way of completing a bond 'without holding that the dead hear,' which is exactly how v7 states his road. When a Confucian has wronged a parent who has died, he goes to the ancestral hall and reports his fault before the tablet. That is the third road, and v7 names it."
*Result: Held.* No repair. Road two states Xunzi accurately (the human way of completing a bond; no claim that the dead hear), and so does the line on Confucius (3.12, sacrificing as though present; 11.12, no more said). Mozi's guests-without-guests is accurate to the *Mozi* ("Gongmeng"; verify).

**Exchange 4: the sacred-order row and the Western Inscription.**
*Opponent.* "The row says Heaven's order 'gives life to each thing and intends none,' and that it cannot say '*this* one is wanted for her own sake.' But the most loved text of the school of principle, Zhang Zai's Western Inscription, begins: Heaven is my father and Earth my mother, and I, this small thing, find my place between them. It goes on: wealth and honor enrich my life; poverty and sorrow are meant to polish me like jade into completion; while I live I serve them, and when I die I am at peace (paraphrase; verify). That is a Parent who forms *me*. And Mencius says that when Heaven is about to lay a great charge on a man, it first tries his mind and body with hardship (6B15). Heaven intends a particular man."
*Defense.* "Zhu Xi, who prized the Western Inscription, read it through the formula 'principle is one, its distinctions many': one principle, whole in each, with distinct duties to each. That is the one moon in ten thousand rivers, which the row already gives you. On that reading the parental language describes the stance the sage takes toward Heaven and Earth, as a filial son, not a Parent's intention toward him; and the row cites Zhu Xi's own words that principle has no intention. Mencius 6B15 is like Confucius in danger at Kuang: the tradition's own heart reaches for a Heaven that knows and charges *this* man. The row grants that in plain words: 'this shows where the account strains for its own teachers, not that it fails.' The Western Inscription is the strongest case of that strain, and it honors your school."
*Opponent rejoinder.* "Then the strain runs through the whole tradition, not only through Confucius's bad days."
*Defense.* "Yes, and the text does not claim more than that. 'Minds under threat reach for a knowing Heaven, ours as well' applies the same explanation to both sides, which keeps the fairness rule. Whether the reach is a projection or a clue is what the case weighs. One more point: in 6B15 the hardship is for the man's *own* completion. That matches what v7 says of God in the evil section: 'for a person who suffers, the good the Church hopes for is a good offered to her.' Mencius and the Church agree on the shape of a good Heaven's use of suffering."
*Result: Held.* No repair. The Western Inscription and 6B15 go to §7.

**Exchange 5: A6's new line on Christ and Kolbe, Analects 15.9, and the narrowed A heading.**
*Opponent.* "v7 says a man may 'accept the death that comes, even at the hands of those who wrongly kill him,' but 'may not aim at his own death, by his own hand or another's.' 15.9 says the man of *ren* will 'kill his body' (*sha shen*) to complete *ren*. Qu Yuan drowned himself in protest and the whole nation keeps his feast. Loyal ministers died by their own hand with their dynasty. The A heading used to say that many deny 'the Church's teaching on chosen death'; it now names only the deniers of 'the line at every number and against a man's own request.' Qu Yuan made no request. Has v7 dropped my tradition's honored suicides from the list of deniers?"
*Defense.* "On 15.9: the phrase means not clinging to life at the cost of *ren*, and the type Confucius himself names is Bi Gan, who remonstrated with a tyrant and was killed for it, one of Yin's 'three men of *ren*' (18.1). That is exactly v7's case: death accepted 'at the hands of those who wrongly kill him.' In the same passage Confucius calls Weizi, who left, a man of *ren* too: the Master did not make death the aim. On Qu Yuan and the loyalist suicides: A6 forbids them by name ('by his own hand'), labels that as 'this book's claims ... not shared ones,' and the Church does not judge the persons (CCC 2282–2283). On the heading: its list of deniers is a set of examples ('None is beyond denial: ...'), not a closed list, so leaving one group unnamed misstates nothing."
*Opponent rejoinder.* "Granted that it misstates nothing. But v6's wording covered my honored suicides and v7's does not, while A6 still says 'request or consent.' The narrowing was a side effect of another school's repair."
*Defense.* "Fair. The heading should carry A6's own words. 'Request or consent' covers both the man who asks another and the man who consents to his own death by his own hand."
*Result: Held with repair (Minor).* R-h. No step of the argument rests on it; it is a matter of the heading matching A6.

**Exchange 6: "God says no to the wound and yes to her," and Heaven's heart to give life.**
*Opponent.* "v7 adds: 'A yes said to whatever happens is said to no one; ... it cannot take her side. God says no to the wound and yes to her.' Heaven's heart is to give life. Cheng Hao said the man of *ren* feels all things as his own body, so that to be numb to another's pain is like a paralyzed limb. Through the sage, Heaven says no to the wound too."
*Defense.* "The sentence answers an affirmation of everything that happens (the 'god beyond good and evil' named just before it), not Heaven's heart to give life, which does not affirm the breaking. Your 'no' to the wound is real. But on your account it is said in the sage's heart, and on Zhu Xi's reading principle itself 'intends none.' On Mou's reading it is said by the infinite heart, which is mine at my deepest. Either way, at the ground of things there is no one besides her who says yes to *her*. That is the union-versus-communion standoff, already stated fairly in the row."
*Result: Held.* No repair. Also checked: "faith adds that He permits no evil from which He will not draw a good, though that good is never the evil's wage." Against Mencius 6B15 the clause lands well: the hardship is not a wage that makes the hardship good; it is for the man's completion.

**Exchange 7: the conclusion's new comparison, and graded love.**
*Opponent.* "The conclusion now asks whether the exposed infant was 'wronged no less than a strong newborn left beside her, or a grown man left there to die.' Chinese law graded crimes by the five mourning degrees: the same blow was a graver crime against a father than against a stranger. Graded love grades wrongs. Can a Confucian say 'wronged no less'?"
*Defense.* "The grading in the code follows the *doer's* relation and duty: a son who strikes his father breaks more bonds than a stranger who strikes a stranger. The conclusion holds the doer and the hillside fixed and varies only the victim: her strength (the strong newborn beside her) and her mind (the grown man). On the victim's side your school grants the premise at once: each carries the whole of principle; Mencius's child at the well is any child; the man in the street can become a Yu. 'Equal worth is not equal duty' (A4) is the line where graded love sits, and v7 kept it."
*Opponent rejoinder.* "Our ground is the sprouts, a nature that can grow into sagehood. The phrase 'not yet able to know herself' fits us: she has the sprouts."
*Defense.* "And 'this book answers' labels 'what she is' as the book's answer, not as something the reader must grant. A Confucian who grounds worth in a nature whole in each, not in its present exercise, already stands on the near side of that answer. The comparison is aimed at the reader who grounds worth in present mind (McMahan, Singer), and for him the grown man is the contrast that was missing in v6."
*Result: Held.* No repair. The new comparison is fair to this school and does not misstate it.

**Exchange 8: W1(2)'s new wording on the right to take life, and ordinary care.**
*Opponent.* "In Round 6 I noted that 'no voice that claims His name can make it ours' depended on the word 'claims' to stay consistent with Augustine and Aquinas on a hidden divine command. v7 now says: 'That right is never ours, and no one, whatever voice he claims to have heard in His name, may claim it for himself (CCC 2258).' Is it still consistent?"
*Defense.* "More so. It follows CCC 2258's own shape: no one may claim *for himself* the right directly to destroy an innocent human being. On Augustine's and Aquinas's view of the rare saint under a divine command, the saint did not claim the right for himself; it was not his. The sentence forbids every human claim and makes no ruling on what God may do with what is His."
*Opponent.* "And the new clause on care: 'the ordinary care owed to the sick, food and water among it, may not be withdrawn.'"
*Defense.* "Your Master already said it more sharply: if filial piety means only feeding one's parents, even dogs and horses are fed; without reverence, what is the difference (2.7)? The Confucian would never withdraw food and water from a dying parent; the question for him is reverence beyond feeding. Here the two traditions stand together."
*Result: Held.* No repair. The "claims" point I logged for the censor in Round 6 is now carried by CCC 2258's wording itself.

**Exchange 9: A2's new phrase, "a wrong done to a person needs forgiving."**
*Opponent.* "Our tradition stresses the correction of faults more than forgiveness: to err and not correct it, that is the real error (15.30)."
*Defense.* "A2 grants both: 'a flawed character needs reforming' is your 15.30; 'a wrong done to a person needs forgiving' is the other half. Your own history has its best image: the general Lian Po, having insulted the minister Lin Xiangru, bared his back, carried brambles to Lin's door and asked his pardon (Sima Qian, *Shiji* 81; verify). Correcting the fault was not enough; he went to the one he wronged. The new phrase is also better than v6's 'a wronged person needs forgiving,' which seemed to say the victim needed pardon."
*Result: Held.* No repair. Lian Po goes to §7.

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**As written: NONE.**

No Fatal or Serious objection from this school stands against v7 as written. v7 took both Round 6 repairs (R-f, R-g) word for word. Its new wording breaks nothing that this school can see: A6's line between accepting death and aiming at it fits the Confucian exemplar Bi Gan exactly and labels the honored suicides it forbids as the book's own claim; the W1(2) sentence on the right to take life now follows CCC 2258's own shape; the conclusion's new comparison varies only the victim's strength and mind, which a school of graded love grants on the victim's side. The strongest new probes (Xunzi's measured mourning, the Western Inscription and Mencius 6B15, graded wrongs in Chinese law) are answered at parity or better from the text's own premises.

**Minor:**
1. A heading: "deny the Church's teaching that the line holds at every number and against a man's own request" is narrower than v6's "on chosen death" and than A6's own "request or consent." The list is of examples, so nothing is misstated, but the Confucian honored suicides (Qu Yuan, the loyalist ministers, the chaste widows) are no longer clearly among the named deniers (Ex. 5). R-h.

**Misstatements of this school:** none. Every Confucian sentence in v7 (Xunzi and Confucius on road two, the ancestral form, Mozi, the sacred-order row with 7.23, 9.5, 11.9, 14.35, Zhu Xi, Mou, Sima Qian, the Neo-Confucians in W3, the Great Unity) is accurate as written.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**
- *The ancestral third road.* The named dead kept by the memory of their kin against the dead kept by One who forgets no one. They part on what keeps her when memory fails.
- *Rites that complete and rites that release.* Xunzi's measured mourning ends grief well; it does not claim to give her pardon. Theism is ahead only where guilt asks for *her* pardon (Ex. 3).
- *Equality without capacity.* The moon in the rivers matches the theist ground on equality; theism is ahead on "for her own sake" and "for good."
- *A Parent in the language, a principle in the metaphysics.* The Western Inscription and Mencius 6B15 speak of a Heaven that forms *this* man; Zhu Xi reads them as one principle with many distinctions. Projection or clue is what the case weighs (Ex. 4).
- *Life held in trust.* Trust from parents (*Classic of Filial Piety*; Mencius 7A2) against trust from the Lord of life. Mencius's absolute (2A2) rests in the doer's rightness; the book's rests in her.
- *Honored deaths.* Each tradition honored some who chose death in ways its later teaching does not commend.
- *Union vs. communion.* Mou's infinite heart, mine and yours, against Someone besides us (Ex. 6).
- *The problem of evil.* A principle that intends no one carries a lighter problem and promises the seeker his own completion; a God who knows each one carries the heavier problem and promises this child.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**R-h (Minor). A heading: replace**
"deny the Church's teaching that the line holds at every number and against a man's own request, which A6 names as this book's claims."
**with:**
> deny the Church's teaching that the line holds at every number and against a man's own request or consent, which A6 names as this book's claims.

(This uses A6's own words. "Consent" covers the man who chooses his own death by his own hand, as well as the man who asks another, and so restores what v6's "chosen death" covered without undoing the Utilitarian's repair.)

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

1. **Bi Gan, the man of *ren* who was killed (Analects 18.1).** Confucius named three men of *ren* in the fall of Yin: one who left, one who feigned madness and was enslaved, and Bi Gan, who remonstrated and was killed. Bi Gan fits A6's line exactly: he accepted the death that came at the hands of one who wrongly killed him; he did not aim at it. And Confucius called the one who left a man of *ren* too: death was not the aim. (Ch. 12, A6; Ch. 9 on sacrifice.) Verify.
2. **Lian Po's brambles (Sima Qian, *Shiji* 81).** A proud general who had insulted a minister bared his back, carried brambles to his door, and asked his pardon. Correcting the fault was not enough; he went to the one he wronged. A Chinese image of A2's "only guilt asks her pardon." (Ch. 12, A2; Ch. 8 on mercy.) Verify.
3. **The Western Inscription (Zhang Zai).** "Heaven is my father, Earth my mother": the Confucian heart reaches for a Parent, and sees hardship as polishing the self like jade. The book can honor it as the deepest place where an impersonal order strains toward Someone. (Ch. 12, C; Ch. 3 on love as sacred.) Verify.
4. **Mencius 6B15 and the good offered to the sufferer.** When Heaven means to lay a great charge on a man, it first tries him, for his own completion. The Confucian shape of suffering's use is the same as the Church's: a good offered to the one who suffers, never a wage that makes the evil good. (Ch. 12, Evil *Second*; Ch. 9.) Verify.
5. **Analects 2.7 and ordinary care.** Even dogs and horses are fed; what makes care filial is reverence. The Confucian would never withdraw food and water from a dying parent, and asks more than feeding. (Ch. 12, W1(2); Ch. 7 on justice.)
6. **The rite ends the mourning, not the debt.** Xunzi's sages set a term to grief; they did not set a term to gratitude, and the seasonal offerings go on. A form can tell me when to stop weeping; it cannot tell me she has forgiven me. (Ch. 12, W1(1).) Verify Xunzi's "Discourse on Rites."
7. **Verify before anything enters the book:** *Analects* 2.7, 3.12, 9.5, 11.12, 15.9, 15.30, 18.1; *Mencius* 2A2, 6A10, 6B15, 7A35; Xunzi chs. 19 and 23; the *Mozi*, "Gongmeng"; Zhang Zai's Western Inscription and Zhu Xi's reading of it; *Shiji* 61 and 81; *Liyun*. Paraphrase only.

**Lines worth keeping (short, plain, warm):**
- "Heaven and Earth give life to things; God wanted *you*." (v7)
- "God says no to the wound and yes to her." (v7)
- "On that road the dead are kept by the living, and memory fails." (v7)
- "A rite can tell me when to stop weeping; it cannot tell me she has forgiven me."
- "Bi Gan did not aim at his death; he would not stop telling the truth."
- "Something can respond. Only someone can forgive." (Round 4, still true of Zhu Xi's *qi*.)

## 8. Score

**v7 as written: 9.5/10. With the repairs: 9.5/10.** v7 took both of this school's Round 6 repairs word for word, and its new wording (A6's accept-versus-aim line, the CCC 2258 sentence, the care clause, the conclusion's two contrasts) is accurate to Confucius, Mencius, Xunzi, Zhu Xi and Mou and breaks no step; the one Minor item is a heading that should carry A6's own "or consent." What keeps the score from 10 is not a defect but the honest standoffs (what keeps the dead when memory fails, union against communion, a Parent in the tradition's language against a principle in its metaphysics), and the book states them fairly.
