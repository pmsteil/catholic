# Round 7: Contractualism and the Second-Personal Standpoint vs. v7

## 1. School and voice

The voice is a composite of T. M. Scanlon (a wrong is what could not be justified to the one it is done to), Stephen Darwall (obligation is the standing of free and equal persons to make claims on one another), John Rawls and R. Jay Wallace (guilt and blame as the attitudes by which we hold one another to account). H. L. A. Hart's will theory of rights, Locke's "workmanship" argument as Darwall reads it, and Scanlon's case in *Moral Dimensions* that an agent's intention does not settle whether an act is permissible are the tools used against v7's new wording. All positions are paraphrased. There are no direct quotations from these thinkers.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

1. **Justice.** What anyone is owed follows from principles that no one affected could reasonably reject. *Holds, but it presupposes the equal standing it then applies.*
2. **A hard case.** The TRC amnesty (*AZAPO*, 1996; verify): a state may waive punishment, but it cannot forgive what was done to Biko. *Holds, and it supports A3. It has no account of mercy as a gift that heals.*
3. **Equal dignity.** Being born of human parents is a reason to count her among those we answer to, through a trustee. *The scope holds. The ground is a reason that "simply holds."*
4. **Ultimate good.** Mutual recognition in a social union of social unions. *Coherent, but it ends at death.*

**Exchange 1. The new conclusion: does "wronged no less" reach "worth"?**
*Contractualist:* In Scanlon's terms, how grave a wrong is depends on the reasons the person has to reject a principle that would permit it. It does not depend on a quantity called "worth." The infant left on the hillside loses her whole life. So does the grown man. On a "future like ours" view (Marquis; verify), she loses more. So I can grant "wronged no less than either" and then say that the wrong follows what is *lost*, not what she *is*. Your step from "wronged no less" to "her worth never followed her strength or her mind" switches from the gravity of the wrong to worth.
*Defense:* The step it actually takes is negative: whatever the wrong follows, it is not strength or mind, because both contrasts vary those and the wrong stays the same. Your loss-based reading accepts that. The positive step, "it follows what she is," is labeled "this book answers," and the text does not present it as entailed. Your view also needs an answer to *why* her loss counts as one we must answer for. Your own school's answer is that she was born one of us, which is a fact of kind. The C row says this school takes that as given, and the row stays fair.
*Contractualist:* Granted. The inference is valid as far as it claims to go, and the claim beyond it is marked as the book's.
*Verdict: Held.* The Round 6 gap is closed.

**Exchange 2. A6: "accept the death that comes ... but he may not aim at his own death."**
*Contractualist:* Scanlon and Thomson argue that intention bears on what an act means, not on whether it is permissible. v7 now rests the whole line between Kolbe and the requested killing on "aim," which is double effect again. It also says Kolbe "offered himself in a condemned man's place." An offer to be killed instead of another sounds like a request to be killed.
*Defense:* The line rests on double effect, and A6 says so in its own words: "Those are this book's claims, argued in W1(2), not shared ones." On Kolbe, v7 keeps the order right. What he offered was to take the man's place, and those who killed him are "those who wrongly kill him." The counterfactual test from Round 6 still separates the cases. If the guards had spared them both, Kolbe would have had everything he asked for. If a man who asks to be killed is not killed, what he asked for fails. "Aim at" means what the Church means by it, death "willed as either an end or a means" (CCC 2279). v7 uses the Catechism's own wording in place of v6's "make his own death the thing he chooses." That is more exact.
*Contractualist:* The standoff is the same as in Round 6, and it is labeled. The new wording breaks nothing, and "his asking never makes it right for anyone to kill him" fits Kolbe: his request did not make the guards' act right, and the sentence does not say it did.
*Verdict: Held, at parity.*

**Exchange 3. The dissenter's question, now in the text: "why may he not ask for death?"**
*Contractualist:* v7 puts my school's best question into A6: if he may lay down his life for others, as a firefighter does, why may he not ask for death? That is fair. But then it answers with "may not aim," and the only reason it gives why aim is decisive is the trust in W1(2), which works only for someone who grants a Giver.
*Defense:* Yes, and the text says so: "as the Church holds," "on this account," "a reader who holds it at every number has here a reason for it." The book does not say a non-theist must accept the line against requested killing. It says that one who accepts it has a reason here that his own account lacks. Your school can also hold much of the line without a Giver. A principle permitting killing on request could reasonably be rejected by the vulnerable, the disabled and the elderly, who would carry the pressure such a principle creates. Some contractualists argue against legalizing euthanasia on exactly this ground (verify).
*Contractualist:* That argument depends on circumstances and would allow exceptions. The Church's line has none. So the honest position is the one A6 states: shared at five, contested at the request, and in the end a dispute over whether an inalienable right needs a Giver.
*Verdict: Held.* It is labeled, stated in the dissenter's strongest form, and answered at parity.

**Exchange 4. W1(2): "any right to take her life" in place of "any right over her life."**
*Contractualist:* My Round 5 repair was "no number of others has any right over her life." The censor narrowed it to "to take" (CCC 2310). Does the narrower wording let something back in? Others could still have a right to *spend* her without taking her life, for example a dangerous experiment she survives, or forced service.
*Defense:* The sentence answers one question: why the line against killing her has no exceptions. Spending her in other ways is ruled out elsewhere: in A4 ("never to be used as a mere means, not for the many and not for the few"), in A6 (the prisoner experiment; CCC 2295), and in the next lines of W1(2) ("not ours to spend, not even for many"). The narrower wording also avoids saying something false. Legitimate authority can require citizens to serve in defense of the nation (CCC 2310), so "any right over her life" said too much. The job that matters to this school is unchanged: her protection still rests on no third party's title. It rests on others lacking any standing to kill her.
*Verdict: Held.* The narrowing is correct and costs nothing.

**Exchange 5. Ordinary care: "food and water among it, may not be withdrawn."**
*Contractualist:* In Round 6 I praised CCC 2278–2279 for making the trust less paternal: she may refuse burdens, and a doctor who forces them on her wrongs her. v7 adds "the ordinary care owed to the sick, food and water among it, may not be withdrawn." Read alone, that admits no exception. It would cover food and water that no longer nourish, or that cause serious suffering at the end of life. Hart's question comes back here more sharply than anywhere else in the text: does the trust bind those who care for her even when what they give her no longer serves her good?
*Defense:* No. The sentence says "ordinary care *owed*," and what is owed is owed to *her*, for her good. CCC 2279 says ordinary care "cannot be legitimately interrupted." The CDF's 2007 Responses, which *Samaritanus Bonus* V.3 follows, call food and water in principle ordinary and proportionate, and so obligatory for as long as they actually nourish the patient (verify the exact wording on vatican.va). When they can no longer be assimilated, or would cause grave discomfort, they are no longer the care owed. Read with "owed," the sentence already carries that limit.
*Contractualist:* That is a fair reading, and I do not claim the sentence is false. But the limit is exactly what shows that the trust serves her and does not take her over, which is the point this school most needs to see. Said plainly, it would be exact, and it would match the Church's own wording more closely than v7 does.
*Verdict: Held with repair (Minor).* The Debunker and Realist flagged the same point independently. Wording in section 6 (R1).

**Exchange 6. "That right is never ours ... whatever voice he claims to have heard in His name, may claim it for himself (CCC 2258)."**
*Contractualist:* "For himself" is CCC 2258's phrase. It leaves open that God might use a human hand without that person claiming the right for himself (ST II-II q.64 a.6 ad 1). For a second-personal theory, that is the place where a Creator who stands above us could order what cannot be justified to her.
*Defense:* v7 keeps the possessive, "never ours," and adds 2258's "for himself." No human holds the right, and no human may claim it on a voice. Whether a real divine command could make a human the instrument of God's own sentence is Aquinas's question about Abraham. Chapter 3 answers voluntarism at its root: God cannot command what is against His goodness, and "He who gives life ... may call it home" is the standing of the Giver, not a power to dictate. From this school it remains the standoff over whether a Creator's standing, which is not equal to ours, can still be second-personal. The text's "the Judge lets Himself be asked (Genesis 18:25)" is the right answer for us to hear.
*Contractualist:* The sentence is better than v6 for my purposes. It gives no human a way to use God's name as a license against the one in front of him, which is what my school cares about most.
*Verdict: Held.* The standoff remains at parity, as before.

**Exchange 7. A4: "nor 'for his own good' without his asking," with the new parenthesis.**
*Contractualist:* A4 is a shared premise, and it says never "for his own good" without his asking. A reader may infer that asking could make it right, and I would welcome that. Is the shared premise quietly granting my school's view on euthanasia?
*Defense:* The censor saw the same risk, and v7 closes it. The parenthesis now states the Church's denial ("whether a man's own asking could ever make it right to kill him, which the Church denies (CCC 2277; *Evangelium Vitae* 65–66), A6 names as this book's claims"). What is shared is that no one may be killed for his own good *without* his asking. What happens *with* asking is the book's labeled claim. Neither side is presumed to have won.
*Verdict: Held.* (The Debunker's point about reading the nested parenthesis aloud is fair. It is a wording matter only, and I do not repeat it as my own item.)

**Exchange 8. Quick check of the rest of v7 as it touches this school.**
- *C, What we owe each other:* unchanged. It is still the fairest statement of this school in the lab, including the trustee sentence. *Held.*
- *Evil section:* "God never spends anyone as a mere means against her own good" now matches W1(2), so the inconsistency I flagged in Round 6 is gone. "God says no to the wound and yes to her" is second-personal in exactly the right sense: the yes is said *to* her. *Held.*
- *W1 frame and W1(1):* unchanged except for "a wrong done to a person needs forgiving," which is more exact than v6. Her pardon stays hers (CCC 1441, 1442, 1459). *Held.*
- *Kant row:* "not as impunity: the one forgiven must still repair what he can (CCC 1459)." Not this school's row, but it supports what this school says about what is owed to the victim. *Held.*
- *CCC 2278 itself:* decisions "by the patient if he is competent ... or, if not, by those legally entitled to act for the patient." The Catechism has the same trustee structure that the C row gives Scanlon. It is a point of agreement, not an objection.

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**As written: NONE.**

No Fatal or Serious objection from this school stands against v7 as written. Both Round 6 repairs were taken, each in a better form than I proposed. The new wording this school was most likely to attack holds under pressure from Hart, Scanlon on intention, and Locke as Darwall reads him. That wording is "may not aim at his own death," "his asking never makes it right for anyone to kill him," "any right to take her life," ordinary care, and "whatever voice he claims ... for himself." Where the text rests on contested ground (double effect, and the request), it says so in its own words.

**Minor (wording; none changes a conclusion):**
1. **W1(2), "the ordinary care owed to the sick, food and water among it, may not be withdrawn."** It is true when read with "owed," but it omits the limit that *Samaritanus Bonus* V.3 and the CDF's 2007 Responses themselves state: food and water are obligatory for as long as they nourish. For this school, the limit is what shows that the trust serves her and does not take her over (Exchange 5). The Debunker and Realist flag the same point. The censor should verify it.
2. **Conclusion, "though she was weak and not yet able to know herself."** The clause now comes after "a grown man left there to die," so "she" reaches back past two other people. I second the Debunker's point. One move fixes it.
3. **A heading, "the line."** It refers ahead to a line A6 has not yet named. I second the Debunker and Realist. One phrase fixes it.

**For the censor (citation only; not this school's domain):** A6 cites *Samaritanus Bonus* III for "even against a man's own request or consent." The explicit prohibition of euthanasia and assisted suicide is in SB V.1. If III was chosen deliberately, keep it. Otherwise "V.1" (or "III; V.1") is the more exact citation. Verify on vatican.va.

**Misstatements of this school:** none. The C row, the W1 frame and the W1(2) list of the best accounts without God ("each person must be answered one by one, and no total answers her"; "a person has the standing of one who may not be violated") state this school as its best defenders would.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects; v7 names nearly all of them):**
- Whether intention bears on permissibility (double effect) or only on an act's meaning (Scanlon, Thomson). The line between CCC 2279 and CCC 2277, and between Kolbe and the requested killing, rests on it, and it is labeled. The counterfactual test is on our side.
- Whether a competent, suffering adult may ask for death. Contractualists themselves split, some holding the line for the sake of the vulnerable but allowing exceptions. The Church holds it without exception. It is labeled and argued in the text, not assumed.
- Whether an inalienable right needs a Giver to explain why it cannot be waived, or can be explained by reasonable rejection alone ("keeping, not disposal" against Scanlon).
- Whether the gravity of a wrong follows what she *is* or what she *loses* (Exchange 1). After v7 this is a dispute over the labeled answer, not a gap in the inference.
- Whether the dead are owed forever as they were (road two), or each is still someone who can forgive (road three).
- Whether "each must be answered one by one" needs a ground beyond the worth of mutual recognition.
- Whether a Creator's standing, which is not equal to ours, can still be second-personal, and with it whether a real divine command could make a human the instrument of what no human may claim for himself.
- Whether truths about reasons need a home (W2; the realist standoff).
- Whether the good "offered" to each sufferer is kept. That is a promise made in advance, which this school cannot inspect.

## 7. New insights and lines worth keeping

- **The Catechism has the same trustee structure.** CCC 2278 has decisions made "by the patient if he is competent ... or, if not, by those legally entitled to act for the patient." That is the same structure the C row gives Scanlon's trustee for the newborn. The two accounts agree on *who* speaks for her. They part over why she is one of those who must be answered at all.
- **Narrower is stronger.** "No right to take her life" says exactly what the line needs and nothing that CCC 2310 contradicts. A claim that is no wider than it must be leaves the opponent nothing to work with.
- **The firefighter question belongs in the text, and v7 put it there.** Stating the dissenter's best question in his own words ("why may he not ask for death?") before giving the Church's answer is the most persuasive thing A6 does. It shows the reader that the book has heard the question.
- **The limit on ordinary care makes the case stronger.** Saying that food and water are owed *for as long as they nourish her* shows that the trust serves her and does not take her over. A reader who fears "stewardship" means paternalism needs to see that limit.
- **Some contractualists also hold the line against requested killing, for the vulnerable.** The reason is that the elderly and disabled could reasonably reject a principle that puts them under pressure to ask. The book need not use this. It shows that the Church's line is not unreasonable even to those who do not share its ground, though only the Giver explains why it has no exceptions.

**Lines worth keeping:**
- From v7: "His asking never makes it right for anyone to kill him."
- From v7: "No number of others has any right to take her life."
- From v7: "That right is never ours."
- From v7: "God says no to the wound and yes to her."
- From v7: "Never spends her as a mere means against her own good."
- From v6, kept: "Gave her life to her keeping and not to her disposal."
- New: "Had the guards spared them both, Kolbe would have had all he asked."

**To verify before use:** CDF, *Responses to Certain Questions ... concerning Artificial Nutrition and Hydration* (2007), and *Samaritanus Bonus* V.3, for the "for as long as" limit; *Samaritanus Bonus* III against V.1 for the request/consent citation; Marquis on the "future like ours" view; contractualist arguments against legalized euthanasia from the standpoint of the vulnerable; Scanlon, *Moral Dimensions* (2008); Thomson on double effect; *AZAPO* (1996). All are paraphrased only. John 10:18 (RSV-CE): "No one takes it from me, but I lay it down of my own accord."

## 8. Score

**9.5 / 10 as written; 9.5 / 10 after the repairs.** v7 took both of this school's Round 6 repairs, each in a better form than I proposed. Its new wording on the request, on Kolbe and Christ, on the right to take life and on CCC 2258 holds under this school's strongest pressure. The three Minor points are wording only. What keeps the score below 10 is the honest standoffs at parity, double effect and the inalienable right chief among them, which no change of wording can remove.
