## 1. School and voice

The staunch "a good God would never allow this" atheist speaks here, in the voices of Mackie, Rowe, Draper, Schellenberg, Maitzen, Law, Adams and Talbott, Morriston and Wielenberg, and Ivan Karamazov. In this final round he checks whether v7 took his four Round 6 repairs, then presses each new sentence against the rest of v7, against Thomism, and against Scripture's hardest texts.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

1. **Justice.** The brute badness of agony (Rowe), invention (Mackie), or ordinary moral intuition (Maitzen). *Holds as a position. The moral force of the argument from evil borrows A1.*
2. **Hard case.** A newborn in pain that cannot be relieved and who cannot be fed: many in this school would end her life. *Coherent, but it rests on a premise the book denies (A4, A6). v7's ordinary-care sentence now matters here (Exchange 9).*
3. **Equal dignity.** Invented, brute, or interests that come in degrees. *Breaks, or stops at a brute fact. The argument from evil presses hardest on the premise this school cannot itself ground (A4).*
4. **Flourishing.** Love, knowledge and relief of suffering, within mortality. *Coherent and bounded. On this view history's victims are never answered, and v7 rightly declines to count that as evidence.*

### Exchange 1. Maitzen and Draper: "faith adds that He permits no evil from which He will not draw a good, though that good is never the evil's wage"

*Attack (Maitzen):* Now you guarantee it. Every evil God permits will yield a good. Then ordinary morality is undercut. When I see a child suffer, I am told that God will draw a good from exactly this suffering. Why should I rush in? On your theism, the permitted evil is never simply bad. *Draper adds:* "Never the evil's wage" is a relabel. If God permits the evil *on condition* that a good comes of it, the good is what the evil buys.

*Reply:* (a) Against Maitzen, v7 answers in the same paragraph. The hands that pull a child from the fire are part of what God made real, "so His permission never excuses ours (CCC 2269)." We are among the causes, and the good God draws from an evil He permits includes the rescue He commands us to give. The evil itself "never thereby becomes good" (CCC 312), so a reason to prevent it remains. And one may never let or do evil so that good may come of it (CCC 1789). That a good will be drawn is a promise about God's action, not a judgment that the evil was worth having. (b) Against Draper, "wage" is exact, and Scripture uses the word in this very sense: "the wages of sin is death, but the free gift of God is eternal life" (Romans 6:23, RSV; verify). A wage is earned by what is paid for. The good God draws is not earned by the evil, not bought at her expense for someone else ("a good offered to her"), and not owed to the evil as its fruit. It is a gift given in the evil's place. Even the Church's teaching on merit fits this: what is rewarded is the love with which a suffering was borne, by grace (CCC 2008, 2011), never the evil done to her. (c) v7 says "a good," following CCC 324. It does not say "a greater good for the whole," so it makes no promise that Rowe could test case by case. Rowe's policy question stays on the scale as before.

*Rejoinder (Maitzen):* The comfort still blunts urgency in practice.

*Final reply:* That is a claim about psychology, not a contradiction. The Church's own saints are her best rescuers, and v7 grounds the duty to rescue in the dignity of causes, not in uncertainty about God's plan.

**Held.** The new clause joins the two answers without making a trade.

### Exchange 2. Mackie's felix-culpa dilemma against the Mary and St. Leo sentence

*Attack:* "Why He did not keep the first man from sinning, as by grace He kept Mary free of every sin ... the Church answers, with St. Leo the Great, only with what He brought from it." So the Fall was the occasion of the Redemption, and God answers my 1955 question with the good that came of the sin. Then either the Redemption needed the sin, and God had reason to want sin, or it did not, and the Fall bought nothing, so why permit it? And "only" is false: CCC 311 also answers with freedom.

*Reply:* (a) "Only" is exact in context. v7 has just said that free will "answers much of the evil we do to one another," and then asks the question that freedom cannot answer: why not keep him from sin, as He kept Mary, who was free? To *that* question, CCC 412 answers with St. Leo and with Aquinas, and both answer with the good brought. (b) The first horn is denied. God did not need the sin. Aquinas says that nothing prevents human nature from being raised to something greater even after sin (CCC 412), not that it could be raised only through sin. The evil never becomes good (CCC 312), and the good is not its wage (Exchange 1). (c) On the second horn, it is the Mackie 1955 standoff, already owned: why not free creatures who in fact never sin, given that God gives every act its being? v7 claims no settlement of how grace and freedom meet.

*Rejoinder (Mackie):* Then the honest answer is that you do not know why.

*Final reply:* That is what v7 says: "Beyond that she claims no explanation; only Christian faith as a whole answers it (CCC 309)."

**Held at parity.** "With St. Leo the Great" and "by grace" (CCC 491–493) make the sentence more accurate to CCC 412 and 493 than it was in v6. There is also a small gain: "gave to the very ones who fell" is Adams's own requirement for divine goodness, that the good be good *to the one who suffered*. It is stated here for the first sin. Whether it holds for those who refuse the gift is the hell standoff.

### Exchange 3. Rowe against "God says no to the wound and yes to her"

*Attack:* A bystander who says "no" while he watches, and could stop it, has only said a word. Worse, your own Aquinas says that in willing the order of the whole, God wills some things to be naturally corrupted (ST I q.19 a.9; q.49 a.2), and v7 quotes him. Then for the child with cancer, God does not say no to the wound. He wills it *per accidens*.

*Reply:* (a) The sentence follows the amor fati line, and its subject is "what was done to her," which is moral evil. God is "in no way, directly or indirectly," its cause (CCC 311). He judges it (*Spe Salvi* 44), and He takes her side. That is what a "no" is when it is not prevention. v7 never claims prevention ("He did not keep her from what was done to her"). (b) For the natural wound, v7 has already drawn the line that answers Aquinas. The order gives one *animal's* loss to another's life, but "a person it never gives away" (SCG III.112), and "the parasite takes from the child; God does not give her to it." For persons, the Church teaches that death is contrary to the Creator's plan (CCC 1008; verify) and that "God did not make death, and he does not delight in the death of the living" (Wisdom 1:13, RSV; verify). So the "no" holds for her wound too. (c) The bystander test is answered as in Round 3. A bystander cannot raise the child, heal her, and answer for her. Whether God's "no" is enough, without prevention, is the weight that v7 places on the scale.

**Held at parity.** The line is true as scoped, and it is the best short statement in the section of how permission differs from approval. Wisdom 1:13 could stand beside it (optional; Section 7).

### Exchange 4. Ivan on Christ's sacrifice: "he may not aim at his own death ... so Christ"

*Attack:* A6 now says a man may accept death at the hands of those who wrongly kill him, "so Christ," but "he may not aim at his own death." Your Christ is priest and victim. He "offered himself without blemish to God" (Hebrews 9:14, RSV; verify). He set His face to Jerusalem and said He had power to lay down His life. A sacrifice is intended by the one who offers it. Did He not aim at His death?

*Reply:* (a) The Church places the sacrifice in the will's love and obedience, not in death taken as the goal. Christ "freely accepted" His Passion (CCC 609). It is love "to the end" that gives His sacrifice its value (CCC 616; verify). He was obedient "unto death" (Philippians 2:8), which is how far His obedience went, not its object. Aquinas says He caused His death only by not preventing it, though He could have (ST III q.47 a.1; verify), and that the Passion was a sacrifice on the side of His will while His killers committed a crime (ST III q.48 a.3; verify). In Gethsemane He asked to be spared, and accepted. (b) Even on Ivan's stronger reading there is no contradiction, because v7 contains the reason. Christ says, "I have power to lay it down, and I have power to take it again" (John 10:18). W1(2) gives the principle: "He who gives life, and can give it back, may call it home; we, who can do neither, may not take it." A6's line binds those who cannot give life back. The one who could was not under it. (c) "Wrongly kill him" agrees with Scripture's own word for the killers, "and killed the Author of life" (Acts 3:15, RSV; verify). It agrees with "given, not taken" because the killing was theirs and the gift was His.

**Held.** The R2 repair, in the censor's form, makes A6 and the second reason agree, and W1(2) answers the hardest form of the attack.

### Exchange 5. Ivan on Kolbe: he *asked* to die

*Attack:* Kolbe stepped forward and asked the commandant to kill him instead of the other man. v7 says "his asking never makes it right for anyone to kill him," and holds him up as a model. So his asking was good and the killing was wrong. Which did he ask for?

*Reply:* He asked for the other man's life. He offered his own, and the guards chose the death. His asking did not make the killing right. It was murder, and the Church honors him as a martyr of charity. v7's wording ("offered himself in a condemned man's place"; "may not aim at his own death"; "his asking never makes it right for anyone to kill him") says exactly this.

**Held.** The censor's recast removed the Round 6 collision cleanly.

### Exchange 6. Morriston's dilemma against "whatever voice he claims to have heard in His name"

*Attack:* Either the sentence forbids acting on *any* claimed divine command to kill the innocent, and then it condemns Abraham, whom Hebrews 11 praises, and contradicts Aquinas's instrument reading (ST II-II q.64 a.6 ad 1). Or it allows acting on a *genuine* one, and then the terrorist says that his is genuine, and that he claims nothing "for himself," since he is only God's instrument.

*Reply:* (a) The first horn is not taken. The sentence says no one may claim the right *for himself*, in CCC 2258's own shape. Abraham claimed nothing for himself. The text says God tested him and stayed his hand, and Hebrews reads his faith as faith that God "was able to raise men even from the dead" (Hebrews 11:19, RSV; verify), which is W1(2)'s "He who gives life, and can give it back." (b) The second horn is closed by what the Church teaches about revelation in this age. Public revelation is complete in Christ, and the Church cannot accept any "revelation" that claims to surpass or correct it (CCC 66–67; verify). In Christ the Church teaches that the direct and voluntary killing of an innocent human being is always gravely immoral (*Evangelium Vitae* 57; verify). So no voice heard now can be credited as God's when it commands that. The man who acts on one is claiming for himself the standing that the sentence denies. (c) Whether God ever in fact commanded what 1 Samuel 15:3 reports is the conquest-texts standoff, owned since Round 6 (*Dei Verbum* 15; *Verbum Domini* 42, verify).

*Rejoinder (Wielenberg):* The closure of revelation is your premise, not mine.

*Final reply:* Granted. But the objection was that the sentence is either false or empty on the book's own terms, and on those terms it is neither.

**Held at parity.** One read-aloud point: "claims ... may claim" repeats the verb (optional; Section 6).

### Exchange 7. Draper: "God's permission is not a trade" against the lion

*Attack:* The second reason opens by saying that God's permission is not a trade. Four sentences later it says the order "does give one creature's loss to another's life," and CCC 324 speaks of God permitting *physical* evil. The fawn is traded for the lion.

*Reply:* This was settled in substance in Round 4. "Trade" in this book is the trade A6 forbids, the spending of a *person*. v7 scopes it in the same paragraph ("A person it never gives away"), and it owns the fawn's thinner hope.

**Held.** The text answers the objection itself. The opening clause could carry its scope in its own words (optional; Section 6).

### Exchange 8. Ivan and the mother: "God does not take her place in giving the pardon that is hers to give"

*Attack:* My brother Alyosha's God forgives everyone, and the mother embraces the torturer of her child. I will not have it. She has no right to forgive what was done to her child. Only the child can. That harmony is bought with the child's tears, and I return the ticket.

*Reply:* On the first half, v7 stands with Ivan. No one on earth gives the pardon in the victim's place, "not her friends, not a nation by vote," and "God does not take her place in giving the pardon that is hers to give." Grace "does not make wrong into right," and the wrongdoer does not sit beside his victim "as though nothing had happened" (*Spe Salvi* 44). On the second half, v7 now says in so many words that the good God draws is "never the evil's wage," and that it is given to the very ones who suffered. No harmony is bought *with* her tears. What is promised is that she herself will be kept, raised, and able to forgive or not.

*Rejoinder (Ivan):* I still return the ticket.

*Final reply:* That is love's protest, and it attacks no premise. The book honors it.

**Held at parity.** This is a real gain for v7. Its W1(1) agrees with the strongest moral premise in Ivan's revolt, and its "never the evil's wage" denies the purchase that Ivan refuses.

### Exchange 9. Rowe's newborn and the ordinary-care sentence

*Attack:* v7 now says that "the ordinary care owed to the sick, food and water among it, may not be withdrawn." Take a dying newborn whose gut can no longer take in food. Your God's law would have food forced into her as she dies. That is cruelty in the name of life.

*Reply:* That is not the Church's teaching. *Samaritanus Bonus* V.3 (checked this session) calls nutrition and hydration obligatory care, but says they are to be suspended when the body "cannot absorb them or cannot metabolize them," or when they no longer benefit the patient. CCC 2279's "cannot be legitimately interrupted" concerns care that still does what care is for.

*Rejoinder (Rowe):* Then v7's sentence says more than the document it cites.

*Final reply:* Granted. The doctrine answers the attack at parity, but v7's wording overstates it, and an opponent of this school will quote the wording.

**Held with repair (Minor).** One clause from *Samaritanus Bonus* V.3 (R1). This is a fidelity point that the censor shares, and it changes no conclusion.

### Exchange 10. Schellenberg, Adams and Talbott: anything new?

*Attack:* Is hiddenness, hell, or the unbaptized infant any better off in v7?

*Reply:* No, and v7 does not pretend otherwise. The only new wording is CCC 1257, which places the asymmetry in what the Church knows, not in what God is bound to. Everything else stays as owned weight.

**Held at parity.** The standoffs are unchanged.

## 7. New insights and lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **"Wage" is Romans 6:23's word.** The good God draws from an evil is gift, never wage. That is why "permits no evil from which He will not draw a good" is not a trade, and why the Church's teaching on merit does not contradict it. What is rewarded is the love that bore the suffering, never the evil done (Exchange 1).
- **v7 stands with Ivan where Ivan is right.** No one, not even God, gives the pardon in the victim's place. The harmony Ivan refuses is one bought *with* the child's tears, and v7 now denies that any good is bought that way (Exchange 8).
- **John 10:18 and W1(2) say the same thing.** The one who has "power to take it again" may lay His life down. We, who cannot give it back, may not take it. That is why Christ is the pattern for A6 and not an exception to it (Exchange 4).
- **"Gave to the very ones who fell" is Adams's own condition.** God must be good *to* each one who suffers. v7 states this for the first sin. The open question is the one who refuses the gift, and that is the hell standoff (Exchange 2).
- **Optional:** Wisdom 1:13 (RSV: "God did not make death, and he does not delight in the death of the living") could stand beside "God says no to the wound." It shows that the "no" covers the natural wound of a person, not only the moral one (Exchange 3).

**Lines worth keeping**
- "God says no to the wound and yes to her." (v7)
- "Though that good is never the evil's wage." (v7)
- "That right is never ours." (v7)
- "A yes said to whatever happens is said to no one." (v6, kept)
- "God gives the act its being, never its defect." (v6, kept)
- "Given to her keeping and not to her disposal." (v6, kept)
- "He did not keep her from what was done to her; that weight is on the other side of the scale, and this book weighs it there." (v5, kept)
- "He may take her to Himself; He never gives her to what took her." (Round 5, still optional)
