# Argument Lab, Round 7 (final): Utilitarianism / Consequentialism vs. v7

## 1. School and voice

The opponent speaks for impartialist consequentialism at full strength: Sidgwick's axioms of benevolence, Hare's two levels, Singer's preference utilitarianism and his selective debunking of deontological intuitions, de Lazari-Radek and Singer's realist hedonism and their defence of esoteric morality, Hooker's rule consequentialism with its disaster rule, and the consequentialist leg of Parfit's Triple Theory. This round checks whether v7 took my five Round 6 Minors, and whether v7's new wording (the A heading, A4's parenthesis, A6 on Christ and Kolbe, the utilitarian C row, W1(2)'s CCC 2278–2279 sentence, and the conclusion's "grown man") breaks anything.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges (short)

1. **Justice:** each counts for one, and no one for more than one. Justice is the set of rules whose general acceptance best protects everyone's most vital interests. **Coherent, and borrows nothing. It breaks** where "owed" is made to come after "best" (A2).
2. **Hard case:** the hijacked airliner heading for a full stadium: shoot it down, since the passengers die either way and thousands are saved. **Holds as a decision**, and it is a parity case, since Catholic moralists divide on it. The school pays its price at the secret transplant (A6), which it can forbid only as a bad rule, not as a wrong to the patient.
3. **Equal dignity:** equal *consideration* of interests, yes. Equal *worth* whatever one's capacities, no (Singer; McMahan's graded view of the newborn). **Breaks at the ground, honestly.** W1(2) and the conclusion, now with two contrasts, answer this directly.
4. **Ultimate good:** well-being (pleasure, or satisfied preference), seen from the point of view of the universe. **Coherent, but thin at the summit.** Impartial reason can see that each one counts; only a person can want each one (W3).

## 3. Debate highlights

**First: did v7 take my Round 6 repairs?**
- **Minor 1 (A6 "since" clause): taken in substance.** v7 turns it into the dissenters' own question: "if he may lay down his life for others, as a firefighter does (John 15:13), why may he not ask for death?" The mercy dissent is named two sentences later ("many who hold the rest of A would let a dying man ask for death"). The question now sets up the Church's answer, which follows at once. Closed.
- **Minor 2 ("wrongly take it, as Christ did" against "given, not taken"): taken.** It now reads "accept the death that comes, even at the hands of those who wrongly kill him: so Christ, who laid down His life of His own accord (John 10:18; CCC 609)". A6 and Evil, *Second* now say the same thing. Closed.
- **Minor 3 (utilitarian C row, Hare vs. Hooker): taken.** Rule consequentialism is now matched to "hold firm rules against killing the innocent," and Hare to "live by them day to day as if they had no exceptions." Nothing in the row is now false of Hooker. Closed.
- **Minor 4 (A heading singular "claim"): taken.** "deny the Church's teaching that the line holds at every number and against a man's own request, which A6 names as this book's claims." Closed.
- **Minor 5 ("a wrong done to *her*" in A6): taken.** It now reads "a wrong done to *him*, the one spent." Closed.

All five taken. Exchanges 1–10 test the new wording.

**Exchange 1: the A heading now names the numbers. Does that overclaim what "many who hold all the rest" deny?**
*Utilitarian:* The heading says those who hold "all the rest" deny only the Church's teaching "that the line holds at every number and against a man's own request." But Singer and I deny A4's non-voluntary clause too.
*Defense:* That is why the heading names Singer separately ("denies parts of A2, A4, A6 and A7"). "Many who hold all the rest" is a different group: threshold deontologists and the Foot-style secular moralist, who grant A4 entire and part from the Church only on vast numbers and the request.
*Result:* **Held.** The heading, A4's parenthesis and A6 now name the same two claims as the book's.

**Exchange 2: A4's parenthesis turns questions into "claims."**
*Utilitarian:* "(Whether those lines hold however vast the numbers, and whether a man's own asking could ever make it right to kill him, which the Church denies (...), A6 names as this book's claims.)" A question is not a claim. And "which the Church denies" sits inside a parenthesis inside a parenthesis, so a reader hearing it aloud may take the Church to deny that the lines hold at vast numbers.
*Defense:* Read in order, "which the Church denies" attaches to "could ever make it right," and the sense is clear on the page.
*Result:* **Held; Minor** (read-aloud). The meaning is right; the syntax makes the reader work, and it leans on the reader to turn two "whether" questions into the Church's two answers (repair in §6).

**Exchange 3: Christ "laid down His life of His own accord." Then what is the difference from aiming at one's death?**
*Utilitarian:* I accept the success test for Kolbe: had the guards spared all ten, he would have had everything he wanted. But apply it to Christ. The Church says He redeemed us *by His death* (Mark 10:45, a ransom). Then His death is the means to the end, just as the volunteer's death is the means to the data. v7 has Christ "laying down His life of His own accord" in the same clause as "accept the death that comes." Either Christ aimed at His death, which A6 forbids, or the volunteer did not, which A6 needs.
*Defense:* Three points, all within Catholic teaching. First, the text places Christ under "accept the death that comes, even at the hands of those who wrongly kill him." He did not kill Himself or ask anyone to kill Him. Second, the Church locates the redeeming value of the Cross not in death as such but in love: it is love "to the end" that gives Christ's sacrifice its value as redemption and satisfaction (CCC 616; verify wording), and His humanity was the "free and perfect instrument" of that love (CCC 609). Aquinas likewise says Christ was the cause of His own death only in that He did not prevent it when He could (ST III q.47 a.1; verify). What His will sought was obedience to the Father and our salvation; the death was what lawless men did (Acts 2:23, "crucified and killed by the hands of lawless men"). Third, the volunteer's case is the reverse: the data need his death, and the researcher who gives it to him claims a right to kill. A6's last clause ("his asking never makes it right for anyone to kill him") applies to the researcher whatever the volunteer intends.
*Rejoinder:* Acts 2:23 also says He was "delivered up according to the definite plan and foreknowledge of God." The plan included the death.
*Defense:* It included the wrong men would do and the love that would meet it. God's plan permitting and using a sinful act does not make the act right, which is the book's own line in Evil, *Second* (CCC 311–312).
*Result:* **Held at parity.** This is a unique case and the theology is deep, but the text claims no more than John 10:18 and CCC 609, and the line it draws (accept, never aim, never authorize another to kill) sorts Christ, Kolbe and the volunteer correctly.

**Exchange 4: "By his own hand or another's" and "his asking never makes it right for anyone to kill him": the battlefield request.**
*Utilitarian:* A soldier, mortally wounded and about to be taken for torture, begs his comrade to shoot him. My school says the comrade does well. v7 says his asking "never makes it right."
*Defense:* Yes, and A6 says plainly that this is the book's claim, "not shared," and that many who hold the rest of A would allow it. The book argues it in W1(2): the trust forbids aiming at her death, not easing her dying, and the right to take her life is not ours. The comrade may stay with him, ease his pain, and fight for him; the Church does not ask more of him than that.
*Result:* **Held at parity.** This is the honest euthanasia standoff, now named in A4, A6 and the heading. Censor item 2's fix ("his asking never makes it right," replacing "no one may kill him because he asks") also removes the collision with Kolbe that I raised in Round 6, Exchange 4.

**Exchange 5: "Yet even those who would cross the line for a city will not cross it for five."**
*Utilitarian:* As a report on most people, true. As a report on my school, not quite. A6's surgeon case stipulates "even if no one would ever know." The strictest act utilitarians grant that in that idealized case the killing would be right, and say only that in the real world the case never arises and the rule should never be taught (Smart; de Lazari-Radek and Singer, on esoteric morality; verify the exact positions). So some who would cross for a city would also, in principle, cross for five. "Almost everyone sees these at once" two sentences earlier grants exceptions; this sentence, with "even those," grants none.
*Defense:* The sentence is about the many who bomb a city to end a war, not about a handful of theorists, and the A heading already says Singer denies parts of A6.
*Result:* **Held; Minor.** The point stands as a small misstatement of my school's strictest members, and adding "most" fixes it. It also gives the book an opening it can use: the few who would cross for five say it must be kept secret, which is A7's case against them (§6, §7). This sentence was in v6 as well; I raise it now as a Minor, not as something v7 broke.

**Exchange 6: Selective debunking (Singer, de Lazari-Radek and Singer, Greene).**
*Utilitarian:* My school's strongest answer to A4 and A6 is not error theory. It is selective debunking. The intuition against pushing the man off the footbridge tracks up-close personal force, which evolution gave us for reasons that have nothing to do with truth. The axiom of universal benevolence, by contrast, is not something evolution would select for, so it survives. The A heading's own test ("shows that what produced the belief had nothing to do with its truth") then condemns A6's constraints and spares my axiom.
*Defense:* Three replies. First, A6's cases are not personal-force cases. A city that secretly hangs an innocent man, a surgeon's anaesthetic, a lethal experiment ordered from an office: the intuition holds without pushing anyone. Second, the test cuts the other way at the place the book cares most about. Evolution favors the healthy, the beautiful and our own, and A4 says so: "for the infant no one is drawn to hold." The conviction that the exposed, deformed newborn was wronged no less than a strong one runs *against* what selection would give us, so by Singer's own criterion it survives debunking better than the instincts that left her on the hillside. Third, the axiom of benevolence is not safe either: philosophers have argued that evolution can explain a general disposition to care, and that the debunking argument, once begun, does not stop where the utilitarian wants it to (Kahane, "Evolutionary Debunking Arguments," 2011; paraphrase, verify). By the fairness rule, a genealogy we will not let our side use against him he may not use against us, and here the same genealogy reaches both.
*Result:* **Held at parity.** The A heading points to W2 and the conclusion for this, and the conclusion's "the newborn whom instinct passed by" is the right reply. Worth making explicit in the chapter (§7).

**Exchange 7: The utilitarian C row as v7 now states it.**
*Utilitarian:* "It makes every person, in principle, someone who may be deliberately killed for better totals (A4, A6)." Hooker would say his ideal code forbids killing the innocent.
*Defense:* It forbids it because, and as far as, such a rule produces the best totals, and his code keeps a rule to prevent disaster that can override it. "In principle" is exact. And since A4 no longer claims the line at every number as shared, the citation of A4 and A6 together is fair: A4 for the shared clauses, A6 for the book's claim.
*Result:* **Held.** The rest of the row (CCC 1754, the betrayed secret and the letter, Sidgwick and esoteric morality, Sidgwick's fellowship) is accurate and fair to this school.

**Exchange 8: W1(2)'s new CCC 2278–2279 sentence: food and water.**
*Utilitarian:* v7 says "the ordinary care owed to the sick, food and water among it, may not be withdrawn (CCC 2278–2279; *Samaritanus Bonus* V.3)." Then your trust forces tube-feeding into a dying body that can no longer absorb it, which is cruelty in the name of a rule. That is exactly the rule-worship my school accuses deontology of.
*Defense:* The Church does not teach that. *Samaritanus Bonus* V.3, the very passage cited, says nutrition and hydration are owed "insofar as and until" they demonstrably nourish the patient; when the body cannot absorb or metabolize them, "their administration should be suspended"; and artificial feeding is owed provided it does not cause harm or intolerable suffering (checked on vatican.va this round). In any other circumstance, withdrawal would be unjust. v7's word "owed" carries this: what no longer nourishes her is not owed.
*Rejoinder:* "Owed" carries it for a careful reader. The sentence as it reads aloud is unconditional, and it cites a source that states the condition.
*Result:* **Held; Minor.** The argument is untouched (the trust forbids aiming at her death, and suspending food that cannot nourish her does not aim at it). But the citation should state what it cites, and it costs one clause (§6). Everything else in the sentence matches CCC 2278–2279: "burdensome or out of proportion to what it can do," "even at the risk of shortening her days," and the ordinary care that "cannot be legitimately interrupted."

**Exchange 9: The conclusion's grown man: McMahan, Singer, Marquis.**
*Utilitarian:* In Round 6 the Realist was right that the strong child varied strength only. The grown man now varies mind. Singer and McMahan will answer directly: killing the newborn is a lesser wrong than killing the grown man, because her time-relative interest in going on living is weak; she has few psychological connections to her own future. That is the graded view, and v7 now faces it squarely. But a third reader slips between the two: the deprivation theorist (Marquis's "future like ours"). He agrees she was wronged no less than the grown man, because she loses as much future, or more. Then her worth follows her future, not "what she is."
*Defense:* The text survives both. Against Singer and McMahan, it asks the question it should: which are you surer of, that she was wronged no less, or the argument that says she was not? That is not a refutation and does not claim to be one ("it cannot compel the one who gives up A4"). Against the deprivation reader, the inference v7 draws still holds: if she was wronged no less than either, her worth did not follow her present strength or her present mind. The next step, "it follows what she is," is labelled "this book answers," so it is offered as the book's answer, not as entailed. And the deprivation answer has its own price, which W1(2) names: it makes the disabled newborn with little future worth less, and A4 says no one is.
*Result:* **Held.** The repair the Realist asked for is in and works: one contrast for each ground eliminated, and the book's own answer labelled as such. The deprivation account is an honest standoff, and the text does not overclaim against it.

**Exchange 10: Is the book's God a consequentialist? (Evil, *Second*)**
*Utilitarian:* "Faith adds that He permits no evil from which He will not draw a good." That is a greater-good theodicy, the very reasoning A6 forbids us. Your God may let the child suffer for the total; we may not push the man for the five.
*Defense:* The text keeps three things apart. God permits; He does not do the evil, which is the creature's own (CCC 311). The good drawn is "never the evil's wage," so the evil is not the price paid for it; and "God never spends anyone as a mere means against her own good" (SCG III.112). Why He permits is the setting, a world of real causes, which is a good of persons as causes (CCC 306), not a sum bought by spending some. And the text does not claim this settles it: "whether it is worth this world's cost is part of the weighing, not settled by it."
*Rejoinder:* Permitting a foreseen evil for the sake of a good order is my structure.
*Defense:* It is also the structure of every ethic that has a doing/allowing line, and "His permission never excuses ours" (CCC 2269) keeps our duties where A6 put them.
*Result:* **Held at parity.** Not a defect of the text; the weight is honestly left on the scale.

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

- **The infant whom instinct passed by is the book's answer to selective debunking.** Singer's test spares any belief evolution would not have given us. The conviction that the exposed, deformed newborn was wronged no less than a strong one runs against everything selection favors, which is why the ancient world left her on the hillside. By the utilitarian's own test, that conviction is among the hardest to debunk. One sentence in the chapter, near A4 or the conclusion, would turn the strongest utilitarian weapon around: "Evolution explains why we pass her by; it does not explain why we know we should not."
- **The few who would cross the line for five must hide it.** That is the plainest link between A6 and A7 against this school: a morality that can be true only if most people never learn it has already conceded something about truth. The book has Sidgwick in the C row; A6 can point there.
- **Christ's death and the success test.** Where the success test grows hard (the Cross), the Church's answer is CCC 616: the value lies in love "to the end," not in death as such. That sentence, in Chapter 9 (sacrifice), would keep a careful reader from turning Kolbe's test against Calvary.
- **v7's seam between A, W and C held under the last round's probes.** Every "never," "every" and "not even" in A is now either shared or named as the book's claim in the same words in three places (heading, A4, A6).

**Lines worth keeping**
- "He may give his life for another; he may not aim at his own death."
- "His asking never makes it right for anyone to kill him."
- "Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent."
- "No one may take what is not his to take, even to keep others from taking more."
- "Given to her keeping and not to her disposal."
- "God says no to the wound and yes to her."
- "Evolution explains why we pass her by; it does not explain why we know we should not." (new; proposed)
