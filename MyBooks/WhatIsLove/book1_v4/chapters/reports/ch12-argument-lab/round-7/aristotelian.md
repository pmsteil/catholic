# Argument Lab, Round 7 (final): Secular Neo-Aristotelian Naturalism / Virtue Ethics vs. v7

## 1. School and voice

This is the school of Philippa Foot, Rosalind Hursthouse, Michael Thompson, John McDowell and Martha Nussbaum. Goodness is measured against the form of life of one's kind, and justice is bipolar: a wrong is done *to* someone, and she has the complaint. In Round 6 this school reported "As written: NONE," with one Minor point ("mere means") and one optional point ("those lines"). This round checks both, then tests v7's new wording sentence by sentence against v6.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

*(The Round 1–6 answers stand. Briefly:)*
- **Q1. Justice.** Each is given what is due to her, and the wrong is done to her (Thompson; NE V). **Holds.** It borrows only the *equality* of all parties.
- **Q2. A hard case.** Do not take a ventilator from a stable disabled patient to give it to a younger one. That is doing harm, not failing to help (Foot). **Coherent.**
- **Q3. Equal dignity.** Membership in the rational kind, plus the shape of justice. **Holds** for equality and for "not for others." **Breaks**, by the Church's lights, on chosen death (Foot, 1977).
- **Q4. Ultimate good.** *Eudaimonia*: a complete life of virtuous activity among friends, bounded by death. **Coherent.** It remains the strongest secular reason to deny that W3's longing belongs to human nature.

### 3b. Exchanges

**E1. A6: "he may not aim at his own death, by his own hand or another's." Double effect against Foot's doing and allowing.**
*Opponent:* v6 said "make his own death the thing he chooses"; v7 says "aim at." That is the language of double effect, and this school's founder was its best-known critic (1967). Her closeness problem asks what stops a volunteer from saying he aims only at the knowledge the experiment yields, with his death a foreseen side effect. So "aim" lets the volunteer through, and A6 names the volunteer as a case the Church forbids.
*Defense:* The sentence has two clauses, and they do different work. The first, "he may not aim at his own death," governs the giver's will. The second, "his asking never makes it right for anyone to kill him," governs the other agent's act, and it is drawn by *doing*, Foot's own category. The lethal experimenter kills, whatever the volunteer intends, so the second clause catches him on Foot's distinction and leaves no room for the closeness dodge. The case Foot's distinction alone might let through is the one where death comes by omission: a patient who refuses treatment *in order to* die. The first clause catches that one, and it does so in CCC 2277's own terms ("an act or omission which, of itself or by intention, causes death"). Run the cases in the text. Kolbe: the guards kill him, and his aim is the other man's life, so he is innocent on both principles. The firefighter: the fire kills him, and he aims at the rescue, so again both principles clear him. Euthanasia at request is a killing and is aimed, so both principles classify it alike; the one thing left in dispute is whether a waiver could make it right, which is the Foot/CCC 2280 standoff and not a dispute over principles. The volunteer for a lethal experiment is caught by the doing clause.
*Opponent's rejoinder:* Granted. The coupling is better than either principle alone, because each clause covers the other's blind spot. One gap remains, and it is a precision point. "Aim at" in English most naturally names an *end*. CCC 2279's test is that death is not willed "as either an end or a means." A man who chooses death as the way to end his pain can say he aims at relief.
*Result:* **Held.** The coupling is a real improvement on v6 (Insight 1). The end-or-means point is **Minor (optional)** (R7.2). The second clause already closes the gap whenever another person does the killing, and W1(2) "what the trust forbids is that her death be the thing aimed at" carries the same sense.

**E2. "So Christ, who laid down His life of His own accord (John 10:18; CCC 609)," against "he may not aim at his own death."**
*Opponent:* If Christ laid down His life "of His own accord" and came "to give his life as a ransom" (Mark 10:45), He seems to have willed His death. The book's model case then breaks its own rule.
*Defense:* The grammar places Christ under "accept the death that comes, even at the hands of those who wrongly kill him." Those who killed Him did wrong; He freely accepted it (CCC 609). And John 10:18 goes on: "I have power to lay it down, and I have power to take it again." That is exactly the title W1(2) says we lack: "He who gives life, and can give it back, may call it home; we, who can do neither, may not take it." So the verse that seems to threaten the rule is the clearest statement of why the rule binds us and not Him.
*Result:* **Held at parity.** This also closes the censor's Round 6 point that "wrongly take it" sat badly with John 10:18.

**E3. W1(2): "the ordinary care owed to the sick, food and water among it, may not be withdrawn (CCC 2278–2279; *Samaritanus Bonus* V.3)."**
*Opponent:* In Round 6 (E7) I granted that v6's sentence answered a fair charge before anyone made it: the trust is not a duty to prolong every dying at any cost. v7's new clause partly reopens that charge. Read strictly, it says food and water may *never* be withdrawn, including from a dying patient whose body can no longer take them in, for whom forced fluids cause swelling, choking and distress. Foot's argument from charity (1977) would call that cruelty in the name of a trust. The sentence also sits uneasily beside "she may refuse treatment that is burdensome."
*Defense:* The Church's teaching is not what the strict reading says. *Samaritanus Bonus* V.3 calls nutrition and hydration care rather than therapy, and obligatory "insofar as and until" it actually nourishes the patient. When the body can no longer absorb or metabolize it, it should be suspended, and doing so does not unlawfully hasten death. Artificial feeding is owed provided it does not cause harm or intolerable suffering (paraphrased from vatican.va; verify exact wording). Because food and water are care and not therapy, there is no conflict with "refuse treatment that is burdensome": the two clauses govern different things.
*Opponent's rejoinder:* Then the Church has the answer and the sentence leaves it out. A reader who knows only this page cannot tell.
*Result:* **Held with repair (Minor).** The argument does not depend on the clause, and our side answers at parity from the very document cited. As written, though, the clause overstates the teaching and hands the charitable critic an opening that v6 had closed (R7.1). A doctrinal-precision point for the censor as well.

**E4. Flourishing row: "as though, the Church would say, a person owned his life rather than holding it in trust (CCC 2280; this book's claim, A6)."**
*Opponent:* Foot never said a person owns his life. Her ground was that the right to life, like other rights, can be waived by the one who holds it.
*Defense:* "The Church would say" marks the ownership reading as the Church's diagnosis, not as Foot's premise. A right whose holder may waive it, and with it the holder's life, is what CCC 2280 means by being an owner and not a steward ("We are stewards, not owners of the life God has entrusted to us"). The phrase "holding it in trust" also matches W1(2)'s "held in trust from the One who alone is Lord of life," so the row and the witness now use one image.
*Result:* **Held.** The row states this school's position fairly and labels the Church's reply.

**E5. The conclusion: "a strong newborn left beside her, or a grown man left there to die."**
*Opponent:* Test it as an argument by elimination. The strong newborn varies strength and holds immaturity fixed; the grown man varies mind. If she is wronged no less than both, her worth follows neither strength nor mind. That is valid; the Round 6 Serious item is repaired. Now a Footian check: on the doing/allowing distinction the wrong of abandonment depends on who left her and what he owed her. If the grown man were only a stranger whom someone failed to rescue, the contrast would change a second variable.
*Defense:* "Left there" is said of both, on the same hillside, in one breath. The natural reading is that the same hands put each there, which is exposure, a doing, and a breach of the duty owed to one in one's care. And "left there to die" says he cannot leave, so helplessness is held fixed as well. The two contrasts vary only what they are meant to vary.
*Opponent:* Then this school signs the conclusion, and that is worth saying plainly. "It follows what she is" is our answer too: membership in the rational kind (Thompson's natural-historical judgment; Foot's "natural goodness"). The conclusion is common ground with the Aristotelian. The God-work is done earlier, in W1(2) ("a gift exactly as far as her being is a gift"; "no order wants").
*Defense:* That is by design. The conclusion addresses "the one who gives up A4" (Singer, McMahan), and "this book answers" marks "what she is" as the book's answer, not a theorem. The Aristotelian has already granted A4.
*Result:* **Held.** No repair.

**E6. W1(2): "no number of others has any right to take her life. No one may take what is not his to take, even to keep others from taking more."**
*Opponent:* v6 said "any right over her life," and v7 narrows it to "any right to take her life." Does the narrowing weaken the case against using her as a mere means short of killing?
*Defense:* The mere-means clause stands in A4 and in "never spends her as a mere means against her own good," so nothing is lost. The narrowing is what CCC 2310 requires, since the community may ask citizens to serve, even at risk. "Even to keep others from taking more" is the agent-relative constraint, and Foot pressed it against consequentialism in her own name: one may not kill one to prevent two killings, because there is no impersonal "best state of affairs" that commands it (Foot, "Utilitarianism and the Virtues," 1985; paraphrase, verify).
*Result:* **Held.** It is shared ground with this school's founder.

**E7. "Whatever voice he claims to have heard in His name, may claim it for himself (CCC 2258)."**
*Opponent:* In Round 6 (E6) I held v6's "no voice that claims His name can make it ours to take" as consistent with Aquinas's Abraham exception (ST II-II q.64 a.6 ad 1). Does the new form still fit?
*Defense:* Yes, and more closely. CCC 2258 says no one can "claim for himself" the right directly to destroy an innocent human being. Even at God's true command, the one who obeys acts as instrument, so the right remains God's, never "his." The sentence keeps the possessive ("That right is never ours").
*Result:* **Held.**

**E8. Smaller points.**
(a) A4's parenthesis now states the Church's denial outright: "whether a man's own asking could ever make it right to kill him, which the Church denies (CCC 2277; EV 65–66)." That removes v6's hint that asking might make it right, and the claim stays labeled as the book's. **Held.**
(b) A6: "if he may lay down his life for others, as a firefighter does (John 15:13), why may he not ask for death?" This is put as the dissenters' own question. It is not Foot's exact route (hers is the waiver of a right), but it is the common form of the question and is fairly stated. **Held.**
(c) God row: "why that line has no exceptions and is not even her own to cross," labeled "as the Church teaches and this book argues, though not every reader grants it." Foot is among those who do not grant it, and the label covers her. **Held.**
(d) W1(2): "few say why the line is not even hers to waive." This is accurate as to Foot, who held that it is hers to waive. **Held.**
(e) W1(2): "doing harm is not the same as failing to help, which is Philippa Foot's own answer to the magistrate and the mob." This is accurate: the magistrate-and-mob case is Foot's own (1967), and she answered it with negative against positive duties. **Held.**

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**Against v7 as written:**

**As written: NONE.**

v7 took both of this school's Round 6 repairs. Its new wording breaks no step of the argument. A6's new line is stronger against this school than v6's was. The intention clause governs the giver and the doing clause governs anyone else, so the line survives Foot's critique of double effect and still catches omissions aimed at death. On every case in the text it agrees with doing/allowing (E1). Christ's case fits the rule by John 10:18's own words (E2). The conclusion's two contrasts make the elimination valid, and this school grants its result (E5). The one new flaw is that one clause states the teaching too broadly. Our side answers it at parity from the document the clause cites, so it is Minor.

**Minor (wording and precision):**
1. **W1(2) food and water** (E3). "The ordinary care owed to the sick, food and water among it, may not be withdrawn" leaves out *Samaritanus Bonus* V.3's own limit: such care is owed as long as it actually nourishes the patient, and it is suspended when the body can no longer take it in, or when giving it causes harm or intolerable suffering. Read strictly, the clause partly reopens the "duty to prolong every dying" charge that v6 had closed. (The censor should check this too.)
2. *(Optional)* **A6 "aim at his own death"** (E1). CCC 2279's test is "either an end or a means." "Aim" most naturally names an end. The doing clause already closes the gap whenever another person does the killing.

**Misstatements of this school:** none. Foot (the magistrate and the mob; waivable rights; voluntary euthanasia in principle), McDowell (no standpoint outside the life of reason), Nussbaum (a human life must end to be human) and Xunzi's placement are all stated fairly.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**
- Whether a nature's standards need a source, or whether asking for one wants a standpoint outside the life of reason (McDowell).
- Whether necessary truths, the core of the moral law among them, need a ground in a Mind; the reach of minds to what holds for any mind (W2).
- The tragic view of the dead (a wrong fixed forever that no one receives) against "she is still someone" (W1(1)).
- Whether the shape of justice that protects her is simply "what justice is," or is explained by a title no number of others has, from One who wills each for her own sake.
- Double effect against doing/allowing. On every case in the text, v7's two clauses give the same verdict as Foot's distinction (E1). Edge cases outside the text, such as Captain Oates walking out into the snow, are still disputed between the two principles.
- Foot against CCC 2280: whether a life that is no longer a good to the one who lives it may be ended at his request. This remains the one real moral disagreement between this school and the Church, stated at its true size and labeled as the book's claim.
- Whether W3's longing belongs to human nature or is a Christian shaping of the wish for more (Nussbaum).
- The theist's heavier load on the lion and the parasite, against the naturalist's weaker hold on the tortured child.

## 7. New insights and lines worth keeping

**New insights**
1. **A6's two clauses cover each other's blind spots.** "He may not aim at his own death" governs the giver's will and catches omissions aimed at death, which Foot's doing/allowing alone can miss. "His asking never makes it right for anyone to kill him" governs the other agent's act by *doing*, which catches the lethal experimenter whatever the volunteer says he intends, and so answers Foot's closeness problem. So the book's line does not depend on winning the debate between double effect and doing/allowing. Keep both clauses (E1).
2. **John 10:18 states the rule's reason in Christ's own mouth.** "I have power to lay it down, and I have power to take it again" is the title W1(2) says we lack. If a critic presses Christ's free death against the rule, the next clause of the same verse answers him (E2).
3. **The neo-Aristotelian signs the conclusion.** "Her worth follows what she is" is this school's own answer. For this reader the conclusion is common ground, and the case for God rests on W1(2): worth that is a gift as far as being is a gift, a life held in trust, and the person whom no order wants for her own sake. The book need not press the conclusion harder against this school than it does (E5).
4. **Food and water: state the Church's teaching with its own limit.** The teaching is more humane than its shortest summary. Saying so takes the charge of cruelty off the table (E3).

**Lines worth keeping**
- "Accept the death that comes, even at the hands of those who wrongly kill him."
- "His asking never makes it right for anyone to kill him."
- "What the trust forbids is that her death be the thing aimed at."
- "No one may take what is not his to take, even to keep others from taking more."
- "He who gives life, and can give it back, may call it home; we, who can do neither, may not take it."
- "Wronged no less than a strong newborn left beside her, or a grown man left there to die."
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it."

## 8. Score

**v7 as written: 9.5/10. With repairs: 9.5/10 (this school's ceiling).** v7 took every repair this school proposed and made its martyr line stronger against Foot than v6's was. One clause about food and water states the teaching too broadly. What remains beyond it are honest standoffs at parity, chief among them the real moral disagreement between Foot and CCC 2280, which no wording can or should remove.
