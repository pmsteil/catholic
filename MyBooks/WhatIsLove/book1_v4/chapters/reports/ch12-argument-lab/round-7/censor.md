# Round 7: Doctrinal Censor's Fifth Read (v7, pre-imprimatur, final round)

**Scope.** v7 only ("## v7 — Round 7" in EVOLUTION.md). First I checked every Round 6 finding (N1-6 to N6-6, M1–M4, and the optional rows). Then I read every sentence that changed from v6 to v7, found by a sentence-level diff (27 changed or new sentences), against Scripture (RSVCE), the Catechism, *Evangelium Vitae*, *Samaritanus Bonus*, *Spe Salvi* and St. Thomas. I gave the places named in the assignment particular attention. This read is not scored.

**Bottom line: No Errors.**

All six Round 6 Needs-care items are fixed, five of them word for word. All four minor items (M1–M4) were taken except M2, which was optional. Nothing in v7's new text is something a censor would be bound to strike.

One new item needs care:
- **N1-7, W1(2): food and water.** The new clause says food and water "may not be withdrawn" with no limit, and it cites *Samaritanus Bonus* V.3. But that section, verified on vatican.va this session, has its own limit. Food and water are owed "insofar as and until" they achieve their purpose of nourishing the patient, and "their administration should be suspended" when the body "cannot absorb them or cannot metabolize them." The reviewer is right. As written, the sentence attributes to the cited document a flat rule that the document itself qualifies. This is an incomplete statement of true teaching, not a contrary one, so it is not an Error. The fix is a few words.

Two other new items are sound. Each gets an optional refinement: "never the evil's wage" (the metaphor is opaque), and "God says no to the wound" (it can take an anchor in Wisdom 1:13).

## 2. Findings on v7's new text

| 1 | **A6**: "accept the death that comes, even at the hands of those who wrongly kill him: so Christ, who laid down His life of His own accord (John 10:18; CCC 609), and so St. Maximilian Kolbe, who offered himself in a condemned man's place; but he may not aim at his own death, by his own hand or another's, and his asking never makes it right for anyone to kill him." | Sound. "So Christ" offers Him as an instance of what a man may do, and He is true man. A bishop's reader will ask one question: since Christ willed His Passion, did He "aim at His own death"? St. Thomas answers no. Christ caused His death only indirectly, by not preventing it, and He died voluntarily while suffering violence (ST III q.47 a.1, ad 3). What He willed was the gift of Himself in love and obedience. CCC 616 says it is love "to the end" that gives His sacrifice its value, not death as such. The line between *accepting* death and *aiming at* it is CCC 2279's own line, and it now governs A6 and W1(2) alike. Kolbe: "offered himself" is exact. | OK | Optional, for a reader who presses the point: "(John 10:18; CCC 609, 616; ST III q.47 a.1)". |
| 2 | **A4**: "(Whether those lines hold however vast the numbers, and whether a man's own asking could ever make it right to kill him, which the Church denies (CCC 2277; *Evangelium Vitae* 65–66), A6 names as this book's claims.)" | Sound. | 3 | **W1(2)**: "That right is never ours, and no one, whatever voice he claims to have heard in His name, may claim it for himself (CCC 2258)." | Sound. CCC 2258 (verified R6): "no one can under any circumstance claim for himself the right directly to destroy an innocent human being." The sentence follows its shape, "claim for himself". Three checks. (a) *Legitimate defense* (CCC 2263–2265) and the old penal question are not in view: the referent is innocent life, and in defense the aggressor's death is not intended. (b) *Abraham.* St. Thomas holds that at God's own command death may be inflicted without injustice (ST I-II q.94 a.5 ad 2; q.100 a.8 ad 3). Even then the agent does not claim the right "for himself": the right stays God's. So "may claim it for himself" keeps the sentence consistent with St. Thomas. (c) *Private revelation.* "Whatever voice he claims to have heard" fits CCC 67: no private revelation can correct the deposit, and none can make an intrinsically evil act good. The sentence names no group and is not polemical. | OK | Optional: "(CCC 2258; cf. CCC 67)". |
| N1-7 | **W1(2)**: "but the ordinary care owed to the sick, food and water among it, may not be withdrawn (CCC 2278–2279; *Samaritanus Bonus* V.3)" | **The reviewer is right.** *Samaritanus Bonus* V.3 (verified on vatican.va this session) says nourishment and fluids are owed "insofar as and until this demonstrably attains the purpose of providing hydration and nutrition for the patient." "When the provision of nutrition and hydration no longer benefits the patient," because the body "cannot absorb them or cannot metabolize them," "their administration should be suspended." Artificial administration is obligatory "provided that it does not cause harm or intolerable suffering to the patient." Only "in any other circumstance" is withdrawal "an unjust action". The CDF *Responses* of 2007 and St. John Paul II's address of 2004 put it the same way: "in principle," and "insofar as and until". As written, v7 puts "food and water" under an unqualified "may not be withdrawn" and cites SB for it, so the cited document is made to say more than it does. A reader could take it to require feeding a dying body that can no longer use food. The word "owed" carries the limit implicitly, and CCC 2279's own sentence is unqualified, so this is incompleteness, not contrary teaching. It is not an Error. | **Needs care** | "but the ordinary care owed to the sick may not be withdrawn, and food and water are part of it for as long as they nourish her (CCC 2278–2279; *Samaritanus Bonus* V.3);" (Fuller form, if wanted: "…for as long as they nourish her and do not harm her".) |
| 4 | **Evil, *Second***: "*Why* He permits is, so far as reason can see, the setting above: a world of real causes, and we are among them; faith adds that He permits no evil from which He will not draw a good, though that good is never the evil's wage (CCC 311, 312, 324, 412)." | Sound, and an improvement. It keeps reason's answer and faith's answer distinct, and that is honest. The four citations all support the clause. CCC 311: God "knows how to derive good from it". CCC 312: "evil never becomes a good". CCC 324 and CCC 412 as quoted. **"Never the evil's wage"** is true in the strict sense: the evil earns nothing, and the good is God's gift. This echoes Romans 6:23, where the wage of sin is death and eternal life is the free gift. That reading would be wrong: what merits is charity, by grace (CCC 2008, 2011), never the evil itself. The sentence also repeats, a few lines on, the last sentence of the weight paragraph ("Faith gives the certainty … (CCC 324), though the evil never thereby becomes good (CCC 312)"). > but the ordinary care owed to the sick may not be withdrawn, and food and water are part of it for as long as they nourish her (CCC 2278–2279; *Samaritanus Bonus* V.3);

**Verdict for the bishop's reader: No Errors.**

Every Round 6 refinement has been taken. Three passages now say exactly what the Church and St. Thomas say:
- Christ's free self-offering in A6 (John 10:18; CCC 609; ST III q.47 a.1).
- The denial that asking can make killing right (EV 66; SB III).
- The line between accepting death and aiming at it.

The new sentences on permission ("faith adds … CCC 324"), on Mary and St. Leo, on CCC 2258's "for himself," and in the conclusion are faithful.

The one item a careful censor would still ask for is N1-7: carry *Samaritanus Bonus*'s own limit on food and water ("for as long as they nourish her") into the sentence that cites it. That is a change of a few words.

With it, I would expect v7 to receive a *nihil obstat* as it stands, and to be ready for condensing into the chapter.
