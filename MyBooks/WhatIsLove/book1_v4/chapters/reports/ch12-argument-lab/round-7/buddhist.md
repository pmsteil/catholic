# Argument Lab, Round 7 (final): Buddhist opponent vs v7

## 1. School and voice

The same composite voice as Rounds 1–6: the Theravada Pali Canon and its commentaries (Buddhaghosa), and Indian Madhyamaka (Nagarjuna, Candrakirti, Santideva, Kamalasila), with modern readers such as Harvey, Keown, Garfield and Siderits. It holds that there is no lasting self, that craving rooted in ignorance keeps the round turning, that karma is intention ripening with no one appointing it, that the four boundless states are the right shape of the heart, and that nirvana (for the Madhyamaka, emptiness realized) is the highest good. Argued in the spirit of *Nostra Aetate* 2.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges (short)

1. **Justice.** No one is dearer to anyone than himself, and each holds himself just as dear, so harm no one (SN 3.8; Dhp 129–130). The one wronged must be asked for pardon (Vinaya, Cullavagga I). **Holds;** nothing borrowed.
2. **Dilemma** (a long-ill monk asks his brothers to stop feeding him). If he stops eating *in order to die* while care is at hand, that is an offence; if his life cannot be prolonged and he declines what no longer helps, it is not (Samantapasadika on Parajika 3, as reported by Keown; verify). To help him die is expulsion (Parajika 3). **Holds,** without borrowing; the line falls where the will aims.
3. **Equal dignity.** Every being suffers and holds its life dear, and the mind is luminous by nature (AN 1.51); the Mahayana adds buddha-nature. **Holds for equal compassion and an equal precept; bends for equal weight,** because the commentaries grade the blame of killing a human by the victim's virtue (DN-a on DN 1; see Exchange 6), and the highest good is open according to capacity (AN 8.29).
4. **Ultimate good.** Nirvana, or emptiness realized, in which wisdom and compassion cannot be separated. **Holds;** coherent. On this view love is not the ground of things.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1. A6 now says "may not aim at his own death." The tigress Jataka, once more.**
*Buddhist (Mahayana voice):* In Round 6 I set our best-loved gift, the prince who gave his body to the starving tigress, against A6. v7 sharpens A6 by the will's aim. In the Jatakamala telling the tigress is too weak to kill, so he throws himself from the cliff; in the Suvarnaprabhasa telling he opens his own throat with a bamboo splinter so she can taste blood (both paraphrased; verify). His death is the very means of the gift. On v7's wording, he aimed at it. The Church's Christ also "laid down His life of His own accord." Why is that not the same act?
*Patrick's side:* Because Christ did not take His own life. He freely accepted a death that others inflicted wrongly (CCC 609; John 10:18, RSV: "No one takes it from me, but I lay it down of my own accord"), and the Church places the saving value in His love to the end, not in death as such (CCC 609). Kolbe likewise offered himself; the Nazis killed him. A6 now draws exactly that line, "accept the death that comes, even at the hands of those who wrongly kill him ... but ... may not aim at his own death, by his own hand or another's." The prince, in both canonical tellings, kills himself to make the gift possible. A6 says the Church forbids that, and says plainly that this is "this book's claim, argued in W1(2), not shared." It does not say the Buddhist holds it.
*Buddhist rejoinder:* Then A6 is clearer than before, and the line between our traditions is drawn where it really falls. We too hold back the gift of the body until compassion is pure and the purpose great (*Bodhicaryavatara* 5.87).
*Verdict:* **Held at parity.** v7's "aim" is a precise statement of the Catholic line, stated as the book's claim. The standoff from Round 6 stands, now exactly located: whether the line ever opens (the bodhisattva's case) and whether it rests on the giver's mind or on a life held in trust.

**Exchange 2. "Aim" and the hard cases of closeness.**
*Buddhist (Madhyamaka voice, Siderits-style analytic pressure):* Change the story. A man lies down before a tigress strong enough to kill; or a soldier falls on a grenade. In neither does he kill himself. Is his death "aimed at"? If being eaten simply *is* being killed, the line between intending the gift of one's flesh and intending one's death looks thin. Does "aim" bear the weight A6 puts on it?
*Patrick's side:* The soldier on the grenade is the textbook case the Catholic tradition allows: the body shields; death is foreseen, not sought. Where the good comes *through* the death itself, the death is chosen as a means. Where the boundary is close, the tradition admits hard cases. But our school is not alone here: yours grounds the whole of morality in intention (*cetana*; AN 6.63), and Buddhaghosa's own line for the sick monk is whether he declines food "in order to die." Every account that judges by the will's aim meets the same close cases. That is parity, not a defect peculiar to A6.
*Verdict:* **Held at parity.** This is the Round 6 standoff over double effect versus doing and allowing, here met from the Buddhist side. Our two traditions use the same test (the will's aim) and meet the same hard edges.

**Exchange 3. W1(2): the new ordinary-care wording, read beside Buddhaghosa.**
*Buddhist:* I welcome the new sentence, "what the trust forbids is that her death be the thing aimed at." It is almost exactly Buddhaghosa's ruling on the sick monk: an offence if he stops eating with the aim of dying while care is at hand; no offence if he declines what can no longer prolong his life (Samantapasadika; Keown, *Buddhism and Bioethics*; verify). But v7 also says flatly that "the ordinary care owed to the sick, food and water among it, may not be withdrawn." A Buddhist reader who knows our commentary will ask: even when the dying body can no longer take them in? If so, the Church is stricter than Buddhaghosa, and stricter than it needs to be.
*Patrick's side:* The Church is not stricter on that point. *Samaritanus Bonus* V.3, which v7 cites, says that nutrition and hydration are owed "insofar as and until" they achieve their purpose, and that when the patient's body cannot absorb or metabolize them, their administration should be suspended. Any other withdrawal is unjust. The censor checked that paragraph in Round 6. v7's sentence is true of the case it means. But it leaves out the qualifier the cited paragraph carries.
*Verdict:* **Holds, with an optional precision repair** (Repair 2). This is not a misstatement of this school. It is a point where v7 is briefer than its own source, and a Buddhist reader is exactly the reader who would notice. With the qualifier, the two traditions agree almost word for word, which is worth having.

**Exchange 4. "God says no to the wound and yes to her."**
*Buddhist:* The new line follows "a yes said to whatever happens is said to no one." Is equanimity (*upekkha*) the target?
*Patrick's side:* No. Equanimity does not say yes to what happens: suffering's cause is to be abandoned. The sentence answers the god beyond good and evil and the "yes" to fate. Your compassion also says no to the wound. Where we part is the second half.
*Buddhist:* Agreed. Loving-kindness says "may she be well," which is a yes to her welfare. It does not say that it is good that she exists, and at the ground of things she is not a self to say yes to.
*Verdict:* **Holds.** The line does not misstate this school; it names the root standoff in seven words. Worth keeping.

**Exchange 5. "Never the evil's wage."**
*Buddhist:* In our account the suffering *is* a ripening of the sufferer's own past deeds, at least in part (SN 36.21). Does "the evil's wage" quietly set karma aside?
*Patrick's side:* No. The clause is about the good God draws from an evil: that good is never payment that makes the evil worth doing (CCC 312, 324). It says nothing about where suffering comes from. Karma is treated where it belongs, in the Dhamma row, which grants that it "explains some of how suffering falls."
*Verdict:* **Holds.** No collision.

**Exchange 6. The conclusion's new contrast: "wronged no less than ... a grown man."**
*Buddhist (Theravada commentary voice):* The new contrast is fair to the Realist's point, but our commentaries answer it differently. Buddhaghosa grades the blame of taking life: among beings without moral qualities, killing a small one is less blameworthy and a large one more; among humans, killing one of little virtue is less blameworthy and one of great virtue more (Sumangalavilasini on DN 1, *appagune ... appasavajjo, mahagune mahasavajjo*; checked in the Pali). By that measure, the killing of a man of great virtue weighs more than the killing of an infant whose virtue is not yet formed. So I am not sure that she was "wronged no less than a grown man." Does the conclusion assume an answer a serious tradition denies?
*Patrick's side:* Three answers. First, the conclusion assumes nothing: it lists "or that it was a lesser wrong" among the answers the reader must weigh, and asks only which he is surer of. Second, your own Vinaya makes the same point in its penalty: killing a human being is expulsion whatever the victim's virtue, and the human being is counted from conception (Parajika 3). The grading is of the killer's blame, not of the victim's standing as a human being. Third, the Church also grades the gravity of a deed by its circumstances (CCC 1754). But it grades the killing of the defenceless innocent as graver, not lighter: Pope St. John Paul II sets out the gravity of killing the unborn by noting that no one could be more innocent, and that such a child is weak and defenceless (*Evangelium Vitae* 58; verify wording). What the conclusion asks about is whether her worth followed her strength or her mind; the commentary's scale follows attained virtue, which is neither strength nor intellect, and the book's question is exactly whether worth follows anything attained.
*Buddhist rejoinder:* Granted that the conclusion leaves the answer to the reader. Our commentary does give the "lesser wrong" answer, though only for the weight of the deed, not for the precept.
*Verdict:* **Held at parity.** Not a defect and not a misstatement: the conclusion names no Buddhist and lists the answer the commentary gives. It is a new honest standoff (section 5), and it confirms my Round 6 answer to challenge 3: equal compassion and an equal precept, but not equal weight.

**Exchange 7. W3's rewritten Smith sentence, and the Buddhist account of the longing.**
*Buddhist:* v7 now says, "the longing alone settles nothing." Good. But W3 still says Smith "does not as easily explain a longing to be told ... that it is good that we exist." We have an explanation: craving for being (*bhava-tanha*) and the conceit "I am."
*Patrick's side:* W3's claim is about Smith's explanation, not yours. Yours is in the next sentence: "Part of that longing grasps, and both the Buddhist and the Catholic say grasping love must die." The book does not claim the longing proves anything; it says "we part over what is left." You say nothing is left of it but craving; we say "it is good that you exist" is left, healed.
*Verdict:* **Holds.** The root standoff over the goodness of being, unchanged.

**Exchange 8. A small read-aloud slip in A6.**
*Buddhist:* "... and so St. Maximilian Kolbe, who offered himself in a condemned man's place; but he may not aim at his own death." Read aloud, "he" lands on Kolbe, the nearest name.
*Patrick's side:* The sense is plain on the page (the subject is "a man"), but a listener hears it the other way. Repeat the subject.
*Verdict:* **Holds; optional read-aloud repair** (Repair 1).

**Exchange 9. "This book weighs it below."**
*Buddhist:* In the repaired parenthesis, in a sentence about weighing, "weighs it below" can be heard as "ranks it lower," which would mean dismissing the very weight it promises to carry.
*Patrick's side:* The meaning is "later in this chapter." Say so.
*Verdict:* **Holds; optional read-aloud repair** (Repair 3).

**Exchange 10. The weighing, last time.**
*Buddhist:* With every concession now made, does "best explanation" still beat us?
*Patrick's side:* It names the same points, and v7 has made none weaker. Guilt asks to be forgiven by *her, herself*, after she is gone. Her worth is a call to this one, not a nature held whole by all. Love is the first word and not only the seed and fruit of a path. And it is good that she exists, not only that her birth is a chance to be free of birth. The book claims a judgment about these points, not compulsion.
*Verdict:* **Held at parity.** The deepest standoff, honestly drawn.

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**As written: NONE.**

No Fatal or Serious objection stands against v7 as written. Both Round 6 repairs were taken. I compared every changed sentence with v6 and tested each that touches this school. The two strongest candidates fail as defects. First, A6's "may not aim at his own death" against the bodhisattva's gift of his body (Exchanges 1–2): A6 states the line as the book's own claim, and its Christ and Kolbe cases are drawn precisely by the will's aim, a test our own tradition uses too. Second, the conclusion's new "grown man" contrast against Buddhaghosa's grading of blame by the victim's virtue (Exchange 6): the conclusion lists that answer and asks the reader to weigh it, and it attributes nothing to the Buddhist.

**Minor (all optional)**
1. **A6, read-aloud:** "but he may not aim at his own death" follows Kolbe's name, so a listener hears "he" as Kolbe (Exchange 8).
2. **W1(2), precision:** "food and water among it, may not be withdrawn" omits the limit in the paragraph it cites (*Samaritanus Bonus* V.3: owed insofar as and until they achieve their purpose; suspended when the body can no longer absorb them). Not a misstatement of this school; flagged because a Buddhist reader comparing Buddhaghosa would notice. The censor should confirm (Exchange 3).
3. **C-Dhamma, read-aloud:** "this book weighs it below" can be heard as "ranks it lower" (Exchange 9).

**Misstatements of this school:** none found in v7.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Whether persons are real at the ground of things. Rebirth keeps a stream; guilt asks for her, herself.
- Whether the dead victim herself can still pardon. Both traditions agree a pardon heals the bond and does not undo the deed (CCC 1459; MN 86).
- Whether the line against aiming at one's own death never opens (the Church) or almost never (the bodhisattva's body-gift, held back by pure compassion and great purpose, BCA 5.87; Channa as an arahant's case), and whether it rests on the giver's mind or on a life held in trust (Exchange 1). Both traditions judge by the will's aim and meet the same close cases (Exchange 2).
- **New:** whether killing the infant is "no less" a wrong than killing a grown man. The Church says no less, and if anything graver for her defencelessness; the Theravada commentary keeps the same precept and penalty but grades the blame by the victim's virtue (Exchange 6).
- Whether compassion is the root of the path only, or love is also the root of the world.
- Whether the fit of hearts and fortunes asks for a mind or arises from conditions and is empty; and whether a world where pain does not follow justice weighs against a just Mind.
- Whether the animal's loss is given by an order God wills or comes from its own past deeds.
- Whether the longing to hear "it is good that you exist" is only craving for being, or the trace of a first word (Exchange 7).
- Whether it is good that we exist, or only that a human birth is a precious chance to be free of birth. This is the root of every other parting.

## 7. New insights and lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **On the dying, the Church and Buddhaghosa draw the same line.** The commentary on Parajika 3 rules that a sick monk who stops eating in order to die, while care is at hand, commits an offence; one who declines what can no longer prolong his life does not (Samantapasadika; Keown; verify). That is v7's own sentence, "what the trust forbids is that her death be the thing aimed at." With Repair 2, the book could note that a tradition with no Creator reached the same line by looking at the will. This shows that the line is reason's, as A6 hopes, even where the reason for it (a life held in trust) is not shared.
- **Both traditions judge by the will's aim, so they share the same hard edges.** The grenade and the tigress test the Catholic "aim" and the Buddhist *cetana* alike. A6 need not apologize for the close cases.
- **The commentary's grading of blame by the victim's virtue is the clearest Buddhist answer to the conclusion's question.** It keeps one precept and one penalty for every human being from conception (Parajika 3), yet weighs the deed by the victim's attained virtue (DN-a on DN 1, checked in the Pali). The Church keeps the same precept and weighs the killing of the defenceless as graver (EV 58; verify). The contrast shows exactly what "her worth never followed her strength or her mind" adds: it refuses to let worth follow anything attained, even virtue.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "God says no to the wound and yes to her."
- "A pardon heals the bond; it does not undo the deed."
- "Compassion is the root of the path; love is also the root of the world."
- "One precept for every human life; the question is whether its weight follows what she has become, or what she is."

**Checked this round:** Sumangalavilasini on DN 1 (killing among humans is less blameworthy where the victim's virtue is small and more where it is great; among beings without moral qualities, by body size), Pali and English at tipitaka.theravada.su; *Samaritanus Bonus* V.3 on vatican.va ("insofar as and until" it achieves its purpose; suspended when the body cannot absorb or metabolize; otherwise withdrawal is unjust); Buddhaghosa on the sick monk who refuses food, as reported in a summary of Keown's study of the Vinaya and commentary (urbandharma.org; the primary text is still to verify). **To verify before use:** *Jatakamala* 1 and *Suvarnaprabhasa* (the tigress, the cliff, the bamboo splinter); *Evangelium Vitae* 58 wording; AN 6.63 on intention as kamma; Vinaya Parajika 3 on the human being from conception; BCA 5.87 (checked in Round 6).

## 8. Score

**v7 as written: 9.5/10. With the optional repairs: 9.5/10.** v7 took both Round 6 repairs verbatim, and none of its new wording misstates this school. The sharpened A6 meets the bodhisattva's gift of his body only at an honest standoff, more cleanly drawn than before. The conclusion's new contrast meets Buddhaghosa's grading of blame only at parity, because it already lists that answer. The three repairs are polish. What remains between this school and the book is only the standoffs over whether persons, and being itself, are real and good at the ground of things, and those are not defects in the argument.
