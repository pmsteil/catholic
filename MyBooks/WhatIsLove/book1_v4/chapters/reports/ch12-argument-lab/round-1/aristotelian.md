# Argument Lab, Round 1: Secular Neo-Aristotelian Naturalism / Virtue Ethics vs. v1

## 1. School and voice

The voice is the school of Philippa Foot (*Natural Goodness*, 2001), Rosalind Hursthouse (*On Virtue Ethics*, 1999), Michael Thompson (*Life and Action*, 2008) and John McDowell (second nature), with Martha Nussbaum's capabilities approach (*Frontiers of Justice*, 2006) as its political wing. It holds that "good" in ethics works the way it does in "good root" or "good wolf": it measures a living thing against the life-form of its kind. A virtue is what a human being needs to live well as the rational, social, dependent animal it is. No God is required, because the standard lives in the form itself.

This is the rival nearest to the book's own position. Aquinas built on Aristotle, so the opponent shares most of the book's moral content and denies only its source.

---

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**Q1. Starting point for justice.**
*Their answer:* Justice is a virtue, a settled will to give others what is due to them. Human beings need it because we depend on one another, cooperate, make promises and hold property; a person without it is defective as a human being in the same way a wolf that won't hunt with the pack is defective as a wolf (Foot). What is due comes from what members of a human community need in order to flourish, and from the practices they share (promises, contracts). Nussbaum's version: each person is owed a threshold of central capabilities (life, health, bodily integrity, practical reason, affiliation and others) because each is owed "a life worthy of human dignity."
*Verdict:* **Holds in structure; borrows on equality.** The account explains why justice is owed without consent and without a commander. But Aristotle's own justice was proportioned to worth and found "natural slaves." The neo-Aristotelians reject that, and the equality they put in its place comes from the Stoic, Christian and Kantian inheritance, not from natural goodness. Nussbaum is open about treating dignity as a basic intuitive idea rather than deriving it.

**Q2. A hard case resolved by their principles.**
*Case:* A man with advanced ALS, lucid and suffering, asks his doctor for a lethal injection.
*Their answer:* Ask two questions. Does it violate justice? A right to life protects a person's own good and, Foot argued, can be waived by its holder. Does it violate charity? Life is normally a good to the one who has it; where it has truly stopped being a good to him, charity may not forbid ending it. Foot ("Euthanasia," 1977) concluded that voluntary active euthanasia can sometimes be permissible on these grounds, though she was wary of making it legal. Hursthouse would add practical wisdom: do what a virtuous agent would characteristically do. That means looking at the whole case, not running a rule.
*Verdict:* **Coherent and borrowing nothing, but it breaks the book's A3 and A4.** The school resolves the case cleanly with its own virtues (justice, charity, practical wisdom), so the charge that it must "borrow truth, justice, mercy and self-gift" is unfair here: Aquinas took much of that list from Aristotle in the first place. What the case shows is that natural goodness alone does not give the absolute. When life is valued as "a good to its possessor," a life can lose that value, and then the person can sign it away. Catholic Aristotelians (Anscombe, Geach, MacIntyre) refuse Foot's conclusion, and the split inside the school is itself evidence: the absolute does not come out of the form by itself.

**Q3. Ground of equal, unconditional dignity.**
*Their answer:* Dignity comes with membership in the rational kind. The newborn, the dying and the severely disabled are all members of a rational life-form, whether or not they can use reason now. A three-legged wolf is still a wolf, and still owed whatever wolves are owed. Nussbaum grounds dignity in being a human being with the capabilities proper to the species norm, to be supported up to the threshold wherever possible.
*Verdict:* **Holds for equal membership; bends on unconditional.** This is the strongest secular ground for the equal standing of the newborn and the disabled. It beats any view that bases dignity on present capacity, and the book should say so. But it breaks in two places. (a) Natural goodness grades members *relative to their kind*. It yields "good for humans" and "defective as a human." It does not yield a worth that the good of the whole may never override. Nothing in "good-of-kind" stops a community from sacrificing its weakest members for the flourishing of the rest, as Sparta did; you have to add a virtue that forbids it, and then explain why that virtue is the human one. (b) Nussbaum has written that some profoundly impaired lives (she instances permanent vegetative state and anencephaly) fall outside a human life in her sense. Neither ground covers the enemy except through borrowed universalism.

**Q4. Ultimate human good.**
*Their answer:* *Eudaimonia*: a complete life of activity in keeping with the virtues, with friends, family and community, and with enough of the outward goods it needs. For Nussbaum, a life in which the central capabilities are secured. Practical wisdom is the guide. The good is complete within a human life and ends with it.
*Verdict:* **Coherent, but bounded.** It is honest and livable. It breaks at the edges the book cares about most. Aristotle denies that a child can be called happy, because the child cannot yet act from virtue, so the newborn who dies has no share in the ultimate good. Death simply ends flourishing, so grief is fitting only because a good was lost, not because "love was meant to last." And Aristotle himself, in *Nicomachean Ethics* X.7–8, puts the highest happiness in contemplation, which he calls something divine in us. The secular heirs mostly leave that book out.

## 3. Debate highlights

**E1. Attack on A5 and the is/ought claim.**
*Opponent:* A5 says that describing human nature never yields an "ought." That holds for a statistical description, a list of what humans happen to do. It fails for a description of a *life-form*. "Humans have 32 teeth" and "the wolf hunts in packs" are not averages. They set the standard by which a particular human or wolf is defective (Thompson's "natural-historical judgments"). Describing what a human *is*, in this sense, already includes what it is *for*. Worse for you, your own Aquinas agrees. Natural law is the rational creature's share in eternal law "whereby it has a natural inclination to its proper act and end" (ST I-II q.91 a.2), and the order of its precepts follows the order of natural inclinations (q.94 a.2). Your A5 splits what Thomas held together, and if the "for" lies outside our nature, your natural law is no longer natural.
*Defense:* Patrick's distinction stands if "human nature" in A5 means what it plainly means there: what we are capable of, as we find ourselves, able both to tell the truth and to lie. That range really does not tell us which to choose. But the opponent is right that the "for" is not outside us. It is written into us as an inclination. The repair keeps Patrick's distinction and moves the question to where the real dispute is. Both sides agree we are *for* something. The question is *who made us for it*.
*Result:* **Held with repair.** As written, A5 can be read as contradicting q.91 a.2. That is dangerous in a Catholic book and an easy target for this opponent. See Repair A5.

**E2. Rejoinder: ends need no giver.**
*Opponent:* Nature acts for an end without deliberating. Aristotle says art does not deliberate either, and if shipbuilding were in the wood, the wood would act like nature (*Physics* II.8). A heart is *for* pumping because that is the work it does in the life of the animal, and evolution explains why. An end inside a thing needs no one outside the thing to set it. "We are for something, so Someone made us for it" is as invalid as your old "laws require lawgivers."
*Defense:* Granted: a heart's function does not prove a designer, and the book should not argue that it does. The real question is where the human form came from, and it faces a dilemma. **Either** the form is a passing product of evolution. Then the standard it carries is the local pattern of one species and could have been otherwise. Darwin himself imagined rational creatures reared like hive bees, who would think it a sacred duty to kill their brothers (*Descent of Man*, ch. 3, paraphrased). Then torturing a child for fun is wrong only *for creatures shaped like us*, and A1 does not mean that. **Or** the form of a good rational life is fixed and could not be otherwise. Then there is an eternal order of ends that minds can know, which is a brute Platonic fact or else the wisdom of a Mind.
*Result:* **Held, at parity.** The dilemma is real, but it does not knock the opponent out. Each horn has a coherent defender (see E3). That is the right place for a converging case to stand.

**E3. Opponent bites both horns.**
*Opponent:* Foot takes the first horn. Yes, the good is relative to the life-form; rational bees would have a different good, and that is no embarrassment, any more than it is embarrassing that "good root" means something different for an oak and a carrot. For *us*, cruelty is a defect without exception, and "us" is everyone A1 is about. Thompson takes the second horn without God. The "rational life-form" is a logical category known a priori, not a biological species, and it needs no Mind behind it.
*Defense:* On the first horn: A1 claims more than "wrong for our kind." It claims that the wrong would hold even if every culture approved, and the natural reading is that it would hold even if our kind had been built differently. Asked plainly, almost no one believes that cruelty to a child could have been good if evolution had gone another way. The opponent has to bite that bullet. On the second horn: an eternal, a priori form of rational life, binding on any possible rational creature, is exactly the "brute moral fact" that the C comparison already weighs. It explains A1 and leaves guilt, communion and the question "why this order and not another?" unexplained. The Aristotelian who takes the second horn has stopped being a naturalist and become a Platonist.
*Result:* **Held with repair.** The C comparison should show that "human flourishing alone," when pressed, either becomes species-relative or collapses into "brute moral facts." See Repair C.

**E4. Attack on W1, using Anscombe.**
*Opponent:* Your own Catholic Anscombe ("Modern Moral Philosophy," 1958) argued that "moral obligation" in the law sense only makes sense within a divine-law conception of ethics, and that without a lawgiver it should be dropped. We took her advice. We keep "unjust," "cruel," "dishonest," "cowardly" and drop the juridical "ought." Then W1 collapses. What is owed is owed to *the victim*. A3 is explained without any higher claimant: no consent can make cruelty right because cruelty is never good for anyone, so it is a defect of the will whatever the victim says. "A claim no human can cancel was not laid by any human" is invalid. There is no claim laid at all, only a standard set by our form.
*Defense:* Anscombe is the book's best witness here, not the opponent's. She showed that the choice is real: either keep morality as *law* and accept a Lawgiver, or drop law and keep only excellence of a kind. So the question is what the second choice costs. (a) **Debt, not only defect.** A flawed character needs reforming; a wronged person needs forgiving. These are different, and A2 records the second. (b) **The dead victim.** A man who murdered a hermit with no surviving kin does not experience his guilt as a flaw to improve. He experiences it as a debt with no one left on earth to forgive it. On the opponent's account that debt is owed to no one living, and so to no one at all. (c) **Forgiveness and release are different things.** The victim *can* forgive, and the book should say so. What he cannot do is make the act not wrong, or consent it into being right beforehand. A3 as written runs these together.
*Opponent's rejoinder:* What you call "debt" is remorse, and the remorse is a fitting response to one's own vice. Feeling that the debt must be paid to *Someone* is a Christian formation of feeling, which is Anscombe's very point. You are using a theory-laden datum as a premise.
*Result:* **Held with repair, at parity.** The rejoinder is fair. A2's "need to be forgiven" is not something an honest Aristotelian *cannot* deny; he denies it with reasons. The book should present A2 as something every account must explain, show that the rival explains it by shrinking it down to remorse, and let the reader judge. W1 must also stop presenting "owed, so owed to Someone above us" as a deduction. See Repairs A2, A3, W1.

**E5. Attack on C's "human flourishing" line and A4.**
*Opponent:* C says flourishing cannot explain "equal dignity for those who cannot flourish." That is a straw man. They are members of the rational kind, and they are owed the help their kind is owed. A three-legged wolf is still a wolf. MacIntyre's *Dependent Rational Animals* (1999) builds the whole virtue tradition around our dependence and the "virtues of acknowledged dependence," and the disabled sit at its center, not its edge. C also says flourishing cannot explain A3, but E4 showed that it can.
*Defense:* Both points are conceded, and C must be corrected. (MacIntyre, a Catholic, is better cited as the book's ally than as the opponent's.) The claim that survives is narrower and stronger. Natural goodness gives equal *belonging*. It does not give a worth that may never be traded for the good of the whole, or that the person himself cannot sign away when his life "stops being a good to him." Foot's own euthanasia argument and Nussbaum's threshold are the evidence: once worth is measured as good-for-the-possessor, or by a species norm, it can fail. *Dignitas Infinita* 15 says dignity "cannot be lost"; good-of-kind cannot say that.
*Result:* **Held with repair.** See Repair C.

**E6. Attack on W2's "never finally contradict."**
*Opponent:* Two answers. First, the unity of the virtues: no one has any virtue fully without practical wisdom, and with it one has them all (*Nicomachean Ethics* VI.13). That explains why truth, justice and mercy cohere in a good person, and no God is needed. Second, Nussbaum (*The Fragility of Goodness*, 1986) argues the opposite of your claim. Goods do conflict tragically. Agamemnon at Aulis and Antigone against Creon are cases where every option wrongs someone. "Never finally contradict" is false to life.
*Defense:* The two answers pull against each other: the school cannot both explain why the goods cohere and deny that they do. On tragedy, the Catholic claim is precise and more modest than W2's wording. A person may face cruel costs, and Chapter 11 does not hide them. But no one is ever forced to do evil: there is always a way that wrongs no one, though it may cost everything (1 Cor 10:13; *Veritatis Splendor* 52 on precepts that forbid in every case). Aquinas holds that a man is "trapped" between two wrongs only on the assumption of an earlier wrong of his own (ST I-II q.19 a.6 ad 3). Agamemnon could refuse; the price was the fleet. If duty could ever corner an innocent person into doing wrong, morality would not be one law. Whether it is one is exactly what is in dispute.
*Result:* **Held with repair.** Change "never finally contradict" to "never require us to do evil." The phrase "conscience is only an evolved survival tool" aims at the evolutionary debunker, not at this opponent, whose McDowell-style moral perception is a trained second nature. Against the Aristotelian, W2 has to run through the dilemma in E2. See Repair W2.

**E7. Attack on A7 and W3.**
*Opponent:* Friendship is enough. Aristotle's friend is "another self," and the best friendship knows and is known. A longing to be known *wholly and forever* is not part of human nature. It is a hope shaped by Christianity, or an extension of real longings into an infinite that does not exist. Aristotle is honest that the happy are blessed "as human beings" are blessed (NE I.10, paraphrased): enough, within limits.
*Defense:* That is a fair denial, and A7 stays a clue, not a premise everyone has to grant (as v0 Option 3 already found). Against *this* opponent, though, the clue carries more weight than it does against the materialist. The Aristotelian holds that nature does nothing in vain and that our desires reveal our ends. If the longing of A7 is natural to us (and Aristotle himself put the highest happiness in "something divine" in us, NE X.7), then by the opponent's own principle it is not likely to be for nothing (cf. ST I-II q.3 a.8). He has to call the longing unnatural, and that is a heavy claim to make about something so common across peoples and times. And Aristotle's word on the dying child, that a child cannot yet be called happy, is the hardest sentence in his ethics. The book can answer it: the child is made for communion with God, and that communion does not wait on her achievements.
*Result:* **Held with repair.** See Repair W3.

**E8. Attack on A6.**
*Opponent:* For the virtuous person, virtuous action is pleasant. The generous person gives gladly. Aristotle's good man who gives up wealth, honors and even his life for his friends is "taking the greater share of the noble for himself" (NE IX.8, paraphrased). Good for another does not *always* cost; often it is a gain.
*Defense:* There is no conflict. A6 says it costs *something*: time, attention, comfort, and sometimes life. Aristotle's own picture is cost and gain together, a lower good laid down for a higher one, which is close to the book's view that self-gift is a gift, not a loss, and becomes sacrifice in a fallen world. The second half of A6 is Aristotle's own position: some acts one must not be compelled to do and should rather die (NE III.1).
*Result:* **Held.** A light wording repair makes "costs" read as "asks something of," which removes the opening.

**E9. Parsimony.**
*Opponent:* My stopping point is a nature we can observe. Yours is a God you posit. Other things equal, stop at the observable.
*Defense:* We observe instances of the form; we do not observe the form's authority, its necessity, or its origin. Those are exactly what the dilemma in E2 asks about, and the rival has to posit something there too: brute contingency, or an a priori order of rational life. C compares stopping points by how much each explains, with evil and hiddenness weighed against God.
*Result:* **Held, at parity.** This is the honest standoff a converging case (CCC 31) should expect. It is not a defeat.

## 5. Remaining sound objections

Against **v1 as written**, four objections stand that v1's own text cannot answer:

1. **W1's inference is invalid against this school** (*Serious*). An uncancellable standard can come from nature with no claimant behind it. *Answered to parity by Repair W1.*
2. **C misstates the flourishing view** on A3 and A4 (*Serious*, because it is the kind of straw man a careful reader notices first, and it would cost the chapter credibility). *Answered by Repair C.*
3. **A5 can be read against Aquinas** (q.91 a.2; q.94 a.2), splitting nature from its end (*Serious* in a Catholic book; the fix is in wording, not doctrine). *Answered by Repair A5.*
4. **W2 aims at the wrong opponent**, and "never finally contradict" invites the tragic-conflict counterexamples (*Minor*). *Answered by Repair W2.*

**After the repairs: NONE.** Two standoffs remain where the sides are even: (a) whether an end inside a nature needs a giver (E2, E3, E9), and (b) whether guilt is a debt or a fitting remorse (E4). In each, the opponent has a coherent position and our side answers it fully, without overclaiming. A converging case is supposed to end in standoffs like these, and the book should say so openly instead of claiming a win.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**A2.**
> **A2. Doing them leaves us guilty: not merely mistaken, and not merely flawed.** A wrong sum needs correcting; a flawed character needs reforming; a wronged person needs forgiving. We feel the wrong as a debt, not only as a defect in ourselves.

**A3.**
> **A3. No one on earth can make the wrong right.** The one I wronged can forgive me, and that is a real gift. But he cannot make it not wrong, and he could never have consented it into being right. Neither can a nation by vote, nor the whole human race. And if the one I wronged is dead, the debt does not die with him.

**A5.**
> **A5. What we are able to do does not settle what we ought to do.** As we find ourselves, we are capable of truth and lies, justice and injustice, mercy and cruelty, giving and grasping. Listing what we can do never by itself tells us which to choose. The "ought" lives in what we are *for*: an end written into our nature as an inclination we did not give ourselves (ST I-II q.91 a.2). So the real question is not only what we are for, but who made us for it.

**A6.** (first sentence only)
> **A6. Real good for another always asks something of the giver**: time, attention, comfort, sometimes far more. That is how reality works. And some things must never be done, even to save oneself.

**W1.**
> **W1. Obligation points to a Lawgiver.** The best rival account roots morality in what our kind needs to flourish, and it sees something true: the standard is not made by consent, so no vote can make cruelty good. But it pays for this. It makes the standard a fact about one kind of animal, and it turns guilt into a flaw to be fixed rather than a debt to be forgiven. Elizabeth Anscombe saw the choice clearly: either morality is a law, and then it needs a lawgiver, or we stop calling it law and keep only the excellences of our kind. If what we find in A2 and A3 is real (a debt, owed even when the one we wronged is gone), then morality is a law. A law is an ordering of reason for the common good, made by the one who has care of the whole (ST I-II q.90 a.4). That is the Creator. He is not a commander picking rules, but the One who made us for the good and is Himself that Good.

**W2.**
> **W2. The law and the mind that reads it point to one Source.** Our minds can read the moral law because the law and the mind belong to one nature. The best rival account sees this rightly. Then ask where that nature came from. If it is only a passing stage of evolution, the law it carries is the local custom of one species and could have been otherwise. Darwin himself imagined rational creatures reared like hive bees who would think it a sacred duty to kill their brothers. Yet no one really believes that torturing a child for fun is wrong only for creatures shaped like us. If instead the pattern of a good life for a rational being is fixed and could not be otherwise, then there is an eternal order of ends that minds can know, and that is very near what the Church means by God's wisdom ordering all things (ST I-II q.91 a.1). And this law is one: its demands can be hard, even cruel in their cost (Chapter 11), but they never require us to do evil (1 Cor 10:13). A law that is one, unchanging for any mind that reads it, and a mind fitted to read it, are best explained by one Source who made both.

**W3.** (add after the second sentence)
> Even Aristotle, who held that nature does nothing in vain, placed the highest happiness in something divine in us. If our nature is truly *for* something, a longing found in all of us is not likely to be for nothing. That is a clue, not a proof, but it is a clue the friend of nature should weigh.

**C: replace the "Human flourishing alone" line.**
> **Human flourishing alone** sees the most: what our kind needs, why virtue is good for us, why no vote can make cruelty good, why the weak still belong. It stops at a fact about one kind of animal. Pressed, it must say either that the standard could have been otherwise, or that it rests on an eternal order it cannot explain. And it does not explain why we owe and need forgiving, not merely improving (A2), why each person's worth, however weak, may never be traded for the good of the whole or signed away (A4), or why our longing reaches past every human friend (A7).

**Chapter text (beyond the v1 items):**
- The chapter's fish-in-water image (Law of the Gospel section) *is* this opponent's own argument: a fish is good when it lives as a fish should, and no God is needed for that. Add one sentence where the image appears: *"And the fish did not make itself for water. Someone made it for water."*
- "The Atheist's Dilemma" names the Kantian rival ("the noblest of these attempts") but never the Aristotelian, who is the nearest rival of all. Add one sentence there naming him and pointing to W1 and C.

## 7. New insights and lines worth keeping

**New insights**
- **Anscombe is the hinge of the chapter.** The title "The Lawgiver Is Love" is exactly the choice she posed: keep morality as law and accept a Lawgiver, or drop law and keep only excellence of a kind. This opponent chose the second honestly. The book wins this school over by showing what that choice gives up, not by denying that the choice exists.
- **Patrick's distinction is correct but needs to be placed carefully.** "What we are" (what we are able to do) vs. "what we are for" (our end) is right, and it is what defeats the bare is/ought charge. But Aquinas puts the "for" *inside* our nature as an inclination. The dispute with the Aristotelian is not *whether* we are for something (both agree) but *who made us for it*. The repaired A5 says this, and it closes off the opponent's sharpest line of attack.
- **The real dividing line on dignity is equal *belonging* vs. worth that *cannot be lost*.** This school grants the first better than any other secular school. Foot on euthanasia and Nussbaum's threshold show where it cannot grant the second. *Dignitas Infinita* 15 ("cannot be lost") is precisely the point at issue.
- **"Nature does nothing in vain" belongs to the opponent and works in the book's favor.** v0's Option 3 failed against the selectionist, but against the Aristotelian the argument from longing becomes his own principle turned back on him.
- **MacIntyre's *Dependent Rational Animals*** (a Catholic author) is the best bridge source for A4 and A6: the tradition's own discovery that our dependence is at its center.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "The best rival sees that we are for something. It does not ask who made us for it."
- "A flawed character needs reforming; a wronged person needs forgiving."
- "If the one I wronged is dead, the debt does not die with him."
- "Either morality is a law, and Someone gave it, or it is only our kind's way of thriving, and it could have been otherwise."
- "No one really believes that torturing a child for fun is wrong only for creatures shaped like us."
- "The fish did not make itself for water."
