# Round 1 — Kantian Constructivism vs. v1

## 1. School and voice

The opponent speaks for the Kantian tradition at full strength. That means Kant himself (the *Groundwork*, the second *Critique*, the *Metaphysics of Morals*, *Religion within the Bounds of Mere Reason*) and the constructivists who came after him: Rawls ("Kantian Constructivism in Moral Theory"), Christine Korsgaard (*The Sources of Normativity*, *Self-Constitution*, *Fellow Creatures*), Onora O'Neill, Barbara Herman, Allen Wood. Its core claim is that the moral law is the law of practical reason itself. A rational will is bound only by a law it gives itself as rational, and humanity (rational nature) is an end in itself with dignity, not price. God is at most a postulate of practical reason, needed for the hope of the highest good. He is never the ground of obligation, because grounding duty in any outside will, even God's, would make morality heteronomous.

This is the strongest secular opponent v1 has met so far. It agrees with much of A, it rejects voluntarism as firmly as the book does, and it explains several of the "undeniables" without a Lawgiver. The fair finding: v1's central witness, W1, fails against this school as written, and it can be repaired. Several of the Kantian's own answers also lean on God, and its founder admitted as much.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.**
*Kantian answer:* Justice starts from the one innate right every human being has by being human: freedom, meaning independence from being forced by another's choice, so far as it can coexist with everyone's freedom under a universal law (Kant's Universal Principle of Right). What anyone is owed comes from that. Rawls's version asks what free and equal persons would agree to behind a veil that hides who they are. Kant even derives duties to children: parents brought a person into the world without that person's consent, so they owe the child care (*Doctrine of Right* §28).
*Verdict: Holds.* It is coherent and does not borrow from the book. It is narrow, though. It grounds what is owed in *freedom*, not in the good of the person. So it is strong on rights against force and thin on what we owe to those who cannot exercise freedom, and on mercy (see Challenge 2).

**2. A hard case, resolved by their own principles.**
*Kantian answer:* A sheriff can stop a deadly riot only by framing and executing an innocent man. The Formula of Humanity says never treat a person merely as a means. So the sheriff may not do it, whatever the numbers. No borrowing is needed, and the answer is clean.
*The harder test:* Kant's own answer to the murderer at the door (you may not lie even to save your friend) is the famous place where his formal principle let truth crush mercy. Kantians repair it: Korsgaard ("The Right to Lie," 1986) uses the Formula of Humanity to permit the lie. Kant's mercy is also thin. He calls the sovereign's right to pardon the most slippery of rights, allows it only for crimes against the sovereign himself, and holds strict retribution otherwise.
*Verdict: Holds, with an internal break on mercy.* The repaired Kantian resolves hard cases well. But the repair works by judging what persons are actually owed beyond the bare form of the law, which is very close to the book's prudence (Ch. 11). Kant's unrepaired answers show the book's claim from Chs. 6–9 at work: truth and justice torn away from mercy break down when they are lived.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.**
*Kantian answer:* Kant gave the modern world the distinction between dignity and price. Every rational being is an end in itself, equally and unconditionally, and the enemy included. No God is needed: anyone who values anything at all must value rational nature, the source of all valuing (Korsgaard's regress argument).
*Verdict: Holds for the enemy and the competent adult. Borrows or breaks for the newborn, the severely disabled, and advanced dementia.* If dignity rests on the capacity to legislate (rational agency), those who cannot yet or can no longer exercise it are left out. The best Kantian repairs are Wood's (respect rational nature even in undeveloped or broken forms) and Patrick Kain's reading (Kant gives moral status to every member of the rational *kind*, infants included). Both move dignity from what someone can *do* to the *kind of being* someone *is*. That is the natural-law answer, not a constructivist one. Korsgaard's later work (*Fellow Creatures*, 2018) goes the other way and extends end-in-itself status to animals, which keeps worth but loosens the specifically equal human dignity the book defends. *Dignitas Infinita* 24 names this exact error: tying dignity to the capacity for knowledge and freedom, which would leave out the unborn, the dependent elderly and people with mental disabilities.

**4. The ultimate end of human flourishing.**
*Kantian answer:* Kant: the highest good, which is virtue (being worthy of happiness) joined to happiness in proportion to it, in an "ethical commonwealth" or kingdom of ends. Secular constructivists: the unified, self-constituted agent living in a just society.
*Verdict: Kant borrows openly; the secular version holds but is thinner.* Kant argued that the highest good is commanded, so it must be possible, and it is possible only if there is a God and a life beyond death. Hence his postulates. In the *Critique of Judgment* (§87) he pictures a righteous man, like Spinoza, who is convinced there is no God and no future life. Kant says such a man must either give up his moral end or assume a moral Author of the world. Secular constructivists drop the postulates as Kant's piety. What remains is coherent, but it has no answer for the victims who died unrighted, and no hope that goodness and happiness ever meet.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1: Self-legislation against W1 (the main hit)**
- *Kantian:* W1 says a claim no human can cancel "was not laid by any human." That is false on my view. The claim is laid by every human being *as a rational legislator*. No particular person's wishes can cancel it, and the reason is that it is not anyone's private will. It is reason's law, which is the same in each of us. A3 is fully explained, and no Someone above every human is needed. Your chapter even says "only a Someone" can lay a claim. I agree, and the Someone is you, me and the victim.
- *Patrick's side:* A lawgiver who cannot repeal, amend or decline his own law is not its author. He is its subject. This is the "Kantian paradox" of self-legislation, and some of Kant's best recent readers (Kleingeld and Willaschek, "Autonomy without Paradox," 2019) conclude that Kant never meant we *author* the moral law. He meant we are bound only by laws of our own reason. So reason stands to the law as a judge stands to a law he finds already in force. "Reason legislates" names where the law is read, not who made it.
- *Kantian rejoinder (Korsgaard):* No author is needed beyond reason. The law is *constitutive* of willing, the way the rules of chess make chess what it is. You cannot opt out, because you cannot opt out of acting.
- *Reply:* Inescapability is not authority. This is Enoch's "shmagency" objection: showing that I cannot avoid a norm does not show that I *owe* anything by it. Constitutivism also explains why the law shapes *my* acting, but not why I am *guilty toward you* when I break it, or why that guilt can be forgiven. W1 should stand there.
- **Verdict: W1 Failed as written** (it trades on "laid by a human" in a way this school rightly rejects). **Held with repair:** move W1's weight from "who laid the claim" to guilt and forgiveness, and present the Catholic view as "participated theonomy" (see §6).

**Exchange 2: Heteronomy**
- *Kantian:* If the law binds because God commands it, morality is obedience to an outside will, which makes it heteronomy. Even the Holy One of the Gospel, Kant says in the *Groundwork*, must first be measured against our own idea of moral perfection before we recognize Him as good. So God is either redundant (we already know the good) or corrupting (we obey for the wrong reason).
- *Patrick's side:* The Catholic view is not command-based. Euthyphro was answered in Ch. 3: God's commands reveal the Good He is. The natural law is reason's own participation in the eternal law (ST I-II q.91 a.2). *Veritatis Splendor* 40–41 grants the rightful autonomy of practical reason, rejects an autonomy that makes reason the creator of values, and calls the true relation "participated theonomy": human reason and will share in God's wisdom. On this view the law is ours because it is in our reason, and His because our reason is His gift.
- *Kantian rejoinder:* Then God is idle. Reason knows the law and obeys it without Him.
- *Reply:* Idle for *knowing* the law, yes. Romans 2:14–15 says exactly that: the Gentiles do by nature what the law requires without knowing its Author. Not idle for the law's *being*. The question was never whether you can see the law without God. It is what the law you see *is*, and why it has authority over your reason itself.
- **Verdict: Held.**

**Exchange 3: A2/A3 and forgiveness (a contradiction caught)**
- *Kantian:* A3 says "not the victim" can release us. But your own Lord tells victims to forgive, and makes their forgiveness by God depend on it (Matt 6:14–15). If the victim's forgiveness is real, A3 is false. If it is not real, the Lord's Prayer is empty.
- *Patrick's side:* Fair hit; A3 needs a distinction. The victim can forgive what was done to *him*, and he should. What he cannot do is make the act not have been wrong, or lift the guilt the wrongdoer bears before the good itself. Sin is a wrong against the neighbor *and* against God (CCC 1849–1850). The victim's forgiveness heals the bond between two people. It does not undo the wrong against the law.
- *Kantian rejoinder:* The leftover "wrong against the good" is a wrong against humanity in my own person. It is met by self-reform, Kant's "change of heart," in which the new man takes on the penalty of the old. No Person is required.
- *Reply:* Kant's own *Religion* says otherwise. He admits that radical evil cannot be rooted out by human powers, that the old debt is not cancelled by later good conduct, and that we may need a help from beyond whose working we cannot know. Reason can condemn us. It cannot forgive us. An abstraction like "humanity" cannot forgive anyone.
- *Kantian rejoinder:* Then perhaps guilt is simply permanent. A need does not prove there is something to meet it (your own v0 lesson).
- *Reply:* Granted as logic. But Kant's own principle, that ought implies can, says that if we ought to become good again, it must be possible. The Kantian must either give that principle up or hope as Kant did. This is an inference to the best explanation, not a proof, and that is all a converging case claims.
- **Verdict: Held with repair** (A3 reworded; W1 rests on this ground).

**Exchange 4: Equal dignity (A4)**
- *Kantian:* "Dignity, not price" is our phrase. Every rational being has equal, unconditional worth, the enemy included, and no God is needed.
- *Patrick's side:* Granted for the enemy and the competent adult, and the book should say so warmly. But the newborn cannot yet legislate, and the man with advanced dementia no longer can. On your ground, what is their dignity?
- *Kantian rejoinder (Wood, Kain):* We respect rational nature in its undeveloped and damaged forms, and Kant gives status to every member of the rational kind.
- *Reply:* Then dignity belongs to what a being *is*, not to what it can do. That is the natural-law answer, and the constructivist ground (agency confers value) has been left behind. You are now a realist about the worth of a nature. The question returns: why does a nature have worth that is unconditional and equal? *Dignitas Infinita* 24 warns against tying dignity to the present ability to know and choose. Korsgaard's other road, extending end-in-itself status to animals, keeps worth but gives up equal human dignity in the book's sense.
- **Verdict: Held (for our side).** On the newborn and the disabled, the Kantian borrows or breaks.

**Exchange 5: A5 turned against theism**
- *Kantian:* Your A5 says no description of what we are yields an "ought." Then "God is the Good" is a description of what God is, and it yields no ought either. And ends given to us, even by a Creator, ground only hypothetical imperatives: *if* you want to reach your end, do this. A categorical ought cannot come from any end someone else set for you.
- *Patrick's side:* Aquinas agrees with you on the first step. Reason grasps "good is to be done" as first and self-evident (ST I-II q.94 a.2), not inferred from a description, and here the Catholic and the Kantian stand together. They part over what that grasp is a grasp *of*. God is not one more fact beside others. He is the Good itself, and the end we are made for is not an outside goal set for us. It is that Good, which reason already recognizes as good.
- *Kantian rejoinder:* That is a stopping point by stipulation.
- *Reply:* So is yours. Kant calls the moral law a "fact of reason": given, not derived, not further explained. Every account stops somewhere (as the chapter already says). The question is which stopping point explains more: guilt *and* forgiveness, the dignity of those who cannot reason, and the hope Kant himself could not drop.
- **Verdict: Held with repair.** W1's line about what we are "for" must not read as a *derivation* of the ought (see §6).

**Exchange 6: W2 and reason as the law's own form**
- *Kantian:* W2's "luck" argument does not touch me. I am neither a realist about outside facts nor a mere evolutionist. The law is the form of the mind itself, so there is no "meeting" to explain. And true duties never conflict (Kant says so in the *Metaphysics of Morals*), because they flow from one supreme principle. Unity explained, no God needed.
- *Patrick's side:* Then the question moves. Why does a reason that arose in an animal give the *same* law in every mind? Why does it bind me toward others I never chose? Why does it condemn me with a judgment I cannot appeal? Kant answers by pointing beyond nature to a freedom he says can never be known by theory. So the Kantian also stops beyond nature, at something he calls unknowable. The theist stops at Someone partly known, and personal. And unity of *form* is not unity of truth, justice, mercy and self-gift. Kant's unrepaired answers (the murderer at the door, his narrow mercy) show a formally unified law letting truth and justice crush mercy.
- *Kantian rejoinder:* The repaired Kantian (Korsgaard) allows the lie and treats being unforgiving as a vice.
- *Reply:* Fair; the repaired version integrates better. But the repair works by judging what persons are owed beyond the form, which is love's prudence, not the bare universal law.
- **Verdict: Held with repair.** W2 must speak to this school directly.

**Exchange 7: The critical limit on reasoning to God**
- *Kantian:* C reasons to the "best explanation": a transcendent Person. Theoretical reason cannot go beyond possible experience. I offered a moral argument too, but it gives practical faith, not knowledge.
- *Patrick's side:* The Church teaches that God can be known with certainty from created things by the natural light of reason (Vatican I, *Dei Filius* ch. 2; CCC 36). That is a direct disagreement with Kant's limit, and the limit is itself a contested philosophical thesis, not a finding. v1 also claims far *less* than *Dei Filius* allows: "converging and convincing," able to "predispose" to faith. And Kant's own moral argument (the highest good; the righteous unbeliever of CJ §87) shows practical reason itself reaching toward God.
- *Kantian rejoinder:* Then do not cite my postulate as evidence that God exists. I called it a need of reason, not knowledge.
- *Reply:* Agreed. The book should cite Kant as an *opposing witness* to the shape of moral life ("even he found…"), never as an authority for its conclusion. Kant's limit contradicts *Dei Filius*, so under the lab rule he cannot be cited for our side.
- **Verdict: Held (parity).**

**Exchange 8: The chapter text itself**
- *Kantian:* The chapter says "every attempt to build morality on human reason divorced from God collapses." Mine has not collapsed; it is the main framework of modern human-rights thought. It says "a law requires a lawgiver," and I answer that I am the lawgiver. Its reply to Korsgaard, that "only a ground outside the practice can close that gap," begs the question for realism, and by your own A5 a ground that is merely a fact closes nothing.
- *Patrick's side:* Conceded on "collapses": overclaim. The honest claim is that reason divorced from God can see a great deal (DI 1) but leaves particular things unexplained: forgiveness, the dignity of those who cannot reason, the hope of the highest good. The "lawgiver" line needs the paradox reply from Exchange 1. The Korsgaard paragraph should be rewritten along the lines of Exchanges 3–4, not by appeal to "a ground outside the practice."
- **Verdict: Held with repair** (chapter text, see §7).

## 5. Remaining sound objections (after the repairs in §6)

1. **"Need does not prove supply" (against the forgiveness form of W1). Minor.** The Kantian can hold that guilt is real and simply permanent. Our answer, that Kant's own ought-implies-can drives him to hope for help, reaches parity but does not compel. That is acceptable for a converging case, which claims only to be the best explanation, and v1 says so.
2. **"God is idle for obligation." Minor.** Answered by separating knowing from being (Rom 2:14–15). The Kantian can keep pressing that an unneeded ground is an idle wheel. Our side answers to parity: a ground that explains guilt, forgiveness, the dignity of those who cannot reason, and the highest hope is not idle.
3. **The critical limit on reasoning to God. Minor.** A standing philosophical disagreement, not a defeat. Our side's premises (*Dei Filius*, the self-undermining nature of the limit) are at least as plausible as Kant's.

No Fatal or Serious objection remains once W1, A3 and C are repaired. As written, W1 and A3 carry a **Serious** objection (Exchanges 1 and 3), and C's missing rival is **Serious** by omission.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**A3 (replace):**
> **A3. No one on earth can fully release us.** The one we wronged can forgive what was done to him, and he should. But he cannot make it not have been wrong, and he cannot sign away his own dignity. Neither can a nation by vote, nor the whole human race, nor our own reason, which can condemn us but cannot forgive us.

**A4 (add one clause):**
> **A4. Every human being has the same dignity**, because of what each one is, not what each one can yet do or can still do. The newborn, the dying, the severely disabled, the enemy: equal, prior to anyone's recognition (*Dignitas Infinita* 1, 15, 24).

**A6 (replace first sentence):**
> **A6. Real good for another costs the giver something:** time, comfort, safety, sometimes far more. That is how reality works. And some things must never be done, even to save oneself.

**W1 (replace):**
> **W1. Guilt points to a Lawgiver.** When we do wrong, we owe a debt to the one we hurt, and he can forgive it. But part of the debt no human can lift (A3). The law we broke is truly our own: it is written in our reason, and our reason judges us by it. Yet our reason did not make that law, cannot repeal it, and cannot forgive us for breaking it. A law no human mind made, which binds every mind alike, and a guilt only a person could forgive but no human can, are best explained by Someone above every human being. He is not a commander who picks rules but the Creator, who made us and our reason, established what we are for, and is Himself the Good He asks of us. The law is ours because it is in our reason, and His because our reason is His gift. The Church calls this a "participated theonomy" (*Veritatis Splendor* 41; cf. ST I-II q.91 a.2).

**W2 (replace):**
> **W2. The law and the mind that reads it point to one Source.** There are two ways to deny this, and both stop short. If moral facts simply exist outside us and conscience is only an evolved survival tool, it is luck that they ever met. And conscience reads the law most surely exactly where survival pulls against it: truth told at cost, mercy to an enemy, care for those who can never repay. If instead the law is simply the form of reason itself, the meeting is no longer luck, but the question moves. Why does a reason that arose in an animal give the same law in every mind, bind it toward others it never chose, and judge it with a verdict it cannot appeal? Even Kant could answer only by pointing past nature to a freedom he said we can never know. Truth, justice, mercy and self-gift, followed to their depths, never finally contradict. A law that is one, and minds fitted to read it, are best explained by one Source who made both.

**W3 (add after the first sentence):**
> Reason itself cannot stop hoping that goodness and happiness will finally meet, and that the good who died unrighted are not simply lost. Even Kant, who would not make God the ground of duty, found he could not keep that hope without Him.

**C (insert after "Brute moral facts"):**
> **Reason alone** (the law as reason's own law, as Kant held) explains much: why the law binds every rational being alike, why no one can repeal it, and why we judge ourselves by it (A1, A3, A5, part of A2). It leaves three things unexplained. Reason can condemn but not forgive (A2–A3). It struggles to hold equal dignity for those who cannot yet reason, or can no longer reason (A4). And by its founder's own admission, it cannot keep its highest hope without God (A7).

## 7. New insights for the book, and lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **The Kantian is the opponent to answer by name.** He shares A1, A5 and half of A6, rejects voluntarism as firmly as Ch. 3 does, and gave the world the vocabulary of dignity. The chapter's "noblest attempt" paragraph should be rebuilt on Exchanges 3–4. The question is not whether practice reaches the world. It is whether reason can forgive, and whether dignity based on agency covers the newborn.
- **Present natural law as participated theonomy, not as command.** It disarms the heteronomy objection in one line (VS 40–41), and it keeps faith with Ch. 3's answer to Euthyphro.
- **Forgiveness, not "who laid the claim," is the strongest ground for W1.** Every secular school can name a claimant (the neighbor, humanity, reason). None can name someone who forgives the part of the debt no human can lift.
- **Cite Kant as an opposing witness, never as an authority.** His limit on reasoning to God contradicts *Dei Filius*. But two of his admissions are fair game: radical evil cannot be rooted out by human powers (*Religion*), and the righteous unbeliever must either give up his moral end or assume a moral Author (CJ §87).
- **Chapter-text fixes this round exposed:** soften "every attempt to build morality on human reason divorced from God collapses" (overclaim; compare DI 1). Answer "I am the lawgiver" wherever the chapter says "a law requires a lawgiver." The "only a Someone can" line must go on to say why the Someone is above every human, using the forgiveness argument.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "Reason can condemn us. It cannot forgive us."
- "The one we wronged can forgive what was done to him. He cannot make it not have been wrong."
- "The law is ours because it is in our reason, and His because our reason is His gift."
- "We did not write the law. We found ourselves already under it, and we cannot repeal it."
- "A newborn cannot yet reason. She is still someone."
- "Even the philosopher who worked hardest to keep God out of the ground of duty could not keep Him out of its hope."

## 8. Score

**6/10 as written; 8/10 with the repairs in §6.** As written, W1 and A3 give way to self-legislation and to the victim's real forgiveness, and C omits the strongest secular rival. Repaired, the case meets the Kantian at parity on obligation and beats him on forgiveness, on the dignity of those who cannot reason, and on hope, the three places where Kant himself reached for God.
