# Round 1 — The Problem of Evil and Divine Hiddenness

Version under test: v1 (EVOLUTION.md). Chapter text checked: chapter_12_current.md. Book sources used for our side: Ch. 1 ("But Why Does God Permit Evil?", privation, sin as choosing a lesser good), Ch. 2 ("A Note on Natural Evil"), Ch. 11 (the cellar case).

---

## 1. School and voice

The staunch evidential atheist: the logical problem in the line of J. L. Mackie, the evidential problem of William Rowe (the fawn burned in a forest fire, the child beaten and killed), Paul Draper's "hypothesis of indifference" (pain and pleasure are distributed the way an indifferent universe would distribute them), J. L. Schellenberg's hiddenness argument (a perfectly loving God would make a conscious relationship with Him available to every non-resisting person), and Stephen Law's "evil-god challenge" (whatever lets you set aside an evil creator because of the world's goodness should let me set aside a good one because of its evil). His core charge against this chapter: the argument from love proves too much. The higher you lift love and human dignity, the darker the world looks, and a God who IS Love is exactly the God the world counts against.

---

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.**
*His answer:* Suffering is bad in itself, and sentient beings are owed not to be made to suffer for nothing. Justice is giving each being's interests equal weight and never using the innocent as a mere tool. Most in this school are secular moral realists; the badness of agony is a brute fact, known directly.
*Verdict: Holds as a position, stops at brute facts.* It is coherent and does not secretly borrow from theism; it simply stops where C says "brute moral facts" stop. But note what matters most for this debate: **the argument from evil does not need his starting point at all.** He can run it as an internal test of our own premises (A1, A4, "God is Love"). Our side may not treat "you have no standard to complain with" as an answer to him (see Exchange 2).

**2. A hard case resolved.** The Ch. 11 cellar: twenty hidden, the infant cries, soldiers above.
*His answer:* Many in this school would say the parent may silence the child even unto death, on a "no one is made worse off" principle: the child dies either way, and twenty live. The more cautious would call it a tragic act, not a wrong one. Either way the principle is his own (minimize death and agony where no one loses), not a disguised version of the book's truth, justice, mercy and self-gift.
*Verdict: Holds, but the disagreement is real.* He does not borrow; he differs. He grants A6's second half ("some things must never be done") in general and denies it here. Ch. 11's answer (the question is *who does the evil*) is the one our side needs, and he will turn it back on God (Exchange 7).

**3. Ground of equal, unconditional dignity.**
*His answer:* Two voices. The sentience-based voice (the larger party) grounds equal *consideration of interests*, not equal dignity, and admits that capacities come in degrees: the anencephalic newborn and the permanently unconscious count for less. The realist voice affirms equal dignity as a brute fact.
*Verdict: Breaks, or stops at a brute fact.* The sentience voice denies A4 outright. The realist voice asserts it without a ground. The irony deserves noting fairly: the problem of evil hits hardest exactly when the suffering child is treated as of unconditional worth, which is our premise, not his. That is not a refutation, since he may borrow our premise to test us, but it means his *own* view cannot make the dying child's agony weigh as much as ours does.

**4. Ultimate human flourishing.**
*His answer:* A life of love, knowledge, friendship and relief of suffering, within mortality. Nothing is ultimately good beyond death. Guiding principles: compassion, honesty, solidarity, courage to face a world that does not care.
*Verdict: Holds as coherent, breaks on A7.* He answers A7 ("love refuses death") with tragic acceptance: the longing is real and simply goes unmet. That is consistent. But his view does not remove evil; it removes hope for its victims. The child who died in agony is, on his account, finally and forever unredeemed. That is not evidence that God exists (truth does not bend to what is hopeful, and he is right to say so), but it is a fair point for C's "where each account stops."

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1 — The logical problem.**
*Attack:* An all-good being removes evil as far as he can; an all-powerful one can remove all of it; evil exists; so no such being exists.
*Reply:* Ch. 1: God will not create slaves. For a creature still on the way to God, the freedom to love includes the freedom to refuse (CCC 1730, 311). Augustine, quoted at CCC 311 and by Aquinas (ST I q.2 a.3 ad 1): God permits evil only because He can bring good even out of it. Alvin Plantinga's free-will defense (Protestant, but built on Augustine) shows there is no contradiction: it is possible that a world with free creatures and no moral evil was not in God's power to make, since it depends on their choices.
*Rejoinder:* The literature, including Rowe and Draper, largely grants that the logical version fails. But free will covers only the evil free creatures do.
**Held.** The logical problem is answered. The evidential problem remains.

**Exchange 2 — "Your rage appeals to a standard."**
*Attack:* Ch. 1 (and Ch. 12: "Every time an atheist says 'That's wrong!' they're providing evidence for God's existence") answers the problem of evil by saying the atheist's outrage presupposes a moral law he cannot ground. That is a dodge. I run the argument inside your premises: *you* say a child's torture is absolutely wrong (A1), that she has dignity "prior to any recognition" (A4), that God is Love. I am showing that your three claims plus the world do not fit together. I need no standard of my own.
*Reply:* Granted. The move answers the moral nihilist, not the atheist who runs evil as an internal test.
**Failed (the move).** Our side must stop using "your complaint presupposes a standard" as a reply to the problem of evil. It may keep it against the person who denies that anything is really wrong.

**Exchange 3 — Your own premises sharpen the problem.**
*Attack:* A1 and A4 are the data of the argument from evil. The higher the dignity, the heavier the permission. A thin "designer" hypothesis predicts little about suffering; "a God who IS Love, who knows each person wholly and loved each first" predicts a world where children are not tortured to death. The more you claim about God's love, the lower the likelihood of this world on your hypothesis. That is what "proves too much" means.
*Reply:* Three things. (a) Catholic faith does not say God merely permits from a distance. In Christ He entered suffering Himself; the Cross is where God answers the "why" — not with a theory, but by suffering with us and for us (Salvifici Doloris, esp. the sections on Christ's suffering and the Gospel of suffering). (b) Permission is ordered to an end: God brings good out of evil (CCC 312), and the end includes the raising of the dead and every tear wiped away (Revelation 21:4). Marilyn McCord Adams (Anglican, a scholar of the medieval Catholic tradition) argued that horrendous evils can be *defeated*, not merely outweighed, only by God Himself: the victim's whole life is made, within union with God, a great good to her, so that she herself would not wish it erased. (c) A6 cuts both ways: the hypothesis "God is self-giving Love" predicts a world in which love costs, and so a world in which loss is possible.
*Rejoinder:* (a) and (b) tell me what God would have to do if He exists. They show the story is not contradictory. They are not evidence. And Ivan Karamazov's protest stands: even if the child is compensated, a harmony bought with her torture is one I refuse. (c) Your own brief says self-gift within the Trinity is "total and without loss," and heaven is love with no agony. So love does not *require* agony. Why not start there?
*Second reply:* Ch. 1 answers "why not heaven first" for free creatures: the fullness of freedom cannot be installed at the start, because a love never freely given is not love. And Ivan's protest is itself love's voice. The Church does not silence it; Salvifici Doloris treats the "why" as a question a person puts to a Person, and Job, who protests, is vindicated over the friends who explain.
**Held with repair.** As a *defense* (no contradiction; a coherent hope that answers horror at its worst) our side reaches parity. As *evidence*, it adds nothing, and C must say so. The "why not heaven first" answer covers free creatures only (see Exchange 4).

**Exchange 4 — Gratuitous suffering and animals.**
*Attack:* Rowe's fawn: trapped in a forest fire, burned, dying slowly over days, seen by no one, shaping no one's soul. Multiply by hundreds of millions of years of predation, parasitism and disease before any human chose anything. Free will (Ch. 1) cannot touch it; Ch. 1 itself defers it to Ch. 2, and Ch. 2 says the Church holds "not a solution but a testimony." Draper: an indifferent universe predicts this pattern of pain far better than a loving God does.
*Reply:* (a) CCC 310: God willed a world "in a state of journeying" toward its perfection; in such a world, alongside coming-to-be there is passing-away, and physical evil exists as long as creation has not reached perfection. (b) Aquinas (ST I q.22 a.2 ad 2; q.48 a.2): a universe with real gradations of being, and real natures acting by their own powers, includes things that can fail; the lion's life requires the death of other animals. (c) Romans 8:21: "the creation itself will be set free from its bondage to decay." (d) CCC 314: "the ways of his providence are often unknown to us"; only at the end will we fully know them. We are not in a position to judge that God has no reason. This is "skeptical theism": Stephen Wykstra's point that failing to see a reason is evidence of no reason only if we would expect to see one, and the gap between God's mind and ours is greater than between a parent's and an infant's.
*Rejoinder:* (a)–(b) justify predation as an ecological fact, but they treat the fawn's agony as a cost of the system. A God who sees every sparrow fall (Matthew 10:29) could keep the order and spare the agony. (d) is a double-edged sword. If you cannot judge what God's reasons would produce, you cannot judge that a loving God would produce A1–A7 either; W1–W3 all say "best explained by." And it paralyzes morals: if God may have reasons for the fawn, maybe I should not pull the child from the fire.
*Second reply:* On the double edge: the two cases are not alike. A1–A7 are things we *see*; God's reasons for a permission are things we would *not expect to see*. Not seeing a reason is weaker evidence than seeing a love. On moral paralysis: our duty does not come from guessing God's permissions; it comes from the law written on our hearts. We may never do evil that good may come (CCC 1789), and we are not the Creator who can raise the dead and make the victim whole. The parent in Ch. 11 is bound in ways God is not, because the parent cannot undo death.
*Final rejoinder:* The asymmetry is real but partial. You still have no account of why the fawn suffers; at best you have shown I cannot prove it is pointless. That leaves weight on my side of the scale. Your C says "A God who is Love explains all of it at one stopping point." He explains none of *this*.
**Held with repair on gratuitousness; Failed for C as written.** Our side reaches parity on "you cannot show this evil is pointless." It does not reach parity on "God explains it." The book must stop claiming God explains *all* of it.

**Exchange 5 — Hiddenness: W3 is the first premise of the argument against you.**
*Attack:* W3 says the longing for communion is best explained by "a Person who knows us wholly, can hold us forever, and loved us first." Schellenberg's argument begins exactly there: a perfectly loving God would always make a conscious, two-way relationship available to anyone not resisting it. But there are honest seekers, people who pray for years to find Him and do not, whole peoples who never heard of Him. Your own Church forbids you to call them culpable (CCC 847, LG 16). So non-resisting non-belief exists. So that God does not exist. And Ch. 12 says some do not believe "not because the evidence is insufficient." That is false on your own Church's terms.
*Reply 1 (freedom):* Overwhelming evidence would coerce; a gift forced is no gift (Ch. 4).
*Rejoinder:* Knowing God exists does not force anyone to love Him. "Even the demons believe — and shudder" (James 2:19). Many believers resist Him. Belief is not coercion.
**Reply 1 Failed.**
*Reply 2 (veiled availability):* The Church holds three things together. (a) Knowing God by reason alone is hard for us as we are (CCC 37), so the chapter's "not because the evidence is insufficient" is an overclaim and must go. (b) God is a God who hides Himself (Isaiah 45:15); Pascal (Catholic) held that He gives enough light for those who want to see and enough shadow for those who do not, so that the search itself forms the heart. (c) Most important: Providence does not deny the helps needed for salvation to those who, without blame, have not yet come to an explicit knowledge of God and, by His grace, strive to live a good life (LG 16). The relationship is already offered under the form of the good their conscience shows them. The chapter already says it: we follow "a light without yet knowing its name." A child is loved, held and fed long before he can say who loves him; the relationship is real before it is conscious, and the full face-to-face knowing comes at the end (1 Corinthians 13:12).
*Rejoinder:* An anonymous influence is not a personal relationship. A loving parent does not stay behind a curtain while the child cries for him.
*Third reply:* Not behind a curtain: in the face of every neighbor and the voice of every conscience; and, in the end, face to face. W3 itself says no one knows the whole of us; love that grows over a lifetime is not less love. The Catholic claim is not that the honest seeker is outside the relationship, but that he is inside it without yet knowing its name.
*Final rejoinder:* That is a coherent answer, and better than the free-will one. It is still costly: conscious, reciprocal communion is what W3 says we long for, and many die without it.
**Held with repair.** Parity, provided W3 owns the hiddenness question, drops any claim that the evidence is plain to all, and borrows the chapter's own "light without yet knowing its name." A real but no longer decisive cost remains.

**Exchange 6 — "Proves too much": the evil-god challenge.**
*Attack (Law):* You set aside an evil creator because the world is full of love, beauty and goodness. By the same reasoning I set aside a good one because the world is full of cruelty. The argument from love and the argument from evil are mirror images. Why should your half of the mirror win?
*Reply:* The mirror is not symmetrical. Evil has no being of its own; it is always the wounding or lack of some good in something that is good (Ch. 1, Augustine). So there can be a Supreme Good, but there cannot be a supreme Evil (Aquinas, ST I q.49 a.3): evil has nothing of its own to be supreme in. Power, intelligence, existence itself are goods; a perfectly evil being would need them all and so would be good in all that it is. Darkness in the world is the darkness of good things being harmed, and that is why we can recognize it as darkness.
*Rejoinder:* That is classical metaphysics, a separate argument, not something v1 contains. Within v1 the mirror stands.
*Second reply:* Ch. 1 already teaches privation; one sentence in C brings it in. And the love-data are positive: obligation, guilt, dignity, communion. An indifferent or evil source does not predict them.
*Final rejoinder:* An indifferent universe plus evolution predicts much of the felt side of them. That fight belongs to the debunker and the realist; I grant you the evil-god version is answered.
**Held with repair.** The evil-god challenge is answered by privation, but v1 does not yet say it. Add one line to C.

**Exchange 7 — The bystander, and A3 turned against mercy.**
*Attack, part one:* Ch. 11 says the parent in the cellar may not smother the child to save twenty, because "who does the evil" matters more than the count. Fine. But God is the one who could stop the soldiers at no cost and does not. Any human who could stop a murder at no cost and stood by would be guilty.
*Reply:* Ch. 11's distinction holds for God: permitting is not doing, and the soldiers' murder is theirs. And God is not one bystander among others. He is the Creator who holds every agent in being, whose permission is ordered to a good He alone can bring (CCC 311–312), and who alone can raise the victim and set every wrong right. A human bystander's duty to intervene comes from what he is: a creature who cannot undo death.
*Rejoinder:* Some Catholic thinkers (Brian Davies, O.P.) go further and say God is not a moral agent at all, so the problem misfires. If you take that road, "God is Love" loses its meaning and the whole argument from love, which reasons from our love to His, falls with it.
*Second reply:* Agreed, and we decline that road. God's love is not less than ours but more (ST I q.13 a.5: what we say of Him, we say by analogy, truly but not in the same measure). We hold only that His permission differs from a bystander's because His power to restore differs.
*Attack, part two:* A3 says not even the victim can release the wrongdoer. Then how can God forgive the man who tortured the child? Ivan's point: the mother may forgive her own pain, but not the child's.
*Reply:* Every wrong is also a wrong against God (CCC 1849–1850; Psalm 51:4), so He is never a third party forgiving someone else's debt. And His mercy does not cancel justice; in God they are one (divine simplicity; Chs. 7–8). The torturer must repent, and the child is not left unrepaid: she is raised and made whole. Forgiveness that left the victim in the ground would be injustice. God's does not.
**Held with repair.** Both parts reach parity, but W1 should say plainly what A3 implies: only the One against whom every wrong is finally done, and who can make the victim whole, can forgive without injustice. The bystander objection collapses into the same leftover as Exchange 4: why not stop it? CCC 314 is the honest answer: we do not yet know.

**Exchange 8 — The bookkeeping.**
*Attack:* Look at C. A1–A7 get seven lines and three witnesses. Evil and hiddenness get one line, a parenthesis, and a promise that Ch. 1 answered it "in part." That is not a scale; it is a thumb on one.
*Reply:* Granted. C must give the other side its full weight, say what our answers do and do not cover, and then say why the weight does not overturn the case.
**Failed as written; repair below.**

## 4. Ledger for v1

- **Form (converging case, CCC 31/35):** **Holds.** This is what saves v1 from a fatal result: it never claims proof, so evil being real weight does not refute it.
- **A1 (some things are really wrong for everyone):** **Holds.** This school grants it, and uses it as the first premise against us.
- **A2 (guilty, not merely mistaken):** **Holds.** Not this school's target.
- **A3 (no one on earth can release us):** **Holds with repair.** Read alone, it seems to bar God from forgiving too (Ivan). Fix in W1, not in A3.
- **A4 (equal dignity):** **Holds.** It heightens the problem of evil, and the book should say so openly.
- **A5 (human nature does not settle the ought):** **Holds.** Not this school's target.
- **A6 (real good costs the giver):** **Holds.** It cuts both ways: it fits a God of self-giving Love in a fallen world, and it raises "why build agony into the cost?" Ch. 1 answers this for free creatures only.
- **A7 (longing to be known wholly; love refuses death):** **Holds with repair.** "We grieve because love was meant to last" assumes the conclusion. Say "we grieve as though love were meant to last."
- **W1 (obligation points to a Lawgiver):** **Holds with repair.** Add that only He can forgive without injustice, because only He can make the victim whole.
- **W2 (law and mind, one Source):** **Holds** against this school (left to the debunker and realist).
- **W3 (communion points to One who is Love):** **Fails as written.** It is the first premise of the hiddenness argument and does not answer it; "best explained" ignores the evidence against. With the repair below it reaches parity.
- **C, the four account lines:** **Holds**, with one addition: atheism does not remove evil; it removes hope for its victims. This is a fact about where each account stops, not evidence.
- **C, "A God who is Love explains all of it at one stopping point":** **Fails.** He does not explain the fawn or the child with cancer; He answers them in part, and we hope for the rest. Limit the claim to A1–A7.
- **C, evil and hiddenness as one line:** **Fails.** It is asymmetric bookkeeping, and it is the line a skeptical reader will quote.
- **C, conclusion:** **Holds with repair.** It must say that evil weighs against but does not overturn the case, and why.

**Chapter text (outside v1, flagged because it is live in chapter_12_current.md):**
- "Yet some still don't believe—not because the evidence is insufficient." **Fails** against CCC 37 and hiddenness.
- "Every time an atheist says 'That's wrong!' they're providing evidence for God's existence." **Holds against the nihilist only;** it must never be used as a reply to the problem of evil.
- "that takes far more faith than belief in God ever could" is already ruled out by the brief as an overclaim; this school would quote it alongside the evil the chapter passes over.
- Ch. 1, "it exposes the complaint as incoherent." **Overbroad.** It is true only of the complaint against moral evil; Ch. 1 itself defers natural evil to Ch. 2.

## 5. Remaining sound objections

1. **Natural and animal suffering without any visible reason** (Rowe's fawn; hundreds of millions of years before human choice; the child's cancer). Our side reaches parity on "you cannot show it is pointless" (skeptical theism with the seeing/not-seeing asymmetry, CCC 310, 314), but has no account of *why*, and free will (Ch. 1) does not apply. **Against v1 as written: Serious**, because C claims God "explains all of it." **Against v1 repaired: Minor**, because the repaired C owns this as real weight on the other side and claims only that it does not overturn the case. This is a cost the version carries, not a refutation of it.
2. **Divine hiddenness from non-resisting seekers.** **Against v1 as written: Serious** (W3 sets it up and does not answer it; the chapter says the evidence is not insufficient). **Against v1 repaired: Minor**: LG 16's veiled availability, CCC 37, and the chapter's own "light without yet knowing its name" bring it to parity; the residue is that conscious communion comes late for many.
3. **The bookkeeping in C** (evil as one line). **Serious** as written; fully answered by the repair.

Nothing here is Fatal, because v1 is a converging witness that already admits evil weighs against it. But it does not yet admit how much.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**A7 (replace):**
> **A7. We long to be known and loved wholly and for good, and no finite person can receive the whole of us.** Love resists death: we grieve as though love were meant to last.

**W1 (add as final sentence):**
> And only such a Someone could forgive a wrong without wronging the victim, because every wrong is finally done against Him (Psalm 51:4), and only He can raise the victim and make her whole.

**W3 (replace):**
> **W3. Communion points to One who is Love.** Only a person can love us back; a law, a force or an idea cannot. No finite person can receive the whole of us, because no one knows the whole of us, not even ourselves (A7). A longing does not prove that what it longs for exists, but it shows what kind of answer would fit: a Person who knows us wholly, can hold us forever, and loved us first (1 John 4:19). This witness also raises the hardest question: if such a Person loves us, why do many honest seekers not find Him? The Church does not say they are hiding from Him (CCC 847). She says that knowing God by reason alone is hard for us as we are (CCC 37), that He is a God who hides Himself (Isaiah 45:15), and that He does not withhold what is needed for salvation from anyone who, through no fault of his own, does not yet know Him and, by grace, tries to live a good life (*Lumen Gentium* 16). Such a person is already following a light without yet knowing its name. Every child is loved before he can say who loves him. Revelation gives the Lover's name: God is Love (1 John 4:8).

**C (replace the last three lines, from "A God who is Love explains all of it" on):**
> - **A God who is Love** explains A1–A7 at one stopping point. Every other account stops somewhere too; the account without God does not remove evil, it removes hope for evil's victims. That is not evidence, but it is where that account stops.
> - **On the other side of the scale is real weight, and it is heavy.** Children suffer horribly. Animals suffered for hundreds of millions of years before any human chose anything. Honest seekers look for God and do not find Him. Free will (Chapter 1) answers much of the evil we do to one another; it does not answer the fawn in the forest fire or the child with cancer. Here the Church does not claim an explanation: only Christian faith as a whole answers it (CCC 309); God permits evil only because He can bring a greater good out of it (CCC 311–312); and we will know His ways fully only at the end (CCC 314).
> - **Three things keep that weight from overturning the case.** First, evil has nothing of its own: it is always a good thing harmed (Chapter 1). There can be a Supreme Good, but there cannot be a supreme Evil (St. Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* I, q.49, a.3), so the world's darkness is not evidence of a dark God; it is the darkness of good things wounded. Second, the love in A1–A7 is something we see, while God's reasons for permitting evil are something we would not expect to see: the distance between His mind and ours is greater than between a parent's and an infant's. Not seeing a reason is weaker evidence than seeing a love. Third, the God this book describes is not a designer who watches from a distance. He entered suffering Himself, on the Cross, and promises to raise the dead and wipe away every tear (Revelation 21:4; *Salvifici Doloris*). That does not explain why. It means the last word over the suffering child is not the suffering.
> - **Conclusion:** weighing both sides honestly, the best explanation of love as we actually find it is a Lawgiver who is Love; evil weighs against that conclusion, but does not overturn it. This is reason's witness, converging and convincing (CCC 31); it can predispose to faith (CCC 35) but does not replace it.

**Chapter text (chapter_12_current.md, "From Moral Argument to Personal Encounter"):**
Replace "Yet some still don't believe—not because the evidence is insufficient. Sometimes the reason is a wound or an ignorance that is no fault of their own; sometimes it is what St. Paul warns of, holding down a truth we would rather not face (cf. Romans 1:18)." with:
> Yet some still don't believe. The Church is honest that knowing God by the light of reason alone is hard for us as we are (CCC 37). Sometimes the reason is a wound or an ignorance that is no fault of their own; sometimes it is what St. Paul warns of, holding down a truth we would rather not face (cf. Romans 1:18). Only God sees which, and He does not abandon the honest seeker (*Lumen Gentium* 16).

**Book-level note (Ch. 1, for Patrick's later pass, not this lab's file):** in "But Why Does God Permit Evil?", the "rage is an appeal to a standard" paragraph answers the person who denies that anything is truly wrong, not the person testing whether a good God fits the world; and "it exposes the complaint as incoherent" should be limited to the evil we do to one another.

## 7. New insights and lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **The problem of evil is the argument from love run backward.** It uses the same premises (A1, A4). The book should face it as the shadow of its own argument: the more truly love is lifted up, the darker evil looks. Ivan's protest is love's own voice, and the Church honors it (Job is vindicated over his explaining friends).
- **The chapter already holds the best Catholic answer to hiddenness** — "following a light without yet knowing its name" (¶ "Why Only God Can Ground Love") — but does not connect it to W3. LG 16 makes it doctrine, not just a fine phrase.
- **A3 and divine mercy belong together.** Because the victim cannot release the wrong, God's forgiveness must include making the victim whole. The resurrection is justice for victims, not only mercy for sinners. This ties Chs. 7–8 to Ch. 12.
- **Decline the "God is not a moral agent" escape.** It defeats the problem of evil only by cutting the analogy the whole argument from love depends on.
- **Skeptical theism helps us only with its asymmetry stated:** we can see love; we would not expect to see God's reasons. Without that sentence it is a sword that cuts W1–W3 too.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "Every child is loved before he can say who loves him."
- "Not seeing a reason is weaker evidence than seeing a love."
- "Evil has nothing of its own to be supreme in."
- "The last word over the suffering child is not the suffering."
- "Free will answers the evil we do to one another; it does not answer the fawn in the fire."
- "The account without God does not remove evil; it removes hope for evil's victims."

## 8. Score

**v1 as written: 4/10.** It survives only because it claims to be a witness, not a proof. C's one-line treatment of evil, its claim to explain "all of it," and a W3 that hands Schellenberg his first premise give this school its best openings.
**v1 with the repairs above: 7/10.** The logical problem, the evil-god challenge, hiddenness and the bystander objection all reach parity. What remains is an honestly owned weight: natural and animal suffering, whose reason we cannot see.
