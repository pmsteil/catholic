# Argument Lab, Round 1 — Confucian opponent vs. v1

## 1. School and voice

The opponent speaks for the Confucian tradition at full strength. That means the *Analects*; Mencius on the nature as good and the four sprouts; Xunzi on the nature as bad and goodness as cultivated; the *Doctrine of the Mean* and the *Great Learning*; and the Neo-Confucians (Zhang Zai, the Cheng brothers, Zhu Xi, Wang Yangming). It also draws on modern interpreters: Herbert Fingarette (ritual as "the secular as sacred"), Roger Ames and Henry Rosemont (role ethics), Tu Weiming and Mou Zongsan ("immanent transcendence"), Philip J. Ivanhoe and Joseph Chan. Its claim is that a person becomes humane (*ren*) inside relationships and roles, shaped by ritual propriety (*li*). On this view the moral order is rooted in Heaven (*Tian*): a generative, life-giving order that "does not speak" (*Analects* 17.19). It is sacred, and it is not a personal Lawgiver who gives commands.

*Note on accuracy.* "Tian as impersonal order" is the Xunzi and Neo-Confucian reading. Heaven in the *Analects* and *Mencius* is ambiguous and at times plainly person-like. That ambiguity carries real weight in this debate, and both sides use it below. *Analects* numbering follows common modern editions (Slingerland), which differ by one or two in places.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**Challenge 1. What is your starting point for justice?**
*Answer.* Justice starts from rightness (*yi*), which grows from the sprout of shame and disdain that every human heart has (*Mencius* 2A6). What anyone is owed is fixed by the relationship they stand in. There are five: ruler and minister, parent and child, husband and wife, elder and younger, friend and friend. Getting names right (*Analects* 13.3) means a father must be a father and a ruler a ruler. Debts run both ways: a child owes three years of mourning because a parent carried him for three years (*Analects* 17.21). Reciprocity is the general rule: do not do to others what you would not want done to you (12.2, 15.24). For the people as a whole, a ruler owes them a living, and one who loses the Mandate by tyranny is "a mere fellow," not a king (*Mencius* 1B8).
*Verdict: Holds inside relationships, and strains at the edges.* Within a role, this is the most worked-out account of what is owed that any non-theist school offers. At the stranger who holds no role, it has to reach past roles. It reaches either to the shared heart, which is compassion and not a debt, or to Heaven, which "sees as the people see" (*Mencius* 5A5). It does not borrow the book's terms. It does lean on Heaven exactly where roles run out.

**Challenge 2. How would you resolve a hard dilemma?**
*Answer, with two classic cases.*
(a) *The drowning sister-in-law* (*Mencius* 4A17). Ritual forbids a man and his brother's wife to touch hands, and she is drowning. You pull her out. Anyone who would not is a wolf. The tool is *quan*, "weighing": ritual serves humaneness, and when the two collide, humaneness wins.
(b) *Shun's father commits murder* (*Mencius* 7A35). Shun is emperor. He does not stop the judge from arresting his father. Instead he gives up the empire "like an old sandal," carries his father on his back to the coast, and lives there content. Justice is not obstructed, filial love is not betrayed, and the cost falls on Shun.
*Verdict: Coherent, and it borrows.* Case (a) is prudence ordering a rule to its end (the book's Ch. 11). Case (b) is justice kept, mercy shown, and self-gift paid in full: the book's facets, reached without the book's vocabulary. That is evidence *for* A6, not against the book. Case (b) also exposes a weak point. The murdered man's family is never served, and Chinese scholars have argued for two decades over whether 7A35 and *Analects* 13.18 (the son who covers for a father who stole a sheep) license favoritism (Liu Qingping against Guo Qiyong and others). Graded love settles some cases at the expense of the stranger.

**Challenge 3. What grounds equal, unconditional dignity?**
*Answer.* Every person has the four sprouts (2A6). The sage and I are of the same kind (6A7). Anyone can become a Yao or a Shun (6B2). There is a "nobility of Heaven" (humaneness, rightness, loyalty, good faith) that is not the nobility men hand out, and what a powerful man confers he can take away (6A16–17). A cruel man is like Ox Mountain stripped of its trees: the mountain's nature is still to grow them (6A8). For Xunzi, humans are the most precious thing under Heaven because they alone have rightness (Xunzi ch. 9). For Zhang Zai, all people are my brothers and sisters, because Heaven is my father and Earth my mother (*Western Inscription*). And King Wen's government cared *first* for the widow, the widower, the orphan and the childless, the people with no relationships at all (1B5).
*Verdict: Holds for equal worth, and wobbles on unconditional.* Mencius 6A17 is striking: it is close to *Dignitas Infinita* 15 ("not conferred... cannot be lost"). Three things wobble:
- Confucian love is explicitly graded (*ai you cha deng*), and Mencius attacked Mozi's love without distinction as "having no father" (3B9). This is *not* a problem for A4, because Catholic teaching also grades love: Aquinas's order of charity (*ST* II-II q.26 a.6–8). Equal worth does not mean equal duties.
- Mencius's rhetoric that one without compassion "is not human" (2A6) is normative, not literal, but it shows dignity riding on a capacity.
- In Xunzi's naturalized Heaven, worth rests on a capacity the order itself is indifferent to. Heaven "does not change its course" for a good king or a bad one (Xunzi ch. 17).
*Telling historical point:* in classical China the case for impartial, equal concern for every person was made by Mozi, and Mozi grounded it in a Heaven that *wills* and *loves all people* (the "Will of Heaven" chapters). The Confucians who argued against him for graded love were also the ones who made Heaven less personal. That is not a proof, but it is a real correlation.

**Challenge 4. What is the ultimate human good?**
*Answer.* To become a person of complete humaneness: the exemplary person (*junzi*), and finally the sage. The *Great Learning* gives the order of growth: study things, make the will sincere, set the heart right, cultivate the self, order the family, govern the state, bring peace to all under Heaven. Through complete sincerity a person "forms a triad with Heaven and Earth" (*Doctrine of the Mean* 22). Ren is the life-giving power of Heaven and Earth (*Yijing*, Great Appendix: their great virtue is giving life) made present in a human heart. Wang Yangming adds that the great person forms one body with all things. Beyond death, the tradition says little on purpose: "not yet knowing life, how can you know death?" (11.12). What it offers instead is continuity through descendants, ancestral rites, and the "three things that do not decay": virtue, deeds and words.
*Verdict: Coherent and noble, and it stops short exactly at A7.* The tradition answers love's refusal of death with ritual. It sacrifices to the dead "as if present" (3.12), and it declines to say what the dead are. Xunzi says outright that the gentleman treats such rites as cultured form, while common people treat them as serving spirits (chs. 17, 19). That is a coherent stance. But it is a decision to leave A7's object unnamed, not an account that explains it.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1: the Confucian grants nearly all of A.**
*Opponent.* "Your list of things we cannot honestly deny is mostly ours. A1: tyrants lose the Mandate however many follow them. A6: the humane person will give up his life to complete humaneness (15.9), and there are things we want more than life and hate more than death (*Mencius* 6A10, the fish and the bear's paw). A5: the *Doctrine of the Mean* opens by saying what Heaven confers is the nature, and following it is the Way. Our quarrel is not with your data. It is with your inference from them to a Person."
*Defense.* "Then the A list has done its job. A non-theist tradition 2,500 years old grants A1, A3, A5, A6 and most of A4 in its own words. The contest is now where it should be: in W and C."
*Result: Held.* This is a strong sign that A was chosen well.

**Exchange 2: W1's inference is invalid against role ethics.**
*Opponent.* "W1 says a claim no human can cancel was not laid by any human, so Someone above every human laid it. That step fails. A son cannot release himself from filial duty, and his father cannot release him either. Filial piety is not the father's to give away; it is the shape of being a son. The claim is laid by the *relationship*, the Way of human bonds, which no single human made and which is still no one. Ames and Rosemont would add that the self is its roles, so there is no 'person above' standing behind them. 'Not laid by any human' does not entail 'laid by Someone.' It is also consistent with 'laid by no one, because it belongs to what the relationship is.'"
*Defense.* "A relationship can bind you in the way a shape binds. But it cannot *see* you, call you to account, or forgive you. A2 is not about a shape you fail to fit. It is about being guilty *before* someone. Your own Master says this. Asked about currying favor, he answers that one who offends against Heaven has nowhere to pray (3.13). Accused of staging a show, he asks: whom would I deceive, Heaven? (9.12). A pattern cannot be offended, and it cannot be deceived."
*Opponent rejoinder.* "Those are figures of speech, and Xunzi took them out on purpose."
*Defense.* "Then the Xunzi Confucian has dropped the one thing that let him say a cruel act wrongs more than its victim. He is left with human bonds plus rites the sages devised. A3 bites on exactly that: if the sages made the rites, why can't a later council remake them?"
*Result: Held with repair.* As written, W1 leans on "laid by," and that step is invalid. It must lean on "answerable to": seen, called to account, forgiven. See repair §6.

**Exchange 3: A2's guilt is a Western frame.**
*Opponent.* "'Guilty, needing forgiveness' is a Christian frame. Our tradition speaks of shame (*chi*), and shame is not just losing face. Lead people by virtue and ritual and they will have a sense of shame and correct themselves (2.3). What a wrongdoer needs is to be restored to right relation and to reform. A third party's 'forgiveness' is not needed."
*Defense.* "A2 does not need the word 'guilt.' It needs three things: (i) a person was wronged, (ii) blame is fitting, (iii) a mistake can be fixed, but the one we wronged must be set right with us. You grant all three. And your canon goes further. In *Analects* 20.1, King Tang confesses before the Lord on High (*Shangdi*): if I have sinned, let it not fall on the people; if the people sin, let it rest on me. That is guilt before Someone, at the very root of your tradition."
*Result: Held with repair.* A2 should be worded so that a shame-and-repair tradition sees itself in it. See §6.

**Exchange 4: Mencius against A5. The nature contains its "ought."**
*Opponent.* "A5 treats human nature as neutral capacities: truth or lies, giving or grasping. Mencius denies that. The sprouts are not neutral. They are tendencies that grow toward humaneness the way barley grows toward ripeness. The 'ought' is simply the full growth of what we are. Cut the trees on Ox Mountain and you injure its nature. You don't add a new one. Your is/ought gap is closed by a growing thing."
*Defense.* "Then why does Mencius have to *choose* which tendencies count as the nature? In 7B24 he grants that the mouth's desire for flavors is part of our nature, yet the gentleman does not call it 'nature.' He calls humaneness between father and son 'decree,' and still refuses to treat it as mere fate. He sorts our tendencies by a standard. And he says the standard is what Heaven confers (*Mean* 1; *Mencius* 7A1). That is A5: the 'ought' points past what we are to what we are *for*. Mencius and Patrick draw the line in the same place."
*Opponent rejoinder.* "Granted. But what we are *for* can be built into the kind, as in Aristotle. It needs no Purposer."
*Defense.* "That is the immanent-teleology rival, and it belongs in C. A5 itself stands."
*Result: Held.* A5 is confirmed by the strongest Mencian reading. The fight moves to C.

**Exchange 5: W2 does not reach a Person.**
*Opponent.* "W2 says a law that is one, and a mind fitted to read it, are best explained by one Source who made both. We agree. Fully develop your heart and you know your nature; know your nature and you know Heaven (7A1). The heart-mind and the Way come from one Source, Heaven. But W2's 'who' is smuggled in. Nothing in the argument shows the Source is a someone. Zhu Xi's principle (*li*) does all of it."
*Defense.* "Fair. W2 rules out matter and brute facts. It does not, by itself, choose between God and Heaven-as-principle. The case for a *Person* is carried by W1 and W3. W2 should say so. There is also a Catholic reply on record: Matteo Ricci, in *The True Meaning of the Lord of Heaven* (1603), argued that Zhu Xi's principle depends on things rather than standing on its own, and that what has no understanding cannot be the source of beings that do understand."
*Opponent rejoinder.* "Ricci's causal principle, that a cause must have what it gives, is exactly what emergence denies."
*Defense.* "Then we are at parity on that principle. Neither side wins on it alone."
*Result: Held with repair.* W2 must claim one Source and leave its personhood to W1 and W3. See §6.

**Exchange 6: W3 and the relational self.**
*Opponent.* "W3 says no finite person can receive the whole of us. That is a modern, individualist longing. In our tradition there is no hidden 'whole self' behind our roles waiting to be known. A person is fulfilled by being fully present across the generations, in a family and a people, honoring ancestors and raising descendants."
*Defense.* "Your own canon says otherwise. Near the end of his life the Master laments that no one understands him. He says he does not complain against Heaven or blame others; he studies what is lowly and reaches toward what is high; and it is Heaven that understands him (14.35). When Yan Hui died he cried out that Heaven had bereft him, and he wept beyond what ritual allowed (11.9–10). Here a man full of roles, students and honors longs to be known by Someone who is not a man, and grieves as though love was meant to last. That is A7, spoken by Confucius himself."
*Opponent rejoinder.* "The ancestral rite answers the grief. We sacrifice 'as if present.'"
*Defense.* "'As if' is honest, and it is also an admission. Either the dead are there to receive what you offer, or the rite is theater the living perform for themselves. Xunzi chose the second. Mencius and Confucius never chose. Leaving A7's object unnamed is a stance. It does not explain the longing."
*Result: Held.* A7 and W3 are strengthened by the Confucian sources. W3 needs a short clause for the relational self. See §6.

**Exchange 7: the missing rival in C.**
*Opponent.* "Your comparison lists matter, flourishing, and brute facts. We are none of these. We hold a sacred order. It is generative ('the great virtue of Heaven and Earth is giving life'), it is normative (Heaven confers the nature), and it is not a person. It explains A1, A3, A5, A6 and W2 as well as your God does. And it pays nothing for evil, because Heaven 'does not change its course' for Yao or for Jie. Your C omits your strongest opponent."
*Defense.* "Conceded: that is a real gap, and it must be fixed. But put this rival on the scale and look where it stops. (1) A pattern cannot be offended or forgive (A2), yet your Master spoke of offending Heaven and having nowhere to pray. (2) An order indifferent to whether each one lives or dies is an odd ground for each one mattering unconditionally (A4). It grounds the kind's worth, not *this* child's. (3) It cannot know or love anyone (A7, W3), yet your Master said Heaven knows him. (4) Your own key word is *Tianming*, the *mandate* or *decree* of Heaven, and a mandate is command language. At exactly the points where the impersonal reading has nothing to say, your greatest teachers reach for personal words."
*Opponent rejoinder.* "Personal words for the Way are metaphor. Laozi said Heaven and Earth are not humane."
*Defense.* "Then the Confucian must choose. Keep Mencius's Heaven that sees, decrees and knows, and you hold a personal Lawgiver in all but name. Or follow Xunzi's indifferent sky, and A3 and A4 lose their ground: the rites become sage-made tools, and worth becomes a capacity the order does not care about. The middle path, Mou Zongsan's 'immanent transcendence,' places the infinite inside the human heart-mind. Then something in my heart that is not mine to cancel is calling me. That is a person-like Other within, and it sits very close to Augustine's God, 'more inward than my inmost self.'"
*Result: Held with repair.* C must add an "impersonal sacred order" row and concede its one advantage (no problem of evil). Even then, theism comes out ahead on A2, A4 and A7/W3, while the two run level on A1, A3, A5, A6 and W2. That is a converging case, not a knockout, which is what CCC 31 claims.

**Exchange 8: the word "Lawgiver."**
*Opponent.* "The whole frame of 'law' and 'Lawgiver' is Legalism, the school Confucius opposed. Lead by edicts and punishments and people learn to avoid them without shame (2.3). We lead by virtue and ritual so that goodness becomes second nature. A Lawgiver ethic makes people law-dodgers."
*Defense.* "The book agrees with the Master about this. The New Law is 'not imposed from outside'; it is written on the heart (Jer 31:33; CCC 1965–66) and is chiefly the grace of the Holy Spirit (Aquinas, *ST* I-II q.106 a.1). It is followed 'out of love,' not fear (CCC 1972). Patrick's 'law' sits nearer the Confucian *li* made second nature than the Legalist's code of punishments. W1 already says 'not a commander who picks rules.'"
*Result: Held with repair (chapter text, not v1).* The chapter should say early that "law" here means what we are *for*, not a penal code. See §7.

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**Against v1 as written:**
1. **C omits the impersonal-sacred-order rival** (Confucian Heaven, Stoic Logos, Neo-Confucian principle). This is the most serious non-theist alternative for a book about love, because it is sacred, generative and normative. — **Serious.**
2. **W1's "not laid by any human, therefore laid by Someone" is invalid** against the view that a relationship constitutes its own claims. — **Serious.**
3. **W2's "Source *who* made both" overreaches.** The argument reaches one Source, not a personal one. — **Minor.**
4. **Graded love looks like a counterexample to A4** unless A4 separates equal worth from equal duties. — **Minor.**

**After the repairs in §6:** NONE that our side cannot answer at least to parity. Here is why:
- The Confucian grants A in his own words.
- On W1 and W3, his own canon supplies the personal language (3.13, 9.12, 14.35, 20.1, *Tianming*).
- His best remaining position is the Xunzi or Mou Zongsan line. There, the honest result is parity on A1, A3, A5, A6 and W2, theism ahead on A2, A4 and A7/W3, and an impersonal order ahead on evil. That is exactly what a converging case should admit.

One objection stands *at parity*, not below it, and should be named honestly: **the causal principle** ("what has no understanding cannot give rise to beings that understand," Ricci). It is contested by emergence theorists, and neither side can settle it on its own terms. The case should not rest on it.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**A2:**
> **A2. Doing them leaves us guilty, not merely mistaken.** We have wronged someone, we can rightly be blamed, and we must be set right with the one we wronged. Every serious moral tradition knows this, whether it speaks of guilt or of shame. A wrong sum needs correcting; a wronged person needs forgiveness.

**A4:**
> **A4. Every human being has the same dignity.** The newborn, the dying, the severely disabled, the enemy: equal, prior to anyone's recognition (Dignitas Infinita 1, 15). Equal worth is not equal duty: we owe more to our own parents and children than to strangers (Aquinas, ST II-II q.26), but no one is worth less.

**A6:**
> **A6. Real good for another always asks something of the giver:** time, goods, comfort, the wish to have one's own way. That is how reality works, even when love makes the giving glad. And some things must never be done, even to save oneself.

**W1:**
> **W1. Obligation points to a Lawgiver.** A duty is owed, and what is owed is owed to someone (A2). The one we wronged can forgive his part. But no human can cancel the claim itself (A3), and even the wrong no one will ever discover leaves us guilty. A custom, a relationship or a pattern can shape us, but none of them can see us, call us to account, or forgive us. A claim that does all three is the claim of a Person. So the claim of the good on us comes from Someone above every human being: not a commander who picks rules, but the Creator, who made us, established what we are for, and is Himself the Good He asks of us.

**W2:**
> **W2. The law and the mind that reads it point to one Source.** If moral facts simply exist and conscience is only an evolved survival tool, it is luck that they ever met; and conscience reads the law most surely exactly where survival pulls against it (truth told at cost, mercy to an enemy, care for those who can never repay). Truth, justice, mercy and self-gift, followed to their depths, never finally contradict. A law that is one, and a mind fitted to read it, are best explained by one Source of both. Many wise traditions have seen this much. W1 and W3 show why that Source is Someone, not something.

**W3:**
> **W3. Communion points to One who is Love.** Only a person can love us back; a law, a force, an order or an idea cannot. No finite person can receive the whole of us, because no one knows the whole of us, not even ourselves (A7). Even if we are made of our relationships, the longing remains: to be received whole, across every role and every year, by someone who will not die. The longing is best explained by a Person who knows us wholly, can hold us forever, and loved us first (1 John 4:19). Revelation gives His name: God is Love (1 John 4:8).

**C: add after "Brute moral facts":**
> - **An impersonal sacred order** (Heaven in the Confucian tradition, the Logos of the Stoics) explains a great deal: why the law binds everyone (A1, A3), why we are made for something (A5), why the mind fits the law (W2). It has no problem of evil to answer. But an order cannot be wronged or forgive (A2). An order indifferent to whether each one lives or dies cannot say why each one matters without condition (A4). And it cannot know or love anyone (A7, W3). At exactly those points, its own great teachers reach for personal words: a Heaven that sees, that decrees, that can be offended, that knows.

**C, replace "On the other side of the scale":**
> - **On the other side of the scale:** evil and God's hiddenness weigh against, and honesty requires saying so. An impersonal order owes no one an answer for suffering; a God who is Love does (Chapter 1 answers it in part; CCC 309).

## 7. New insights for the book

1. **The A list passes the hardest test.** A non-theist tradition older than Christianity grants A1, A3, A5, A6 and A7 in its own words. Ch. 12 could say so briefly: what is written on the heart (Rom 2:14–15) shows up where we would expect it, in the moral wisdom of peoples who never heard of Sinai.
2. **Catholic and Confucian agree on graded love.** Aquinas's order of charity sides with Mencius against Mozi: equal worth, unequal duties. This answers a likely skeptic's complaint that "equal dignity" means loving everyone the same. Fits Ch. 7 (justice) or Ch. 10 (communion).
3. **The Confucian "as if."** Sacrificing to the dead "as if present" is love refusing death without knowing what to hope for. It is a moving, non-Christian witness for A7 and Ch. 10.
4. **"Law" is not Legalism.** The chapter should say early on that the law of love is closer to a second nature, formed by grace and practice, than to a code of punishments. Confucius warned that rule by punishment makes people dodge rules without shame (2.3), and the Church says the New Law is followed out of love, not fear (CCC 1972). Suggested place: the opening section, after "laws require lawgivers."
5. **Chapter-text flags this school raises (outside v1, for later rounds):**
   - "Without God, every attempt to ground objective morality fails... collapses" (Premise 1 section). The Confucian tradition has not collapsed in 2,500 years. Better: "stops short," not "collapses."
   - The Atheist's Dilemma lists three options and omits a fourth: objective morality with an impersonal sacred ground.
   - "Laws require lawgivers" meets *Analects* 17.19 (Heaven does not speak, yet the seasons turn). The chapter needs the W1 repair to answer it.
   - "Far more faith than belief in God ever could" still overclaims (per BRIEF).
6. **A Catholic witness already on record:** Matteo Ricci's *True Meaning of the Lord of Heaven* (1603) is the classic Catholic engagement with this exact rival. It is worth a line in Further Study.

**Lines worth keeping (short, plain, warm):**
- "A pattern cannot be wronged, and it cannot forgive."
- "Equal worth is not equal duty."
- "Even if we are made of our relationships, the longing remains: to be received whole, by someone who will not die."
- "Where the wise reach for personal words, they are telling us something."
- "We honor the dead 'as if present.' Love will not let them go, even when it does not know what to hope."

## 8. Score

**v1 as written: 6/10. With the repairs above: 8/10.** The Confucian grants nearly all of A. As written, v1 has two serious gaps: it leaves out its strongest rival, the impersonal sacred order, and W1 rests on an invalid "laid by" step. Repaired, it reaches parity or better on every point. Its edge on guilt, unconditional worth and communion is earned partly in the Confucian canon's own words.
