# Argument Lab, Round 1: Buddhist opponent vs v1

## 1. School and voice

A composite voice drawn from the Theravada Pali Canon and its commentaries (Buddhaghosa), and from Indian Madhyamaka (Nagarjuna, Candrakirti, Santideva), with Dharmakirti brought in for the classical Buddhist case against a creator (Isvara). This voice holds that there is no enduring self, that craving (tanha) rooted in ignorance is the cause of suffering, that karma is intentional action ripening by its own order with no one appointing it, that loving-kindness and compassion are what the awakened mind is naturally like, and that nirvana, the unconditioned, is the highest good. It argues as a serious tradition, not as a debating trick. The Church herself says she "rejects nothing that is true and holy" in it (Nostra Aetate 2).

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.**
*Their answer:* Justice does not start from what is owed. It starts from non-harm (ahimsa) and from a reflexive insight: every being trembles at violence and wants to live, so, putting yourself in their place, you should neither kill nor cause killing (Dhp 129–130; SN 55.7 gives the same reasoning as a golden rule). Desert is handled by karma: intentional action (AN 6.63: intention *is* karma) ripens in the one who acts, so the cosmos needs no human to balance its books. Social justice follows from compassion and from cause and effect: the Cakkavatti discourse (DN 26) traces theft and violence to a king's failure to give to the poor.
*Verdict:* **Holds, on its own terms.** It does not borrow. But it gives a thin account of what a victim is *owed*. "I have a claim on you" turns into "your act will ripen in you," and that is not the same thing. The victim's standing as someone wronged gets weaker. This is where Patrick's side gets its best purchase (see Exchange 2).

**2. A hard dilemma, and by what principles.**
*Their answer:* The Mahayana Skill-in-Means Sutra (Upayakausalya) tells of a ship's captain, a bodhisattva, who learns that a robber on board plans to murder five hundred merchants. Out of compassion for the merchants, and for the robber, whom he wants to spare the karma of mass murder, the captain kills the robber himself and willingly takes the karmic cost of the act on himself. The principles at work are compassion (karuna), wisdom (prajna), the purity of the intention, and a willingness to bear the cost for others. Theravada would refuse him. In its Abhidhamma, killing always arises with a mind rooted in aversion (dosa), so "compassionate killing" cannot happen psychologically, and the first precept stands without exception.
*Verdict:* **Holds, and converges rather than borrows.** The principles line up almost one for one with the book's four facets: wisdom with truth, karma with justice, compassion with mercy, and the captain bearing the cost with self-gift. The Buddhist reached them without a Lawgiver. Two honest consequences follow. (a) This is real evidence for W2 and Rom 2:14–15: one law, read everywhere. (b) It weakens the chapter's slogan "a law requires a lawgiver," because here is a whole civilization that reads the law and denies there is a giver. Also notable: the two Buddhist schools split on the case, just as natural-law reasoners need prudence (Ch. 11). Principles do not settle hard cases without practical wisdom, on either side.

**3. Ground of equal, unconditional dignity.**
*Their answer:* There are three grounds. (a) All beings suffer and want happiness. Equal capacity for suffering gives equal claim to compassion (the Metta Sutta, Sn 1.8: love all beings as a mother protects her only child). (b) Santideva's "no-owner" argument (Bodhicaryavatara 8.101–103): since there is no self that owns suffering, "my pain" and "your pain" are not different in kind, so all suffering should be removed impartially. (c) In Mahayana, buddha-nature: every being can awaken, and that capacity cannot be lost. Theravada adds that a human birth is very rare and precious, because it is the chance to awaken (MN 129, the turtle and the yoke).
*Verdict:* **Holds in part; it does not borrow, but it bends.** The no-owner argument is one of the strongest non-theist grounds for impartiality anywhere, and the book should respect it. Three points need pressing. (i) It grounds equal concern for *suffering* across *all sentient beings*, not the equal dignity of *the human person*. The newborn and the insect are on one scale. (ii) Anatta makes "the person" a conventional label for a stream of processes, so dignity attaches to a convention. Nagarjuna would answer that the conventional is real enough for ethics (MMK 24.8–10), and that answer holds to parity. (iii) Traditional karma explains disability and short life as the ripening of past deeds (MN 135). It does not license contempt, and compassion is still owed. But it sits uneasily with dignity that is "prior to any recognition" and unearned (Dignitas Infinita 15). Buddha-nature comes closest to the book's claim, but Madhyamaka (Candrakirti) reads it as a provisional teaching pointing to emptiness, not as a lasting worth of persons.

**4. Ultimate good and guiding principles.**
*Their answer:* Nirvana: the unborn, unmade, unconditioned (Ud 8.3), the end of craving and so of suffering, "the highest happiness" (Dhp 204). The guiding principles are the Noble Eightfold Path and the four boundless states (brahmaviharas): loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy and equanimity.
*Verdict:* **Holds; it is coherent and lived by millions.** The weak point is the one the book can honestly press (Exchange 6). The road to the highest good is love of persons, but the highest good itself is no one. The tradition carries that tension openly in the Diamond Sutra's teaching that the bodhisattva leads countless beings to nirvana, and yet in truth no being is led.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1. W1: the false fork.**
*Buddhist:* W1 runs: what is owed is owed to someone; a claim no human can cancel was not laid by any human; so it was laid by Someone above every human. That is a two-way choice where there are three options: laid by a human, laid by a higher Someone, or *laid by no one at all*, as an order of consequence. Karma is the third. The Buddha said that whether or not Tathagatas arise, this order of things stands (SN 12.20). He was its discoverer, like a man who finds an overgrown ancient city, not its founder (SN 12.65). And we agree with your A3 completely: "by oneself is evil done, by oneself one is purified" (Dhp 165, paraphrase). No victim, no nation and no god can cancel the fruit of an act. But that is *because* no one laid it. A3 is better explained by an impersonal law than by a lawgiver, since a lawgiver *could* release you.
*Patrick's side:* An order of consequence gives only "if you want to avoid suffering, do not do this." That is prudence, not obligation. A torturer willing to pay the price is, on that view, unwise, not wrong.
*Buddhist rejoinder:* That misreads karma. Karma does not *make* the act wrong. The act is unwholesome (akusala) in itself, because it is rooted in greed, hatred or delusion and brings affliction to self and others (MN 61). Karma is the fruit, not the ground. And your own book puts the "ought" in what we are *for* (A5). So does ours: the path is for liberation. If our "ought" is merely prudential because it is aimed at an end, so is yours.
*Verdict:* **Failed as stated.** The fork is invalid against an impersonal moral order. The prudence charge also rebounds on the book's own teleology (beatitude is our end, ST I-II q.1–5). The Catholic answer is that our end is Someone, the Good who is also a Person, so the ought carries a Person's claim. That answer holds only if W1 shows *why* the end must be Someone. A3 cannot show it, because karma explains A3 as well. Repair required (Section 6).

**Exchange 2. A2: does guilt need pardon, or only purification?**
*Patrick's side:* Then argue from the shape of guilt, not from the fact that it cannot be cancelled. Take the case where the victim is dead. Ajatasattu killed his father, King Bimbisara, and came to the Buddha in anguish (DN 2). What he needed was not information about consequences. He needed pardon. King David, after Uriah was dead, prayed "against you, you only, have I sinned" (Ps 51:4). An impersonal law can purify or punish. Only a person can forgive.
*Buddhist:* The Buddha did accept Ajatasattu's confession, in the very words of that sutta: since you see your fault as a fault and make amends by the Dhamma, we accept it. We have confession, amends and the victim's pardon (khama). We also know forgiveness of the living, and Angulimala the murderer became an arahant (MN 86). What we deny is that, beyond these, a further pardon is needed from a cosmic Person. The Buddha told the assembly that the patricide had blocked the king's insight; the fruit still ripened. And the felt need to be pardoned by Someone above often carries the clinging to "I," which our tradition sorts into healthy shame and dread of wrongdoing (hiri and ottappa, which "guard the world," AN 2.9) and unhealthy self-torment (kukkucca).
*Patrick's side:* We draw the same line. Contrition is not scrupulosity, and both of our traditions reject self-torment. But notice what you have conceded. Your own patricide comes to the Buddha, a person, and is *accepted* by a person. The sutta's drama is personal through and through. And your tradition leaves the deepest case, the dead victim, with no one who can pardon. Experience presses against that, not only in Christians. The salt-crystal discourse (AN 3.100) shows that you, too, do not treat karma as a strict ledger, because the developed mind changes how a deed ripens. Something in your account already leans toward mercy.
*Verdict:* **Held with repair.** The theist reading of guilt has real support: guilt is personal in shape, confession goes to a person, and the dead-victim case stays open. But A2's clause "we need to be forgiven" is not something a careful Buddhist "cannot honestly deny." He honestly denies its theistic sense. So A2 has to be split. The datum "guilty, not merely mistaken" stays in A, and "a pardon no human can give" moves into W1 as the thing to be explained.

**Exchange 3. W1's Creator: does a creator destroy responsibility?**
*Buddhist:* The Buddha named three doctrines that lead to doing nothing: that everything is due to past deeds, to God's creation, or to chance (AN 3.61). If God made everything as it is, no one is responsible. Worse, the idea of a creator comes from a confusion: Brahma, first to appear in a new world-cycle, wrongly concludes he made the others (DN 1). And the Jataka verses (e.g., J 543, J 528) ask why a lord of the world would make it so full of injustice.
*Patrick's side:* The Buddha's target was a God who *determines* everything. The Catholic God creates free creatures and works through secondary causes. He does not choose their choices (CCC 307, 311). The Brahma story is aimed at a being who arises inside the world-cycle. Classical theism's God is not a first arrival inside time but the act by which there is a world-cycle at all. The suffering objection is real and is already on the scale in C.
*Verdict:* **Held.** AN 3.61 and DN 1 miss the classical God. The evil objection is acknowledged honestly in v1.

**Exchange 4. Dharmakirti and divine simplicity.**
*Buddhist:* A permanent, unchanging cause cannot produce effects one after another. If it is fully able to act, everything should come at once; if it is not, it never acts. An eternal, simple God is therefore either idle or not simple. Nagarjuna adds that anything with own-being (svabhava) could neither cause nor change (MMK ch. 1, ch. 15). Your God is the ultimate case of own-being.
*Patrick's side:* Aquinas distinguishes willing a change from changing one's will (ST I q.19 a.7). God's one eternal act wills each thing to be at its own time. The succession is in the creatures, not in God. That Nagarjuna's arguments defeat the own-being of *things within the world* is granted; God is not a thing within the world, nor one more item alongside them (ST I q.3 a.5).
*Verdict:* **Held to parity.** This is an old dispute, and neither side wins it in a paragraph. It does not damage v1, which already rests on comparing stopping points rather than proving one.

**Exchange 5. W1 and W2: a law that reads the heart.**
*Patrick's side:* Look at what karma is. Gravity does not read intentions. Karma does. Its input is the moral quality of the will, and its output is a fitting result, across lives, into bodies and worlds (MN 135: killing leads to a short life, cruelty to sickness). A law that sorts by the heart and returns what is fitting has the structure of judgment. It is not physics. An impersonal order that does this is a brute moral fact of extraordinary precision. A mind that knows hearts is the better explanation.
*Buddhist:* Mind conditions mind; there is no judge. And the modern Buddhist simply drops the rebirth mechanism and keeps karma as psychology: hatred makes a hateful life.
*Patrick's side:* Then you lose what made karma answer A1 and A3. On the psychological reading, the torturer who dies at ease has escaped. On the traditional reading, the moral sorting reaches into the physical world, and that needs explaining.
*Buddhist (Madhyamaka):* You ask why there is this order at all. That question presupposes a "why" outside all conditions. The beginning of samsara cannot be found (SN 15.1–3), and the Buddha set aside such questions as not leading to liberation. Dependent origination goes all the way down, and emptiness is itself empty (MMK 24.18, 13.8), so there is no "totality" to need a ground.
*Verdict:* **Held with repair; this is the deepest stand-off.** Against traditional karma, the "law that reads hearts" argument is strong and new to the lab. Against Madhyamaka, the debate comes down to stopping points: dependence all the way down versus a necessary, simple ground. Each stopping point is principled. Our side holds to parity, not to victory. The book should say so honestly and not imply the Buddhist has no answer. v1's C section must name this rival. It currently lists only matter, flourishing and brute facts.

**Exchange 6. A7 and W3: is the longing the clue, or the disease?**
*Buddhist:* A7 says we long to be known and loved wholly and for good, and we grieve because love was meant to last. We have a name for that: bhava-tanha, the craving for continued existence. It is the second of the three cravings and a cause of suffering. Your longing to be held forever assumes a lasting self to be held, and there is none. W3 says no one knows the whole of us, not even ourselves. Correct: there is no whole to know. When Kisa Gotami begged for a cure for her dead child, the Buddha sent her for a mustard seed from a house where no one had died. She found none, and the seeing of impermanence was her healing. Grief is not evidence that love was meant to last; it is the second arrow. Your witness takes the disease for a clue.
*Patrick's side:* Our tradition, too, says grasping love must die. Augustine distinguishes loving creatures *for* God from resting in them as if they were God. John of the Cross asks for detachment from everything. But look at what remains when grasping is purified *in your own tradition*. The arahant's mind is boundless loving-kindness. The bodhisattva vows to stay for all beings. The Buddha called admirable friendship "the whole of the holy life" (SN 45.2). And when Brahmins asked the way to union with Brahma, he answered with the four boundless states (DN 13). In both traditions, love outlives the death of craving. And what is that purified love? Josef Pieper put it this way: love's first word to the beloved is "It is good that you exist." Gabriel Marcel held that to love someone is, in effect, to tell him he will not die. That is not clinging to my continued existence. It is affirming *yours*, and it cannot be said for a while only. So the clue is not craving. It is the love that is still there after craving is gone.
*Buddhist rejoinder:* At the level of conventional truth, yes, the bodhisattva loves persons. At the level of ultimate truth, there are no beings and no one is saved (Diamond Sutra). The love is real; its object is empty. And DN 13 meets the Brahmins on their own terms: Brahma is a god within samsara, not the ultimate.
*Patrick's side:* Then your account has a strange shape. The best thing in us, love of persons, is the road. But at the end of the road there is no one, and the persons loved along the way were, at the last, conventions. The Catholic says the opposite: what love affirms, the other's existence and goodness, is true all the way down, because the ground of all things is Someone who said it first (ST I q.20 a.2). That is not a proof. It is the point where the two accounts honestly part, and the reader can weigh which one does justice to what love itself claims.
*Verdict:* **v1's A7 and W3 failed as worded; held to parity after repair.** As written, A7 shows the longing in its *craving* form ("to be held forever," "the whole of us"), and the Buddhist diagnosis fits that form better than W3 does. Re-founded on purified love ("it is good that you exist," willed for the other's sake), the witness survives the Buddhist cure and lays bare the real fork: is the highest good Someone or no one?

**Exchange 7. A4 against anatta.**
*Buddhist:* Equal dignity "prior to any recognition, and it cannot be lost": whose? There is no lasting self. Yet our no-owner argument gives you impartial compassion more firmly than "dignity" does, because it removes the very line between mine and yours.
*Patrick's side:* Your argument proves equal concern for suffering. It does not prove that killing a sleeping man painlessly wrongs *him*. The first precept says it does, but the no-owner argument cannot say why. And it puts the newborn and the gnat on one scale, while your own precious-human-birth teaching (MN 129) does not.
*Buddhist rejoinder:* The first precept rests on the intention: killing arises from aversion, and that is what makes it unwholesome. The precious human birth is precious as an opportunity, not as a worth.
*Verdict:* **Held with repair.** A4 stands as a datum; the Buddhist does not deny equal worth. But C's claim that rivals cannot ground equality must be made precise. The Buddhist grounds equal *compassion*. What the theist explains better is equal *dignity of persons*, given and not lost: worth that belongs to the one who has it, and not only to the suffering he might undergo.

## 4. Ledger for v1

- **A1. Some things are really wrong, for everyone.** **Holds.** Buddhism affirms it fully: the precepts, and karma operating regardless of approval. It is also equally explained by impersonal moral order, so it does no witnessing work against this rival.
- **A2. Guilty, not merely mistaken; we need to be forgiven.** **Holds with repair.** "Guilty, not mistaken" holds (akusala, blame, moral shame). "We need to be forgiven" is honestly denied in its theistic sense and must move to W1.
- **A3. No one on earth can release us.** **Holds**, but it is shared and better explained impersonally (Dhp 165). It cannot by itself carry W1.
- **A4. Every human being has the same dignity.** **Holds with repair.** Buddhism affirms equal compassion for all beings, with real strength (Santideva). The point of difference is dignity of *persons*, unearned and not lost, against karmic accounts of disability and anatta. Make that precise in C.
- **A5. Human nature does not settle what we ought to do.** **Holds.** Buddhism agrees independently (wholesome and unwholesome roots; the ought points to liberation). It is neutral between the rivals, which is fine for an A-item.
- **A6. Real good for another costs the giver; some things must never be done, even to save oneself.** **Holds.** Strong Buddhist agreement: generosity (dana), and the simile of the saw (MN 21: even if bandits cut you limb from limb, hatred would betray the teaching).
- **A7. We long to be known and loved wholly and for good; love refuses death.** **Fails as worded.** It presents the longing in its craving form, which Buddhism diagnoses as bhava-tanha with a coherent rival account. Repaired by re-founding it on purified love that affirms the other's existence.
- **W1. Obligation points to a Lawgiver.** **Fails as stated.** It is a false two-way fork that leaves out impersonal moral order; A3 is better explained by karma. Holds with repair if made abductive and argued from the personal shape of guilt and from a law that reads hearts.
- **W2. The law and the mind that reads it point to one Source.** **Holds, strengthened.** Buddhism's independent arrival at counterparts of truth, justice, mercy and self-gift is fresh evidence for "one law read everywhere." The Buddhist answer (the mind is shaped by the very order it reads, by dependent origination) holds only to parity, not better.
- **W3. Communion points to One who is Love.** **Fails as worded; holds to parity with repair.** It rests on A7's craving form. Re-founded, it exposes the honest fork: is the highest good Someone or no one?
- **C: Matter alone.** **Holds.** Not at issue here.
- **C: Human flourishing alone.** **Holds.** Not at issue.
- **C: Brute moral facts explain at most A1.** **Holds with repair.** True of bare realism, but the strongest impersonal rival (karma and dharma) explains A1, A3, A5, A6 and part of A2. Omitting it makes the comparison look rigged.
- **C: A God who is Love explains all of it at one stopping point.** **Holds with repair.** Against Madhyamaka it is parity on the stopping point. The claim must stay "explains more," not imply the rival has nothing.
- **C: Evil and hiddenness weigh against.** **Holds.** It already absorbs the Jataka objection. Honest.
- **C: Conclusion (best explanation; CCC 31, 35).** **Holds.** The modesty is right and is what lets the case survive this school.

## 5. Remaining sound objections

Against v1 *as worded*:

1. **W1's fork leaves out impersonal moral order.** The argument is invalid against karma, which explains why no one can release us better than a lawgiver does. **Serious.** (Answered to parity by Repair W1.)
2. **A7 and W3 present the longing in its craving form.** Buddhism's diagnosis (bhava-tanha, anatta) accounts for A7 as worded at least as well as W3 does. **Serious.** (Answered to parity by Repairs A7 and W3.)
3. **The A-list claims undeniability that it does not have.** A2's "need to be forgiven" is honestly denied by a careful Buddhist. **Serious** for the structure, because "what we cannot honestly deny" must be undeniable. (Fixed by Repair A2.)
4. **C leaves out the strongest impersonal rival.** **Serious.** (Fixed by Repair C.)

After repairs: **NONE remain below parity.** The Madhyamaka stand-off (dependent origination without a ground, versus a necessary, simple ground) is not answered *beyond* parity, but it is answered *to* parity, which meets the lab's threshold. The book should name it as the honest point where the two accounts part, not claim a win there.

Chapter-text note (outside v1, for the editor): several lines in chapter_12_current.md would lose to this school outright and also break the lab's own rules. These are: "a law requires a lawgiver" used as a self-evident premise (the Buddhist reads the law and denies the giver, so it assumes the conclusion); "no *thing* ... has ever laid a claim on anyone" (karma is not offered as a claim but as a consequence, so the line misses); "Every time an atheist says 'That's wrong!' they're providing evidence for God's existence"; and "far more faith than belief in God ever could" (overclaim).

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**A2 (replace):**
> **A2. Doing them leaves us guilty, not merely mistaken.** We have wronged someone, we can rightly be blamed, and the fitting answer is not only to correct ourselves but to seek pardon from the one we wronged. A wrong sum needs correcting; a wronged person is owed an apology.

**A7 (replace):**
> **A7. Love, at its purest, does not grasp; it affirms.** It says to the beloved, "It is good that you exist," and it wills that good for the other's sake, not for our own. It cannot say this for a while only. We grieve because love was meant to last.

**W1 (replace):**
> **W1. Guilt points to Someone who can pardon.** What no human can cancel, no human laid. Either it was laid by no one, an order of consequence like the Buddhist's karma, or by Someone above every human being. An impersonal order explains why the debt cannot be cancelled. It does not explain why guilt seeks pardon and not only purification, or why, when the one we wronged is dead, we still need to be forgiven (Ps 51:4). Nor does it explain why the law weighs the heart that wills an act and not only the act. Only a person can forgive, and only a mind can read a heart. So the claim of the good on us is best explained by Someone: not a commander who picks rules, but the Creator, who made us, established what we are for, and is Himself the Good He asks of us.

**W3 (replace):**
> **W3. Communion points to One who is Love.** Even traditions that deny a Creator make love of persons the road to the highest good. If the highest good is no one, then love, the best thing in us, is answered in the end by no one, and the persons it affirmed were not, at the last, real. Love itself says otherwise: it says, "It is good that you exist." No finite person can say that to us wholly, because no one knows the whole of us, not even ourselves. The longing is best explained by a Person who knows us wholly, said it first, and loved us first (1 John 4:19). Revelation gives His name: God is Love (1 John 4:8).

**C (insert after "Brute moral facts"):**
> - **An impersonal moral order** (the Buddhist's karma) explains a great deal: that some things are wrong for everyone (A1), that no one can release us (A3), that human nature does not settle the ought (A5), and that real good costs (A6). Its grounds for equal compassion are among the strongest anyone has offered. It does not explain why guilt seeks pardon and not only purification, why the law reads the heart, or why love of persons should be the road to a highest good that is no one.

**C, "A God who is Love" (replace):**
> - **A God who is Love** explains more of it, and at one stopping point. Every account stops somewhere. The Buddhist stops at causes that depend on causes without beginning. We stop at a Good who is Someone. Which stopping point does justice to what love itself claims is the question the reader must weigh.

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- The four facets have counterparts the Buddhist reached without a Lawgiver: wisdom with truth, karma with justice, compassion with mercy, and generosity and bearing cost with self-gift. That counts for natural law (Rom 2:14–15) and for W2, but it means "a law requires a lawgiver" cannot be stated as self-evident. The argument has to show *why* this law points to a Giver. The best reasons are the personal shape of guilt, a law that weighs the heart, and love's affirmation of persons.
- The dead-victim case (Ajatasattu and Bimbisara; David and Uriah) is the sharpest single test of W1 found so far. It is plain, universal and concrete.
- Karma is the best test of the "impersonal law" objection because it is the most moral impersonal law ever proposed. If the case survives karma, it survives the bare realist a fortiori.
- The Catholic and Buddhist traditions agree that *grasping* love must die (Augustine on loving creatures for God's sake; John of the Cross on detachment). The argument should never rest on craving. It should rest on what remains after craving is purified.
- The honest fork with Buddhism is not "law or no law" but "is the highest good Someone or no one?" The book can state that respectfully, in line with Nostra Aetate 2.
- In the Tevijja discourse (DN 13), the Buddha answers the question of union with Brahma with loving-kindness, compassion, joy and equanimity. Used honestly, as an affinity and not a concession, it is a fine bridge for the chapter's salvation paragraph, which already names "a Buddhist."

**Lines worth keeping**
- "An impersonal law can purify or punish. Only a person can forgive."
- "Gravity does not read intentions."
- "If the highest good is no one, then love, the best thing in us, is answered in the end by no one."
- "Love's first word is: it is good that you exist. It cannot say that for a while only."
- "Both of us say grasping must die. We part over what is left."

## 8. Score

**v1 as worded: 5/10. With the repairs above: 7/10.** As worded, W1's fork and A7's craving-shaped longing fall to the most moral impersonal law ever proposed. Repaired and argued from pardon, from a law that reads the heart, and from love's affirmation of persons, v1 holds to parity against Buddhism's deepest stopping point and wins clearly against traditional karma's need for a reader of hearts.
