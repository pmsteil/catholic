# Argument Lab, Round 1: Nietzschean opponent vs. v1

## 1. School and voice

Friedrich Nietzsche in his mature works (*Beyond Good and Evil*, *On the Genealogy of Morality*, *Twilight of the Idols*, *The Antichrist*, *Thus Spoke Zarathustra*), argued here the way his strongest recent readers argue him: Brian Leiter's naturalist reading, Bernard Williams's use of genealogy, and Philippa Foot's essay "Nietzsche's Immoralism", which treats him as the challenge moral philosophy never answered. He does not try to refute God. He says the death of God is a cultural fact, explains how moral beliefs arose, and treats that explanation as enough (*Daybreak* §95). Guilt, pity and equal dignity are what the "slave revolt in morality" produced. Values are made, not found. The honest atheist must accept rank and give up equality, and the secular humanist who keeps Christian morality without the Christian God is living on borrowed capital (*Twilight*, "Skirmishes" §5, on George Eliot; *BGE* §202).

All Nietzsche material below is paraphrased. The only direct wording is a few famous short phrases.

---

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** Justice begins in exchange between parties of roughly equal power: buying, selling, creditor and debtor. Everything has its price and everything can be paid off (*GM* II §8; *Human, All Too Human* §92). As a community grows strong it can afford to let offenders go, and justice ends by overcoming itself in mercy, which remains the privilege of the most powerful (*GM* II §10). No one is owed anything simply for being human. What the weak receive, they receive from the generosity of the strong.
*Verdict: it holds as a consistent position, and it breaks against A4.* It is coherent and does not borrow. But on this view the newborn, the dying and the severely disabled are owed nothing, because they cannot trade or threaten. Nietzsche accepts that openly. One remarkable point: he makes mercy the highest form of power, which is what the Church prays (see section 7).

**2. A hard case.** Take one ventilator, two patients: a gifted young composer, and a man with profound, permanent disability who cannot know he is alive. Nietzsche's principle is ascending life against declining life: what raises the type "man" and the exceptional individual. The ventilator goes to the composer. He would also urge the declining patient toward a proud "free death" and calls the doctor's duty to the sick man a duty to life rising, not to life ebbing (*Twilight*, "Skirmishes" §36; *Antichrist* §2).
*Verdict: it holds, but binds no one.* It decides the case without borrowing truth, justice, mercy and self-gift as the book defines them. But by its own terms the standard of ascending life is a legislation, a taste of the higher type, and nobody is obliged to share it. Zarathustra says outright that this is his way and there is no single way (*Zarathustra* III, "On the Spirit of Gravity"). The only value it cannot do without is honesty, and Nietzsche himself admits that honesty is a borrowed faith (*Gay Science* §344). So it decides cases without borrowing, but it cannot tell anyone else they *ought* to decide the same way.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** He denies it, openly and as his central claim. "Equality of souls before God" is the lie the weak used to bring down the strong (*Antichrist* §43). Every raising of the type "man" has needed an aristocratic society, rank, and the "pathos of distance" (*BGE* §257). Democratic and humanitarian equality is Christianity's heir (*BGE* §202). The English moralists who dropped God but kept Christian morality do not see that the morality falls with the God (*Twilight*, "Skirmishes" §5).
*Verdict: it holds as a consistent position, and it is the strongest outside witness the book could have for the conditional "no God, no ground for equal dignity."* Two things have to be recorded honestly. (a) Nietzsche's claim is historical, not a proof: knowing where a belief came from does not show that no other ground for it is possible. Kantians and Rawlsians would say he is wrong, and that dispute belongs to another round. (b) On history he is largely right, and secular historians such as Tom Holland (*Dominion*) agree. Equal dignity *in its full reach*, including the deformed newborn, the slave and the dying, entered the West through Christ. Even Seneca, who argued that slaves are men (*Ep.* 47), took the drowning of weak and deformed infants as an act of reason (*De Ira* 1.15).

**4. Ultimate flourishing.** The goal is to raise the type "man": the higher individual who creates values, overcomes himself, says yes to the whole of life (*amor fati*), and could will the same life again forever (eternal recurrence). His guiding rules are will to power, self-overcoming, rank, "become who you are," and intellectual honesty.
*Verdict: coherent, but it breaks at two points.* (a) It borrows truthfulness, by his own admission (*GS* §344; *GM* III §§24–27). (b) *Amor fati* means a yes to everything, so Nietzsche must will the torture of every child again, forever, as part of the whole he affirms. He accepts this. Most readers will see it as a reductio of the view, not a feature of it. And at its peak this ethic of flourishing reaches for eternity ("all joy wants eternity," *Zarathustra* IV, "The Drunken Song"), which leads straight into A7.

**Summary.** Nietzsche answers all four challenges coherently and borrows almost nothing. He keeps that consistency by *giving up* A1's "for everyone," A2, A4, and any binding "ought." He is the one opponent in the lab who really does live without the borrowed capital, and he is the most eloquent witness that the borrowing is real.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1: "God is dead"; the genealogy replaces the refutation.**
*Nietzsche:* I don't need to disprove God. I show how belief in God and in your moral "data" arose, and that makes a counter-proof unnecessary (*Daybreak* §95). Your A-list is what is left over from a dead faith. You were raised to feel A2 to A4 by the very religion you now use them to prove. That is circular.
*Patrick's side:* Your own announcement is the genetic fallacy. Where a belief came from does not settle whether it is true; people learn arithmetic from teachers, and the sums are still right. More important, ours is a *comparative* argument. You can only debunk A1 to A4 by assuming the best explanation of them is naturalistic, and that is the very question under test. And the method turns on you: your values of nobility, rank and honesty have a history too, and you yourself trace honesty back to the Christian faith that God is truth (*GS* §344).
*Rejoinder (Leiter):* This is a debunking argument, not the genetic fallacy. If the best causal account of a belief never mentions its truth, the belief loses its warrant. Herd utility and priestly power explain A2 to A4 well.
*Reply:* Then the dispute is about which account is better, and that is exactly what C is for. But v1 is in trouble here. It calls A1 to A7 "what we cannot honestly deny," and Nietzsche denied A2 and A4 honestly, at great cost, and in print. The heading overclaims.
**Held with repair.** The argument survives at parity. The heading does not.

**Exchange 2: Guilt as debt (GM II).**
*Nietzsche:* *Schuld* (guilt) comes from *Schulden* (debts). Bad conscience is instinct turned inward once man was shut up inside society. The priest made the debt infinite and owed to God, then sold the cure: God pays it Himself. Your A3, "no one on earth can release us," is the priests' product, not a discovery. As belief in God declines, guilt should decline with it. Atheism brings a "second innocence" (*GM* II §§16–21).
*Patrick's side:* Thank you. You grant W1's conditional exactly: if guilt is real, it is owed to someone. You simply run the argument the other way: no Creditor, so guilt is an illusion. The issue is whether A2 sees something true. The test case is a man who betrays a friend who trusted him. What he feels is aimed at a *person*: he wants that friend's pardon, not just relief for himself. That pattern is found well outside the line of Christian priests. Mencius makes shame at wrongdoing one of the four sprouts of the human heart. Early Buddhism names moral shame and moral dread as guardians of the world. Small children tell a wrong done to a person from a broken classroom rule long before any catechism (Turiel's research, cited as secular corroboration). Your alternative is that remorse is cowardice toward one's own act, and that the bite of conscience is indecent (*Twilight*, "Maxims" §10). Put that in front of the father who killed his child while driving drunk. Is his remorse indecent?
*Rejoinder:* You are running together *regret* and *guilt*. The noble man feels regret, even shame, at harm he caused. What he rejects is the sense of owing something to a creditor.
*Reply:* A2 is not about a debt to a creditor. It is about having wronged someone, being rightly blamed, and needing that person's forgiveness. Regret does not go looking for forgiveness. Guilt does. Your genealogy explains why guilt grew infinite in a priestly culture. It does not explain why the betrayer goes back to his friend.
*Nietzsche presses on A3:* Then which is it? Your Christ commands the victim to forgive. If the victim *can* forgive, a human can release. If no human can, you have brought back the infinite debt.
*Patrick's side:* Fair hit. A3 is ambiguous as written. It has to separate forgiveness, which the victim can and should give, from *making the wrong right* or signing away what is owed to every human being, which no one can do.
**Held with repair (A2 wording; A3 fixed).**

**Exchange 3: Equal dignity and rank (Antichrist §43; BGE §257).**
*Nietzsche:* "Equality of souls before God" was the weapon that let the many bring down the few. Rank is the condition of every human greatness. Your A4 is not reason's finding. It is Christianity's legacy, and your humanist allies are Christians who have forgotten they are Christians.
*Patrick's side:* We accept your second claim and record it gratefully: the most honest atheist confirms that the equal dignity of the newborn and the disabled does not come free once God is gone. As for history, the Church agrees that reason can see this truth (*Dignitas Infinita* 1). It also teaches that fallen reason needs revelation's light so that moral truths within reason's reach can be held by all firmly and without error (CCC 37–38). Seneca and the drowned infants are exactly what CCC 38 would lead us to expect. Then the reader's choice is simple. Look at the disabled newborn and ask which is easier to believe: that her worth equals yours, or that God is absent.
*Rejoinder:* So it comes down to a show of hands: "most people can't give it up." Your own A3 says a nation's vote cannot make anything right or wrong. Now you want the herd's vote to make your premise true.
*Reply:* Good hit on the wording; it must not be a headcount. The appeal is to each reader's own seeing, the way we trust perception: first person, not a poll. You make the same appeal when you *see* that the noble is higher than the base. That puts seeing against seeing. It is parity, not victory. But A4 does not stand alone: under one explanation it fits with A2, A3 and A7. Your view is unified too (will to power), so the comparison comes down to which data are more credible. For almost every reader, the newborn's worth is more credible than your rank order.
**Held with repair, at parity.** A4 cannot be presented as undeniable, but it holds as the reader's own conviction, with Nietzsche shown as what giving it up costs.

**Exchange 4: Pity, sacrifice and the giving virtue (Antichrist §7; Zarathustra I, "On the Gift-Giving Virtue").**
*Nietzsche:* Pity multiplies suffering and keeps alive what is ripe for ruin. "Self-sacrifice" is the morality of unselfing, life turned against itself. The noble give from overflow, as the sun gives, and it costs them nothing. Your A6 ("always costs the giver") is the ascetic ideal talking.
*Patrick's side:* Three answers. (a) Max Scheler, writing as a Catholic in *Ressentiment* (1912), answered you directly. Christian love is not the resentment of the weak. It is a bending down out of fullness, toward the lowly, because one is rich enough to do so. (b) Aquinas separates pity as a passion, which can wallow, from mercy as a virtue governed by reason that acts to relieve (*ST* II-II q.30 a.3). What you attack is Schopenhauer's pity, not Thomist mercy. (c) We agree that the highest giving comes from fullness: within the Trinity, self-gift is total and without loss. Sacrifice is what self-gift becomes for finite creatures in a fallen world. Even your Zarathustra, at the very start, has to "go down" like the sun to give his gift. His giving costs him.
*Rejoinder:* Then you admit the highest giving costs nothing, and A6's "always costs" is Christian colouring.
*Reply:* In God, no cost; in us, always some, because we are finite. A6 must be worded for human givers, and in terms of giving *of oneself*, not loss for its own sake. Your noble man also has things he will not do even to save himself, out of reverence for himself (*BGE* §287), and your Greek nobles called themselves "the truthful." So A6's second clause has a counterpart even in master morality. The disagreement is over *what* must never be done, not *whether* anything must never be done.
**Held with repair (A6 wording).**

**Exchange 5: Truth, Patrick's counterattack (GS §344; GM III §§24–27).**
*Patrick's side:* Your one unbroken virtue is intellectual honesty. You call it the conscience that killed God. By your own account it takes its fire from the faith, as old as Plato and Christianity, that God is truth (*GS* §344). You say that even "we godless anti-metaphysicians" still draw on that flame. So you live on borrowed capital too, and W2 (a law, and a mind fitted to read it, from one Source) has its best witness in you.
*Nietzsche:* Consistent to the end, I *do* put the value of truth itself in question (*GM* III §24). I don't claim truth binds unconditionally.
*Patrick's side:* Then your genealogy of guilt cannot claim our assent *as true*. It becomes one more perspective, and the reader may decline it.
*Rejoinder:* Perspectivism is not relativism. The more eyes we bring to a thing, the more complete our grasp of it (*GM* III §12). Some perspectives are more honest than others.
*Reply:* "More honest" is the very value you have just put in question. You cannot use honesty against us while suspending its authority.
**Held.** This is where the Nietzschean is weakest. It also backs the chapter's existing line that "follow the evidence" is itself an ought.
*But Nietzsche scores against W2's examples:* "Conscience reads the law most surely exactly where survival pulls against it: mercy to an enemy, care for those who can never repay." No. Slave morality *is* a survival strategy: the many weak protecting themselves against the strong few. Universal care for the weak works like insurance for every weak person. Even the ascetic ideal is life's trick for preserving declining life (*GM* III §13).
*Reply:* That explanation is coherent, and the mercy examples do not survive it at better than a draw. But W2 needs only truth: you yourself insist that error is a condition of life (*BGE* §4) and that unconditional truthfulness does not serve survival (*GS* §344). Move W2's evidence onto truth.
**W2: Held with repair.**

**Exchange 6: The Lawgiver (BGE §211; GS §301).**
*Nietzsche:* Nature has no value; value is given to it by those who create (*GS* §301). True philosophers are commanders and legislators (*BGE* §211). So yes, a law needs a lawgiver. With God dead, the office falls to the highest human being.
*Patrick's side:* Then you agree with the shape of W1: an ought needs someone who gives it. You fill that office with a human will that invents values. That is voluntarism exactly, the "mere power to dictate" the book refuses. A value someone invents binds only those who choose it, as you admit. So the choice is not "Lawgiver or no lawgiver." It is a will that invents, or the Good that is. Chapter 3 answers which can bind: God does not invent the good; He *is* the Good, and His command reveals it.
*Rejoinder:* Your God is a will too: the slaves' fantasy of a master in the sky who takes revenge for them. Your own Tertullian and Aquinas promised that the blessed would see the punishments of the damned (*GM* I §15).
*Reply:* Grace comes first: God loves first (1 John 4:19). He predestines no one to hell, which is self-exclusion (CCC 1033, 1037). The Aquinas text is from the *Supplement*, compiled after his death from his early *Sentences* commentary. Even there the blessed rejoice in God's justice and their own rescue, not in suffering as such (*Suppl.* q.94 a.3). The charge concerns how the tradition has been read, not W1's logic.
**Held.** v1's W1 is sound against him, and it is improved by naming the choice between a will and the Good.

**Exchange 7: Eternity (Zarathustra IV; eternal recurrence).**
*Nietzsche:* A7 is the wish for a "true world" behind this one: tired life looking for an exit. My answer is to love fate and will this same life again.
*Patrick's side:* You grant the longing itself: "all joy wants eternity." You answer it with the same life repeated, received by no one. *Amor fati* is love aimed at something that cannot love back, and it requires you to will every child's torture again. W3's claim stands: only a person can love us back.
*Rejoinder:* Your God also lets the torture happen.
*Reply:* He permits it; He does not will it as good. He promises to wipe away every tear (Rev 21:4), and evil is already on the scale in C.
**Held (as a clue, as earlier rounds found).**

## 4. Ledger for v1

- **A heading ("What we cannot honestly deny"): Fails as worded.** Nietzsche denied A2 and A4 honestly. Repair: present these as what the reader finds he cannot give up, and show the cost of giving them up.
- **A1. Some things really wrong, for everyone: Holds with repair (heading only).** Nietzsche denies "for everyone" and approves of enslaving the weak (*BGE* §257). But the price of his denial, willing the child's torture forever under *amor fati*, makes A1 more credible than his alternative.
- **A2. Guilty, not merely mistaken: Holds with repair.** The genealogy explains *infinite* guilt. It does not explain guilt aimed at a person, which seeks forgiveness from the one wronged. Reword to put that in front.
- **A3. No one on earth can release us: Holds with repair.** It is ambiguous between forgiveness (which the victim can give) and making the wrong right (which no one can). Nietzsche exploits the ambiguity. Fix it.
- **A4. Equal dignity: Holds with repair, at parity.** Frontally denied, coherently. It survives as the reader's own seeing, backed by CCC 37–38 on history and by Nietzsche as witness to the conditional. It must not be phrased as a headcount.
- **A5. Human nature does not settle the ought: Holds.** Nietzsche agrees that nature has no value (*GS* §301). He even keeps a "what we are for" (the overman). He fills it with created values.
- **A6. Real good costs the giver; some things never done: Holds with repair.** The giving-from-overflow objection is answered by Scheler and by the Trinity rule. Reword "costs" as giving *of oneself*. The "never" clause has a counterpart in noble self-reverence.
- **A7. We long to be known and loved wholly and for good: Holds.** Nietzsche concedes it ("all joy wants eternity") and offers recurrence, which no one receives.
- **W1. Obligation points to a Lawgiver: Holds, improved by repair.** Nietzsche grants the conditional and legislates in God's place. Name the choice between a will that invents and the Good that is.
- **W2. Law and mind, one Source: Holds with repair.** The survival claim fails for the mercy examples (herd-preservation explains them). Rest it on truth, where Nietzsche's own *GS* §344 and *BGE* §4 concede the point.
- **W3. Communion points to One who is Love: Holds** (as a clue).
- **C. Comparison: Holds with repair.** The most honest rival is missing: power and rank. Add it.
- **Evil and hiddenness on the scale: Holds.**
- **Conclusion (reason's witness, CCC 31/35): Holds.** Its modesty is exactly right against Nietzsche: the argument does not compel the man who gives up A4, and it should not claim to.

## 5. Remaining sound objections

Against **v1 as written**, three objections stand that our side cannot answer at parity without changing the text:

1. **The A-heading overclaims ("cannot honestly deny").** A great atheist did deny A2 and A4, honestly and in print. As written, v1 can only answer by implying he was dishonest, which is uncharitable and contrary to CCC 847 and LG 16. **Serious.**
2. **W2's survival examples (mercy to an enemy, care for those who cannot repay)** are explained as herd self-preservation at least as well as by one Source. **Serious.**
3. **A3's ambiguity** between forgiveness and making the wrong right lets the opponent turn Christ's command to forgive against the premise. **Minor.**

Plus one gap: **C leaves out the Nietzschean rival.** **Minor.**

**After the repairs in section 6: NONE.** The genealogical debunking of A2 and A4 is answered at parity: a comparative argument cannot assume naturalism; the debunking turns on its own values; and guilt aimed at a person is found outside the priestly line. Nietzsche's open denial of A4 is not a sound objection, because its key premise (the disabled newborn has no equal worth) is far less plausible to almost every reader than A4 itself. What remains is an honest *limit*, not a defeat. The argument cannot move a reader who, like Nietzsche, truly gives up A4 and A2. For him it becomes a stark choice, and v1's claim to be "reason's witness... can predispose to faith" already allows for that.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**A heading** (replace "What we cannot honestly deny"):
> **A. What we find we cannot give up.** None of these is beyond denial. One great atheist, Friedrich Nietzsche, denied several of them openly. But look at what he had to give up to do it, and ask whether you can.

**A2** (replace):
> **A2. Doing them leaves us guilty, not merely mistaken.** A wrong sum needs correcting; a wronged friend needs to be asked for forgiveness. Guilt points at a person we have wronged, and we go back to that person for pardon, not only to ourselves for peace.

**A3** (replace):
> **A3. No one on earth can make the wrong right.** The one we wronged can forgive us, and should. But neither he, nor a nation by vote, nor the whole human race can make what we did to have been good, or sign away what is owed to every human being. Forgiveness heals the bond; it does not remake the law.

**A4** (replace):
> **A4. Every human being has the same dignity.** The newborn, the dying, the severely disabled, the enemy: equal, prior to anyone's recognition (*Dignitas Infinita* 1, 15). Reason can see this, though history shows how easily it is lost: the same ancient world that taught that slaves are men also left weak and deformed infants to die. The Church teaches that reason needs revelation's light to hold such truths firmly and without error (CCC 37–38). Nietzsche saw that if God is gone, this equality goes with Him, and he let it go. Look at the newborn in front of you and ask whether you can.

**A6** (replace):
> **A6. Real good for another always asks something of the giver:** time, strength, comfort, sometimes safety. Even the one who gives out of fullness gives of himself. That is how reality works for creatures like us. And some things must never be done, even to save oneself.

**W1** (add after "...is Himself the Good He asks of us."):
> Even Nietzsche agreed that guilt is owed to someone; that is why he expected guilt to fade once God was gone, and why, with God gone, he handed the work of making laws to the strongest human will. So the question is not whether an ought needs someone behind it. It is who: a will that invents values, which can command but cannot bind, or the Good Himself.

**W2** (replace the parenthesis and the sentence it sits in):
> If moral facts simply exist and conscience is only an evolved survival tool, it is luck that they ever met. And conscience reads the law most surely where survival gains nothing from it: we want the truth even when a lie would comfort us and no one would ever know. Nietzsche himself admitted that life often runs on useful errors, and that the unconditional will to truth takes its fire from the old faith that God is truth.

**C** (add as a new line after "Brute moral facts"):
> - **Power and rank** (Nietzsche) is the most honest rival. It explains our moral sense as a history of the weak and the strong, and it stays consistent by giving up guilt (A2), equal dignity (A4) and the claim that anything is wrong for everyone (A1). It cannot give up truthfulness, and by its own admission that is a borrowed faith.

## 7. New insights for the book

1. **Nietzsche is the chapter's best outside witness, not just its worst opponent.** He grants the very conditionals the chapter needs: no God means guilt has no one to be owed to (*GM* II §20), equal dignity has no ground (*Antichrist* §43; *Twilight*, "Skirmishes" §5), and unconditional truthfulness rests on faith that God is truth (*GS* §344). The chapter could use him the way it uses Ratzinger and St. John Paul II: as proof that the stakes are real, coming from the side that wanted them gone. The chapter's "borrowed capital" claim is far stronger in the mouth of the man who first made it.
2. **"The Atheist's Dilemma" has no slot for Nietzsche.** Option (1) answers the man who says "you ought not believe in oughts." Nietzsche never says that. He says "I will not," and he legislates values without claiming moral obligation. The chapter should name a fourth figure: the one who gives up equal dignity and guilt with open eyes. The honest answer to him is not "you refute yourself" but "look at what you had to give up." Likewise, the line "Every time an atheist says 'That's wrong!'" has a clear exception in him; it should say "nearly every."
3. **Mercy as the perfection of power.** Nietzsche makes mercy the privilege of the most powerful (*GM* II §10). The Church prays that God shows His almighty power above all by pardoning and showing mercy (Collect, Twenty-Sixth Sunday in Ordinary Time), and Aquinas holds that mercy is proper to God and is where His omnipotence is chiefly shown (*ST* II-II q.30 a.4). The difference: only in Christ does the most powerful One show mercy to the *weakest*, the newborn and the dying, not just to the offenders a strong community can afford to ignore. This fits Ch. 8 (mercy) and Ch. 12 together.
4. **"All joy wants eternity."** Even the most anti-Christian modern thinker heard joy ask for forever. He answered with the same life again, received by no one. The Church answers with Someone. This strengthens A7 and W3 without overclaiming.
5. **Catholic answers already exist and should be named in Further Study.** Max Scheler, *Ressentiment* (1912), on Christian love as bending down out of fullness. Henri de Lubac, SJ (later Cardinal), *The Drama of Atheist Humanism* (1944). René Girard, *I See Satan Fall Like Lightning* (1999): Nietzsche saw rightly that Christ takes the victim's side, and chose against Him. Pope Benedict XVI, *Deus Caritas Est* 3, which answers Nietzsche by name on eros.
6. **History cuts for CCC 38, not against the book.** Equal dignity in its full reach entered the West through Christ. Stoic dignity talk sat alongside infant exposure. That is exactly what the Church teaches: moral truths within reason's reach are, in our fallen state, held firmly by all only with revelation's help.
7. **Amor fati is the sharpest contrast with Christian hope.** It must say yes to every evil as part of the whole. Christianity can hate the evil and still hope for the whole, because Love redeems rather than merely affirms.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "Forgiveness heals the bond; it does not remake the law."
- "A will that invents values can command. Only the Good can bind."
- "Nietzsche saw that if God is gone, equal dignity goes with Him, and he let it go. Look at the newborn and ask whether you can."
- "Mercy is what power looks like when it is perfect."
- "Even the one who gives out of fullness gives of himself."
- "Joy wants eternity. Only Love can give it a face."

## 8. Score

**v1 as written: 6/10.** Its A-list rests on exactly the data Nietzsche denies, and it calls them undeniable. **With the repairs above: 8/10.** The argument then holds at parity against the most honest atheist and turns his central concessions (guilt owed to someone, equal dignity's dependence on God, truth's borrowed faith) into the chapter's strongest evidence.
