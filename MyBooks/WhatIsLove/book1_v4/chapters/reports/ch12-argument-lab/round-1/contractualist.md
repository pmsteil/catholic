# Round 1: Contractualism and the Second-Personal Standpoint vs. v1

## 1. School and voice

The voice is a composite of T. M. Scanlon (morality as what we owe to each other: an act is wrong if any principle allowing it could reasonably be rejected), Stephen Darwall (obligation as the standing of free and equal persons to make claims and demands of one another, the "second-person standpoint"), and John Rawls (justice as fairness between free and equal persons). Contractarians in the line of Hobbes and David Gauthier appear only where they differ. This school agrees with the book more than any other non-theist school: obligation is personal, it is owed to someone, and a wrong is done *to* someone. So it attacks W1 at its weakest point. If what is owed is owed to persons, the persons we owe it to are the obvious claimants, and no further Lawgiver is needed.

---

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.**
*Their answer:* We start from persons regarded as free and equal, each with the standing to demand that others justify how they treat him. A person is owed whatever treatment follows from principles that no one, moved to find terms all could accept, could reasonably reject (Scanlon). For Rawls, it is what free and equal persons would choose without knowing their own place in society (the "veil of ignorance"). Darwall puts the same idea in terms of authority: each of us has equal authority to address claims to every other.
*Verdict: Holds, but borrows its starting point.* The method is coherent and powerful. But it *assumes* that persons are free and equal. It does not derive that equality, and Rawls openly takes it from ideas already present in democratic culture. Where those ideas came from is a fair historical question. Jürgen Habermas, in his 2004 dialogue with Cardinal Ratzinger and in later writing, acknowledged that egalitarian universalism grew out of the Jewish ethic of justice and the Christian ethic of love. *(Paraphrase only. Verify against the published text before it enters the book.)*

**2. A hard case, resolved by their own principles.**
*Case:* An ICU has one ventilator during a surge. Two patients need it. One is a 30-year-old. The other is a 30-year-old with a severe lifelong intellectual disability. A hospital protocol proposes ranking patients by "quality-adjusted life years," which would favor the first patient.
*Their answer:* The disabled patient's trustee can reasonably reject any principle that lowers her priority for a feature that has nothing to do with whether the treatment will work. Equal medical need with equal expected benefit calls for a fair procedure such as a lottery or first-come, not a ranking by the worth of a life. Scanlon also refuses to add up small benefits to many people so as to outweigh a grave burden on one, so contractualism also blocks the transplant-surgeon case (killing one person for five) without any utilitarian sums.
*Verdict: Holds within its scope.* It does not borrow the book's definitions. It uses its own thin notions of fairness and honest justification. But notice two limits. (a) The case could be settled only through a *trustee*, which leads into challenge 3. (b) Contractualism is silent on mercy and self-gift. It can say what is permitted, but not what is good beyond what is owed. Give it a case that turns on mercy, such as whether a mother should forgive the drunk driver who killed her son, and it can only answer "that is up to her." Scanlon concedes that "what we owe to each other" is only one part of morality. So the method holds where the question is justice, and it has nothing to say where the question is mercy or sacrifice.

**3. Ground of equal, unconditional dignity.**
*Their answer:* Equal standing belongs to persons as beings who can give and respond to reasons. Rawls calls this a "range property": everyone above a minimum capacity is equal, whatever their other differences. The enemy is covered fully, since contractualism is strong on the enemy's rights. The dying are covered while they remain competent.
*Verdict: Breaks at the edges, and borrows to patch them.* Darwall's authority presupposes second-personal competence, meaning the ability to make and answer claims. The newborn, the person with advanced dementia, and the profoundly disabled lack it. The school responds to this in three ways:
- Gauthier's contractarianism openly excludes those who cannot contribute.
- Rawls set severe disability aside as a problem for later, and Martha Nussbaum's critique pressed him on exactly this.
- Scanlon extends moral standing to them through *trustees* who speak on their behalf.

But the trustee's duty must rest on something the infant already has before any trustee shows up. That is a value grounded in what she *is*: a human being whose life can go well or badly. That is a claim about nature, not a contract. So contractualism can reach the weakest only by borrowing a nature-based worth that it does not explain. This is exactly the gap Dignitas Infinita 15 names: dignity "prior to any recognition."

**4. Ultimate good for humans.**
*Their answer:* There is none, on purpose. Rawls gives priority to the right over the good: a just society should not impose one picture of the good life. It supplies "primary goods" and leaves the rest to each person. When Scanlon is pressed on why anyone should care about morality, his answer is the value of standing in a relation of *mutual recognition* with others, which means living on terms you could justify to every one of them.
*Verdict: Holds as political theory, breaks as an answer to the question.* Refusing to name an ultimate good is coherent for a theory of public justice. As an answer to "what is ultimately good for a human being," it simply declines. Yet its deepest motive, mutual recognition, is a small, finite form of communion. That is worth noticing (see section 7).

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1. The direct attack on W1.**
*Contractualist:* W1 says that "a claim no human can cancel was not laid by any human." That does not follow. Inalienable claims are familiar: a person holds them and cannot sign them away. Locke denied that anyone can sell himself into slavery. Kant held that I may not treat humanity in my own person merely as a means. Your own chapter says the child's dignity "is truly her own." A claim can belong to a human and still be beyond that human's power to cancel. The premise "no human can cancel it" fits perfectly well with "a human holds it." W1 is invalid.
*Defense:* We grant that the claim is truly the victim's. The point is not that someone other than the victim holds it. The point is the *strange shape* of a claim that is his own and yet not his to dispose of. That is what needs explaining.
*Contractualist:* It is explained from inside the practice. Some claims protect the very standing that makes waiving possible. Signing them away would destroy the authority you were using to sign. That is why a person cannot consent to his own enslavement or to being tortured for fun.
*Verdict: Failed as written.* W1 as stated is a non sequitur. The question it points to survives: why does a person have a standing he cannot cancel? W1 must be rebuilt around that question (section 6).

**Exchange 2. Who binds me to myself?**
*Defense:* Kant saw the difficulty himself. In his treatment of duties to oneself, he noted that if the one who binds and the one who is bound are the same, the binder could always release himself. So duties to oneself look contradictory. He resolved this by dividing the self: rational nature in me (homo noumenon) binds the empirical me (homo phaenomenon). That is the contractualist's move as well. The authority has been placed *inside* the person and called "rational nature." But why does rational nature have authority over me, such that I cannot release myself?
*Contractualist:* Because that is what it is to be a reasoner. The authority is part of what agency is. Asking why reason binds a reasoner is like asking why logic binds a thinker.
*Defense:* That is a stopping point, and the book says honestly that every account has one (C). Ours is that the standing was *received*: not earned, not conferred by others, not self-made, and given by the One who made the person and is the Good. Yours is that the standing is simply part of rational agency.
*Verdict: Held with repair. The two sides reach parity on competent adults.* The chapter's claim that "only a Someone can lay a claim" does not defeat contractualism, because this school *agrees* and names human Someones. The contest moves to which stopping point explains more, and that is decided at the edges (Exchanges 4 and 5).

**Exchange 3. A3 attacks the wrong target.**
*Contractualist:* A3 says no nation by vote and no human race can release us. We agree, and we never said a vote makes morality. "Could not reasonably reject" is a test of justification, not an actual agreement. Hume's objection that no actual contract was ever signed has never touched Scanlon. A3 refutes conventionalism and Hobbes, not us.
*Defense:* Granted. The book must stop treating every non-theist account as majority rule. But a hypothetical reasoner is no one. Who actually issues the claim?
*Contractualist:* Actual persons do: the victim, and every member of the moral community acting on his behalf. The hypothetical test only fixes what they may demand.
*Verdict: Held with repair.* A3 stands against actual-contract theories but does not touch Scanlon. C must name contractualism as its own rival, and A3 must be reworded so it no longer reads as an attack on voting alone.

**Exchange 4. Those who cannot make a claim.**
*Defense:* Darwall's authority belongs to those who can address claims. The newborn, the person with advanced dementia, and the profoundly disabled cannot. Gauthier excludes them, and Rawls postponed the question. Scanlon gives them trustees, but a trustee only speaks *for* a worth that is already there. Where does that worth come from, if not from a practice of mutual accountability?
*Contractualist:* From the value of human life. A being whose life can go well or badly gives us reasons. Contractualism need not explain all value. It explains the part of morality about what we owe to each other.
*Defense:* Then the hardest and most important equality claim, equal worth for those who can never claim it, rests on a value your theory does not supply. It is a worth grounded in what a human being *is*. That is the very worth the book says is "prior to any recognition" (DI 15), and A5 says that worth does not come from mere description of our nature. So the question stands: why is a human being's worth equal and unconditional, whatever her powers?
*Contractualist rejoinder:* We can say that being human is the relevant kind, and that this kind matters.
*Defense:* Membership in a kind defined by what its members are *for*, even when particular members cannot do it, is the natural-law idea. That is our side's ground, not yours.
*Verdict: Held.* This is the strongest point for our side against this school.

**Exchange 5. Forgiveness: the victim can release.**
*Contractualist:* A3 says no one on earth can release us. But victims forgive every day, and your Church *requires* them to. So A3 is false on your own terms.
*Defense:* Fair. A3 blurs two things. Repaired: the victim *can* forgive what was done to him, and should. But (a) he cannot make the wrong not wrong; (b) he could never have given permission in advance for, say, torture for fun; and (c) *no one else has the standing to forgive in his place.* Simon Wiesenthal's *The Sunflower* turns on exactly this: a dying SS man asks a Jewish prisoner to forgive him for murdering other Jews. When the victim is dead, the guilt is still owed, and no one left on earth has the standing to release it. Psalm 51:4 and CCC 1850 say that every sin is also an offense against God. On our account, Someone who is never gone was also wronged and can forgive.
*Contractualist rejoinder:* The moral community still holds the murderer accountable. Reconciliation is possible without forgiveness. And needing forgiveness does not prove that a forgiver exists, just as hunger for the infinite proves no infinite food.
*Defense:* Agreed: this is a clue, not a proof. But your account must say that some guilt is permanently unforgivable. That is coherent, and it is very bleak. Ours explains why we find that intolerable, and why the guilt still seems addressed to someone.
*Verdict: Held with repair, at moderate weight.* It counts as an abductive clue. It is not a deduction.

**Exchange 6. Does a Lawgiver steal the victim's claim?**
*Contractualist:* Darwall traces your model to Pufendorf and Suárez, and Anscombe argued that "moral obligation" only made sense under a divine law. Here is the problem. If the duty not to torture the child is owed to *God*, then the child becomes the occasion of a duty owed to someone else. That gets wrongs backwards. The wrong is done to *her*.
*Defense:* Catholic teaching holds both, and they do not compete. Sin is a failure of genuine love for God *and* neighbor (CCC 1849) and an offense against God (CCC 1850). The child's claim is real and fully her own. It is real *because* God's love made her precious (ST I q.20 a.2), not in place of her. The theist keeps the directedness the contractualist rightly insists on and adds the source of her standing. And because W1 rejects voluntarism, God is not a third-party commander who picks rules. He is the Good that makes her claim a claim.
*Verdict: Held.* But W1's phrase "the claim of the good on us" mixes two things: what is owed to persons (the right) and what is worth having (the good). It should be reworded so the victim's claim stays at the center.

**Exchange 7. Pufendorf's point: authority must be recognizable.**
*Contractualist:* Even on your view, a command binds only if the one addressed can recognize its authority by his own reason. Otherwise it is mere force. So the second-person standpoint comes *before* any divine command, and God would need our standing in order to address us at all.
*Defense:* Yes, and this is Catholic, not a defeat. The natural law is our *participation* in the eternal law, knowable by reason (ST I-II q.91 a.2; CCC 1954-1955; Rom 2:14-15). God addresses us as free persons. Grace comes first, and love presupposes freedom (Ch. 4). A law offered to be recognized and freely received is exactly the book's "gift" framing. The point does not show that our standing is self-made. It shows that the Giver made us able to recognize Him.
*Verdict: Held.* The point actually helps the chapter (see section 7).

**Exchange 8. W2 against a reasons-realist and a constructivist.**
*Contractualist:* W2 says it is "luck" that moral facts and conscience meet. Scanlon is a realist about reasons who needs no causal contact with them. They are known the way mathematical truths are, by reflection and reflective equilibrium. A constructivist (Rawls, Korsgaard) goes further: the law is reason's own work, so of course reason can read it. There is no coincidence to explain.
*Defense:* To the realist: then nothing explains why a mind formed by survival pressure is fitted to necessary truths about what we owe. "Luck" is the wrong word, but "unexplained" is the right one. To the constructivist: if the law is only what reasoners like us would accept, then a differently made reasoner would have a different law. That gives up A1 ("wrong even if every culture approved") one level up. Sharon Street, a constructivist, accepts this contingency openly.
*Verdict: Held with repair.* W2 must stop aiming only at the evolutionary debunker. It should replace "luck" with "nothing explains the fit," and it should name the price the constructivist pays.

## 5. Remaining sound objections

Each of the following stands **against v1 as written**. The repairs in section 6 answer each one at least to parity. No objection remains standing against the repaired version, for the reason given at the end of this section.

1. **W1 is invalid.** It infers "not laid by any human" from "no human can cancel." Inalienable rights are held by humans who cannot cancel them, and the chapter itself says dignity "is truly her own." **Fatal** for W1's present wording.
2. **C leaves out contractualism.** On obligation, the second-person standpoint is the most serious non-theist rival. A comparison that omits it, and that credits "brute moral facts" with explaining only A1, is not honest by the chapter's own standard. **Serious.**
3. **A3 is false of forgiveness and attacks only votes.** Victims can forgive; the Church requires it; and Scanlon is not a voting theory. **Serious.**
4. **A5's last clause is not undeniable.** A contractualist denies that the ought points to "what we are for": for him, it points to what can be justified to others. **Minor** (move the clause).
5. **W2's "luck" and "never finally contradict."** The first misses realists and constructivists. The second is asserted without support. **Minor.**

Problems in the current chapter text with the same root, to fix in the full pass:
- "an ought is a claim laid on a person … Only a Someone can." Contractualism agrees and names the neighbor.
- "Only a personal God grounds personal obligations." Human persons ground personal obligations on this view.
- Atheist's Dilemma option 2, "incoherent." Contractualism is not incoherent; it is incomplete.
- "far more faith." This is the overclaim the brief forbids.

**After repair: NONE at Fatal or Serious.** On competent adults, the contractualist and the theist reach honest parity: both name a person-shaped stopping point. Ours goes further than theirs in three places their theory cannot reach without borrowing: (a) equal, unconditional worth for those who can never make a claim; (b) guilt owed when the victim is gone and no one on earth can forgive; (c) why persons have standing at all, which contractualism assumes and theism explains as a gift. The contractualist's best counter is that "received, therefore given by Someone" is an inference, not a deduction. That is answered by stating it, as the repair does, as the best explanation and not a proof (CCC 31).

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**A2:**
> **A2. Doing them leaves us guilty, not merely mistaken.** We have wronged someone: a real person, with a real claim on us that is truly his own. We can rightly be blamed, and we need to be forgiven. A wrong sum needs correcting; a wronged person needs forgiveness.

**A3:**
> **A3. Some claims cannot be signed away, and some guilt no one on earth can lift.** The victim can forgive what was done to him, and should. But he cannot make it not wrong, and he could never have given permission for it beforehand. No one else can forgive in his place: not his friends, not a nation by vote, not the whole human race. When the one we wronged is gone, the debt is not.

**A4 (small addition):**
> **A4. Every human being has the same dignity.** The newborn, the dying, the severely disabled, the enemy: equal, prior to anyone's recognition, including those who will never be able to make a claim for themselves (*Dignitas Infinita* 1, 15).

**A5:**
> **A5. Human nature does not settle what we ought to do.** We are capable of truth and lies, justice and injustice, mercy and cruelty, giving and grasping. Describing what we are never by itself tells us which to choose.

**W1:**
> **W1. Obligation points to the Giver of our worth.** A wrong is done to someone, and his claim is truly his own (A2). Yet it is a claim he did not make and cannot sign away. No vote gave it and no vote can take it, and it belongs as fully to the newborn who cannot speak as to the man who can (A3, A4). A standing that is ours but not ours to cancel, that we did not earn and no one conferred, is best explained as received. The best account of who gave it is the Creator, who made each of us, established what we are for, and is Himself the Good He asks of us. He is not a commander who picks rules but the Love that made each person precious before anyone recognized her. And because every wrong against a person is also a wrong against the One who gave her worth (Psalm 51:4; CCC 1850), there is still Someone with standing to forgive when no one on earth is left who can.

**W2 (replace the first sentence and the "never contradict" sentence):**
> **W2. The law and the mind that reads it point to one Source.** If moral truths simply exist and conscience was shaped only for survival, nothing explains why the two fit. Yet conscience reads the law most surely exactly where survival pulls against it: truth told at cost, mercy to an enemy, care for those who can never repay. If instead the law is only what reasoners like us would agree to, a differently made reasoner would have a different law, and A1 is lost. In the hardest cases we have faced, truth, justice, mercy and self-gift could be held together without betraying any one of them (Chapter 11). A law that is one, and a mind fitted to read it, are best explained by one Source who made both.

**C (insert after "Human flourishing alone"; amend "Brute moral facts"):**
> - **What we owe each other** (persons holding one another to account on terms no one could reasonably reject) explains a great deal: why a wrong is done to someone, why we answer to the one we wronged, why no vote can change it. It does not explain why persons have that standing at all (it assumes it), why the newborn and the severely disabled have it equally though they can make no claim (A4), or who can forgive when the one we wronged is gone (A3).
> - **Brute moral facts** explain A1, and with more work some of A2; they leave release, equal dignity, how we know any of it (W2), and communion unexplained.

**C conclusion (no change needed).** The conclusion already says "best explanation" and cites CCC 31 and 35.

**Chapter text, for the full pass:**
- In "Why Only God Can Ground Love," change "only a personal God grounds personal obligations" to "only a personal God grounds the personal worth no person gave himself."
- In the "One last skeptic" paragraph, after "Only a Someone can," add: "The wisest unbelievers agree, and point to one another. They are right that we owe each other. The question is who gave us to each other."
- In the Atheist's Dilemma, option 2: change "incoherent" to "incomplete: it can say what we owe each other, but not why anyone is owed."

## 7. New insights for the book

- **Contractualism is the near neighbor, not the enemy.** It agrees that obligation is personal and directed, and that a wrong is done to someone. The book should say so warmly, and then ask the one question it cannot answer: where does the standing come from? This is truer, and more persuasive, than lumping it in with relativism.
- **The hinge is "ours, but not ours to cancel."** Kant felt the difficulty himself in duties to oneself. It is the plain-language form of the whole argument, and a reader feels it at once.
- **Standing to forgive.** Only the one wronged can forgive. This is the strongest new datum for A3 and the most human one. *The Sunflower* is a concrete, well-known case. Psalm 51:4 and CCC 1850 give the Catholic answer: every wrong also wrongs Someone who is never gone.
- **Pufendorf's point is a gift to the chapter.** A law must be recognizable by the one it binds, or it is only force. This is Romans 2:14-15 and Chapter 4 (a gift forced is no gift) in philosophical dress. God does not merely command; He makes us able to recognize Him.
- **Mutual recognition is a small communion.** Scanlon's answer to "why be moral?" is the value of living with others on terms you could justify to each of them. The book can honor that longing and show its limit: it reaches the reasonable, not the forgotten, and no finite circle of recognition holds the whole of anyone (A7, W3).
- **The edges decide the contest.** Every rival does well with competent adults. The newborn, the profoundly disabled, and the murdered child who cannot forgive are where the explanations separate. The chapter should spend its words there.
- **Habermas and Ratzinger (2004).** A secular philosopher acknowledging the Christian roots of equal dignity is a strong, fair witness for C, *once the wording is verified against the published dialogue*.

**Lines worth keeping:**
- "Her claim is truly her own. But she did not make it, and she cannot give it away."
- "A worth no one gave her, no one can take."
- "What we owe each other is real. The question is who gave us to each other."
- "When the one we wronged is gone, the debt is not."
- "Only the one we wronged can forgive us, and every wrong also wrongs Someone who is never gone."
- "Unbelief at its best is not incoherent. It is incomplete."

---

## 8. Score

**5 / 10 as written, 7 / 10 with the repairs above.** As written, W1 commits a plain non sequitur that this school spots at once, and C leaves out the rival that matters most on obligation. Repaired, W1 reaches honest parity on competent adults and pulls ahead at the newborn, the disabled, and the victim who is gone. Those are cases where contractualism must borrow a worth it cannot supply.
