# Argument Lab, Round 1: Non-Naturalist Secular Moral Realism vs. v1

## 1. School and voice

This is the "godless normative realism" of Erik Wielenberg (*Robust Ethics*, 2014; *Value and Virtue in a Godless Universe*, 2005), David Enoch (*Taking Morality Seriously*, 2011), Derek Parfit (*On What Matters*, 2011), Russ Shafer-Landau (*Moral Realism: A Defence*, 2003) and Michael Huemer (*Ethical Intuitionism*, 2005). Where it helps, it also draws on Stephen Darwall's second-personal account of obligation (*The Second-Person Standpoint*, 2006) and on the "third-factor" replies to Sharon Street's Darwinian dilemma (Enoch; Wielenberg; Knut Skarsaune). The view: moral facts are objective and necessary. They rest on non-moral features (pain is bad because of what pain is), they are known by rational intuition and reflection, and they need no God. The view is hostile to relativism and to materialist reduction, and on most first-order questions it sides with the book against both.

---

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**Challenge 1: the starting point for justice.**
*Their answer:* Justice is giving each person what he is owed. What he is owed rests on necessary normative truths about persons: his suffering is bad, his agency and standing must be respected, and he may not be used merely as a tool. These truths are brute. They are not derived from anything more basic, and they are known the way "a contradiction cannot be true" is known.
*Verdict:* **Holds as a working principle, borrows its form.** It is coherent and close to the book's account of justice. It takes the classical formula (*suum cuique*) and leaves its metaphysics behind. It does not *explain* why persons have the standing it names. It posits the standing.

**Challenge 2: a hard case.**
*Their answer:* (a) In the transplant case (kill one healthy patient to harvest organs for five), the realist forbids the killing on intuitionist grounds and through reflective equilibrium: persons are not resources, and doing harm differs from allowing it. That is the book's verdict, reached through "justice as respect" and the refusal to force a sacrifice on someone else. It is close to Ch. 4's "a gift forced is no gift." (b) In voluntary euthanasia (a dying, lucid patient in severe pain asks to be killed), most of the school permits it. Consent waives the right not to be killed, and suffering is bad. Shafer-Landau-style deontologists may hold out. Parfit-style convergentists generally do not.
*Verdict:* **Coherent, and it shows a real fault line.** In (a) the school borrows truth and justice in all but name. In (b) it lets the victim *release* the wrong by consent. That is exactly what v1's A3 says cannot happen. So A3 is common ground only for paradigm cases where consent is impossible or irrelevant (torturing a child for fun), not across the board. The book should not present A3 as something every godless realist grants without qualification.

**Challenge 3: equal, unconditional dignity.**
*Their answer:* The school splits. (i) Kind-based realists say it is a necessary truth that every member of a rational kind has equal basic worth, whatever that member can do now. (ii) Capacity-based realists (Jeff McMahan in *The Ethics of Killing*; Rawls's "range property" as Jeremy Waldron discusses it in *One Another's Equals*) ground worth in capacities above a threshold. So they either rank the severely cognitively impaired below other humans or admit that equality is hard to secure. Waldron, a secular thinker, defends equal dignity firmly but treats its basis as an open problem.
*Verdict:* **Breaks or borrows. This is the strongest ground for our side.** Capacity grounds are not equal: capacities come in degrees, and some humans lack them. Kind grounds are equal, but they appeal to a nature *directed toward* rationality even where it is impaired. That is "what we are for", the teleology of A5 and the natural law, now posited as one more brute necessary truth. And the realist has no answer to the question "why the kind and not the capacity?" except an intuition. Under pressure, members of his own school give that intuition up. The theist grounds worth in the individual, not only the kind: each person is loved into being (ST I q.20 a.2), and that worth is prior to any recognition (*Dignitas Infinita* 15).

**Challenge 4: the ultimate good for humans.**
*Their answer:* An objective-list pluralism: knowledge, friendship, achievement, pleasure, autonomy and beauty, lived with integrity. There is no single final end. Death ends the person, and a life is good to the degree these goods fill it.
*Verdict:* **Coherent as a plan for a life. Breaks on unity and on longing.** The list is a list. Nothing explains why these goods belong together, or why we long for a good that no item on the list can supply (the A7/W3 experience). The realist's honest reply is that a longing does not prove its object, and that reply is correct. So this is a gap in explanation, not a contradiction.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1: W1 is invalid.**
*Realist:* W1 runs: what is owed is owed to someone; no human laid this claim; so Someone above every human laid it. That equivocates. "Owed to someone" (a directed duty) is true of many wrongs, and the someone is the victim. "Laid by someone" is a further claim that I deny: obligations are not *laid* at all. They hold because of what persons are. Not every "ought" is owed to anyone (Parfit: reasons are not debts). "No human laid it" gives you "no one laid it" as easily as "Someone above laid it."
*Defense:* Granted: as a deduction, W1 does not go through. But the data in A2 are not "a reason applies to me." They are relational: I *wronged* someone, I am *guilty*, I need to be *forgiven*. W1 should be an inference to the best explanation of that relational shape, not a deduction from the word "owed."
*Result:* **Held with repair.** W1 must be recast as abductive, and the word "laid" must go (see Exchange 2).

**Exchange 2: the release test proves too much.**
*Realist:* A3 says no one on earth can release us. By your own tradition, God cannot release us either. Aquinas holds that God cannot dispense from the precepts of the Decalogue (ST I-II q.100 a.8). Your Ch. 3 says God cannot command cruelty, because goodness is His nature. So the release test shows that the wrong is *necessary*, not that it is *personal*. My necessary facts pass the test exactly as your God does.
*Defense:* Correct, and it sharpens the argument. The release test was never evidence of a will that could cancel the law. Catholic teaching rejects that voluntarism. It shows that the claim is *not human*. Two things then remain to be explained. (1) The wrong is necessary, and on that both accounts are level. (2) The guilt is personal and still needs lifting: every sin is also an offense against God (CCC 1850). God cannot make cruelty right, but He can *forgive*. A fact can be true; only a person can forgive.
*Realist rejoinder:* Forgiveness by the victim is enough. When the victim is dead, the guilt is never lifted. That is tragic, not mysterious. Guilt is a fitting response to having done wrong; it needs no creditor.
*Defense:* Then the realist must say three things. Some guilt, rightly felt, can never be lifted by anyone. Guilt for wrongs against those now beyond reach (the dead, the never-born, the secret contempt that touched no one) is fitting yet directed at no one. And our experience of guilt as *owed to someone* misreads its own object in exactly the cases where it is most acute. That is coherent. It does not match the data as well as an account on which the Good that binds is also Someone who can receive what no human creditor is left to hold.
*Result:* **Held with repair, at parity plus.** The repaired W1 holds both points: necessity (shared) and forgivability (ours).

**Exchange 3: Euthyphro again.**
*Realist:* You say God *is* the Good. Is God's nature good because it is loving, just and truthful? Then the goodness of love and justice is prior, it is my set of facts, and God adds nothing. Or are love and justice good because they belong to God's nature? Then you face the counterpossible question of a cruel God, and goodness is a label for whatever God is.
*Defense:* Aquinas takes neither horn. God is not good by *having* good properties measured against a standard. He *is* Goodness itself, and creatures are good by participating in it (ST I q.6 a.4). The standard is not prior to God, and it is not a label for His will. A cruel God is no more possible than a round square: the necessity runs both ways.
*Realist rejoinder:* Then call the standard "the Good," and we disagree only about whether the Good is a person. You have renamed my facts.
*Defense:* Yes. That is precisely the question, and it should be argued, not assumed. Does the standard behave like a fact (true, inert, indifferent) or like a person (it calls us, forgives us, loves first)? A2, W2 and W3 bear on exactly that.
*Result:* **Held.** The debate narrows to the right question. The book should say plainly that this is where the disagreement lies.

**Exchange 4: parity of bruteness and the charge of extra posits.**
*Realist:* C says a God who is Love "explains all of it at one stopping point." Your stopping point is God's necessary nature, which is as brute as my necessary truths. And you posit more: a mind, a will and creative power. By Ockham's razor, mine is leaner.
*Defense:* Counting posits is not the only measure. Unification counts too. The realist stops at many separate brute necessities (pain is bad; betrayal is wrong; each human has equal worth; guilt is fitting; and so on) plus the contingent luck that minds track them (Exchange 5). The theist stops at one necessary being, by Catholic teaching simple, not composed, which also explains why there are minds and an ordered world at all. One stopping point instead of many.
*Realist rejoinder:* Divine simplicity is itself contested, and a necessary being with power and knowledge is not "one" posit in any sense that matters for parsimony.
*Defense:* Fair. Here we reach parity, not victory. The book must not claim the theist's stopping point is free. It should claim the theist's stopping point does more work.
*Result:* **Held with repair.** C's wording must change from "explains all of it" to an honest comparison of how much work each stopping point does.

**Exchange 5: W2 and the third-factor reply.**
*Realist:* W2 says that if moral facts simply exist and conscience is an evolved tool, it is "luck" that they met. No: survival and well-being are *themselves* good (a third factor). A mind shaped to pursue survival was roughly aimed at the good. General reasoning, selected for other purposes, then extended those judgments by reflection, just as it reached abstract mathematics.
*Defense:* The third factor gets you "pain is bad" and "care for your young." It does not reach the cases W2 names: mercy to an enemy, care for those who can never repay, the equal worth of the disabled. There selection pulls the other way. "Reflection did the rest" only moves the question: why is reflection reliable about matters on which survival never tested it? Enoch himself concedes that his explanation leaves some residue of luck, which he judges tolerable.
*Realist rejoinder:* Mathematics has the same problem (Field's challenge to platonism), and no one says arithmetic needs God.
*Defense:* Companions in guilt cut both ways. The Catholic tradition has always said that the intellect's fitness for necessary truths, mathematical and moral, comes from its share in the divine mind. One Maker of law and mind explains *both* reliabilities. The realist has two residues of luck; the theist has none.
*Result:* **Held with repair.** W2 must stop saying "it is luck that they ever met". The best secular answer reduces the luck and admits some remains. W2 should say so and claim the comparison.

**Exchange 6: "most surely".**
*Realist:* W2 says conscience reads the law "most surely exactly where survival pulls against it." History refutes that. Slavery, the exposure of infants and contempt for the disabled persisted for millennia, and the circle of concern widened slowly and against resistance. Conscience is *least* sure exactly there.
*Defense:* Conceded. The claim that survives is that conscience *reaches* there at all, and once it reaches there it recognizes those truths as truths, not as preferences.
*Result:* **Held with repair.** Replace "most surely" with "even."

**Exchange 7: smuggled teleology in A5, A6 and A7.**
*Realist:* A is labeled "what we cannot honestly deny." I deny three items. A5's second half ("the ought points past what we are to what we are for") is teleology; I accept the first half (Hume and Moore are my allies). A6's "always" is false: giving directions to a stranger costs nothing that matters, and teaching does not lose the teacher his knowledge. A7 ("love was meant to last") builds purpose into grief. We grieve because a good is lost, not because it was *meant* to last. A6 also does no work in W1–W3.
*Defense:* A5: conceded. The second half belongs in a witness, not among the undeniables. A6: every real good done costs at least time and attention, and the deepest goods cost the most. That is Patrick's "how reality works," stated carefully, and it can feed W2 (conscience reaching against self-interest). A7: conceded. It is a datum of longing, not a neutral fact, so it should sit inside W3, which was already reduced to a clue in v0.
*Result:* **A5 held with repair; A6 held with repair; A7 failed as an undeniable and moves to W3.**

**Exchange 8: C misdescribes the rival.**
*Realist:* C says brute moral facts "explain at most A1." False. I explain A2: guilt is the fitting response to a directed wrong, and that is a necessary truth about fitting attitudes. I explain A3: duties grounded in a person's nature, not in anyone's will, cannot be cancelled by anyone's will. I explain A4: I add the necessary truth of equal worth. I agree with A5. You have straw-manned the strongest rival.
*Defense:* There is a real distinction between *accommodating* a datum and *explaining* it. The realist can make room for each datum by adding one more necessary truth. He cannot say why they hold together, why the mind tracks them, why guilt has a creditor even when no human creditor is left, or why kind rather than capacity grounds worth. C should say that, not "explain at most A1."
*Result:* **Held with repair.** C's line on "brute moral facts" must be rewritten.

**Exchange 9: Finnis already concedes the point.**
*Realist:* Your own best natural lawyers (Grisez, Finnis) hold that the basic goods are self-evident, known without knowing God. Finnis grants that his first-order ethics does not depend on God's existence and adds God only in his closing chapter (*Natural Law and Natural Rights*, ch. XIII). So first-order ethics needs no God, by your own side's admission.
*Defense:* Agreed. The book teaches the same: the natural law is knowable by reason without knowing its Author (Rom 2:14–15; CCC 1954–1960), and unbelievers are not presumed culpable (CCC 847). The argument was never that unbelievers cannot *know* right from wrong. It is that what they know, followed to its depths, points past itself.
*Result:* **Held.** No conflict, as long as the chapter keeps the line between "recognizing" and "grounding" and does not drift into "atheists cannot be moral."

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**Against v1 as written:**
1. **W1 is invalid, and its release test tracks necessity rather than personhood.** *Serious.* Answered to parity plus by the repair below.
2. **C misstates the rival's reach ("explain at most A1").** *Serious*, because a philosophically literate reader will stop trusting the comparison at that line. Answered by the repair.
3. **A5's second half, A6's "always" and A7 are not undeniable.** *Minor to Serious*: they weaken the "what we cannot honestly deny" frame. Answered by moving them.
4. **W2's "it is luck" and "most surely" overstate the case.** *Minor.* Answered by the repair.

**After the proposed repairs: NONE at Fatal or Serious.** One *Minor* objection stands at parity, not below it. The realist can reinterpret the relational shape of guilt as a set of fitting attitudes that need no ultimate creditor, and treat the leftover cases (dead victims, wrongs that touch no one) as tragic remainders. The theist answers that his account fits those cases without remainder. Neither side refutes the other here; it is a contest of explanatory fit, and that is exactly the kind of case the version now claims to be (CCC 31). The realist's deepest residual point, that the theist's stopping point is also brute, is now openly granted in the repaired C, so it no longer counts as an objection.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact replacement wording)

**A3 (replace):**
> **A3. No one on earth can release us from the wrong itself.** The one we wronged can forgive us, and we must ask him. But for wrongs like these he cannot make it not have been wrong, and he could not have made it right by agreeing beforehand; no one can sign away his own dignity. Not a nation by vote, not the whole human race, can make torturing a child right.

**A4 (replace):**
> **A4. Every human being has the same dignity, whatever he can or cannot do.** The newborn, the dying, the severely disabled, the enemy: equal, prior to anyone's recognition (*Dignitas Infinita* 1, 15).

**A5 (replace):**
> **A5. Describing what we are does not by itself tell us what we ought to do.** We are capable of truth and lies, justice and injustice, mercy and cruelty, giving and grasping. Nothing in that list says which to choose. The "ought" comes from somewhere the description does not reach.

**A6 (replace):**
> **A6. Doing real good for another always costs the giver something, if only time and attention, and the greatest goods cost the most.** That is how reality works. And some things must never be done, not even to save ourselves.

**A7: delete from A; its content moves into W3.**

**W1 (replace):**
> **W1. Obligation has the shape of a relationship, and its last link is Someone.** When we do wrong we do not only break a rule; we wrong someone, and we need to be forgiven (A2). Usually the one we wronged is a human being, and we must go to him. But the wrong itself is fixed beyond anyone on earth's power to cancel (A3). Two accounts can make sense of that. One says the moral law is a set of necessary truths that belong to no one. The other says the Good that binds us is Someone: not a commander who picks rules, but the Creator, who made us, established what we are for, and is Himself the Good He asks of us. Even He does not make cruelty to a child right. He cannot, because He is the Good. But He can do what no truth can do: He can forgive, even when the one we wronged is gone, and He can receive the guilt that no one on earth is left to hold. Every sin wrongs a neighbor and also offends God (CCC 1850). The first account can say that guilt is fitting. Only the second can say to whom it is finally owed, and who can lift it.

**W2 (replace):**
> **W2. The law and the mind that reads it fit together.** Some moral knowledge is easy to explain by survival: care for your young, repay kindness. But conscience also reaches where survival pulls against it: truth told at a cost, mercy to an enemy, care for those who can never repay (A6). The best answer without God says that survival and well-being are themselves good, so a mind shaped to pursue them was roughly aimed at the good, and reflection did the rest. That answer is serious, and its best defenders admit that some of the fit is still good fortune. The same question hangs over the mind that reads mathematics. If one Source made both the law and the mind, the fit is not fortune but design. A law and a mind made for each other are best explained by one Maker.

**W3 (replace):**
> **W3. Communion is a clue that points to One who is Love.** Only a person can love us back; a law, a force or an idea cannot. We long to be known and loved wholly and for good, and no one on earth can do it, because no one knows the whole of us, not even ourselves. We grieve as though love was meant to last. A longing does not prove its answer. But if there is a Person who knows us wholly, can hold us forever, and loved us first (1 John 4:19), the longing makes sense; if there is not, it is a longing for nothing that is. Revelation gives His name: God is Love (1 John 4:8).

**C, third and fourth bullets (replace):**
> - **Necessary moral truths that belong to no one** are the strongest rival, and they stand with us against relativism and against matter alone. They can make room for each item in A: each can be added as one more necessary truth. But each has to be added on its own. Nothing then explains why they hold together without finally contradicting one another, why guilt is owed to someone even when no one on earth is left to forgive, why minds should read them truly (W2), or why the newborn and the dying count exactly as much as the rest of us. Without God, equal dignity tends to be grounded either in what a person can do, which is not equal, or in the kind of being a person is, which already speaks of what we are for.
> - **A God who is Love** is also a stopping point; no account avoids one. But it is one stopping point instead of many. It explains the fit between law and mind. And it is Someone, which is what the shape of guilt, forgiveness and communion suggests.

(Other C bullets and the conclusion are unchanged.)

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
1. **This school is an ally before it is an opponent.** It refutes relativism and materialism alongside us. The chapter's present line that affirming objective morality without God is "incoherent, wanting the fruit without the root" (The Atheist's Dilemma) is false of Wielenberg, Enoch and Parfit, and a literate reader will catch it. Say instead that their view is consistent but leaves the most important things unexplained.
2. **The release test proves necessity; forgiveness points to a Person.** That split is the clean form of the obligation witness, and it is fully Thomist: God cannot dispense from the Decalogue (ST I-II q.100 a.8), yet He forgives every sin.
3. **Accommodating a datum is not explaining it.** This one distinction fixes the comparison and makes it fair.
4. **Equal dignity is where godless grounds give way first.** Capacity grounds are unequal; kind grounds borrow "what we are for." That is the most persuasive single point against this school, and v1 underuses it.
5. **Lines in the current chapter that fail against this school** and that v1 should replace rather than keep alongside it:
   - "laws require lawgivers" (it equivocates between descriptive laws like gravity and prescriptive laws);
   - the paragraph on the rules-without-a-rule-giver skeptic (from "nothing stands behind his, and we may politely set it aside" through "Only a Someone can"): an unbacked ought does not thereby stop binding, and the realist agrees that no *thing* lays claims, because he holds that claims are not laid;
   - "Every time an atheist says 'That's wrong!' they're providing evidence for God's existence";
   - "that takes far more faith than belief in God ever could" (already barred by the brief);
   - "Without God, every attempt to ground objective morality fails" (asserted, not shown).

**Lines worth keeping**
- "A wrong sum needs correcting; a wronged person needs forgiveness." (from v1)
- "Even God does not make cruelty right. He cannot, because He is the Good."
- "A truth can be true. Only a person can forgive."
- "One stopping point instead of many, and it is Someone."
- "The fit is either good fortune or design."
- "A longing does not prove its answer, but it tells us what we are looking for."

## 8. Score

**v1 as written: 6/10.** Its witnesses are aimed at the right targets, but W1's gap and C's straw version of this rival would lose a careful reader of Wielenberg or Enoch. **With the proposed repairs: 8/10.** Each remaining disagreement then comes down to a fair contest of explanatory fit in which our side holds at least parity, and leads clearly on equal dignity and on the creditor of guilt.
