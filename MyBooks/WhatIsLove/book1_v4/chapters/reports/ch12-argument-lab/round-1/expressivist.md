# Round 1 — Humean Sentimentalism / Quasi-Realist Expressivism vs. v1

## 1. School and voice

The voice draws on David Hume (*Treatise* Book 3; *Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals*) and Adam Smith (*Theory of Moral Sentiments*). Its modern form comes from Simon Blackburn's quasi-realism (*Spreading the Mind*, *Essays in Quasi-Realism*, *Ruling Passions*) and Allan Gibbard's norm-expressivism (*Wise Choices, Apt Feelings*; *Thinking How to Live*). The core claims: moral judgments express attitudes, not beliefs about moral things. Reason is, in Hume's phrase, "the slave of the passions." The quasi-realist then earns, step by step, the right to the realist's words: "true," "fact," "wrong even if everyone approved," "guilty, not mistaken." He does this without adding anything to the world. He does not deny morality. He denies that morality needs a metaphysics, God's or anyone's. That makes him the opponent best placed to say that A1–A4 are his too.

## 2. The four standing challenges

**Challenge 1: starting point for justice.**
*Their answer.* Hume calls justice an "artificial" virtue. It is a convention that arises among people of limited generosity, facing moderate scarcity, because it serves everyone's interest. Property and promise-keeping are owed because the convention is useful and we come to approve of it through sympathy with the public good. The "natural" virtues are a separate matter: benevolence and gentleness come straight from sympathy. A modern quasi-realist says what anyone is owed is whatever the norms we would endorse after full information and reflection require.
*Verdict: BREAKS at the edge, and Hume says so himself.* In *Enquiry* §3, part 1, Hume imagines creatures living among us who are too weak to make us feel their resentment. He concludes they would be owed gentle treatment under "the laws of humanity," but not justice in the strict sense. He goes on to apply the same reasoning to how Europeans treated native peoples and how men treated women (paraphrased; verify the passage before quoting). A theory that ties "owed" to the power to push back does not reach the newborn, the dying or the severely disabled as a matter of justice. Modern expressivists widen the circle by choice, endorsing an extension of sympathy, and they can do this coherently. But the widening is a commitment they take on, not a debt they find. Coherent as a practice. It does not ground what anyone is owed, and the school says plainly that it does not try to.

**Challenge 2: a hard case resolved.**
*Their case.* A family asks a doctor to hide a terminal diagnosis from their mother, who keeps asking how long she has.
*Their method.* Take Hume's "general point of view," or Smith's impartial spectator: an informed, sympathetic, uninvolved observer. Ask which response such an observer would approve, and which traits are useful or agreeable to the patient and to others. The spectator feels the mother's claim to run her own last days, and resents being lied to on her behalf. He also feels the family's fear. The answer: tell her the truth, gently, at her pace, with the family present.
*Verdict: HOLDS.* The method is the school's own. It borrows nothing from the book's definitions, and it reaches nearly the book's answer of truth held together with mercy. Where it goes soft is when sympathetic observers disagree. It has no tie-breaker beyond more sympathy, more information and more conversation. To be fair, the book's tie-breaker (prudence, Ch. 11) also comes down to trained judgment. This is **parity**.

**Challenge 3: equal, unconditional dignity.**
*Their answer.* "Every human being has equal worth" voices a stance of equal respect that we demand of everyone and would defend in every possible world. Asked what makes it wrong to kill a disabled newborn, the quasi-realist gives a first-order answer, not a metaphysical one: she can be harmed, she is one of us, she is someone's child. Equality itself is a corrective norm. Hume admits that sympathy is naturally partial and graded, with the near counting more than the far. We adopt equality on purpose to correct that: from humility about weighing people's worth, as protection against abuse, and out of solidarity.
*Verdict: COHERENT, BUT IT DECLINES TO GROUND.* It does not contradict itself. It can even say "her worth is prior to our recognition" as a first-order claim. But by its own theory, saying so expresses our attitude of recognition. An unconditional *commitment* is not the same as an unconditional *worth*. And it concedes that the commitment is historically contingent (Hume's weak-creatures passage shows how easily it lapses). It neither borrows nor breaks. It openly offers less than A4 asks for.

**Challenge 4: the ultimate good for humans.**
*Their answer.* There is no single highest good. Hume's essay "The Sceptic" makes happiness plural, and Blackburn treats flourishing as whatever a well-formed sensibility would endorse. The guides are a tranquil mind, friendship, sympathy, useful work, and the quiet satisfaction of looking back on one's own conduct and approving of it. A7's longing to be wholly known and held forever is treated as real and worth honoring, but it is read as attachment that outruns its objects, not as a pointer to one.
*Verdict: COHERENT, AND SMALLER.* No break. It simply accepts that the deepest longing goes unanswered, and calls that grown-up. This leaves room for the book: the school does not explain A7 away. It concedes that A7 is a sorrow it cannot meet.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1. "Your undeniables are mine."**
*Expressivist:* I say torturing a child for fun is wrong, and would be wrong if every culture approved. What makes it wrong is the child's agony, not our disapproval. That is a first-order moral claim, and I make it from my standards about all possible worlds, including ones where people approve. I say the torturer is guilty, not mistaken. Guilt is exactly where my theory lives. Gibbard defines "wrong" as what it makes sense to feel guilty for doing and what others may resent. I affirm A4 too. So A1–A4 are no evidence for God. They are common ground.
*Patrick's side:* Granted, and it is a fair hit on the *chapter* as it stands. The chapter says that any atheist who says "That's wrong!" is appealing to a standard he cannot ground, that denying oughts refutes itself, and that the atheist must "redefine" morality. None of that touches you. You do not deny oughts and you do not redefine them. But v1 was built to survive this. Part A is meant as common ground, and the argument is the comparison in Part C. James Dreier made this point about quasi-realism: once you can say everything the realist says, the difference between you shows up only in what you take to *explain* moral thought. The realist explains it partly as a response to moral reality. You explain it entirely as sentiment and its projection. So the question was never whether you can say "wrong." It is what your saying "wrong" is about.
*Verdict: HELD WITH REPAIR.* v1 stands, but C must name this rival (see §6). The chapter's lines that assume the atheist cannot say "wrong" must go (see §7).

**Exchange 2. W1: "Owed to someone" is the victim, and nobody "lays" the claim.**
*Expressivist:* W1 says a duty is owed to someone. It is: to the child. You have found your "someone," and he is human. W1 then says a claim no human can cancel was not laid by any human. No claim is "laid" at all. That it cannot be cancelled is part of its *content*. I disapprove of torture, and I also disapprove of anyone's approving or waiving it. Blackburn calls this a staircase of attitudes about attitudes. To get from "no human can cancel it" to "a non-human laid it," you need the premise that every binding claim has an author. That was v0's missing step, and v1 still assumes it.
*Patrick's side:* Try Newman instead (*Grammar of Assent*, ch. 5). We do not experience guilt as a broken rule. We feel answerable, ashamed and afraid as before someone. On that basis Newman argued there must be "One to whom we are responsible" (verify the wording before quoting).
*Expressivist:* Adam Smith explained that two centuries ago. Conscience is the impartial spectator, "the man within the breast": an imagined judge we build out of every actual judge we have faced. It feels like a Person because it is modeled on persons.
*Patrick's side:* Then your own best model of conscience is a Someone above every actual human, impartial, all-seeing, and impossible to bribe. Smith himself noted that when this inner judge is shaken, people appeal to a still higher tribunal, the all-seeing Judge of the world (TMS Part III, ch. 2; verify the location before citing). The question is not whether conscience pictures a Judge. Both sides agree it does. The question is whether the Judge is only pictured. Put that as an inference to the best explanation, not a deduction, and it stands. A conscience that answers to Someone is best explained by there being Someone to answer to, and especially so given the rest of the case (W2, W3).
*Verdict: W1 as a deduction FAILED. W1 as an inference to the best explanation HELD WITH REPAIR.* The repaired W1 is in §6.

**Exchange 3. A3 says two different things.**
*Expressivist:* A3 says "No one on earth can release us." But A2 says we need forgiveness, and victims can forgive. Your own Church commands them to. So if "release" means *forgive*, A3 is false. If it means *make the act not wrong*, God cannot do that either. On your own view (Ch. 3), He cannot make torturing children good. Either way A3 does not show that release is reserved for someone above every human. It runs together waiving a wrong in advance with forgiving it afterward.
*Patrick's side:* The objection is right about the wording. What A3 is really after are two separate facts. First, no one on earth can make the act *not wrong*: not the victim beforehand, not a vote, not the race. Second, some wrongs reach past every human who could forgive them. The murdered child is not there to forgive her killer, and a wrong against the innocent seems to wound something more than the one wounded.
*Expressivist:* The first is my staircase again. The second is true, and tragic: then no one can forgive. A felt need for a pardon is not evidence that a pardoner exists, any more than the longing in A7 is.
*Patrick's side:* Agreed that it is no proof. As a fact that needs explaining, though, it counts. On your account, the guilty man whose victim is dead must either carry apt guilt forever with nowhere to lay it down, or let it fade as his attitudes drift. Ordinary moral experience rejects both: real pardon is possible, but only from someone with standing to give it. That counts for the theistic explanation. It does not decide the question.
*Verdict: A3 as written FAILED (equivocation). Repaired A3 HELD.* It counts as a clue weighed with the others, at parity at the level of proof.

**Exchange 4. Our counterattack: Hume's sensible knave.**
*Patrick's side:* Hume himself, at the end of the *Enquiry*, imagines the "sensible knave." This man keeps the rules in general but breaks them whenever he can do so safely and profitably. Hume admits that if the knave's heart does not rebel, and he wants an argument, it will be hard to find one that seems satisfying to him (paraphrased; the passage is public domain, so quoting it is safe). A6 says some things must never be done, even to save oneself. On your view, what is the knave failing to see?
*Expressivist:* Nothing he is failing to see. He is failing to *care*, and I condemn him for it. That is first-order and fully meant. Your God does no better. Either you threaten him with hell, which only appeals to his self-interest, or you invite him to love, which moves him only if he already cares. No argument converts a determined knave, yours included.
*Patrick's side:* We are not claiming an argument that compels him. The difference is what makes your condemnation true. On your view, a fully informed, fully consistent knave is wrong only in that we are against him. On ours, he is acting against what he is *for*. He is making himself less than the creature he was made to be, and trading the Good his whole nature seeks for a lesser good (Ch. 1). For us the moral law and real happiness meet in the end. For you they can come apart for good, and in the knave's case they do.
*Expressivist:* "They meet in the end" is a claim about the afterlife, not something we observe.
*Patrick's side:* It is part of what one account can explain and the other must leave unexplained: why A6 binds even when it costs, and why the person who keeps it at a cost is not a fool. That belongs in C, as a comparison of what each account explains, not as a premise.
*Verdict: HELD WITH REPAIR.* Add it to C as an explanatory gap. Do not claim it compels the knave.

**Exchange 5. Our counterattack: equal dignity.**
*Patrick's side:* Your founder tied strict justice to the power to resent. Sympathy is graded, as Hume admits. Equality, on your account, is a policy adopted to correct that, and history shows how easily it lapses. A4 says equal and *prior to anyone's recognition*. You can say those words. But by your own theory, saying them expresses our recognition.
*Expressivist:* You confuse the first-order claim with the explanation of why we make it. "Her worth does not depend on our recognizing it" is a moral claim, and I hold it unconditionally. If everyone stopped recognizing her worth, she would still be wronged. I say that from where I stand, and where you stand is the only place anyone ever stands.
*Patrick's side:* That last clause is the whole disagreement. We hold that there is a place none of us stands from which we are judged, and that it is the only thing that could make "prior to recognition" more than a strongly held stance. A commitment can be unconditional. Only a worth can be prior to it.
*Expressivist:* And when you say God's love gives her worth, that too is said from where *you* stand.
*Verdict: HELD AT PARITY.* The expressivist is coherent and has not contradicted himself. Theism better explains the plain meaning of "prior to anyone's recognition," the meaning most people intend when they say it. That is an explanatory advantage, not a refutation. The book should claim exactly that much.

**Exchange 6. Companions in guilt: "God is the Good" is your attitude.**
*Expressivist:* You say goodness is God's nature (Ch. 3). Calling that nature *good* is itself a moral judgment. On my analysis it expresses your approval of it. So you stop exactly where I stop, at a sensibility endorsing itself. You have only added a being.
*Patrick's side:* This mixes up how we *know* with what *makes it so*. Our knowledge of the Good always starts from inside a conscience, which we never claimed to leave. The claim concerns what makes the judgment true when it is true: our sensibility responds to Goodness rather than producing it. Your theory says moral judgment responds to nothing. That is not a stopping point we share. It is the very thing we disagree about. And your position has costs of its own. Expressivism still has to explain how moral sentences work inside "if... then" clauses and arguments. That is the Frege-Geach problem, and Mark Schroeder's *Being For* argues the best expressivist solutions carry heavy commitments. Your "minimal" truth comes with a semantic theory that is far from settled.
*Verdict: HELD.* Do not put Frege-Geach in the book. It is too technical, and it is not what the chapter needs. But it means "you add a being" is answered by "you add a contested semantics."

**Exchange 7. Projection, and W2.**
*Expressivist:* Hume says taste "gilds or stains" natural objects with colors borrowed from inward feeling. That is why wrongness seems to sit in the act. W2 says it is luck that moral facts and an evolved conscience ever met. On my view there is nothing for them to meet. Conscience does not read a law; it produces the standard. And conscience reaching beyond the needs of survival (mercy to enemies, care for those who can never repay) is just sympathy generalizing past the setting it evolved in. Hume's extensive sympathy, corrected by the general point of view, explains it.
*Patrick's side:* W2 was aimed at the realist who says moral facts simply exist, and against him it bites. Against you, the honest point is narrower. Moral history includes whole sensibilities being found *wrong*, not merely inconsistent: the slaveholder converted, the eugenicist who came to see. It looks like discovery.
*Expressivist:* Improvement by our own lights: more information, more consistency, more imagination. Blackburn's improvable sensibility.
*Verdict: W2 FAILED AS AIMED (it misses this school) and HELD against the realist only.* The moral-progress point against the expressivist is at parity. W2 needs a one-line scope note, not a rewrite.

**Exchange 8. A5: what we are "for."**
*Expressivist:* A5's first sentence is Hume's is/ought gap. Thank you. Its last clause says the ought "points past what we are to what we are for." I agree completely: the ought lives in what we are *for*, meaning what we are in favor of. Schroeder's book on my school is literally called *Being For*. If you mean a purpose built into nature, that is not something nobody can deny. Hume denied it, and so do I.
*Patrick's side:* Fair. "What we are for" means purpose, not preference, and that claim belongs in W, where it is argued, not in A, where it is assumed.
*Verdict: HELD WITH REPAIR.* Keep A5 to the is/ought fact and move "what we are for" into W1.

## 5. Remaining sound objections (against v1 as written)

1. **W1's inference is invalid against this school.** "No human can cancel it, so no human laid it" needs the premise that every binding claim has an author, and the quasi-realist reasonably denies it. *Serious.* It is fully answered by the repaired W1 below, which moves from deduction to inference to the best explanation.
2. **A3 equivocates.** Victims *can* forgive, and God cannot make wrong into right. *Serious.* It is fully answered by the repaired A3.
3. **C omits the expressivist rival.** So "Matter alone... not why anything binds" is open to the reply that binding is explained as the demand we hold to apply regardless. *Serious.* It is answered by adding the "Sentiment alone" line.
4. **A5's "what we are for" is assumed, not shown.** *Minor.* Move it into W1.

**After the repairs: NONE remaining at Fatal or Serious.** This school cannot be refuted, and the book must not claim it can. The quasi-realist stays coherent at every step. What our side reaches is *parity or better at every point, and a real explanatory advantage on four*. First, the plain sense of "prior to anyone's recognition" (A4). Second, the sensible knave (A6). Third, the wrong no living person can forgive (A3). Fourth, the fact that even the sentimentalist's own best model of conscience is a Judge above every human (W1). In a converging case (CCC 31), that is what reason's witness is supposed to look like.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**A3 (replace):**
> **A3. No one on earth can make such a wrong not wrong.** Not the victim beforehand (he cannot sign away his own dignity), not a nation by vote, not the whole human race. A victim can forgive, and that forgiveness is real. But some wrongs outrun every human who could forgive them: the murdered child is not there to pardon her killer.

**A5 (replace):**
> **A5. Human nature does not settle what we ought to do.** We are capable of truth and lies, justice and injustice, mercy and cruelty, giving and grasping. Describing what we are never by itself tells us which to choose.

**W1 (replace):**
> **W1. Conscience answers to Someone.** When we have done wrong, we do not feel merely that a rule was broken. We feel answerable, ashamed, in need of pardon, as one does before a person (Newman). Even those who say conscience is only feeling picture it as a judge: impartial, unbribable, standing above every human we know (Adam Smith's "impartial spectator"). No one on earth can make the wrong not wrong, and some wrongs outrun every human who could forgive them (A2, A3). So either the Judge conscience pictures is only imagined, or there is Someone to answer to. The second explains more: not a commander who picks rules, but the Creator, who made us, established what we are for and not merely what we happen to favor, and is Himself the Good He asks of us.

**W2 (append one sentence):**
> This answers those who say moral facts simply exist. Those who say moral words only voice our feelings need no such meeting, and for them the question is the one in C.

**C (insert after "Matter alone"):**
> - **Sentiment alone** (moral words voice our attitudes, refined by sympathy and reflection) can say everything in A1–A4 and explain why we say it. What it cannot say, except from inside the very sentiments in question, is that our sentiments are the right ones. It leaves equal dignity a commitment rather than a worth (A4), the "sensible knave" wrong only because we are against him (A6), the wrong no living person can forgive with nowhere to go (A3), and the Judge conscience pictures with no one there (W1).

**C, "Matter alone" (replace):**
> - **Matter alone** explains why we feel moral pressure, but on its own terms only as pressure: not as a claim anyone is truly answerable to (A1–A3).

**C, "A God who is Love" (replace):**
> - **A God who is Love** explains more of it than any rival, and explains it at one stopping point.

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **The chapter currently argues against an atheist who no longer exists in the literature.** Four passages fail against quasi-realism and should be revised when the new argument goes in. First, "Every time an atheist says 'That's wrong!'... appealing to an objective moral standard they can't ground." Second, the Atheist's Dilemma option (1), where denying oughts refutes itself; the expressivist does not deny oughts. Third, option (3), "redefine morality"; he does not redefine it. Fourth, "no *thing* has ever laid a claim on anyone. Only a Someone can." He agrees, and names us as the someones. The honest replacement point: *everyone can say "wrong." The question is what the word is about.*
- **Adam Smith is the bridge.** The father of sentimental ethics built conscience as an imagined judge above every human, and, per his own text (verify before citing), noted that people appeal past it to a still higher tribunal. That is a fair, non-polemical way to make Newman's point to a modern reader.
- **Hume's own two admissions are honest exhibits for the book, used gently.** One: justice in the strict sense is not owed to those too weak to make us feel their resentment (Enquiry §3). Two: the sensible knave has no answer that will satisfy him (Enquiry, Conclusion). Both are public domain, and both show the best godless account conceding exactly what A4 and A6 claim. Present them as honesty to admire, not as gotchas.
- **A sharper wording for the "ought" (A5):** "what we are for," not "what we are in favor of." Keep the distinction explicit, because the expressivist will happily accept the ambiguous version.
- **Do not overclaim against this school.** It is coherent. The chapter's claim should be "explains more," never "cannot say." This fits CCC 31 exactly.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "Everyone can say 'wrong.' The question is what we are saying it about."
- "Even the man who says conscience is only feeling ends up picturing a judge."
- "A commitment can be unconditional. Only a worth can be prior to it."
- "When the victim is gone, who is left to forgive?"
- "We do not claim to stand outside our conscience. We claim our conscience is answering Someone."

## 8. Score

**v1 as written: 5/10.** **v1 with the repairs above: 7/10.** The quasi-realist can affirm every item in Part A and breaks W1's inference as written. Once A3 is split, W1 is recast as an inference to the best explanation and C names "sentiment alone," every remaining exchange ends at parity or with a clear explanatory advantage for our side. That is what a converging case needs. It does not refute this school, and nothing will.
