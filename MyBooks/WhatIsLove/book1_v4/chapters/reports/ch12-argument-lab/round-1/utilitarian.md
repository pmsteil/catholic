# Argument Lab, Round 1: Utilitarianism / Consequentialism vs. v1

## 1. School and voice

The opponent argues as the realist, impartialist wing of utilitarianism at full strength: Henry Sidgwick's *Methods of Ethics*, Mill on justice and the "internal sanction" of conscience, R. M. Hare's two-level theory, Derek Parfit's non-naturalism, and above all Katarzyna de Lazari-Radek and Peter Singer's *The Point of View of the Universe* (2014), which defends an *objective* hedonistic utilitarianism grasped by reason. This is not the "outcome-calculation that only explains feelings" that Chapter 12 currently describes. It is a moral realist who says suffering is really bad, impartial benevolence is a self-evident truth of reason, and no God is needed. Its weak point is just as clear: it gives everyone equal *consideration* and still makes every individual tradeable.

---

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**Challenge 1: Starting point for justice; what is anyone owed?**
*Their answer:* Equal consideration of interests: each person's well-being counts the same as anyone else's ("everybody to count for one, nobody for more than one," the maxim Mill credits to Bentham). Mill: "justice" is our name for the rules that protect the most vital parts of well-being, such as security, keeping faith and not harming. We feel them as stricter than other duties because so much depends on them. Rights are rules whose general keeping does the most good.
*Verdict:* **Coherent, but "owed" breaks.** In this view no one is owed anything *in his own right*. What he is "owed" is whatever the best rule assigns him, and when the totals change, so does his claim. That is why the scapegoat case is so hard for it: hanging one innocent man to stop a riot that would kill many. Indirect (rule or two-level) versions usually forbid this, but only because the practice works badly in general. Once the separate person is gone (Rawls's and Nozick's criticism), justice can be derived but not *owed*.

**Challenge 2: A concrete hard case, resolved without borrowing the book's facets.**
*Their answer:* Allocating scarce ICU beds and ventilators in a pandemic. Maximize expected life-years saved, with priority for those most likely to survive. Use transparent rules, not bedside judgments, because trust in hospitals is itself a large part of well-being. For the ticking-bomb torture case, a two-level utilitarian keeps a firm practical rule against torture. Real cases are never clean, and institutions that allow exceptions abuse them. In theory, he admits, a truly clean case would permit it.
*Verdict:* **Holds as a decision procedure; it borrows the facets only as tools.** In fairness, it does well on triage, and Catholic triage ethics (proportionality, expected benefit) often reaches the same answers. It re-derives truth (trust), justice (security rules), mercy (punishment beyond deterrence is pure harm, so utilitarians were early and real opponents of cruel punishment) and self-gift (Singer's demand for heavy giving). It does not need the book's definitions. It **breaks** where the book treats a facet as non-negotiable: it must in principle allow the clean-case torture, the scapegoat and the transplant surgeon. It handles the hard case well and fails the limit case.

**Challenge 3: What grounds equal, unconditional dignity?**
*Their answer (honest, and Singer's own):* Nothing does, and we deny it. Equal *consideration* of equal interests is the principle. "Equal dignity of all humans" is either species prejudice or a useful social rule. Moral status depends on capacities: sentience for having interests at all; self-awareness and preferences about one's own future for being a "person" with a strong interest in going on living. On this view a newborn is not yet a person. Some severely disabled humans may never be. Some animals are persons. The enemy, by contrast, gets full equal weight, which is admirable.
*Verdict:* **It breaks against A4, openly and without borrowing.** Its credit is the newborn's pain, the dying man's pain and the enemy's pain all count, fully. What it cannot give is *inviolability*: a dignity no total can outweigh. Some utilitarians defend a firm rule against killing any human because it does the most good (security, slippery slopes), but that protection is only contingent. This is the clearest case in the lab of an opponent who *denies* the datum rather than grounding it.

**Challenge 4: The ultimate human good; guiding principles.**
*Their answer:* Well-being. For Sidgwick, de Lazari-Radek and Singer, that means pleasant conscious states (hedonism). For Hare and the earlier Singer it means satisfied preferences. Parfit and others treat an "objective list" as a serious option. The guiding principle is to bring about the most well-being, counted impartially, from "the point of view of the universe" (Sidgwick's phrase).
*Verdict:* **Coherent for those who bite the bullet; it breaks for most people.** Nozick's experience machine (a lifetime of perfect simulated happiness) is refused by most people, because they want *real* friends, *real* truth and *real* achievement, which is the book's communion. Mill's "higher pleasures" already slide toward Aristotle, which means borrowing. The person never appears as an end, only as a place where well-being happens. Singer's "replaceability" argument for beings judged not self-aware states this directly: lose one, make another equally happy, and nothing is lost. Love says otherwise (A7).

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1: A1 does not favor God**
*Utilitarian:* A1 is ours. Torturing a child for fun is objectively wrong, wrong even if every culture approved, because the child's agony is bad from the point of view of the universe. You add nothing by adding God. Worse, you seem to make it wrong for some reason beyond the child's pain, as if the pain were not enough.
*Defense:* Agreed: the child's agony is *why* it is wrong. The book does not say God's command is the wrong-making feature (Ch. 3 rejects voluntarism). The question is what the "point of view of the universe" is. The universe has no point of view. A standpoint that is impartial, complete, aware of every interest and cares about each is not a feature of matter. It is a description of a mind. Your best idea is a picture of God with the Person removed.
*Rejoinder:* It is an idealization, like a frictionless plane. Reasons are agent-neutral facts. No one needs to occupy the standpoint.
*Result:* **Held with repair.** A1 holds, but it does not discriminate: realist utilitarians grant it in full. The "point of view" observation is a strong clue, not a proof. The real contest lies in A2–A4, A6 and W1–W2.

**Exchange 2: "What is owed is owed to someone" (A2/W1)**
*Utilitarian:* W1 assumes its key step. For Sidgwick, "ought" is a dictate of reason, not a debt to a creditor. You ought to reduce suffering, full stop. No claimant, so no Claimant.
*Defense:* Then you cannot tell *making the world worse* apart from *wronging a person*. Take a deathbed promise. I swear to my dying friend to scatter her ashes at sea. Later I bury them in a field because it is cheaper, and no one ever knows. No one's well-being changes. Your theory says nothing wrong happened. Nearly everyone says she was wronged. Guilt has the same shape: we feel guilty *toward her*, not toward a total. A2 records that structure, and utilitarianism has no slot for it.
*Rejoinder:* At the practical level we *should* feel she was wronged, because promise-keeping dispositions do great good. At the critical level, nothing bad happened. Your "wronging" is a useful sentiment.
*Defense:* Then the sentiment is useful because it is false, and you are asking us to keep believing what you think untrue. And notice what the theist can say that you cannot: the dead friend is not gone to God. There is still Someone to whom the promise answers.
*Result:* **Held.** The worse/wronged distinction is the book's sharpest tool against this school. But W1 currently *asserts* "what is owed is owed to someone" when it must *argue* it. Repair below.

**Exchange 3: A3 cuts both ways**
*Utilitarian:* "No one on earth can release us." Of course not, because wrongness is not a debt anyone holds. But by your own Chapter 3, God cannot make torture-for-fun right either; goodness is His nature, not His choice. So A3 shows that wrongness is independent of *every* will, divine included. It is evidence for brute moral fact, not for a Releaser.
*Defense:* Two things are being run together. (a) *Making the act not wrong*: no one can, God included, and the book should say so plainly; this is Ch. 3. (b) *Forgiving the one who did it*: the victim really can forgive the injury to him. Yet our guilt outruns any victim's power. He cannot forgive what I did to others. The dead cannot forgive at all. Even a forgiving victim leaves the wrongdoer knowing he offended against something greater than one man. Catholic teaching names that "more": sin offends God (CCC 1849–1850). God does not make wrong into right. He forgives.
*Rejoinder:* That "more" is just fitting remorse at having made things worse. It calls for reform and repair, not for a forgiver.
*Result:* **Fails as worded; holds with repair.** "No one can release us" invites the obvious reply "and no one in heaven either." Split A3 into "no vote can make it right" (which both sides grant) and "guilt outruns every human power to forgive" (the theist's datum). Then let W1 argue, as an inference to the best explanation, that a debt with no human creditor big enough points to One who is.

**Exchange 4: A4 is honestly denied**
*Utilitarian:* Your list is titled "what we cannot honestly deny." I deny A4, and I am honest. Moral status follows morally relevant capacities. "Human" is a biological category, and resting dignity on it is speciesism. If you mean rational nature, an anencephalic infant lacks it and an adult chimpanzee has more of its precursors.
*Defense:* Two replies. First, the capacity view cannot keep *equality* among the people it does count. If status follows capacities, it comes in degrees, so the brilliant count for more than the dull, and those asleep, in a coma or drunk drop out. Its threshold for "person" is set where it is set, not found. The Catholic view (Spaemann's *Persons*; Lee and George) ties personhood to the *kind of being* one is: a living human individual with a rational nature, whether or not its powers are yet or still in use. That is all-or-nothing, so it is equal. Second, your utility calculus has its own inequality: Nozick's "utility monster," a being who gets enormously more pleasure from resources than anyone else, would be owed nearly everything.
*Rejoinder:* The utility monster is fanciful. Diminishing returns make real-world utilitarianism strongly egalitarian. And "kind membership" is just as arbitrary: why should what your kind *typically* can do decide what *you* are owed?
*Defense:* Because it is not what your kind typically *does*. It is what *you are*: the same living individual who will think, or once thought, or would think if not for injury. The disabled child is not a failed chimpanzee. She is a human being who is wounded.
*Result:* **Held with repair.** The defense is at least at parity. But "cannot honestly deny" is false of A4: Singer denies it honestly. A4 should be presented as what nearly everyone sees and what the Church says reason can see (DI 1), with the honest denial named as the price of the rival view. A4 must also say *untradeable*, not just *equal*, because equal consideration is exactly what utilitarianism gives.

**Exchange 5: A6, cost and constraints**
*Utilitarian:* A6a is either false or trivial. It is false if "cost" means loss: teaching a friend calculus leaves me knowing just as much, and a smile costs nothing. Your own Trinity gives "without loss." It is trivial if it means opportunity cost. Every finite act uses time, scarcity explains that, and it points nowhere. Besides, we demand more sacrifice than you do: Singer's drowning child says you must give until it hurts. A6b ("must never be done") we deny. Two-level theory explains why "never" works as a rule of thumb, and the paradox of constraints shows "never" is irrational: if murder is so bad, why not commit one murder to prevent five?
*Defense:* On A6a: the book means that finite creatures cannot give without giving up something of themselves, such as time, attention, comfort, and sometimes life. In God self-gift is total and without loss; cost is what self-gift becomes in a finite, fallen world. Stated that way it is true and consistent with the Trinity. And your demand for heavy giving supports the book: even the most secular ethic of our day finds that the good asks sacrifice. On A6b: the constraint concerns what *I* do. Choosing to kill an innocent makes my will a murderer's will. Acts shape the one who acts (*Veritatis Splendor* 71, 80). "One may never do evil so that good may result from it" (CCC 1789; cf. Rom 3:8). This is not only Christian: Socrates argued that it is better to suffer wrong than to do it. And the transplant surgeon, who kills one healthy patient to give his organs to five, is refused by nearly every conscience.
*Rejoinder:* That is concern for your own clean hands at the cost of four extra deaths. A strong intuition is still an intuition.
*Result:* **A6a held with repair; A6b held at parity.** Neither side can compel the other on constraints. But like A4, A6b is honestly denied, so it belongs with "what conscience nearly everywhere testifies," with the price of denial shown. "Even to save oneself" misses this opponent, who also forbids favoring oneself. The real contested clause is "or to buy a better outcome for many."

**Exchange 6: W2, the debunking asymmetry**
*Utilitarian:* We accept your data in W2. Indeed de Lazari-Radek and Singer build on it. Evolution would favor kin-preference and reciprocity, never impartial benevolence toward strangers who can never repay. So impartial benevolence is the one moral conviction that *survives* evolutionary debunking. We grasp it with reason, a general power that evolution built for other jobs and that, as a by-product, sees necessary truths, just as it sees mathematics. No God required. And W2's "a law that is one"? Ours is one principle. Monism is our strength, not yours.
*Defense:* The by-product story moves the coincidence; it does not remove it. Why should a mind assembled for survival be fitted to see truths that survival never needed, in morals or in mathematics? "By-product" names the fit; it does not explain it. One Source who made both the law and the mind explains the fit in both fields, which is why the chapter already pairs them. On unity: utilitarianism gets one principle by turning truth, justice and mercy into tools, and its famous crises (the useful lie, the scapegoat, the transplant) come exactly where a tool is put down. The book's claim is unity *without* reduction.
*Rejoinder:* "Followed to their depths, they never finally contradict" cannot be falsified. Every apparent conflict gets sent to "the depths."
*Defense:* Fair. The claim must be tied to cases (for example, Chapters 6–9; CCC 2488–2489 on truth: you never lie, but you do not owe the truth to someone who would use it to kill).
*Result:* **Held with repair.** W2 must name and answer the "general reason" rival, and state the unity claim modestly, with cases.

**Exchange 7: Sidgwick's own admission (on C)**
*Utilitarian:* Your section C says brute moral facts "explain at most A1." Unfair. Our one axiom plus one value explains A1, A3's first half (no vote changes the badness of suffering), all of A5 (Sidgwick was the classic defender of the is/ought gap), A6a (scarcity) and part of W2. And it stops at one axiom. That is simpler than a Trinity.
*Defense:* Granted, the line is unfair and should be corrected. But consider how your tradition's greatest thinker ended. At the close of *The Methods of Ethics*, Sidgwick admitted that reason seemed to endorse both rational self-interest and impartial benevolence, and that without some assurance that the two coincide (he thought this required something like a moral government of the universe), practical reason stayed divided against itself (paraphrase). De Lazari-Radek and Singer try to close the gap by debunking egoism. The founder thought the problem pointed upward. On simplicity, divine simplicity is the book's teaching: God is not a collection of parts. And an axiom explains no reasoner, no wronging and no communion. A personal Good explains all three.
*Rejoinder:* We have resolved Sidgwick's dualism without God, and an appeal to the founder's doubts is not an argument.
*Result:* **Held with repair.** On simplicity the two are roughly at parity. Theism wins on *scope* (wronging, guilt beyond any victim, untradeable dignity, communion), and C must say so fairly, with a separate line for the well-being view.

**Exchange 8 (brief): W3 and replaceability**
*Utilitarian:* A7's longing for complete love is a psychological fact, not evidence of anything.
*Defense:* Then look at what your theory does with the beloved. If the person is a place where well-being happens, one person can in principle be swapped for another equally happy one. Singer's replaceability argument says exactly this for beings judged not self-aware. Every lover knows the opposite: no one can stand in for *this* one. A7 is the datum utilitarianism cannot even describe.
*Result:* **Held.** This is support for W3, not a proof.

## 4. Ledger for v1

- **A1** (some things really wrong, for everyone): **Holds.** Realist utilitarians grant it fully; it does not by itself favor God.
- **A2** (guilty, not merely mistaken; wronged someone): **Holds with repair.** It is the strongest datum against this school (worse vs. wronged), but a bullet-biting utilitarian honestly denies "wronged someone" in no-effect cases. Give the deathbed-promise case.
- **A3** (no one can release us): **Fails as worded.** It invites "not God either" (Ch. 3). It holds once split into "no vote can make it right" and "guilt outruns every human power to forgive."
- **A4** (equal dignity): **Holds with repair.** Leading utilitarians honestly deny it, so it cannot sit under "cannot honestly deny" without a note. It must say *untradeable*, not just equal.
- **A5** (nature does not settle the ought): **Holds with repair.** The first half is granted in full (Sidgwick). "Points … to what we are for" is the book's thesis, not a shared datum; mark it as such.
- **A6** (good costs the giver; some things never done): **Holds with repair.** Restate cost as finitude, consistent with the Trinity's self-gift without loss. The constraint is honestly denied; aim it at "to buy a better outcome for many," give the transplant case, and cite CCC 1789.
- **A7** (long to be known and loved wholly; love refuses death): **Holds.** Utilitarian replaceability is a contrast that strengthens it.
- **W1** (obligation points to a Lawgiver): **Holds with repair.** "What is owed is owed to someone" is asserted when it must be argued. Add worse vs. wronged, and add that God forgives rather than makes wrong right.
- **W2** (law and mind, one Source): **Holds with repair.** It must answer "general reason as a by-product" (it moves the coincidence) and state unity without reduction, with cases.
- **W3** (communion points to One who is Love): **Holds** against this school. Add the replaceability contrast.
- **C, Matter alone:** **Holds** (not this school's line).
- **C, Human flourishing alone:** **Holds**, but it does not cover utilitarianism, which is about well-being, not flourishing, and *does* count those who cannot flourish. A separate line is needed.
- **C, Brute moral facts "explain at most A1":** **Fails as worded.** It is unfair; they explain A1, the first half of A3, A5 and A6a. Correct it.
- **C, A God who is Love explains all at one stopping point:** **Holds with repair.** Parsimony is roughly at parity; claim *scope*, not simplicity alone.
- **C, Other side of the scale:** **Holds.** It is honest. The utilitarian presses evil hard (a world of this much suffering), and Ch. 1 and CCC 309 are the right pointers.
- **C, Conclusion:** **Holds.** It is properly modest (CCC 31, 35).

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**Against v1 as written:**
1. **A3 is symmetric** ("not God either"). As worded, A3 supports impersonal moral fact as much as a Lawgiver. **Serious.**
2. **A4 and A6b are listed as undeniable, but leading utilitarians honestly deny them.** This is an honesty error in the frame, and it hands the opponent an easy point. **Serious.**
3. **W1 asserts that every duty is owed to a person.** The agent-neutral "ought" of Sidgwick and Parfit is the strongest rival, and v1 never meets it. **Serious.**
4. **C's line on brute facts understates the rival** ("at most A1"). **Serious** for credibility with a philosophy-trained reader.
5. **W2 leaves the "reason as by-product" rival unanswered, and its unity claim cannot be falsified.** **Minor-to-Serious.**
6. **A6a as worded clashes with the Trinity's self-gift "without loss"** and does not discriminate between views. **Minor.**
7. **A5 slips the book's thesis ("what we are for") into a shared datum.** **Minor.**

**After the repairs in §6: NONE that cannot be answered at least to parity.** What remains is a *standoff*, not a defeat. A utilitarian willing to deny untradeable dignity, absolute constraints and the difference between wronging and worsening has a coherent position. No converging case can force him out of it. It can only show what each denial costs, and the costs are high: the newborn's standing, the transplant surgeon, the deathbed promise, the beloved who can be replaced. That is exactly the honest scope of CCC 31 ("converging and convincing," not compelling). None of the objections is Fatal.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**Add after the A list (one sentence):**
> Almost no one denies A1, A5 and A7. A2, A3, A4 and A6 some serious thinkers do deny, and honestly; the comparison below shows what each denial costs.

**A3 (replace):**
> **A3. No one on earth can make it right, and no one on earth can fully release us.** No vote of a nation, or of the whole human race, can make such an act right. The victim can forgive what was done to him, and his forgiveness is real; but it does not reach the whole of our guilt. He cannot forgive what was done to others, the dead cannot forgive at all, and even when he forgives we know we have offended against something greater than any one person. (Not even God makes such an act right; goodness is His nature, not His choice, Chapter 3. What God does is forgive.)

**A4 (replace):**
> **A4. Every human being has the same dignity, and it cannot be traded.** The newborn, the dying, the severely disabled, the enemy: equal, prior to anyone's recognition (*Dignitas Infinita* 1, 15). Not only equal in having their pain counted, but equal in this: no one may be killed or used up to make the totals come out better. Some honest thinkers deny this and tie a person's worth to what he can do now; nearly everyone else sees it, and the Church teaches that reason alone can see it (*Dignitas Infinita* 1).

**A5 (replace the last sentence):**
> Describing what we are never by itself tells us which to choose. So the "ought" must come from somewhere other than the description; this book's answer, argued below, is what we are *for*.

**A6 (replace):**
> **A6. Real good for another costs the giver something, and some things must never be done.** Finite creatures cannot give without giving up something of themselves: time, attention, comfort, sometimes life. That is how reality works for us. (In God, self-gift is total and without loss; cost is what self-gift becomes in a finite and fallen world.) And some things must never be done, not to save oneself and not to buy a better outcome for many: a surgeon may not kill one healthy patient to give his organs to five. "One may never do evil so that good may result from it" (CCC 1789; cf. Romans 3:8).

**W1 (replace):**
> **W1. Obligation points to a Lawgiver.** Making the world worse and wronging someone are not the same thing. If I promise my dying friend to scatter her ashes at sea and quietly bury them in a field to save money, no one is ever the worse for it, and still she was wronged. Guilt answers to persons: we are guilty *toward* someone, not toward a total (A2). And the guilt we carry outruns every human power to forgive it (A3). The best explanation is that the claim of the good on us is laid by Someone above every human being: not a commander who picks rules, but the Creator, who made us, established what we are for, and is Himself the Good He asks of us. He does not make wrong acts right. He forgives the one who did them.

**W2 (add before its last sentence, and soften the unity clause):**
> Some answer that reason, a general power evolution built for other jobs, simply sees moral truth the way it sees mathematics. That moves the coincidence; it does not remove it: why should a mind built for survival be fitted to read truths survival never needed? And truth, justice, mercy and self-gift, followed to their depths, do not finally contradict; they hold together without any one of them becoming a mere tool of another, as the cases in Chapters 6–9 show.

**C (add a line after "Human flourishing alone"):**
> **Well-being, counted impartially,** explains why everyone's suffering matters equally and why no vote can make cruelty right (A1, A3), but not wronging and guilt toward a person (A2), not a dignity that cannot be traded (A4), not acts that must never be done (A6), and not why no one can take the place of the one we love (A7).

**C (replace the brute-facts line):**
> **Brute moral facts** explain wrongness that no vote can change (A1, A3) and the gap between what we are and what we ought to do (A5); they leave guilt toward a person, untradeable dignity, how we came to know any of it (W2), and communion unexplained.

**C (replace the God line):**
> **A God who is Love** explains all of it at one stopping point. Rival accounts may stop at fewer words, but they explain less.

**Chapter text (Premise 1 paragraph), suggested fix:** the chapter files utilitarianism under "non-moral explanations" that "explain why we *feel* moral pressure." Realist utilitarians will rightly object. Suggested replacement for the utilitarianism clause:
> Utilitarianism is different: it takes morality seriously and insists that suffering really matters. But it makes every duty a line in a ledger, and anything in a ledger can be outweighed, even an innocent life, if the totals require it. That is exactly the "everything is negotiable" St. John Paul II warned of.

## 7. New insights for the book

- **Worse vs. wronged** is the book's sharpest blade against consequentialism, and it is plain enough for any reader. Only wronging needs forgiveness, and forgiveness needs a person.
- **"The point of view of the universe"** is utilitarianism's own best image, and it describes a mind. The book can honor the image and ask who holds it.
- **Equal consideration is not equal dignity.** Utilitarianism does count the newborn's and the enemy's pain fully, and the book should say so. What it cannot give is a worth that may not be spent.
- **Singer's honesty is a gift to the argument.** He shows exactly what follows when dignity has no ground beyond capacities. The book should thank him for his candor rather than caricature him.
- **Even the most secular ethic of our day demands sacrifice** (giving until it hurts). That supports A6 from an unexpected side.
- **Sidgwick, the greatest utilitarian, ended his great book admitting that reason without something like God stays divided** between duty and self-interest. That is a fair, non-polemical point for the book's honest-comparison section.
- **Replaceability vs. love:** a theory that can in principle swap one happy being for another shows by contrast what love knows: no one can stand in for *this* one (A7, W3).

**Lines worth keeping**
- "The universe has no point of view. God does."
- "Making the world worse and wronging a person are not the same thing; only the second needs forgiveness."
- "Not even God can make cruelty right. What God can do is forgive."
- "A ledger can always be outweighed. A person cannot."
- "Equal concern for everyone's pain is not yet equal dignity; dignity is what may not be spent."

## 8. Score

**v1 as written: 6/10. With the repairs above: 8/10.** The core of v1 (wronging, guilt that outruns every human forgiver, untradeable dignity, love's refusal of replacement) beats the best utilitarian realism on scope. But as written it overstates what is undeniable, leaves A3 open to an easy counter, and treats the agent-neutral "ought" as if it were not there.
