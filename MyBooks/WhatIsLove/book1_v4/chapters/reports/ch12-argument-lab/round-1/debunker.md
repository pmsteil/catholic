# Round 1: Moral Error Theory + Evolutionary Debunking vs. v1

## 1. School and voice

This voice comes from the error-theory tradition: J. L. Mackie, Richard Joyce, Jonas Olson, Bart Streumer, Richard Garner. It is joined by the debunkers: Sharon Street's Darwinian dilemma, Michael Ruse, Joyce's *The Evolution of Morality*, Wilkins and Griffiths. It also draws on the cognitive science of religion: Justin Barrett on agency detection, Pascal Boyer on gods as "full-access" minds, Ara Norenzayan on moralizing "Big Gods", and attachment and terror-management research. Its claim is not "morality is whatever you like." It says our moral thinking *does* presuppose binding, authoritative, uncancelable demands. It grants that such demands would need something like a Lawgiver behind them. It then holds that nothing of the kind exists, and that evolution and culture explain why we feel otherwise. It is the atheist who has counted what morality needs, and pays the price instead of borrowing.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** No one is *owed* anything in the categorical sense. Justice is a set of conventions for mutual advantage. Hume called it an "artificial" virtue, and Mackie treated morality as a social device that makes up for our limited sympathies. "Owed" is kept as a working word inside the practice, or as a useful fiction (Joyce).
→ **Coherent; it borrows only words, and does so openly.** It breaks at the edges. Anyone who cannot reciprocate (the newborn, the severely disabled, the dying, the powerless enemy) gets standing only through other people's sympathy. That is conditional, not owed.

**2. A hard case: parents ask doctors to end the life of a severely disabled newborn.** There is no fact about what is right. We settle it by the desires and sympathies of those involved, plus "if" reasons that depend on what we want, for example: "if we want a medicine people can trust, doctors should not kill." The fictionalist may keep saying "killing the innocent is wrong" if the policy serves us.
→ **Coherent, and it borrows none of the book's definitions.** It replaces them with what people want. But it cannot say any outcome was *right*, only that it suited those with the power to decide. The infant has no vote. This is exactly Ratzinger's point and *Evangelium Vitae* 20: "everything is negotiable."

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** Denied, as something true. It is a valuable belief, historically late, spread by Christianity and the Enlightenment, and worth keeping as a fiction. Abolitionists like Garner would drop the moral vocabulary and keep the compassion.
→ **An honest denial, not a borrowing.** This is the school's highest price. It recognizes that we believe in dignity (*Dignitas Infinita* 1 is right that reason sees it), but holds that nothing makes it true.

**4. Ultimate good for humans.** There is no ultimate good. What is good *for* a person is what satisfies his considered desires or supports his wellbeing as a biological and social animal. Guiding principles: sympathy, honest inquiry (a practical necessity), and keeping the moral practice because it is useful.
→ **Coherent at the level of one life. Breaks at "ultimately."** The school says the question has no answer: life has whatever meaning we give it.

**Fairness note:** this school answers the four challenges more cleanly than a godless realist does, because it doesn't borrow. It denies. Its weakness is not incoherence. Its weakness is cost.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1: "What we cannot honestly deny."**
*Opponent:* Section A's heading is false and quietly accuses. I deny A1–A4, and I deny them honestly. Moral concepts claim categorical authority (Mackie's "queer" properties: facts that would, by their very nature, tell us what to do). Nothing has that authority. A1 is a widely held belief, not a datum.
*Reply:* We are more certain that torturing a child for fun is really wrong than we are of either premise in your case: that such facts would be too strange to exist, or that our moral beliefs come from causes indifferent to truth. A rational person drops the less certain premise. That is G. E. Moore's response to the skeptic, and it applies here.
*Rejoinder:* Your certainty is exactly what my theory predicts. A feeling of certainty produced by selection is not evidence.
*Reply:* Then the same rule dissolves your confidence in the norms of inquiry your argument relies on (*follow the evidence, don't contradict yourself*), which were built by the same process. Either evolved confidence can be trusted unless defeated, or nothing in your argument can be trusted.
*Rejoinder (Olson):* Reasons to believe are only "if" reasons: *if* you want truth, follow the evidence. Nothing strange there.
*Reply:* Then you cannot fault a person who prefers comfort to truth for rejecting error theory. You can only say he doesn't share your goal. Your theory wins no one who doesn't already want what you want. And Streumer's global version (error theory is true of *all* normativity, but we cannot believe it) takes itself out of the contest of reasons.
**Result: Held at parity, with repair.** The Moorean standoff holds, but it is a standoff, not a win. The heading's "cannot honestly deny" overclaims and implies bad faith in those who deny it (CCC 847). Reword it.

**Exchange 2: W1's "owed to someone."**
*Opponent:* W1 equivocates. A debt is owed *to* the victim. That says nothing about who *laid* the obligation. And "a claim no human can cancel was not laid by any human" doesn't follow. Such a claim may have been laid by no one, because wrongness is not a claim at all. Or it may have been laid by selection and culture, which built it to *feel* uncancelable.
*Reply:* We concede the equivocation. The real force of W1 is the conditional: *if* anything truly binds, the best explanation is a Lawgiver who is the Good. The most rigorous atheists in this school grant that conditional. Mackie, in *The Miracle of Theism* (1982), allowed that objective values, if granted, would make a god more probable, which is why he denied the values. Elizabeth Anscombe, a Catholic, argued in "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958) that the special moral "ought" makes sense only within a divine-law framework. Joyce builds his case on the same observation.
*Rejoinder:* Conceding a conditional costs me nothing. I take modus tollens: no God, so nothing binds.
*Reply:* Correct. That is the honest shape of the choice: a Lawgiver, or the claim that torturing a child for fun is not really wrong. Reason does not force the choice. It shows what each side must pay.
**Result: Held with repair.** W1 must be restated as the conditional plus the choice.

**Exchange 3: "God only relocates the strangeness."**
*Opponent (Joyce):* God doesn't help. Either "obey God" needs a further reason (*why* should I?), or God's nature simply *is* to-be-pursued. That is the same strange, intrinsically commanding property, now with a halo.
*Reply:* (a) No voluntarism. The book does not say "obey because commanded" (Ch. 3). The Good is "what all desire" (ST I q.5 a.1). The will cannot help willing its last end (ST I-II q.10 a.1). The "ought" is the pull of the end we are *for*, which we did not pick and cannot cancel. (b) It is one stopping point, not many: the law, the knower's fit to it, dignity, and communion all come from one Source. (c) A Person can lay a claim on a person and love him back. A property cannot.
*Rejoinder:* (a) either makes morality an "if" reason built on a desire we happen to have, which is not strange at all and which I accept, or it rests on natural purpose, which Darwin retired. "What we are for" is the strange item.
*Reply:* It is not an "if" reason. The end binds whether or not you now feel the desire, because it is what you are *for*. And the debunker cannot speak without "for" himself: "conscience is *for* getting along." He will answer that his "for" is causal history, not a standard. True. That is exactly the point at issue, and neither side can force the other on it.
**Result: Held at parity.** C must say plainly that God is a mystery too, but one that explains, not a brute fact that doesn't.

**Exchange 4: W2's "where survival pulls against it."**
*Opponent:* That claim is empirically false. Reputation, indirect reciprocity, costly signaling, sexual selection for generosity, reconciliation behavior in primates (de Waal), and evidence of long-term care for disabled individuals among Neanderthals all predict that we would admire costly honesty, mercy, and care for those who can't repay. Worse, conscience is *least* sure exactly there. Mercy to enemies is historically rare and culture-bound.
*Reply:* We concede the wording. The stronger point is Street's own dilemma. It defeats the godless realist (moral facts plus an evolved conscience means the two met by luck), but not the theist, because one Source made both the law and the mind. Against the error theorist, W2 is not meant to be decisive. It supports the comparison in C. The true, defensible fact is this: evolution explains our bias toward kin, reciprocators, and reputation. On reflection we judge that the stranger and the one who can never repay count *just as much*, and over the centuries consciences have moved toward that judgment, not away from it. Michael Huemer, a non-theist realist, presses this convergence against debunkers.
*Rejoinder:* "Moved toward" is measured by your present values, which is circular. And if God shaped conscience, why did Christian societies approve slavery for centuries?
*Reply:* Catholic natural law predicts exactly this pattern. The first principles are known everywhere. Their application can be clouded by passion, bad custom, and evil persuasion (ST I-II q.94 a.4, a.6, where Aquinas cites a people who did not count theft as wrong). The precepts are "not perceived by everyone clearly and immediately" (CCC 1960). The circularity charge is real but applies equally to both sides: every judgment of progress uses some standard.
**Result: Held with repair.** Replace the empirical claim.

**Exchange 5: "A law that is one."**
*Opponent (Haidt-style pluralism):* Moral psychology shows separate systems for care, fairness, loyalty, authority, sanctity, and liberty, patched together by selection. There is no "one law."
*Reply:* Patrick's own distinction answers this. Those systems describe human *nature*, what we are (A5), with its many pulls. The law is what sorts them. Aquinas lists several natural inclinations (life, family, truth about God, life in society) under one first principle, *good is to be done and pursued, evil avoided*, and one end (ST I-II q.94 a.2). Many inclinations, one law.
**Result: Held.**

**Exchange 6: The felt "Someone" (W1, W3, and the chapter's "Only a Someone can").**
*Opponent:* "No *thing* has ever laid a claim on anyone; only a Someone can" is the agency-detection system speaking in the book's voice. We are built to read agents into rustling grass and minds into storms (Barrett). Gods are imagined as minds with full access to our secrets (Boyer). Moralizing watcher-gods spread because watched people cooperate (Norenzayan). Conscience feels like Someone's gaze because it is an internalized audience: Adam Smith's "impartial spectator." W3's "One who knows us wholly" is exactly the content these systems predict.
*Reply:* Explaining how a faculty works does not settle whether its output is true. That is the genetic fallacy. And these mechanisms work the same whether or not God exists: God works through secondary causes. They discredit the belief only if naturalism is assumed first.
*Rejoinder (Wilkins and Griffiths):* If the belief would arise whether or not it is true, it gives no evidence. Belief in other minds is confirmed by its success: predicting people pays off. Belief in gods has no such check.
*Reply:* Then the *felt presence* of Someone should not carry weight as independent evidence, and v1 must not lean on it. We grant that. But the same rule applied to A1 sends us back to Exchange 1. Wilkins and Griffiths themselves vindicate moral belief only in part, and the Moorean standoff holds there. The argument must rest on the *structure* of obligation (the conditional in W1), not on the feeling of being watched.
**Result: Failed as evidence for the felt "Someone"; Held for the structural argument.** W3 drops from "best explained" to "fits."

**Exchange 7: A3, "not the victim."**
*Opponent:* Victims release wrongdoers all the time. That is what forgiveness is, and the book's own Ch. 8 says so. And the *felt* inescapability of moral demands is what commitment-device theory predicts (Robert Frank; Joyce): demands that can't be bargained away make us trustworthy partners.
*Reply:* Repair the wording. The victim can forgive the debt owed to him. What no one can do is make the act *not wrong*, or sign away his dignity in advance, as in consenting to be tortured for fun. The commitment-device story explains why we *feel* A3. Whether A3 is true is A1 again.
**Result: Held with repair.**

**Exchange 8 (chapter text, not v1): "The denial refutes itself."**
*Opponent:* I never said you *morally* ought not believe in oughts. I said categorical moral claims are false, and that you'd be wise to believe what the evidence supports, given that you want truth. Your self-refutation charge hits only Streumer's global view. As for my crying "That's not fair!": the outrage is a leftover my theory predicts, like the Müller-Lyer illusion, where lines of equal length keep looking unequal after you know the truth.
*Reply:* We know the Müller-Lyer lines are equal because we measure them. When a stick looks bent in water, we pull it out and look. The error theorist has no ruler, only a philosophical premise less certain than the perception it corrects.
**Result: the chapter's self-refutation charge Failed against local error theory; the repaired Moorean form Held.**

## 5. Remaining sound objections

None is **Fatal** once the repairs in section 6 are made. Each of the following stands against v1 *as written*:

1. **W1's inference is invalid as written** (equivocation between owed *to* and laid *by*; "uncancelable, so not human-laid" doesn't follow). **Serious.** Answered by recasting W1 as the conditional plus the choice.
2. **W2's empirical claim is false** (conscience is not "most sure" where survival pulls against it; selection does predict admiration of costly goodness). **Serious.** Answered by the replacement wording.
3. **C omits the error theorist and misdescribes him under "Matter alone."** **Serious.** Answered by a new C line that states his position at full strength and names its price.
4. **God relocates the strangeness** (Joyce). **Serious, answered only at parity.** It cannot be closed beyond parity. Neither side can force the other on whether purpose can be normative. C must say so.
5. **W3 and the felt "Someone" are predicted by cognitive science of religion.** **Serious against v1's "best explained"; answered at parity once W3 claims only fit** and W1 rests on structure rather than the felt presence.
6. **A3 "not the victim"** contradicts the reality of forgiveness. **Minor.** Wording fix.
7. **The "cannot honestly deny" heading.** **Minor** logically, **Serious** pastorally (it presumes bad faith).
8. **The deep standoff.** The error theorist can coherently hold *no God, nothing binds, A1–A4 false*. Our side meets this at parity (the Moorean response, plus the point that his own premises depend on the same kind of trust), not beyond. This is **not a defect**. It is what "converging and convincing, not a proof" (CCC 31, 35) means, and the book should say so rather than claim more.

**Chapter-text objections (outside v1, but this school will hit them hard):**
- (a) "The denial refutes itself in the speaking." Fails against Mackie, Joyce, and Olson (Serious).
- (b) "Every time an atheist says 'That's wrong!' they're providing evidence for God's existence." Tu quoque, and it presumes culpability (Minor).
- (c) "Laws require lawgivers," and "like every law, it requires a lawgiver" (opening section and line 137). These blur laws of nature, which describe what happens, with laws of conduct, which prescribe. The debunker's first move is to point this out (Serious).
- (d) "That takes far more faith than belief in God." Overclaims, which breaks the lab rule (Minor).

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**A heading:**
> **A. What we start from.** These are things nearly everyone knows. A few careful thinkers deny them, and we will see what that costs.

**A1:**
> **A1. Some things are really wrong, for everyone.** Torturing a child for fun, betraying a friend for gain, enslaving the weak. Not merely disliked, and not merely bad for our survival: wrong even if every culture approved, and even if it helped us. Some things must never be done, even to save oneself. We are more sure of this than of any theory that says otherwise.

**A3:**
> **A3. No one on earth can make a real wrong not wrong.** A victim can forgive what was owed to him, and that is real mercy. But he cannot make it that it was never owed, and he cannot sign away his own dignity in advance. No nation by vote, and not the whole human race by agreement, can do it either.

**A5 (last sentence):**
> Describing what we are never by itself tells us which to choose. If there is an "ought," it does not come from what we are; it comes from what we are *for*.

**A6:**
> **A6. Real good for another costs the giver something:** time, attention, comfort, the self he might have spent elsewhere. That is how reality works.

**A7 (last sentence):**
> Love refuses death: we grieve as if love were meant to last.

**W1:**
> **W1. Obligation points to a Lawgiver.** If anything truly binds us (A1–A3), it holds a claim on us that no human has: a claim that stands whether or not we agree, and that no one on earth can cancel. A claim like that is best explained by Someone above every human being. Not a commander who picks rules, but the Creator, who made us, established what we are for, and is Himself the Good He asks of us. Some of the most rigorous atheist philosophers agree with the first half of this. J. L. Mackie granted that real moral values would make God more likely, and so he denied the values. The Catholic philosopher Elizabeth Anscombe argued that the moral "ought" makes sense only inside a law that has a Lawgiver. So reason leaves a plain choice: a Lawgiver, or the claim that nothing is really wrong, not even torturing a child for fun. Each of us must weigh which he is more sure of.

*(Verify the Mackie paraphrase against* The Miracle of Theism*, ch. 6, before it enters the book, per the quotation-verification rule. Keep it a paraphrase.)*

**W2:**
> **W2. The law and the mind that reads it point to one Source.** If moral truths simply exist, and conscience is only a tool shaped for survival, then it is luck that the two ever met, because survival rewards what helps us get along, not what is true. Evolution explains well why we favor our kin, keep faith with those who repay us, and guard our good name. But on reflection we judge that the stranger, the enemy and the one who can never repay count just as much as our own, and over the centuries consciences have moved toward that judgment, not away from it. Our nature carries many pulls (A5). The law that sorts them is one: do good, and avoid evil. And we find what the Church expects. The first principles are known everywhere. Their application is slow, clouded by passion and custom, and corrected over time (ST I-II q.94 a.4–6; CCC 1960). A law that is one, and a mind fitted to read it, are best explained by one Source who made both.

**W3:**
> **W3. Communion points to One who is Love.** Only a person can love us back; a law, a force or an idea cannot. No finite person can receive the whole of us, because no one knows the whole of us, not even ourselves (A7). Science can trace the paths this longing travels in us, as it traces the paths of sight. That tells us *how* we long, not whether there is Someone to long for. This witness does not prove; it fits. A Person who knows us wholly, can hold us forever, and loved us first (1 John 4:19) is the answer the longing is shaped for. Revelation gives His name: God is Love (1 John 4:8).

**C, replace "Matter alone":**
> **No moral truth at all.** The most consistent atheist says that nothing really binds. Our sense of being bound, guilty and unreleasable (A1–A3) is a useful feeling shaped by evolution and culture. This view borrows nothing, and it explains why we *feel* the law. Its price is to say none of it is true: torturing a child for fun is not really wrong, and the newborn and the dying have no dignity except what we choose to give them (A4). It asks us to trust a theory over what we know best.

**C, "A God who is Love":**
> **A God who is Love** explains all of it at one stopping point. This does not remove all mystery: a Good who draws every will is beyond our full understanding. But it is one mystery, not many, and it is a mystery that explains. He is the Good all things seek (ST I q.5 a.1), and He is also a Person who can claim us and love us back.

**C, Conclusion:**
> **Conclusion:** for anyone more sure that some things are really wrong than that there is no God, the best explanation of love as we actually find it is a Lawgiver who is Love. This is reason's witness, converging and convincing (CCC 31). It can predispose to faith (CCC 35) but does not replace it.

**Chapter text (for the later redline):**
- *The Atheist's Dilemma, option (1):* replace "but the denial refutes itself in the speaking … written deeper than his theory" with:
  > The most careful atheists who take this road do not contradict themselves; they accept the cost. They agree that if anything truly bound us, it would point beyond us, so they deny that anything truly binds. Torturing a child for fun is not really wrong; our horror at it is a useful feeling, nothing more. That position is honest, and it is costly: it asks us to trust a philosopher's premise over the thing we know best. When a stick looks bent in water, we learn it is straight by pulling it out and looking. No one has ever pulled cruelty out of the water and found it innocent.
- *"Every time an atheist says 'That's wrong!' …":* replace with:
  > Every time any of us says "That's wrong!" and means it, we are leaning on a standard none of us invented.
- *Opening section, "But laws require lawgivers":* replace with:
  > But a law that tells us what we *ought* to do, not merely what happens, points to a Lawgiver.
  Also at "like every law, it requires a lawgiver":
  > then, as a law that commands and not merely describes, it points to a Lawgiver.
- *"That takes far more faith than belief in God ever could …":* replace with:
  > When you weigh it honestly, the materialist is not free of faith either; the question is whose faith explains more.

## 7. New insights and lines worth keeping

**Insights:**
- **The error theorist is the book's unexpected ally on the conditional.** Mackie, Joyce, and the Catholic Anscombe all agree that a real "ought" needs a law with a Lawgiver behind it. The fight is only over which end to hold. This makes the argument's shape honest and plain: *God, or nothing is really wrong.* It replaces the loaded Premise 1 Patrick disliked with something the strongest atheist voices concede.
- **Patrick's nature-versus-law distinction answers moral pluralism** (Haidt). Many inclinations belong to nature; one law sorts them. Add this somewhere near A5 or W2.
- **Aquinas q.94 a.4–6 is the book's answer to "why did conscience approve slavery?"** The theory predicts what we see: clear first principles, application clouded by passion and custom, slow correction.
- **Retire the "self-refutation" charge** against moral skeptics. It works only against the global version. The stick-in-water response is stronger and kinder.
- **Do not rest anything on the *feeling* of a Watcher.** Rest it on the structure of obligation. This also keeps the book clear of "God of the gaps in neuroscience."

**Lines worth keeping:**
- "A wrong sum needs correcting; a wronged person needs forgiveness." (v1, A2)
- "A Lawgiver, or the claim that nothing is really wrong. Each of us must weigh which he is more sure of."
- "No one has ever pulled cruelty out of the water and found it innocent."
- "Many pulls in our nature; one law that sorts them."
- "Science can trace the paths this longing travels in us, as it traces the paths of sight. That tells us how we long, not whether there is Someone to long for."
- "It is one mystery, not many, and it is a mystery that explains."

## 8. Score

**v1 as written: 6/10. With the repairs above: 8/10.** The structure is right and the opponent concedes its key conditional, but W1's inference, W2's empirical claim and C's missing rival give this school three clean openings. After repair, all that is left is the honest standoff a converging case is supposed to leave.
