# Argument Lab, Round 6: Buddhist opponent vs v6

## 1. School and voice

The same composite voice as Rounds 1–5: the Theravada Pali Canon and its commentaries (Buddhaghosa), and Indian Madhyamaka (Nagarjuna, Candrakirti, Santideva, Kamalasila), with modern readers such as Harvey, Keown, Garfield and Siderits. It holds that there is no lasting self, that craving rooted in ignorance keeps the round turning, that karma is intention ripening with no one appointing it, that the four boundless states are the right shape of the heart, and that nirvana (for the Madhyamaka, emptiness realized) is the highest good. Argued in the spirit of *Nostra Aetate* 2.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges (short)

1. **Justice.** No one is dearer to anyone than himself, and each holds himself just as dear, so harm no one (SN 3.8; Dhp 129–130). The one wronged must be asked for pardon (Vinaya, Cullavagga I). **Holds;** nothing borrowed.
2. **Dilemma** (a dying man asks a monk to help him die). No. A monk who praises death to the sick or helps one to die is expelled (Vinaya, Parajika 3). The will to end a life comes with aversion. What he is owed is care (Mahavagga 8.26). **Holds,** without borrowing.
3. **Equal dignity.** Every being suffers and holds its life dear, and the mind is luminous by nature (AN 1.51); the Mahayana adds buddha-nature. **Holds for equal compassion; bends for equal dignity of persons,** because the highest good is open according to capacity (AN 8.29).
4. **Ultimate good.** Nirvana, or emptiness realized, in which wisdom and compassion cannot be separated. **Holds;** coherent. On this view love is not the ground of things.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1. "Whether or not she ever pardons him": does this make her pardon idle on our path?**
*Buddhist:* The new clause is accurate: Angulimala was freed without his victims' pardon (MN 86). But does a reader now hear that her pardon is worth nothing to us?
*Patrick's side:* No. The same sentence names the funeral at which the living ask the dead person's pardon, and the Dhamma row has the Buddha sending Sudhamma to ask Citta's pardon (Cullavagga I). Read together, the text says what you said in Round 5: her pardon heals the bond between the two of them, and the doer's own purification is what finally frees him. The Church says something parallel of absolution (CCC 1459).
*Verdict:* **Holds.** No repair needed. The parting is now drawn exactly: whether *she herself* can still give her pardon.

**Exchange 2. Three roads, and two forms of the third.**
*Buddhist:* v6 puts the ancestral rites on the third road ("kept by the living ... memory fails"), and puts us there as "another form ... without a Keeper." Then the clue weighs "the third road, on which she is kept as herself." Doesn't "as herself" beg the question against no-self?
*Patrick's side:* The clue is conditional: it "weighs as much as the reader finds" that road truer to what he owes. It asks you to grant nothing. It says which road the clue is about, and it is honest that rebirth, on your own account, is not that road.
*Buddhist rejoinder:* Then the clue does not move me, and the book does not say it should.
*Verdict:* **Holds.** This is the Round 4–5 standoff over whether persons are real at the ground of things, now marked off cleanly. The repair also works against the ancestral form: the named dead who are kept only by memory are not kept as themselves either.

**Exchange 3. A6's new line and the bodhisattva who fed the tigress.**
*Buddhist (Mahayana voice):* v6 now says a man "may not make his own death the thing he chooses, by his own hand or another's." Our most loved story of giving is the prince who saw a starving tigress with her cubs and gave her his body (*Jatakamala* 1; Suvarnaprabhasa, paraphrased; verify). On A6's wording, as I read it, his death was the means of the gift, so it falls on the far side of the line. In Round 5 you also called on the same story to support "given, not taken." Has the new wording turned an ally into a counterexample?
*Patrick's side:* Two answers. First, A6 now says plainly that this is the book's claim, "not shared ones," argued in W1(2). The book does not say the Buddhist holds it. It says why the Church does: a life is given to her keeping and not to her disposal. Second, "given, not taken" in the evil section is said of Christ, who laid down His life and bore a death that others wrongly inflicted (John 10:18). That is exactly the Kolbe pattern A6 now describes. It does not lean on the Jataka, so the Jataka cannot break it.
*Buddhist rejoinder:* We limit it too. Santideva says not to give the body while one's compassion is still impure; it should be given only when it serves a great purpose (*Bodhicaryavatara* 5.87, checked in translation). The Vinaya forbids monks even to praise death, and the commentaries read Channa's blamelessness (SN 35.87) as an arahant's case, no license for anyone else.
*Patrick's side:* Then we agree that the line is real and should almost never be crossed. We part over whether it is never to be crossed, and over why: for you, because of what is in the giver's mind; for us, because her life was not hers to spend as a means.
*Verdict:* **Held at parity.** No defect, because v6 states the line as its own claim. It belongs with the Round 5 standoff on chosen death and sharpens it.

**Exchange 4. "Love is the first word and the last": does any Buddhist text make compassion the root?**
*Buddhist (Madhyamaka voice):* Kamalasila opens his *Stages of Meditation* with the *Mahavairocana Sutra*'s line that compassion is the root (paraphrased; verify). So does the new C-God line, "love is the first word," really separate us?
*Patrick's side:* Look at what the line says compassion is the root *of*: the awakened mind and the path to omniscience. That is what the Dhamma row already grants: "For the Buddhist, compassion is the seed and the fruit of the path; for the Catholic, love is also the root of the world." The buddhas love beings who are already there. They gave no one being. "First word" in C-God means the source of beings (1 John 4:8, where God *is* love), and no school, Kamalasila's included, puts compassion there.
*Verdict:* **Holds,** and the text is confirmed. The row's words "seed and fruit of the path" are exactly where the Madhyamaka places compassion.

**Exchange 5. The fit parenthesis and the evil section.**
*Buddhist:* The parenthesis now says "so far as one life lets us see." But the evil section still says flatly that pain in nature falls "where survival would put it, not where justice would." Is karma denied there by assertion?
*Patrick's side:* No. That sentence reports what can be seen, and you do not deny what can be seen. The Buddha himself named many causes of what is felt besides past deeds: bile, phlegm, wind, the seasons, carelessness, assault (SN 36.21). The evil section also names the Buddhist as one who bears this weight more easily, because suffering is what he expects. The two passages agree.
*Buddhist:* Granted. One small point, about reading aloud: "as ours, so far as one life lets us see, does not" makes the listener hold "does not" through two commas.
*Verdict:* **Holds.** An optional smoothing for the read-aloud pass (Repair 1). It is not an objection.

**Exchange 6. The wandering dead whom "a whole people feeds without a name."**
*Buddhist:* In China, the feeding of the nameless dead is largely our rite: the Ullambana festival, which goes back to Maudgalyayana feeding his mother among the hungry ghosts, and the evening rite of feeding the hungry ghosts, performed by monks for every being who has no one (verify). The Pali verses already have the departed waiting at the walls of their old homes (Khp 7). Are you putting our compassion for the forgotten dead on a road "kept by memory"?
*Patrick's side:* The sentence says what the ancestral road leaves the forgotten dead to: a whole people feeding them without a name. That in China monks often lead that feeding fits the very next sentence, which gives the Buddhist his own form of road three. Your rite does not depend on memory, and the book does not say it does. It reaches the nameless as departed spirits to be fed and freed, not as persons kept as themselves. That is the clue's line again.
*Verdict:* **Holds.** It does not misstate this school. A possible insight (section 7).

**Exchange 7. "No one can purify another."**
*Buddhist:* The Dhamma row gives this teaching without a citation, though it now cites everything else.
*Patrick's side:* It is Dhammapada 165: purity and impurity depend on oneself, and no one can purify another (checked in translation). Add it.
*Verdict:* **Holds; optional citation** (Repair 2).

**Exchange 8. The weighing, checked once more.**
*Buddhist:* With every concession now made, does "best explanation" still beat us?
*Patrick's side:* It names the same points, and v6 has made none of them weaker. Guilt asks to be forgiven by *her, herself*, after she is gone. Her worth is a call to this one, not a nature held whole by all. Love is the first word and not only the seed and fruit of a path. And it is good that she exists, not only that her birth is a chance to be free of birth. The book claims a judgment about these points, not compulsion.
*Verdict:* **Held at parity.** This is the deepest standoff, and it is honestly drawn.

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**As written: NONE.**

No Fatal or Serious objection stands against v6 as written. Every Round 5 repair was taken. I checked each new or changed sentence that touches this school: the Buddhist form of the third road, the clue, the new A6 line on choosing one's own death, "first word and last," the fit parenthesis, the wandering dead, "a yes said to whatever happens," and the hiddenness paragraph. None misstates Buddhism on anything the case rests on. The strongest candidate, the tigress Jataka against A6's new wording (Exchange 3), is not a defect: v6 states that line as the book's own claim, and its "given, not taken" rests on Christ, not on the Jataka.

**Minor (all optional)**
1. **C-Dhamma:** for the read-aloud pass, the parenthesis "as ours, so far as one life lets us see, does not" makes the listener carry "does not" through two commas (Exchange 5).
2. **C-Dhamma:** "No one, the Buddha taught, can purify another" has no citation. It is Dhp 165 (Exchange 7).

**Misstatements of this school:** none found in v6.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Whether persons are real at the ground of things. Rebirth keeps a stream; guilt asks for her, herself (Exchange 2).
- Whether the dead victim herself can still pardon. Both traditions agree that a pardon heals the bond and does not undo everything (Exchange 1; CCC 1459; MN 86).
- Whether the line against choosing one's own death is never to be crossed (the Church) or almost never (the bodhisattva's body-gift, held back by pure compassion and great purpose, BCA 5.87; Channa as an arahant's case). Also whether the line rests on the giver's mind or on a life held in trust (Exchange 3).
- Whether compassion is the root of the path only, or love is also the root of the world (Exchange 4). This is the nearest point between the two traditions, together with Shinran (Round 5).
- Whether the fit of hearts and fortunes asks for a mind or arises from conditions and is empty; and, on the other side, whether a world where pain does not follow justice weighs against a just Mind (Exchange 5).
- Whether the animal's loss is given by an order God wills or comes from its own past deeds.
- Whether it is good that we exist, or only that a human birth is a precious chance to be free of birth. This is the root of every other parting.

## 7. New insights; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **The Buddhist feeding of the nameless dead is the nearest Buddhist parallel to the Mass for those "whose faith God alone has known."** The Ullambana and the evening rite of feeding the hungry ghosts reach every forgotten being, not only kin (verify). The contrast is quiet and exact. The Buddhist rite feeds the nameless as departed spirits to be freed. The Church prays for them as persons God has kept by name (Isaiah 49:15). If the book wants to honor the Buddhist here, this is the place.
- **On giving one's life, the two traditions are closer than they look.** Santideva holds back the gift of the body until compassion is pure and the purpose great (BCA 5.87), and the Vinaya forbids even praising death. Both traditions guard the line with great care. They part over whether it ever opens, and over why it holds: for the Buddhist, because of what is in the giver's mind; for the Church, because of what was entrusted to her.
- **"Compassion is the root" (Mahavairocana Sutra, cited by Kamalasila; verify) means the root of the path.** That confirms the Dhamma row's last contrast instead of weakening it: for the Buddhist, compassion is the seed and the fruit of the path; for the Catholic, love is also the root of the world.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "Compassion is the root of the path; love is also the root of the world."
- "His reason guards her as surely as ours; it does not say she was wronged."
- "A pardon heals the bond; it does not undo the deed. We part over whether she can still give hers."
- "Rebirth keeps a stream; guilt asks for her, herself."

**Checked this round:** Dhammapada 165 ("no one can purify another"), buddhanet translation; *Bodhicaryavatara* 5.87 (do not give the body while compassion is impure; give it for a great purpose), several translations at bodhicitta.tsadra.org. **To verify before use:** *Jatakamala* 1 and the Suvarnaprabhasa tigress story; Kamalasila, *Bhavanakrama* I, and its citation of the *Mahavairocana Sutra* on compassion as the root; the Ullambana origin story and the rite of feeding the hungry ghosts; SN 35.87 and its commentary on Channa; MN 86; Vinaya Parajika 3 and Cullavagga I.
