## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

1. **Justice.** Brute badness of agony (Rowe), invention (Mackie), ordinary intuition (Maitzen). *Holds as a position. Rowe's moral force borrows A1.*
2. **Hard case.** A newborn in pain that cannot be relieved: many in the school would end her life. *Coherent. It rests on a premise the book denies (A4, A6).*
3. **Equal dignity.** Invented, brute, or interests that come in degrees. *Breaks, or stops at a brute fact. The argument from evil weighs most on our premise (A4).*
4. **Flourishing.** Love, knowledge and relief of suffering, within mortality. *Coherent and bounded. On his view history's victims are never answered, and v6 rightly declines to count that as evidence.*

### Exchange 1. Draper: "answers only with what He brought" against "two answers must be kept apart"

*Attack:* Your weight paragraph now says that why God did not keep the first man from sinning, the Church "answers only with what He brought from it." Your second reason says that God's permission "is not a trade, and two answers must be kept apart here": *why* He permits is the setting (a world of real causes), and *what* He brings is "a further thing." For the first sin, then, your *why* is your *what*. Aquinas, whom CCC 412 quotes, says God "permits evil in order to draw forth some greater good," and CCC 311 says He permits it because He respects freedom *and* knows how to bring good from it. So the Church joins the two answers that v6 says must be kept apart. Either the first sin was permitted for the good it would yield, and that is a trade, or the second reason's division is false.

*Reply:* (a) The setting cannot answer Mackie's question. Mary was a real cause too, and God kept her from sin. So for the first sin the Church gives a further answer, and v6 now says so honestly. (b) What v6 means by "not a trade" is stated in the same reason: God "never spends anyone as a mere means against her own good," and the good He brings "is a good offered to her." CCC 412 fits that exactly. St. Leo says Christ's grace "gave *us*" more than the devil's envy took away. The good drawn from the Fall goes to the very ones who lost by it. It is not bought at their expense for someone else. (c) "Kept apart" is about the price, not about whether God has a purpose. The evil never becomes good (CCC 312), and the good that comes is not its wage.

*Rejoinder (Draper):* Then say so. As written, a careful reader finds two answers to *why He permits*, one in each paragraph, and is told they must be kept apart.

*Final reply:* Granted as wording. The doctrine is consistent, and the reply above is at parity. But the two sentences should be joined in so many words.

**Held with repair (Minor).** One clause in each place (R1). No conclusion changes.

### Exchange 2. The Thomist grounding problem: if God gives the act all its being, why not the being of the good act?

*Attack:* "God gives the act its being, never its defect." Good. Then every bit of being in Adam's act is from God, and the defect is only what God did not give. God could have given the being of the right act, as He did for Mary. On your Thomism the defect is a creature's failing that God did not prevent, and whether He prevents it is His choice. So the new sentence moves the question back one step. It does not answer it.

*Reply:* Correct, and v6 no longer pretends otherwise. The sentence answers *whose* the evil is (CCC 311). The R1 sentence in the weight paragraph answers *why it was not prevented*, as far as the Church answers it at all (CCC 412). How grace and freedom meet (Báñez, Molina, Maritain's "shatterable motions") the Church has never settled, and v6 claims no settlement.

*Rejoinder (Talbott):* Then God could win every will and does not.

*Final reply:* That is the hell standoff, already owned: "taught, not shown."

**Held at parity.** The repaired sentence is exact. What remains is the Mackie 1955 standoff, now placed where it belongs.

### Exchange 3. Ivan on Christ: "wrongly take it" against "given, not taken"

*Attack:* A6 now says a man may "bear the death that comes, even at the hands of those who wrongly take it, as Christ did." Your second reason says of the same death: "what Christ gave was given, not taken," citing John 10:18, where Christ says no one takes His life from Him. In A6 His life was taken. In the evil section it was not. Then there is the deeper point. A6 says a man "may not make his own death the thing he chooses." Christ laid down His life "of His own accord," set His face to Jerusalem and refused rescue. Did He not choose His death?

*Reply:* (a) On the second point, the distinction holds, and it is the Church's. Christ freely accepted His Passion (CCC 609). What He chose was the gift: obedience to the Father and love for us. The death was inflicted by others, and He bore it. Aquinas says He caused His death only in that He did not prevent it, though He could have (ST III q.47 a.1). That is "bear the death that comes," not "make his own death the thing he chooses." Kolbe is the same: he chose to take the condemned man's place, and the guards chose the death. (b) On the first point, granted as wording. The executioners did wrong, and from their side they killed Him. From His side, no one took it from Him. "Wrongly take it" describes the killers' act in a word that John 10:18 denies.

*Rejoinder (Ivan):* So the word "take" means one thing in A6 and another in the evil section.

*Final reply:* Yes. Change A6's verb to "kill," and both sentences are true as they stand.

**Held with repair (Minor).** One verb (R2). The Kolbe sentence itself holds, and it fixes the Round 5 Serious objection well.

### Exchange 4. Adams: *Benedictus Deus* "teaches," CCC 1261 "hopes," and a water-poured lottery

*Attack:* v6 now says the Church *teaches* that the baptized child who dies before she can choose receives the highest good, and only *hopes* there is a way for the unbaptized one. Then a child's eternal lot, as far as your Church can say, hangs on whether someone else poured water on her. The infant did nothing either way. That is the unequal road from Round 5, now running through other people's hands.

*Reply:* (a) The asymmetry is in what the Church *knows*, not shown to be in what God *gives*. The Church holds that God has bound salvation to Baptism, but that He Himself is not bound by His sacraments (CCC 1257). For the unbaptized child she entrusts her to His mercy and hopes (CCC 1261), and v6 says exactly that, no more. (b) That a creature's good passes through other creatures' hands is the same law of the dignity of causes that v6 already owns for hiddenness (CCC 306, 848), and v6 names the cost: "The Church does not hide that cost; she calls it a summons to mission." (c) Nothing in v6 says the unbaptized child is lost.

*Rejoinder (Adams):* Hope is not knowledge, and the mother who could not have her child baptized is left with hope only.

*Final reply:* Granted. That is a real weight, and it falls on the same side of the scale as hiddenness. It is not an inconsistency.

**Held at parity.** The new "teaches" is accurate to *Benedictus Deus*, and the new "hopes" is accurate to CCC 1261. Citing CCC 1257 beside CCC 1261 would help (optional; Section 6).

### Exchange 5. The infanticide reductio, sharpened by "teaches"

*Attack:* If the Church now *teaches* that the baptized infant who dies goes straight to beatitude, the reductio is stronger. Kill her after Baptism and her salvation is certain, while an adult life risks eternal loss.

*Reply:* Three answers, all in v6. First, the infant who dies has not given love back, which v6 names as a good she lacks ("a good, not a necessity"), so early death is not simply the better lot. Second, A6 and CCC 1789 forbid doing evil so that good may come. Third, and new in v6, A4 now holds as a *shared* premise that the innocent may never be deliberately killed "for his own good" without his asking. The infant cannot ask. So the reductio fails on a premise the non-theist also grants, before the book's own claims are reached.

**Held.** v6's new A4 clause makes the answer stronger than it was in Round 5.

### Exchange 6. Morriston and Wielenberg: "no voice that claims His name" against 1 Samuel 15:3

*Attack:* W1(2) now says: "no voice that claims His name can make it ours to take." Your Scripture records such a voice. Samuel, in God's name, commands Saul to kill the Amalekite "infant and suckling" (1 Samuel 15:3). Abraham is told to offer Isaac. And your own Aquinas says that whoever kills an innocent at God's command does not sin, because God is Lord of death and life (ST II-II q.64 a.6 ad 1). So either your sentence is false of the God of your Scripture and your Thomism, or it means only "no *false* voice," and then it says nothing a terrorist could not agree with. And if God can command the killing of infants, "God never spends her as a means" is false of the Amalekite children.

*Reply:* (a) The sentence cites CCC 2258, and that is how it should be read: "no one can under any circumstance claim *for himself* the right directly to destroy an innocent human being." The right is never *ours*. Aquinas's own reason in q.64 a.6 ad 1 is the same: if God commands, the one who obeys acts as His instrument, under the right of the Lord of life, not under any right of his own. So "ours to take" is exact, and it agrees with Aquinas. (b) The sentence is not empty. It denies every human claim to that right, including every claim made in God's name, and that is exactly the claim the terrorist makes. (c) On the dark texts themselves, the Church teaches that the Old Testament contains "things which are incomplete and temporary" (*Dei Verbum* 15), and Benedict XVI asks that the passages of violence and massacre be read in the light of Christ, as part of a revelation suited to a distant people's moral level (*Verbum Domini* 42; verify). This book does not rest on those texts, and they are not part of v6's case.

*Rejoinder (Morriston):* Then your exceptionless line has one exception: God's own command.

*Final reply:* It has none for any of us, which is what v6 claims ("the line has no exceptions for any of us"). The Lord of life is not under the line; He is its reason. That is W1(2)'s whole point: "He who gives life, and can give it back, may call it home; we, who can do neither, may not take it." Whether God ever in fact commanded what 1 Samuel 15 reports is a question about Scripture, and it stays on the scale as a weight.

**Held with repair (Minor).** As written, "no voice that claims His name can make it ours to take" is true on CCC 2258's reading. But a reader will take it as denying that even a real divine command could authorize anything, which sets it against ST II-II q.64 a.6 ad 1. Saying it in CCC 2258's own shape removes the ambiguity (R3). The conquest texts move from "outside v5" to an owned standoff at parity.

### Exchange 7. Law and Nietzsche turned on God: "A yes said to whatever happens is said to no one"

*Attack:* v6 says amor fati "must say yes to what was done to her as well as to her, and so it cannot take her side." Your God permits whatever happens, and your Church sings "O happy fault" over the Fall (the Exsultet, quoted in CCC 412). Is that not a yes said to what was done?

*Reply:* No. Permission is not approval. God permits moral evil and is "in no way, directly or indirectly," its cause (CCC 311). The evil never becomes good (CCC 312). He judges it: grace "does not make wrong into right," and the wrongdoer does not sit beside his victim "as though nothing had happened" (*Spe Salvi* 44). The Exsultet calls the fault happy for the Redeemer it occasioned, not good in itself. Amor fati affirms the whole as it is and wills nothing undone. God says no to the wound and yes to her, and takes her side against what was done.

**Held.** The new line survives being turned on its author. It also sharpens the contrast in the first reason.

### Exchange 8. Ivan: "gave her life to her keeping" and "yet she is held by Him"

*Attack:* God "gave her life to her keeping," but the newborn on the hillside can keep nothing, and the parasite takes it anyway. And "yet she is held by Him" is said of the infant no one holds, while the Church only *hopes* for her if she dies unbaptized. Held how?

*Reply:* "Keeping" is a trust that binds her and us. It forbids disposal. It is not a guarantee against the world's causes, and v6 never says it is ("He did not keep her from what was done to her"). For the newborn, the keeping falls to those around her, which is why her abandonment is a wrong done to her. "Held by Him" means held in being and in His love, the God who forgets no one (Isaiah 49:15). Final salvation is a further thing, which v6 rightly marks as hope (CCC 1261).

**Held.** The sentences are consistent with "He may take her to Himself; He never gives her to what took her" (Round 5, Exchange 6).

### Exchange 9. Rowe: CCC 2278–2279 and God's own double effect

*Attack:* v6 now lets the doctor give pain relief that shortens her days, so long as her death is not "the thing aimed at." Your God permits every death as a side effect of the order He wills ("never the loss for its own sake"). Same structure. So God's permission is only as good as the doctor's, and the doctor's is excused only when there is due proportion and no better way.

*Reply:* Granted, and that is the right test. The structure is the same, and so the question becomes proportion: is a world of real causes, with love freely given back, worth what it costs? v6 already places that question on the scale ("part of the weighing, not settled by it"). The new sentence adds nothing against v6. It restates Rowe's policy question in the Church's own terms.

**Held at parity.** One fidelity note for the censor, which is not this school's objection: CCC 2279 speaks of relief "even at the risk of shortening" the dying person's days. v6's "even if it shortens her days" is a little stronger than that wording.

### Exchange 10. Talbott and *Spe Salvi* 47: "heals whoever is open to Him"

*Attack:* If His touch heals whoever is open to Him, the guilty too, then hell is for those who are not open, and an all-powerful love could open them.

*Reply:* This is Round 5's Exchange 7 (grace that wins without force). v6 keeps it on the scale, and it rests on the Church's teaching (CCC 393, 1021, 1037), not on the case.

**Held at parity.** Standoff unchanged.

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**As written: NONE.**

v6 took all three of this school's Round 5 repairs, and R2 was taken in a better form than the one offered. **Minor (wording; none changes a conclusion):**

The second reason says *why* He permits is the setting, and that it must be kept apart from *what* He brings. The doctrine is consistent: the good of CCC 412 is given to the very ones who fell, so it is not a trade. But the text should say so (Exchange 1; R1).

Put it in CCC 2258's own shape (Exchange 6; R3).
4. **Optional:** cite CCC 1257 beside CCC 1261, so the reader sees that the asymmetry between the baptized and the unbaptized infant lies in what the Church knows, not in what God is bound to (Exchange 4).

**Where v6 misstates this school:** nowhere.

**For the censor (not this school's objection):** CCC 2279 says relief "even at the risk of shortening" the dying person's days. v6 says "even if it shortens her days."

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**

## 7. New insights and lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **The good from the Fall is given to the losers.** That is what keeps CCC 412 from being a trade, and it is the bridge between "why He permits" and "what He brings." St. Leo's "gave *us*" carries the point (Exchange 1).
- **Permission is not amor fati.** God lets the evil happen and is against it. He judges it, forgives what is against Him, and takes her side. The yes that is "said to no one" says yes to the wound as well. God's says no to the wound and yes to her (Exchange 7).
- **The right is never ours.** CCC 2258's "for himself" is how the book can hold an exceptionless human line and the Lord of life together. Aquinas's instrument reading of the divine command (II-II q.64 a.6 ad 1) is the tradition's own form of the same point (Exchange 6).
- **A4's new clause pays twice.** "Nor 'for his own good' without his asking" is shared ground, and it closes the infanticide reductio on a premise the atheist grants (Exchange 5).
- **The infant asymmetry is in what the Church knows.** God binds salvation to Baptism, but He is not bound by His sacraments (CCC 1257) (Exchange 4).

**Lines worth keeping**
- "God gives the act its being, never its defect." (v6)
- "Given to her keeping and not to her disposal." (v6)
- "A yes said to whatever happens is said to no one; it must say yes to what was done to her as well as to her, and so it cannot take her side." (v6)
- "He may give his life for another; he may not make his own death the thing he chooses." (v6, in substance)
- "He did not keep her from what was done to her; that weight is on the other side of the scale, and this book weighs it there." (v5, kept)
- "God says no to the wound and yes to her." (new, optional, after the amor fati line)
- "He may take her to Himself; He never gives her to what took her." (Round 5, still optional)
