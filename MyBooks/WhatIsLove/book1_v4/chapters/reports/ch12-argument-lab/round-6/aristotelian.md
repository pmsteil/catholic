# Argument Lab, Round 6: Secular Neo-Aristotelian Naturalism / Virtue Ethics vs. v6

## 1. School and voice

This is the school of Philippa Foot, Rosalind Hursthouse, Michael Thompson, John McDowell and Martha Nussbaum. Goodness is measured against the form of life of one's kind. Justice is bipolar: a wrong is done *to* someone, and she has the complaint. In Round 5 this school reported "As written: NONE" with four Minor points and one optional point (R5.1–R5.5). This round checks whether v6 took them, and whether v6's new wording broke anything.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

*(The Round 1–5 answers stand. Briefly:)*
- **Q1. Justice.** Each is given what is due to her, and the wrong is done to her (Thompson; NE V). **Holds.** It borrows only the *equality* of all parties.
- **Q2. A hard case.** Do not take a ventilator from a stable disabled patient to give it to a younger one. That is doing harm, not failing to help (Foot). **Coherent.**
- **Q3. Equal dignity.** Membership in the rational kind, plus the shape of justice. **Holds** for equality and for "not for others." **Breaks**, by the Church's lights, on chosen death (Foot, 1977).
- **Q4. Ultimate good.** *Eudaimonia*: a complete life of virtuous activity among friends, bounded by death. **Coherent.** It is the strongest secular reason to deny that W3's longing belongs to human nature.

### 3b. Exchanges

**E1. A4's new placement of "not for the many."**
*Opponent:* You moved the phrase, but not off the board. "Never to be used as a mere means, not for the many and not for the few" is still among the shared premises. A threshold deontologist would use a person as a mere means to stop a catastrophe.
*Defense:* The parenthesis that closes the list names the numbers question: "Whether that line holds however vast the numbers … A6 names as this book's claims." "The many" means the five, the crowd, the town. "However vast" means the city and the war. A6 draws the same line: "even those who would cross the line for a city will not cross it for five."
*Opponent's rejoinder:* For this school the point is idle anyway. Foot and Anscombe hold the line at catastrophe too. Hursthouse lets it bend, but she says a remainder is owed, which concedes that a wrong was done.
*Result:* **Held as written.** (Optional: "that line" → "those lines," since the numbers question now attaches to two clauses. This is optional, not a defect.)

**E2. Does the waiver come back through the new trust image?**
*Opponent:* "Given to her keeping, not to her disposal" makes her the steward, which is right. But a trustee in law may resign his trust. If she may not destroy the life, may she hand back the office?
*Defense:* Handing the stewardship back is not something the steward can do by her own act. Only the One who appoints can receive it. That is exactly what the next sentences say: "He who gives life, and can give it back, may call it home; we, who can do neither, may not take it," and He "remains its sovereign Master" (CCC 2280). Resignation is His calling-home, not her taking. The image now argues for the claim it is meant to support.
*Result:* **Held.** The fault from Round 5 (God as trustee, her as beneficiary) is gone, and nothing new opens in its place.

**E3. "Nor 'for his own good' without his asking."**
*Opponent:* This is new in A4. Is it shared, or does it smuggle in the Church's line on euthanasia?
*Defense:* It is shared with this school's founder. Foot (1977) held that justice forbids active non-voluntary euthanasia, because it violates the right to life, even when charity might favor it. The words "without his asking" leave the requested death to A6, which labels it as this book's claim. Singer, who would allow killing some infants for their own good, is already listed in the A heading as denying parts of A4.
*Result:* **Held.** The clause is precise. The scare quotes mark "for his own good" as the killer's claim, not the book's.

**E4. "Never spends her as a means (GS 24)."**
*Opponent:* This is new in W1(2), and it drops the word "mere." Elsewhere the book says "never to be used as a *mere* means" (A4) and "God never spends anyone as a *mere* means against her own good" (Evil, *Second*). That section also grants that suffering bears fruit for others (Col 1:24), and it cites CCC 306, under which creatures are "causes for one another." On this school's view of friendship, being useful to another is no wrong. The friend is useful and is also loved for his own sake. Read strictly, "never … as a means" denies something the book affirms two sections later.
*Defense:* "Spends" carries the sense of using a person up at the cost of her own good, so the sentence is not false as most readers will take it. But the precise formula is the one the book uses everywhere else, and one word restores it.
*Result:* **Held with repair (Minor)** (R6.1).

**E5. Kolbe, double effect, and this school's founder.**
*Opponent:* v6 now draws the line by what the will chooses. That is double effect. Foot was its best-known critic (1967): the closeness problem, and the cave explorers blasting out the man who is stuck. She preferred to draw the line between doing and allowing, and between negative and positive duties. So the book rests its martyr line on a principle this school doubts.
*Defense:* Then test it on her distinction. On doing and allowing, Kolbe does not kill himself; the guards kill him. The firefighter does not kill himself; the fire does. Assisted suicide and the lethal experiment are cases of someone killing, and v6 names both: "by his own hand or another's," and "no one may kill him because he asks." So Foot's distinction and the book's give the same verdicts on every case in the text. The closeness problem reaches only the edges of what a volunteer intends. The last clause binds the one who kills on grounds of authority ("no number of others has any right over her life"), whatever the volunteer intends.
*Result:* **Held at parity.** A correction to my own Round 5 report: there I called intention "this school's own distinction." It belongs to Anscombe and, with qualifications, to Hursthouse, not to Foot. The book does not say whose it is, so no wording needs to change.

**E6. "No voice that claims His name can make it ours to take."**
*Opponent:* This is new in v6. In Round 4 I pressed Aquinas's own exception: when God commands a killing, as with Abraham, the one who obeys does not sin (ST II-II q.64 a.6 ad 1; I-II q.100 a.8 ad 3). Does the new clause contradict him?
*Defense:* No. The clause says no voice can make it *ours* to take. On Aquinas's own account, a man who acts at God's true command acts as God's instrument, so the life is still not his to take; it is the Lord of life who calls it home. The clause adds what CCC 2258 says: no one may claim for himself that right. That is a guard against everyone who kills in God's name, and it costs nothing in Aquinas.
*Opponent:* This school welcomes it. A secular reader's first fear about a Lawgiver is that his followers will kill on His word.
*Result:* **Held.** The clause closes the Round 4 point more cleanly than v5 did.

**E7. "What the trust forbids is that her death be the thing aimed at" (CCC 2278–2279).**
*Opponent:* Foot allowed withholding treatment when the life is no longer a good to the one who lives it, with death as the point, for charity's sake. Does v6's new sentence pretend that the two views agree here?
*Defense:* No. It says what the trust allows (refusing treatment that only prolongs dying; relief from pain even if it shortens life) and what it forbids (aiming at death). Foot would allow more than that, and the flourishing row says so and labels the Church's reply as "this book's claim, A6."
*Result:* **Held at parity.** This is the one real moral disagreement between this school and the Church, and v6 now states it at its true size. The new sentence also answers a fair charge before anyone makes it: the trust is not a duty to prolong every dying at any cost.

**E8. "He is offended by us … only because we act against our own good" (SCG III.122).**
*Opponent:* Placed right after "His love refused in her," this could be read to mean that the offense against God lies in the wrongdoer harming *himself*. That would turn the wrong back toward the agent's eudaimonia and away from her.
*Defense:* In Aquinas, "our good" is the human good, hers included, and the book glosses it as "the good He wills for us." The same paragraph has already said that guilt is owed "to her, truly and in her own right, and not to God alone." So the SCG line explains why there is an offense against God; it does not change where the wrong lies.
*Result:* **Held.** No repair is needed. (This sentence was also in v5; I test it here only because it sits beside the R4.5 wording.)

**E9. Smaller points.**
(a) God row: "rest on a standing they take as given." W1(2) gives two answers: "simply what justice is" and "the standing of one who may not be violated." The row compresses them. For Foot, a right just is a standing, so the compression is fair. **Held.**
(b) Flourishing row: "the Church would say" and "this book's claim, A6" mark the diagnosis as the book's own. This is fairer than v5's version. **Held.**
(c) W1 frame: "the wrong is wrong of necessity, not up to anyone." This school would say the norm is necessary relative to our form of life, which is still "not up to anyone." The sentence is unchanged from v5 and holds.
(d) Evil: "if our nature is a wisdom and not an accident (W2), so is theirs." This is honest. It puts the lion's form on the book's side of the scale. **Held.**

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**Against v6 as written:**

**As written: NONE.**

v6 took all five of this school's Round 5 repairs, two of them in better form than proposed. None of the new wording breaks a step. The trust image now supports the claim it is meant to support (E2). The Kolbe line agrees, case by case, with this school's founder's own doing/allowing distinction (E5). The new A4 clause is one Foot held (E3). The new "no voice that claims His name" fits Aquinas and answers a fear this school shares (E6). The one slip is a dropped word.

**Minor (wording and precision):**
1. **W1(2) "never spends her as a means (GS 24)"** drops the "mere" that A4 and the Evil section keep. Read strictly, it denies that God ever orders one person to another's good, which the book affirms (Col 1:24; CCC 306) (E4).
2. *(Optional)* **A4 "that line"** → "those lines," since the numbers question now attaches to both the mere-means clause and the killing clause (E1).

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**
- Whether a nature's standards need a source, or whether asking for one wants a standpoint outside the life of reason (McDowell).
- Whether necessary truths, the core of the moral law among them, need a ground in a Mind.
- Whether the reach of minds to what holds for any mind needs explaining beyond evolution (W2), and whether the fit of the goods is explained by one nature or by one Source.
- The tragic view of the dead (a wrong fixed forever that no one receives) against "she is still someone" (W1(1)).
- Whether the shape of justice that protects her is simply "what justice is" or inviolable standing, or is explained by a title no number of others has, from One who wills each for her own sake.
- Whether intention (double effect) or doing and allowing draws the moral line better. On every case in the text they give the same verdict (E5).
- Whether a life that is no longer a good to the one who lives it may be ended, or its ending aimed at, at his request: Foot against CCC 2280. This remains the one real moral disagreement between this school and the Church, and v6 now states it at its true size (E7).
- Whether W3's longing belongs to human nature or is a Christian shaping of the wish for more.
- The theist's heavier load on the lion and the parasite, against the naturalist's weaker hold on the tortured child.

## 7. New insights and lines worth keeping

**New insights**
- **The steward cannot resign; the Master can call home.** This answers the trust-law rejoinder in one move. It shows why "keeping, not disposal" and "He … may call it home" belong together (E2).
- **The Kolbe line survives Foot's doubts about double effect.** On doing and allowing, which Foot preferred, Kolbe and the firefighter are not killers, and the euthanasia doctor and the lethal experimenter are. So the book's line does not depend on winning the debate over double effect. Worth knowing if a critic raises Foot's cave case (E5).
- **"Ours to take" folds Aquinas's Abraham exception in without conceding anything.** Even at God's true command, the life is not the agent's to take, because the agent is God's instrument. Keep the possessive (E6).
- **Keep "mere" wherever "means" appears.** On this school's view of friendship, being useful to another is part of a good life. The wrong is only in being *merely* used (E4).

**Lines worth keeping**
- "Gave her life to her keeping and not to her disposal."
- "A man may lay down his life for another … and bear the death that comes, even at the hands of those who wrongly take it."
- "What the trust forbids is that her death be the thing aimed at."
- "No voice that claims His name can make it ours to take."
- "What protects her is not a weight that enough lives could outweigh; it is that no number of others has any right over her life."
- "Wronged no less than a strong child would have been."
