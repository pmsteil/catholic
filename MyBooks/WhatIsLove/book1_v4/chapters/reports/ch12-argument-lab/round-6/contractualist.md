# Round 6: Contractualism and the Second-Personal Standpoint vs. v6

## 1. School and voice

The voice is a composite of T. M. Scanlon (a wrong is what could not be justified to the one it is done to), Stephen Darwall (obligation is the standing of free and equal persons to make claims on one another), John Rawls, and R. Jay Wallace (guilt and blame as the attitudes by which we hold one another to account). H. L. A. Hart's will theory of rights, Locke's "workmanship" argument as Darwall reads it, and Scanlon's later case (*Moral Dimensions*) that an agent's intention does not settle whether an act is permissible are the sharpest tools against v6's new wording. All positions are paraphrased; there are no direct quotations.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

1. **Justice.** What anyone is owed follows from principles that no one affected could reasonably reject. *Holds. It presupposes the equal standing it then applies.*
2. **A hard case.** The TRC amnesty (*AZAPO*, 1996; verify): a state may waive punishment, but it cannot forgive what was done to Biko. *Holds, and it supports A3. It has no account of mercy as a gift that heals.*
3. **Equal dignity.** Being born of human parents is reason to count her among those we answer to, through a trustee. *The scope holds. The ground is a reason that "simply holds."*
4. **Ultimate good.** Mutual recognition in a social union of social unions. *Coherent, and it ends at death.*

**Exchange 1. "Remains its sovereign Master": does the landlord come back?**
*Contractualist:* In Round 4 I asked that the trust be held *for her*, and v5 said God "holds her life for her, never against her." v6 drops that and says God "remains its sovereign Master." Locke's workmanship argument says we are His property because we are His workmanship. Darwall's charge against Locke and Pufendorf is that this makes the victim the second creditor in her own case. "Master" is the landlord's word.
*Defense:* "Sovereign Master" is CCC 2280's own phrase, in the same paragraph that says we are "stewards, not owners." More to the point, v6 now gives the Master a single job. The protection against others is "no number of others has any right over her life," which is a claim about *her* standing against everyone else (R2). The frame still governs the order of the wrong: "not a second injury beside hers ... His love refused in her," and "the wrong is wrong because of what it does to her, and so it offends Him." The Master explains only why the line is not hers to waive. The "for her" direction is kept by "willed her for her own sake" and "never spends her" (GS 24).
*Contractualist:* Then the two jobs I asked v5 to separate are separated in the text: no one else has standing to spend her, and she holds her life in keeping, not as an owner. A Master who never spends her and who willed her for her own sake is not Locke's landlord. Locke's God may use His property for His own ends; this one, by the text, may not use her against her.
*Verdict: Held.* The new wording does not reopen the title reading.

**Exchange 2. "Given to her keeping, not to her disposal" against the will theory.**
*Contractualist:* Hart's will theory says a right is a power to waive. If she has the right not to be killed, she may waive it. v5's "held for her" was in fact weaker against Hart than v6's wording. A trust held *for* a sole adult beneficiary is, in English law, one she can call in and end (*Saunders v Vautier*, 1841; verify). The Aristotelian saw this in Round 5. Let me test the new wording instead: is "keeping, not disposal" just an unexplained restriction on her power?
*Defense:* It is the shape of an inalienable right, and the will theory is known to have trouble with those. MacCormick's objection is that children, and anyone holding a right she cannot waive, have rights that a power-to-waive account cannot model (verify). The trust model is the interest-and-status answer: she has a real standing against everyone else, enforceable on her behalf, but not the power to dispose, because the life was given to her keeping by Someone. The text says why, which the will theory does not, and it says so in a sentence the reader can accept or reject.
*Contractualist:* My school does not take the will theory as given either. Scanlon would ask whether a principle allowing a dying adult's request could be reasonably rejected by anyone, and many of us think not. That is the euthanasia dispute, and A6 labels it "this book's claims, argued in W1(2), not shared ones."
*Verdict: Held.* The v6 reversal (God gives, she keeps) is an improvement on my own Round 4 wording, which made her the beneficiary of a trust she could end.

**Exchange 3. CCC 2278–2279: does the right to refuse treatment concede the waiver?**
*Contractualist:* v6 adds: "She may refuse treatment that only prolongs her dying, and be given relief from pain even if it shortens her days (CCC 2278–2279)." So she does have a power over her life: she may refuse what would keep her alive, and the patient's own decision governs (CCC 2278; verify the wording). If she may decline what keeps her alive, why may she not ask for what ends it? You have granted Hart half his premise.
*Defense:* She has a power over what is done *to her body*: no one may force a burdensome treatment on her, and a doctor who did so would wrong her. That is a standing against others, which your school understands better than most. What she lacks is a power to make her death the object of anyone's choice. The text names the line exactly: "what the trust forbids is that her death be the thing aimed at." Refusing a burden aims at freedom from the burden; asking to be killed aims at death.
*Contractualist:* This helps your second-personal reading more than it hurts it. It shows that the trust gives her real authority that others must honor, not only a Master's claim over her. Whether "aimed at" can carry the moral weight is the next exchange.
*Verdict: Held.* The added sentence makes the trust less paternal, not more.

**Exchange 4. "The thing aimed at": Scanlon on intention and permissibility.**
*Contractualist:* Scanlon argues in *Moral Dimensions* (verify) that what an agent intends does not settle whether his act is permissible. It bears on the *meaning* of the act, on what it shows about his attitude to the person, but permissibility turns on the reasons available to him. Judith Thomson argues the same. On that view the doctor who gives morphine foreseeing that it will shorten a dying patient's days, and the doctor who gives a lethal dose at the patient's request, may do the same thing as far as permissibility goes. v6's whole line between CCC 2279 and CCC 2277 rests on the double effect distinction.
*Defense:* It does, and the text does not hide it: the line is labeled as this book's claim, not a shared premise (A6, A heading). Double effect is not a private Catholic doctrine. Quinn, Boyle, FitzPatrick and others defend it in the secular literature, and Kamm defends nearby distinctions (verify). Scanlon's own concession matters here. If intention bears on the meaning of an act, on what it says about the person to whom it is done, then a trust that governs what may be *chosen* about her life is exactly a constraint on meaning: it forbids making her death the thing her life is used to produce.
*Contractualist:* That is a fair standoff. My school splits on double effect, and the text claims no more for it than "this book argues."
*Verdict: Held, at parity.* Listed under standoffs.

**Exchange 5. Kolbe: "no one may kill him because he asks."**
*Contractualist:* Kolbe asked the commandant to take him instead of the condemned man, and he was killed because he asked. Your sentence says "no one may kill him because he asks." So either Kolbe's killers did what is permitted, or your sentence condemns the request you praise. Worse, his death was the very means by which the other man was spared. By double effect's own test, was it not intended?
*Defense:* The first horn is closed in the text: he bore the death "at the hands of those who wrongly take it." The killers were never permitted to kill anyone; Kolbe's request did not make their act right, and the sentence does not say it did. On the second: what Kolbe chose was to take the other man's place, not to die. Apply the test for what is aimed at: if the guards had then spared both men, would his purpose have failed? No; he would have had everything he asked for. If a dying man who asks to be killed is not killed, his purpose fails. That is what "make his own death the thing he chooses" means, and Kolbe did not do it.
*Contractualist:* Granted. The test is clean, and it separates Kolbe from the requested killing without resting on how the guards described their own act. The sentence as written holds; the test itself would be worth a line in the chapter.
*Verdict: Held.* The Kolbe sentence fixes Round 5's Serious item and does not break the euthanasia line.

**Exchange 6. "Never spends her as a means": a word stronger than the rest of the book.**
*Contractualist:* W1(2) says God "never spends her as a means (GS 24)." The evil section says "God never spends anyone as a *mere* means against her own good," and then grants that suffering bears fruit for others (Colossians 1:24) and that He gives His creatures "the dignity of being causes for one another" (CCC 306). Kant forbids using persons *merely* as means; using a person as a means is ordinary and innocent. As written, W1(2) says something stronger than the evil section, stronger than Kant, and stronger than GS 24, which says God willed man for his own sake, not that He never makes one person a means to another's good. A reader who sets the two sentences side by side finds the book disagreeing with itself.
*Defense:* "Spends" does most of the work: to spend is to use up, and the text means God never uses her up for others. But the evil section's wording is the careful one, and W1(2) should match it.
*Verdict: Held with repair (Minor).* Add "mere" (section 6, R1).

**Exchange 7. A4's mere-means clause at every number.**
*Contractualist:* v6 moved "not for the many and not for the few" to the mere-means clause. Is that clause shared at every number? On Parfit's reading of the Mere Means Principle (verify), you treat someone *merely* as a means only if you give no weight to his good or his consent. A threshold deontologist who would hang the innocent man to save a million, and grieves doing it, does not treat him *merely* as a means on that reading. So "never ... a mere means, not for the many" can be shared even by those who let the killing line bend, and the parenthesis "whether that line holds however vast the numbers" correctly points the killing line to A6.
*Defense:* That is the intended reading, and A6's "even those who would cross the line for a city will not cross it for five" supports it.
*Verdict: Held.* My school holds this clause without reservation.

**Exchange 8. The conclusion: "wronged no less than a strong child would have been."**
*Contractualist:* My school does not grade worth by mind; Scanlon counts the newborn as one of us because she is born of human parents, a fact of kind. So I grant the step. But the inference as written has a gap that another reader will use. The strong child left on the same hillside is also a newborn, also "not yet able to know herself." The comparison varies strength and holds mind fixed. So "no less than a strong child" supports "her worth never followed her strength," but not "or her mind," and it does not reach "she is that as fully as you are." A reader on McMahan's time-relative-interest view grants that the two infants were wronged alike, and denies that either was wronged as gravely as you would be.
*Defense:* The concessive "though she was weak and not yet able to know herself" means to set her against a child who has both, but the hillside scene makes "a strong child" read as a strong newborn. Add a second comparison that varies mind, and the reader must choose; the text already gives him the honest way out: "or that it was a lesser wrong."
*Verdict: Held with repair (Minor from this school).* It does not touch this school's view, and the case does not rest on the paragraph, which says it "cannot compel" this reader. Schools that grade worth by mind may rate it higher. Wording in section 6 (R2).

**Exchange 9. A quick check of the rest of v6 as it touches this school.**
- *Contractualism row:* unchanged, and still the fairest statement of this school in the lab, including the trustee sentence. *Held.*
- *W1 frame:* "the realist, the contractualist, the Aristotelian and the Buddhist among them, answer that guilt is owed to the one we wronged." Accurate. Job 35:6–8 for "nothing harms God" strengthens "not a second injury beside hers." *Held.*
- *W1(2):* "others, that a person has the standing of one who may not be violated." That is Darwall's and Kamm's ground stated fairly. "No one may take what is not his to take, even to keep others from taking more" is the constraint-versus-minimizing point my school accepts. *Held.*
- *W1(2):* "no voice that claims His name can make it ours to take (CCC 2258)." My school welcomes the sentence: no claimed command overrides what can be justified to her. Whether a real divine command could authorize a killing (ST II-II q.64 a.6 ad 1) is the problem-of-evil school's question; from here it falls under the parity standoff on a Creator's non-equal standing. *Held.*
- *Evil, para. 2:* "God gives the act its being, never its defect" does not touch this school. "His permission never excuses ours (CCC 2269)" is the right thing for a contractualist to hear. *Held.*

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**As written: NONE.**

No Fatal or Serious objection from this school stands against v6 as written. Every Round 5 repair was taken, three word for word and one improved. The new wording this school was most likely to attack, "sovereign Master," "keeping, not disposal," CCC 2278–2279 and the Kolbe sentence, holds under pressure from Hart, Locke-as-Darwall-reads-him, and Scanlon on intention. Taken together, those additions make the trust *less* like a landlord's title than v5's wording, not more.

**Minor (wording; none changes a conclusion):**
1. **W1(2), "and never spends her as a means (GS 24)."** This is stronger than the evil section's "a mere means against her own good," stronger than Kant, and in tension with Colossians 1:24 and CCC 306, which the book itself cites (Exchange 6). One word.
2. **Conclusion, "wronged no less than a strong child would have been."** The comparison varies strength but holds mind fixed, so the inference to "or her mind" and to "as fully as you are" is invalid against a mind-graded reader (McMahan, Singer) (Exchange 8). This school grades by neither, so from here it is Minor; the Realist rates it Serious, and the Debunker rates it Minor. One clause fixes it.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects; v6 names nearly all of them):**
- Whether an agent's intention bears on permissibility (double effect) or only on an act's meaning (Scanlon, Thomson). v6's line between CCC 2279 and CCC 2277 rests on it, and it is labeled as this book's claim.
- Whether the competent, suffering adult may ask for death. This is the euthanasia dispute, labeled and argued, not assumed (A6, W1(2)).
- Whether an inalienable right needs a Giver to explain why it cannot be waived, or can be explained by reasonable rejection alone (the trust versus Scanlon).
- The dead are owed forever as they were (road two), or each is still someone who can forgive (road three).
- Whether "each must be answered one by one" needs a ground beyond the worth of mutual recognition.
- Whether truths about reasons need a home (W2; the realist standoff).
- Whether a Creator's standing, which is not equal to ours, can still be second-personal ("may call it home"; Genesis 18:25; Job), and, with it, whether a real divine command could authorize what no human voice can.
- Whether the good "offered" to each sufferer is kept: an ex ante promise this school cannot inspect.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact wording)

**R1. W1(2)** (one word). Replace "and never spends her as a means (GS 24)." with:
> and never spends her as a mere means (GS 24).

**R2. Conclusion** (one clause). Replace "and wronged no less than a strong child would have been, though she was weak and not yet able to know herself;" with:
> and wronged no less than a strong child would have been, or than you would have been, though she was weak and not yet able to know herself;

(The rest of the paragraph stands as written. "Or that it was a lesser wrong" already gives the graded reader his honest way out, and "she is that as fully as you are" now follows from a comparison that includes you.)

## 7. New insights for the book

- **The reversal of the trust is right, and it is right for a reason a lawyer would give.** A trust held *for* her makes her the beneficiary, and a sole adult beneficiary can end a trust. A life given *to her keeping* makes her the keeper, with real standing against everyone else and no power to dispose. That is the shape of an inalienable right, which the will theory of rights has never been able to model.
- **CCC 2278–2279 shows the trust gives her authority, not only a duty.** She may refuse a burden, and a doctor who forces it on her wrongs her. A rival who fears that "stewardship" means paternalism should be shown this sentence.
- **The Kolbe test.** Ask whether he would have had everything he asked for if they had spared them both. Kolbe would have; the man who asks to be killed would not. That one question separates laying down a life from choosing one's death, without leaning on anyone's description of the act.
- **Two jobs, now kept apart in the text.** No one else holds a right over her life (which this school can say), and she holds it in keeping, not as an owner (which only the Giver explains). This is the cleanest place in the book where a contractualist can agree with the first half and see exactly what he lacks for the second.

**Lines worth keeping:**
- From v6: "Gave her life to her keeping and not to her disposal."
- From v6: "What protects her is not a weight that enough lives could outweigh; it is that no number of others has any right over her life."
- From v6: "What the trust forbids is that her death be the thing aimed at."
- From v6: "He may lay down his life ... but he may not make his own death the thing he chooses."
- From v6: "Rest on a standing they take as given."
- New: "Had they spared them both, Kolbe would have had all he asked."
