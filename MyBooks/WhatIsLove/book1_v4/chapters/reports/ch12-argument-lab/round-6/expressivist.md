# Round 6: Expressivism / Quasi-Realism vs. v6

## 1. School and voice

The voices are Hume (*Treatise* Book 3; *Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals*, EPM; *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, EHU; *Dialogues*; "Of Suicide"), Adam Smith (*Theory of Moral Sentiments*, TMS), the emotivists Ayer and Stevenson, and the quasi-realists Blackburn and Gibbard. On this view moral judgment expresses attitudes and plans, refined by information, consistency and sympathy; the quasi-realist then earns "true," "wrong of itself" and "owed to her" at first order. This round checks whether v6 took my Round 5 repairs, then tests v6's new wording (A4, A6 on Kolbe, the trust in W1(2), the evil section's causation sentence and Mary, the conclusion's "no less") for anything this school can soundly press or that misstates it.

## 2. The four standing challenges (short)

**1. Justice.** Hume: conventions approved through sympathy with public utility; Smith: fitting resentment of injury to the individual. *Coherent; breaks at the edge, as Hume admits* (the weak are owed gentleness, not justice, EPM §3 pt 1). v6's C row and its conclusion now both name that edge.

**2. A hard case.** The secret told after the friend's death (A2). Hume's theory of promising misses the wrong; the quasi-realist finds it at first order by commitment. *Holds in result; the founding theory breaks on the reason.*

**3. Equal dignity.** A stance of equal regard, held as not depending on our attitudes (Blackburn's rigidified attitudes). *Coherent; declines to ground.* At parity on proof; parts from the book on the further why.

**4. The ultimate good.** Plural happiness and a life one can review with approval. *Coherent and smaller.* Hume's calm death shows W3's longing can be borne, not that it is answered.

### Exchange 1. Kolbe and "the thing he chooses": can Hume pry the distinction open?

*Expressivist:* A6 now says a man may lay down his life "and bear the death that comes, even at the hands of those who wrongly take it, as Christ did and as St. Maximilian Kolbe did," but "may not make his own death the thing he chooses, by his own hand or another's, and no one may kill him because he asks." Hume's heirs, Sidgwick among them, deny that the line between what is aimed at and what is foreseen with certainty carries moral weight. Kolbe knew he would die in the bunker; he asked to. The volunteer for a lethal experiment also knows he will die and also does not aim at death; he aims at the cure. A6 lists him among those the book will not "let." Either Kolbe did what the volunteer does, or the line is drawn by a scholastic distinction this school does not grant.

*Patrick's side:* The sentence is about the one who kills, not only the one who dies. Kolbe did not kill himself, and no one killed him rightly: the guards "wrongly take it." The volunteer is killed by an experimenter who does a lethal act on an innocent man's body as the means to his result, and that is exactly what "no one may kill him because he asks" forbids; CCC 2295 says the subject's consent does not justify research that is contrary to the dignity of the person or exposes his life to disproportionate risk (paraphrase). A6's word is "let": the book faults the one who would permit and perform it. So Kolbe and the volunteer differ in the act of the one who kills, not only in the volunteer's intention. The aim/foresight distinction does carry weight for the dying man's own will, and there the book is open that it is its claim: "Those are this book's claims, argued in W1(2), not shared ones."

*Expressivist:* Then the passage is consistent: the guards and the experimenter both wrong the man; Kolbe and the volunteer may differ only in what each one wills, and the book does not say the volunteer sins. My denial that aim versus foresight matters is a first-order disagreement, and it sits where the text already places it, among the book's claims.

*Verdict: HELD at parity.* The new Kolbe wording fixes the Round 5 Serious objection without opening a new one. The aim/foresight question joins "whose life is it?" as an honest standoff.

### Exchange 2. A4's new clauses: are they shared?

*Expressivist:* A4 now reads "never to be used as a mere means, not for the many and not for the few; never, if innocent, to be deliberately killed for others' sake, nor 'for his own good' without his asking." Last round the utilitarian showed that "not for the many" re-shared the every-number line. Has it simply moved?

*Patrick's side:* "Mere" carries it. To use someone as a *mere* means is to treat her own ends as counting for nothing. Even the theories that would cross the killing line at vast numbers say they do not do that: they count her. Hume says the same of the weak: they are owed gentle usage under the laws of humanity. And the parenthesis that follows ("Whether that line holds however vast the numbers ... A6 names as this book's claims") scopes the every-number reading of the killing line.

*Expressivist:* Granted. On "mere," Hume and Smith both share the clause; Hume's suspension of justice in extreme necessity concerns property and conventions, not treating a person as nothing. "Nor 'for his own good' without his asking" is shared by Hume too ("Of Suicide" defends a man's own choice, never another's choice for him). Singer's denial is already named in the A heading. The implication that asking might change things is cancelled at once by the parenthesis.

*Verdict: HOLDS.*

### Exchange 3. The trust with CCC 2278–2279 against "Of Suicide"'s reductio

*Expressivist:* Hume's reductio was that if only God may dispose of life, then every turn we give to nature's course, from shielding ourselves from a falling stone to refusing a bleeding, trespasses too. In Round 5 I logged that the book's care/disposal distinction met it at parity. Does v6 keep that?

*Patrick's side:* v6 makes it explicit: "She may refuse treatment that only prolongs her dying, and be given relief from pain even if it shortens her days (CCC 2278–2279); what the trust forbids is that her death be the thing aimed at." And the trustee language is now the right way round: God "remains its sovereign Master," and gave her life "to her keeping and not to her disposal." The reductio needs the trust to forbid every intervention; it forbids one aim.

*Expressivist:* That narrows the line to exactly where my school and yours really part, whether a man may aim at his own death to end his suffering, and it removes the cheap form of Hume's argument. The "keeping, not disposal" reversal also closes the gap the Aristotelian found (a beneficiary may claim a trust held for her).

*Verdict: HOLDS, and stronger than v5.*

### Exchange 4. "No voice that claims His name can make it ours to take"

*Expressivist:* Hume's *Natural History of Religion* (paraphrase, verify) charges popular religion with putting frivolous observances and even cruelty in the place of virtue, on the word of a deity. A Lawgiver can be invoked to license killing.

*Patrick's side:* That is why v6 adds this clause, under CCC 2258. It follows from the anti-voluntarism in W1's closing paragraph ("A will that invents values can command, but it cannot bind"; "He did not choose whether cruelty is evil") and from Chapter 3. On this account a command to kill the innocent "in His name" refutes itself. And the fairness rule cuts both ways: secular authorities have licensed killing in the name of the nation or the future. The abuse of an authority does not settle whether it is real.

*Expressivist:* Agreed. The clause turns Hume's historical charge into a test the book passes on its own terms.

*Verdict: HOLDS.*

### Exchange 5. "God gives the act its being, never its defect" against Hume's EHU §8 dilemma

*Expressivist:* The censor's fix walks into Hume's dilemma (EHU §8 pt 2, paraphrase, verify). If the Deity is the first cause of every act, then either the acts have no turpitude, or the Deity shares their guilt. You now say God "gives the act its being" and "is in no way, directly or indirectly, the cause of moral evil." That is the second horn, refused by a distinction.

*Patrick's side:* It is the distinction the Church and St. Thomas draw (CCC 311; ST I-II q.79 a.2, where the defect in a sinful act is compared to the defect in a limp: the power to walk comes from the walker's strength, the crookedness from the leg, verify). A defect is a lack, and a lack needs no giver. Hume did not refute this. He said reconciling God's causing all things with His not being the author of sin is beyond philosophy's power (paraphrase, verify). And his own answer to the *first* horn sides with the book: moral sentiment fixes on the character and act before it, whatever the remote causes, so the turpitude stays in the act and with the agent. The book does not use the distinction to answer *why* God permits. It puts that on the scale ("He permits it"; "Why He did not keep the first man from sinning ... the Church answers only with what He brought from it").

*Expressivist:* Then it is a standoff that Hume himself called a standoff, and the book does not claim more than the distinction gives.

*Verdict: HELD at parity.* There is an insight in §7: Hume's answer to his own first horn is a secular witness to "the evil in what a free creature does is his own."

### Exchange 6. Mary, and Philo's question

*Expressivist:* Philo would seize on the new sentence: if God "kept Mary free of every sin her whole life long," then a world of free creatures who never sin was within His power. The free-will defense loses its ground.

*Patrick's side:* v6 concedes it outright and does not use free will to answer it: "the Church answers only with what He brought from it: Christ's grace gave us more than the devil's envy took away (CCC 412)." Then: "whether it is worth this world's cost is part of the weighing, not settled by it." Free will is used for whose evil it is ("it is ours, not His"), not for why it was permitted.

*Expressivist:* That is Mackie's 1955 point stated by the book against itself and left on the scale. Philo cannot ask for more honesty than that.

*Verdict: HOLDS.*

### Exchange 7. W3's Smith sentence: substance right, punctuation wrong

*Expressivist:* The substance is mine and it is fair. But v6 joined it to the sentence before with a semicolon: "That tells us how we long, not whether there is Someone to long for; Adam Smith traced how, when every human judge misjudges us, we appeal to the all-seeing Judge; that explains a longing to be judged truly." Read aloud, three clauses run together, and Smith reads as part of the claim about "Someone to long for," which is not what he shows.

*Patrick's side:* We could also be pressed on substance: attachment theory explains a child's longing for acceptance before she earns anything. Does "It does not as easily explain" survive that?

*Expressivist:* It does, because "It" refers to Smith's appeal to the Judge, not to every path. The sentence before already gives attachment its due ("attachment, the wish not to die ... That tells us how we long"). The fix is only a full stop.

*Verdict: HELD WITH REPAIR (Minor, wording).* §6, R1.

### Exchange 8. The conclusion's "no less" and Hume's weak creatures

*Expressivist:* The conclusion now asks whether she was "wronged no less than a strong child would have been," against "any argument that says she was not, or that it was a lesser wrong." Hume's middle position, gentleness without justice, is exactly "a lesser wrong," or on his strict terms "not wronged" in justice at all. Can a sentimentalist slip between the two by saying the abandonment is *as wrong*, because it shows as much inhumanity in the one who does it, without her being *wronged* no less?

*Patrick's side:* That is the agent's-vice reading, and the text's word blocks it: "wronged" is a wrong done to her, as in A2. The vice reading is one of the "argument[s] that says she was not," and the reader is asked which he is surer of. The quasi-realist, who says in his own voice that she was wronged, can follow the step to "her worth follows what she is" at first order. The question he faces is the one in C, which is why the conclusion is addressed only to "the one who gives up A4."

*Expressivist:* Fair. Hume's position is now named in his own terms and left to the reader's weighing, and the step from "wronged" to "no less" is no longer skipped.

*Verdict: HOLDS.*

### Exchange 9. The God row's new clause and Smith

*Expressivist:* "Many of the best rivals share and rest on a standing they take as given." Smith does not rest on a standing; he rests on fitting resentment on the injured person's behalf.

*Patrick's side:* "Many" does not claim all. Smith is a rival who shares the rule on another ground, and his ground still stops where the book's question begins: when the injured one is the newborn who can feel no resentment, the impartial spectator has to resent on her behalf.

*Verdict: HOLDS.* (The optional Smith clause for W1(2), §6, would make that visible.)

### Exchange 10 (our side presses). Where do Ayer and Stevenson sit?

*Patrick's side:* The C row "Feeling, refined" names Hume, Smith and the quasi-realists. Your school's twentieth-century founders, Ayer and Stevenson, held that moral sentences state nothing true or false. Is the book unfair to leave them out?

*Expressivist:* No. The C heading states each rival "as its best defenders state it," and the best defenders of expressivism are the quasi-realists, who earned back "true." Ayer's bare emotivism pays the costs of the first row ("Nothing really binds"): torturing a child for fun is not really wrong in any sense that could be true. And the A heading lists deniers as examples, not as a census. Naming Ayer would be a courtesy, not a repair.

*Verdict: Not a defect.* Optional clause in §6, R3.

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**As written: NONE.**

No Fatal or Serious objection stands against v6 as written. All my Round 5 repairs that were not optional were taken, and the God row's fix is better than my wording. The new text this school can press hardest (the Kolbe sentence, the trust with CCC 2278–2279, God giving the act its being but not its defect, and Mary) either holds or meets this school at a parity it already concedes. In particular the Kolbe sentence is consistent: it faults the one who kills, and it scopes the aim/foresight distinction as the book's claim.

**Minor (wording; changes no conclusion)**
1. **W3 ¶1:** the semicolon chain "not whether there is Someone to long for; Adam Smith traced how ... Judge; that explains" reads as one clause run into the next (Ex. 7).

**Misstatements of this school:** none.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- **Is there a further "why"?** The quasi-realist's first-order answer against the book's demand for a ground (C row; W1 closing).
- **Whose life is it?** "Of Suicide" against life held in trust "to her keeping and not to her disposal," now drawn at the aim alone.
- **Aim versus foresight.** Hume's heirs deny that it carries moral weight. The book holds that it does and scopes this as its own claim (A6; Ex. 1).
- **God's causing and the creature's defect.** Hume's EHU §8 dilemma against being/defect. Hume called it beyond philosophy, and the book puts the permission on the scale.
- **The debt to the dead.** Road 2 (owed forever, never received) against road 3.
- **The coherent dissenter** (the knave). Neither side's argument compels him; the two sides differ on what he is missing.
- **The indifferent source** (*Dialogues* XI). Conceded to fit the pattern of pain, and answered only by the whole case.
- **The longing.** Hume's calm death against W3: a longing that can be borne is not thereby answered.

## 7. New insights and lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **The Kolbe sentence is right because it is about the one who kills.** Kolbe and a volunteer for a lethal experiment may intend nearly the same thing. What separates the cases on the book's account is that the guards wrongly took Kolbe's life, while the experimenter is the one the book refuses to "let." That keeps A6 consistent with CCC 2295 and keeps the aim/foresight dispute where it belongs, among the book's claims about the dying man's own will.
- **CCC 2278–2279 is the best answer to "Of Suicide."** Hume's reductio works only if the trust forbids every turn given to nature's course. v6 shows it forbids one thing, aiming at death, and permits refusing burdensome treatment and easing pain. The quarrel with Hume is now as narrow as it truly is.
- **Hume is a witness for "the evil in what a free creature does is his own."** His answer to the first horn of his own dilemma, that moral sentiment fixes on the agent's act whatever its remote cause, is a secular confirmation of the book's point, and he conceded that the second horn is a mystery.
- **"No voice that claims His name" turns the *Natural History of Religion* charge into a test the book passes.** Anti-voluntarism is not only a point about Euthyphro (Chapter 3). It is also the protection against religion used to license cruelty.
- **"Wronged no less," not "wrong no less."** The agent's-vice reading (the deed shows as much inhumanity) cannot carry the reader to "no less," because the conclusion asks about a wrong done to *her*. That small word keeps the sentimentalist's best evasion visible and fairly weighed.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "He may give his life for another; he may not make his own death the thing he chooses." (v6)
- "Given to her keeping, not to her disposal." (v6)
- "The trust forbids aiming at her death, not easing her dying."
- "No voice that claims His name can make it ours to take." (v6)
- "God gives the act its being, never its defect." (v6)
- "Wronged no less than a strong child would have been." (v6)
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it." (v5–v6; a quasi-realist can say it too, which is why it is fair.)
- "Worthiness is not existence: a judge can tell us we are worthy; only love tells us it is good that we exist."
