# Round 6: Kantian Constructivism vs. v6

## 1. School and voice

The opponent speaks for Kant (*Groundwork*, the second and third *Critiques*, *Metaphysics of Morals*, *Religion*, the Collins lectures) and his heirs Korsgaard, O'Neill, Hill and Wood. The moral law is the law of a free will as such. Humanity is an end in itself, with dignity and not price. God is a postulate of practical reason, never the ground of obligation. He logged "As written: NONE" against v4 and v5. This round he checks his three Round 5 Minor repairs and tests v6's new wording where it touches Kant: the clemency sentence, the two-selves parenthesis, CCC 2258 against Abraham, "sovereign Master … her keeping and not her disposal," and the Kolbe and consent wording in A6.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

1. **Justice.** He starts from one innate right: freedom from another's forcing choice, so far as it fits everyone's freedom under universal law (*Doctrine of Right* 6:237). *This holds and borrows nothing.*
2. **Hard case.** His case is the murderer. Kant requires execution (6:333) and allows clemency only for crimes against the sovereign (6:337). *This is coherent, but it is justice with mercy torn away. His heirs bring mercy back as respect for the convict.*
3. **Equal dignity.** Humanity, as capable of morality, has a worth above all price (4:434–435), and the vicious keep it (6:463). *This holds for the enemy. It reaches the newborn only through a practical idea of personhood (6:280–281), which is the "stretch."*
4. **Final end.** The highest good is virtue crowned with proportionate happiness. *Kant borrows openly by postulating God and immortality. His secular heirs drop the postulates and lose the dead.*

### Exchange 1: "God does what Kant's ruler may not": does the book's God commit Kant's "greatest wrong"?

- *Kantian:* My rule says a ruler may not remit punishment for what one subject did to another, because that impunity would be the greatest wrong against his subjects (6:337). v6 now says plainly that God does what my ruler may not: He forgives the sin whole. By my own reason, then, your God wrongs the victim. He grants impunity for what was done to her.
- *Patrick's side:* It is not impunity as far as she is concerned. What God forgives whole is the sin, which is "before all else" an offense against Him (CCC 1440), together with its eternal punishment. Three things stay in place. What is owed to her must still be repaired, since absolution "does not remedy all the disorders sin has caused" (CCC 1459). The temporal punishment remains (CCC 1472–1473). And grace "does not make wrong into right"; the wrongdoer does not sit beside his victim "as though nothing had happened" (*Spe Salvi* 44). God remits nothing that is hers to remit. That is what "still does not take her place" means, and W1(1) says it at length. Your own *Religion* goes further than your *Doctrine of Right*. There you ask how a just Judge could remit the debt of a man who has changed his heart, and you answer that reason may hope for it (6:72–76). Your book on religion grants what your book on law forbids to a human ruler.
- *Kantian:* Granted. My ruler is bound because he cannot see the heart and has no way to secure what she is owed except by punishing. A Judge who sees the heart and still keeps her claim is not bound in the same way. I add one thing in the book's favor. "The pardon that is hers" fits my ethics exactly: placability, the readiness to forgive, is a duty of virtue that belongs to the one wronged (*Doctrine of Virtue* 6:460–461). My ruler cannot do it for her, and neither can your God. The row is now accurate.
- **Verdict: Held.** The row states Kant's rule correctly and is honest that God goes beyond it.

### Exchange 2: "Splitting her into two selves"

- *Kantian:* The parenthesis is fair in substance, and I asked for it. But "two selves" is the critic's word, not Kant's. Kant speaks of the human being "in two different senses" or under two attributes: as a natural being with reason (*homo phaenomenon*) and as a personality with inner freedom (*homo noumenon*) (6:417–418; see 6:239). His best readers insist that this is one person seen from two standpoints, not two selves. "Splitting her" makes his answer sound like a dodge, and the fairness rule forbids that.
- *Patrick's side:* The point is fair, and the wording came from this school's own Round 5 repair. The substance stands. Kant needs the distinction because a duty to oneself would otherwise be one the self could release, and he says so. The book's ground answers the same paradox differently. Her life is given to her keeping and not to her disposal by Another, so she cannot release what she did not make.
- *Kantian:* Then change the word and nothing else. The paradox remains the place where we part, at parity.
- **Verdict: Held (parity). Minor wording (§6, repair 1).**

### Exchange 3: "No voice that claims His name can make it ours to take"

- *Kantian:* This is my answer to Abraham in my own terms. I am certain that I must not kill my son, and I can never be certain that a voice commanding it is God's (*Conflict of the Faculties* 7:63; *Religion* 6:187). The book now says in practice exactly what I say. Read strictly, however, the clause covers every voice, God's own included. Aquinas holds that one who kills the innocent at God's command does not sin (II-II q.64 a.6 ad 1; I-II q.94 a.5 ad 2). Is v6 now at odds with its own Doctor?
- *Patrick's side:* No. The key word is "ours." For Aquinas, a death God commands is God's own taking, done through an instrument. It is the Lord of life calling her home (the sentence before). It never becomes a creature's right. CCC 2258 says that no one may, under any circumstance, claim *for himself* the right to destroy an innocent human being directly. The clause keeps both truths: only He may take, and it is never ours. In practice, the Church's answer to a voice that commands an intrinsically evil act is your answer (VS 80).
- *Kantian:* Then the Abraham standoff has narrowed to theory alone. You leave the Giver free; I deny that any such freedom could be known. Neither of us would draw the knife. That is parity, and v6 has closed the practical door I asked it to close.
- **Verdict: Held (parity).** The harmony with Aquinas runs through "ours." That is a question for the censor, not this school.

### Exchange 4: "Sovereign Master … her keeping and not her disposal": heteronomy?

- *Kantian:* v5 said the trust was held "for her, never against her." v6 now says that her life is "held in trust from the One who … remains its sovereign Master," who "gave her life to her keeping and not to her disposal." This is a dominion ground: she may not end her life because Another holds title to it. A duty grounded in another's title is heteronomous. It is Locke's workmanship argument, and it makes self-destruction wrong as theft from God rather than as a wrong to the humanity in her.
- *Patrick's side:* The order is fixed in the W1 closing: "The wrong is wrong because of what it does to her, and so it offends Him who loves her." The same sentence of W1(2) says He "willed her for her own sake … and never spends her as a means." He is offended only because we act against our own good (SCG III.122). His dominion is not an interest set against hers. It is the form that a received worth takes. She did not make her worth, so she cannot unmake it. Your own lectures held both of these together. You held that self-destruction degrades the humanity in one's person, and you also used the old image of the sentinel posted by God who may not leave his post until he is relieved, while insisting that God forbids it because it is abominable, not the reverse (Collins 27:369–375, paraphrase; verify).
- *Kantian:* That is my own order, and the book keeps it. The dominion clause is the religious statement of what I call the humanity in one's own person. I think the rational self is the better stopping point, and you think the Giver is. That is parity, the same as in Round 5. The change from "for her" to "keeping, not disposal" also fixes a weakness: a beneficiary could have claimed the release of a trust held for her.
- **Verdict: Held (parity).** The new wording is stronger against the beneficiary objection and no weaker against mine.

### Exchange 5: A6 on consent and Kolbe, and the A4 floor

- *Kantian:* A4 now forbids killing someone "'for his own good' without his asking," and A6 marks the line "even against a man's own request or consent" as the book's claim. I am among the "fewer still" who hold that line. One may not dispose of oneself as a mere means to escape pain, nor hand oneself over to it (6:422–423). The new A6 formula, that "he may not make his own death the thing he chooses," is close to my own test, which asks whether one's death is made the means. Kolbe is harder for me. I left open the casuistical questions of Curtius and of chosen martyrdom (6:423–424), and some of my heirs would call Kolbe's offer a choice to die.
- *Patrick's side:* The text chooses its words by what the will aims at. Kolbe chose a condemned man's life and bore the death that others wrongly gave. A6 names this as "this book's claims … not shared ones."
- *Kantian:* It is honestly scoped, and it states nothing about me that is false. Kant would have to decide the casuistical question, and the book has decided it. That is not a defect in the book.
- **Verdict: Held.**

### Exchange 6: The conclusion's "no less"

- *Kantian:* "Wronged no less than a strong child would have been … or that it was a lesser wrong." Dignity has no equivalent and no degrees (4:434–435), so I agree with the conclusion. The question also indicts Kant himself, who let the shame of an unwed mother lessen the crime of killing her newborn (6:336).
- *Patrick's side:* That is why the question is put to the reader's certainty and not to a theory. Kant's own principle condemns 6:336.
- **Verdict: Held, with an ally.**

### Exchange 7: Smaller checks on v6's new wording

- **The Kant row's other sentences** are unchanged and accurate: no one, not even God, is the author of the law (Collins 27:283; 6:227); *Religion* 6:98–99; 4:459; rational faith. *Held.*
- **W1 frame, "and so a turning away from Him" (Job 35:6–8).** Kant denies that there are ethical duties *to* God (6:443–444). That is a standoff already recorded, and the new phrase adds no new claim. *Held.*
- **W1(2), CCC 2278–2279 (refusing burdensome treatment, relief of pain).** Kant would accept the intend/foresee line here. *Held.*
- **God row, "a standing they take as given."** This is true of Kant's fact of reason (CPrR 5:31). *Held.*
- **W3, Smith's all-seeing Judge.** Kant's court of conscience before an ideal Judge (6:438–440) would be a natural companion. Adding it is optional, and nothing is broken without it. *Held.*
- **Evil, second point: "never spends anyone as a mere means against her own good."** This is the Formula of Humanity, applied correctly. *Held.*

## 5. Remaining sound objections

**As written: NONE.**

No Fatal or Serious objection from this school stands against v6 as written. v6 took both substantive Round 5 repairs and the optional CCC 2258 clause. It took the clemency repair in the censor's more accurate form, and it did not need the optional God-row credit. Its new wording breaks nothing for this school:
- "God does what Kant's ruler may not" is true, and it does not make God's forgiveness the impunity Kant forbids, because her claim is kept (CCC 1459; *Spe Salvi* 44).
- "Sovereign Master … keeping, not disposal" keeps Kant's own Collins order (wrong because of her, and so an offense to Him). It also closes the beneficiary objection that v5's "held for her" left open.
- "No voice that claims His name can make it ours to take" closes Abraham in practice. The remaining difference is theoretical and at parity.
- A6's Kolbe and consent wording is honestly scoped, and Kant is among the "fewer still."

**Minor (wording only; the argument stands without it):**
1. **W1(2), "splitting her into two selves."** Kant speaks of one human being "in two senses" (*homo noumenon* and *homo phaenomenon*, 6:417–418), not of two selves. "Splitting" makes his answer sound like a dodge, which the fairness rule forbids. This school introduced the word in its own Round 5 repair (Exchange 2).

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**
- **Postulate vs clue.** Kant holds the moral Ruler as rational faith; the book counts Him as a clue. The Kant row states this.
- **Self-standing dignity vs dignity given with being.** Both are stopping points.
- **Duties to oneself.** Kant answers with one person in two senses; the book answers with a life given "to her keeping and not to her disposal."
- **Abraham.** Both refuse in practice. Only the book leaves the Giver free to call a life home, and never as a creature's right.
- **Remission by God.** Kant's *Doctrine of Right* forbids a human ruler to remit punishment for wrongs between subjects. His *Religion* hopes for divine remission after a change of heart. The book holds that God remits the sin whole and keeps her claim.
- **Duties to God; form vs end; where necessity lives; certainty.** These are unchanged from Rounds 4 and 5.

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **Kant's *Religion* grants God what his *Doctrine of Right* forbids a human ruler.** In *Religion* 6:72–76, Kant asks how a man whose heart has changed can be freed of a debt that no one else can pay, and he concludes that reason may hope for this from a Judge who sees the heart. That is the best Kantian support for "God does what Kant's ruler may not." The ruler is bound because he cannot see the heart and cannot otherwise secure what the victim is owed. (Paraphrase; verify the page range.)
- **Placability is the victim's half.** Kant makes the readiness to forgive a duty of virtue belonging to the one wronged (*Doctrine of Virtue* 6:460–461). Kant's own system therefore has both halves of W1(1): the pardon that is hers (ethics), and what no ruler may remit in her name (right). (Paraphrase; verify.)
- **Kant's sentinel.** In the Collins lectures Kant uses the old image of the sentinel who may not leave his post until he is relieved. He also says that God forbids self-destruction because it is abominable, not the reverse (27:369–375; paraphrase, verify). This is v6's "her keeping and not her disposal" together with the W1 closing's order, in Kant's own classroom.
- **Kant's own stretch to the newborn.** Kant treats procreation as bringing a person into the world without his consent, which grounds the parents' duties (*Doctrine of Right* 6:280–281). That is how Kant stretches personhood to the newborn: by a practical idea, not by present reasoning. It is fair to call this a stretch, and the Kant row does.
- **SCG III.122 is still unverified against a critical text** (carried forward from Round 5).

**Lines worth keeping**
- "Gave her life to her keeping and not to her disposal." (v6)
- "No voice that claims His name can make it ours to take." (v6)
- "God does what Kant's ruler may not, forgiving the sin whole, and still does not take her place." (v6)
- "She cannot release what she did not make."
- "Wronged no less than a strong child would have been." (v6)
