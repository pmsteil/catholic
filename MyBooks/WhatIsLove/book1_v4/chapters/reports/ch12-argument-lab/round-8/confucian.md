# Blind review, Round 8: the Confucian school

## 1. School and voice

I speak for the Ru (儒) tradition as a whole: Confucius and Mencius on humaneness (仁 ren), rightness (義 yi) and ritual propriety (禮 li); Xunzi on a Heaven that is the constant course of nature and a Way the sages built; Zhu Xi on principle (理) that is one in all things and many in its expressions (理一分殊); Wang Yangming on the heart that is itself principle and its innate knowing (良知); Mou Zongsan on "immanent transcendence," an infinite moral heart that does not need a God set over against us; and modern readers such as Tu Weiming (the self as a centre of relationships), Ivanhoe and Angle (verify for specific claims). I am not a theist. I hold almost all of A, so my quarrel with this text is not over whether morality is real. It is over whether the moral life needs Someone besides us: someone who forgives in the victim's place, keeps the dead, and knows each person wholly. I argue that the moral life needs no such Person. The heart we share, the order of life-giving (生生) that the heart expresses, and the rites through which the living complete their bonds are enough.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** What is owed starts from the heart that cannot bear another's suffering (不忍人之心, *Mencius* 2A6). That heart is extended outward (推) through the relationships we actually stand in: parent and child, ruler and subject, husband and wife, elder and younger, friend and friend. Rightness (yi) and ritual give it measure and distinction (Xunzi's 分). **Verdict: holds, and borrows nothing.** It strains with strangers and enemies. What we owe them comes by extension and is graded. Confucius would repay injury with uprightness, not with kindness (*Analects* 14.34/36, verify numbering). The equal claim of the enemy is therefore thinner here than in A4.

**2. A hard dilemma.** Take *Mencius* 7A35. Shun is emperor, and his father Gusou kills a man. Shun may not stop the judge Gao Yao from arresting him, because the law is not his to bend. But he may not abandon his father either, because filial love (孝) is the root of ren. So he throws the empire away like a worn sandal, carries his father on his back to the seacoast, and lives there content. The principles at work are ren, yi, filial piety, discretionary weighing (權, compare *Mencius* 4A17 on the drowning sister-in-law), and the readiness to give up everything one has. **Verdict: coherent and not borrowed.** These are close counterparts to the book's justice, mercy and self-gift, reached on our own road. It also shows honestly where our justice bends: the victim's kin never see the killer answer. This case supports the text's A2/A3 claim that the victim is owed something no one else may waive.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** Mencius distinguishes nobility conferred by Heaven (天爵: humaneness, rightness, loyalty, good faith) from nobility conferred by men. What another man makes noble, he can make base again. Everyone has within himself what is truly noble (*Mencius* 6A16–17). Zhu Xi adds that every person, however dim the endowment of qi, has the whole of principle. **Verdict: holds, with a cost, and borrows only if "Heaven" is taken literally.** *Mencius* 4B19 says that what separates us from beasts is slight and that ordinary people let it go. The achieved level of dignity constantly threatens to swallow the inherent level. Our ground is also either a quasi-personal Heaven that confers (天命之謂性, *Zhongyong* 1), which is close to the text, or our species nature, which is close to the Aristotelian. For the newborn who will never think, Zhu Xi's version holds. For "never lost, even by the worst crimes," our tradition wavers.

**4. Ultimate flourishing.** The ultimate good is to become fully human: sagehood, ren complete, forming one body with Heaven, Earth and the myriad things (Cheng Hao, Wang Yangming). In the social order, it is the Great Unity (大同) in which widows, orphans, the childless and the disabled are all cared for (*Liji*, "Liyun"). The guiding principles are ren, yi, li, wisdom, good faith and sincerity (誠), with filial piety as the root. **Verdict: coherent.** It breaks only at one point, and the text finds that point. For the one who will never think, sagehood cannot be reached in this life, and there is no other life. Her good is real only as being cared for, so her end is passive in a way the Catholic "seeing given" (CCC 1250, 1261) is not.

## 3. Debate highlights

**E1. Shame and guilt (A2).**
*Attack:* A2 sets "shame, left to itself, says I am less" against guilt, which faces her. That repeats the old shame-culture/guilt-culture split. In Mencius, the heart of shame and disdain (羞惡之心) is the sprout of rightness, and it is already other-facing: I failed *him*. Confucian repair is correcting one's fault (過而不改，是謂過矣, *Analects* 15.30), not being pardoned.
*Defense:* The text says "left to itself," which is a hedge, and its argument for A2 does not rest on the shame/guilt contrast at all. It rests on the betrayed secret and the letter in the street, and a Confucian grants that betraying a friend's trust (信) wrongs her. Our own culture also asks pardon face to face. Lian Po carried thorns on his back to beg Lin Xiangru's forgiveness (*Shiji* 81). "Almost none treats it as a mere mistake" is true of us too.
*Rejoinder:* Granted on substance. The wording still invites a reader to think shame is self-regarding, and Mencian shame is not.
**Held with repair** (wording only; see §6, R1).

**E2. The second road and Mozi's guests (W1(1)).**
*Attack:* The text uses Mozi's barb (*Mozi* 48, "Gongmeng"), which was aimed at us, and does not give our answer. Xunzi's answer is that the funeral and sacrificial rites were never ceremonies for guests. They give form to genuine feeling (情) and complete what death broke off, so that the end and the beginning are both done well (*Xunzi* 19, verify). A debt that can never be released is not a defect needing a remedy from beyond. It is the gravity of finite bonds. Wanting release is a desire, not a datum.
*Defense:* The text calls the second road noble, says the Church walks much of it, and makes W1(1) a clue that "weighs as much as the reader finds the third road… truer than the second." Its one firm claim is that only someone can forgive, and a Confucian does not deny that. The question is whether guilt asks for her pardon. Our own practice says it does. Families announce their faults to their ancestors and beg their pardon, which is the text's "form of the third road." Xunzi himself says the common people take the rites as serving ghosts. Only the gentleman takes them as the human way.
*Rejoinder:* Then the gentleman and the common people disagree about what guilt asks, and the text has only taken the common people's side. That is a standoff at parity. The text admits as much by weighting the clue on the reader's judgment.
**Held** (at parity). The fairness repair R2 should give Xunzi's answer one clause.

**E3. "Heaven and Earth give life to things; God wanted *you*" (C, impersonal sacred order).**
*Attack:* Heaven's wanting is not absent. It is carried out in us. The *Liji* says the human being is the heart of Heaven and Earth (人者天地之心). Cheng Hao's ren forms one body with all things. Zhang Zai's Western Inscription calls Heaven father and Earth mother, all people siblings, and the disabled, orphaned and widowed his brothers in distress. And 理一分殊 particularizes: the one principle is *this* daughter's filiality to *these* parents.
*Defense:* Then the infant no one holds is wanted by Heaven only as far as some human heart is moved, and none is. That is exactly the text's line: "for the infant no one is drawn to hold, we learn whether what we meant was worth or only attraction." Particularization assigns roles. It does not make anyone wanted, and the one whose roles are all cut off (the orphan, the unremembered dead) is wanted by no one. The state altars for ghosts without descendants (厲壇) feed them "without a name," as the text says.
*Rejoinder:* The norm addressed to every heart is a real claim on her behalf. You assert a divine wanting that is no more visible in her death than ours is.
*Defense:* The text agrees that God's care is not visible there. It puts that on "the other side of the scale" and weighs it. Its claim is about explanatory reach, not observation. A norm says she is worth wanting. Only a will wants her.
**Held.** The distinction between "worthy of being wanted" and "wanted" is real, and we cannot supply the second without a heart that does not depend on anyone being moved.

**E4. One Source in the heart (W2; Wang Yangming and Mou Zongsan).**
*Attack:* W2 concludes that a law and a mind fitted to each other are best explained by one Source of both. We agree, and we name the Source: the original heart (本心). Wang Yangming says there is no principle outside the heart. Lu Xiangshan says a sage arising by the Eastern Sea or the Western Sea would have this same heart and this same principle (verify source). Mou gives that heart intellectual intuition (智的直覺) and calls it creative reality. This explains the "reach" of the law to every possible mind without a Person besides us.
*Defense:* W2 says plainly that it "weighs, it does not settle" and that only W1 and W3 show the Source is Someone. The C row already meets Mou: then "at the ground of things there is no one besides me who knows me as myself," and no one who can release what I did to another. Mou's infinite heart is also no naturalism. It is a metaphysical posit at least as heavy as God, so it does not undercut W2's argument against those who say conscience is only what survival built.
*Rejoinder:* Agreed. But W2's closing parenthesis lists its audiences and leaves out the one that accepts its conclusion and still declines the Person. The text should say so where W2 ends.
**Held with repair** (R5).

**E5. Heteronomy: an infinite placed outside us (Mou's critique, W1 closing).**
*Attack:* Mou held that Christianity puts the infinite outside the self (external transcendence), so the moral law comes to us from beyond. That is heteronomy. Confucian learning is "for oneself" (為己, *Analects* 14.24): the law is my own heart at its deepest.
*Defense:* The text has already answered this. "Our share in that law is reason's own light, not a foreign rule" (*Veritatis Splendor* 41: participated theonomy). The wrong is wrong "because of what it does to her," not because it was commanded, and God "did not choose whether cruelty is evil." Catholic teaching holds God both transcendent and present to the creature's inmost being (CCC 300). So the law is mine at my deepest and also His, without competition.
*Rejoinder:* Mou would say "participated" still leaves a gap. But our account also has a gap, between the empirical self and the infinite heart, and we close it only by cultivation.
**Held.** The text's answer is to parity or better, and it gains something we lack: a forgiver who is not oneself.

**E6. The absolute at every number (A6, W1(2), C final row).**
*Attack:* Mencius said that none of the sages would commit one unrighteous act or kill one innocent person even to gain all under Heaven (行一不義，殺一不辜，而得天下，皆不為也, *Mencius* 2A2). Xunzi says the same (*Xunzi* 8 and 11, verify). For twenty-three centuries a tradition with no Lord of life held the line at every number. The final C row lists "why that line has no exceptions" among what the theist explains and the rivals "leave out or must take as given." Measured against us, that is an overclaim.
*Defense:* The text never says only Christians hold the line. It says "many of the best rivals share" the rule and "rest on a standing they take as given." Mencius's ground is what the humane person will not do, and that is a stopping point: why does humaneness have that shape? The book's ground, that no number of others has a right to what is not theirs, is one step further back. On the second half of A6 (her own request), our tradition clearly does not hold the line. We honoured those who "kill the body to complete ren" (*Analects* 15.9), and late-imperial practice praised loyalist and widow suicides that aimed at death. The text marks that half as "this book's claims, … not shared ones."
*Rejoinder:* Fair on the second half. On the first, 不辜, "the one without guilt," faces the victim, not only the agent. Our absolute is about *her*, and it is as exceptionless as the Church's. The text loses nothing by crediting it, and A6 gains a non-Christian witness that reason alone can see the line at every number (DI 1).
**Held with repair** (R4). This is a matter of crediting the source, not a flaw in the reasoning.

**E7. Bearing evil without a Judge (evil section).**
*Attack:* We affirm that life-giving is good (生生, "the great virtue of Heaven and Earth is to give life," *Yijing*, "Xici"), and yet we carry no theodicy. We separate what is within our power (ren, rightness) from what is decreed (命). We stand under no tottering wall, accept our proper lot (*Mencius* 7A2–3), and neither resent Heaven nor blame men (*Analects* 14.35/37). The evil section says, "We say that it is good that she exists, and so we must answer for the child." We say it too, and we owe no such answer. So affirming existence is not what creates the burden.
*Defense:* The C row already puts the burden in the right place: such an order is free of the problem of evil "only if it intends no one in particular," and "the more such an order cares, the more it faces our question" (Sima Qian). The burden comes from claiming that Someone willed this child.
*Rejoinder:* Then the evil section's sentence should say so. As written, it suggests that anyone who affirms existence is under the burden.
**Held with repair** (R6).

**E8. Peace at death against the longing (W3).**
*Attack:* Zhang Zai ends the Western Inscription by saying that in life he follows and serves, and in death he is at peace (存，吾順事；沒，吾寧也). The longing W3 describes, to be received whole by someone who will not die, can be educated. The sage outgrows it without grasping.
*Defense:* W3 treats the longing only as a clue and expressly says it "settles nothing." It also concedes that grasping must die. The longing at issue is mainly that *the beloved* be kept, not that I go on. Confucius cried out that Heaven had bereft him when Yan Hui died (*Analects* 11.9), and three years of mourning are not peace with the loss.
*Rejoinder:* Grief is not a demand that the dead continue. We hold that the beloved is honoured by being mourned rightly, not by being kept.
**Held.** This is a standoff at parity, and the text already weights it as one.

## 4. Ledger

- **Form (CCC 31/35/36):** Holds. The claim is calibrated, and "converging" is the right genre.
- **A heading:** Holds. Its epistemic rule ("what still seems true after honest reflection… until something more certain shows it false") is one a Confucian accepts, since it is how Mencius argues from the child at the well.
- **A1:** Holds. Mencius and Xunzi both teach it.
- **A2:** Holds with repair. The substance is granted. The shame sentence misdescribes Mencian shame (R1).
- **A3:** Holds. A relational self (Tu Weiming) does not let kin pardon *in her place*. Their acceptance of reparation is their pardon for what reached them through her.
- **A4:** Holds. We grant it via *Mencius* 6A16–17 and Zhu Xi. The enemy clause outruns us (*Analects* 14.34), which is honest Christian distinctiveness, not a defect. "Equal worth is not equal duty" is Mencius against Mozi's impartial love, and is well judged.
- **A5:** Holds. It is close to Mencius on the sprouts. Xunzi would contest that nature itself aims at good, but A5 leaves that to W.
- **A6:** Holds with repair. The first half is Mencius 2A2 and should be credited (R4). The second half is rightly marked as not shared.
- **A7:** Holds. Sincerity (誠) is the Way of Heaven, and making oneself sincere is the way of man (*Zhongyong* 20).
- **W1 frame:** Holds. It concedes that rivals locate guilt with the victim, and it parts "at the grave, and over what gives her the claim," which is accurate.
- **W1(1):** Holds. Xunzi and Confucius are stated accurately (祭如在, *Analects* 3.12; *Xunzi* 19). It is correctly weighted as a clue. A fairness clause for Xunzi's reply to Mozi is needed (R2). The account of the ancestral rites and the "wandering dead" is accurate.
- **W1(2):** Holds. "A law or an order gives a nature to every member of a kind; it does not want any one of them" survives E3. The claim that the call can be fulfilled for the one who will never think is a real advantage over us.
- **W1 closing ("This Lawgiver…"):** Holds. It answers Mou's heteronomy charge (E5) through VS 41.
- **W2:** Holds with repair. It does not distinguish the book from Wang and Mou, and it says it does not settle the question. Its list of audiences should name the heart-as-Source view (R5).
- **W3 (longing):** Holds. It is a clue, honestly bounded. Zhang Zai's peace is a parity standoff.
- **W3 (hiddenness):** Holds. "Found a principle rather than a Person" is fair. Small nuance: Wang Yangming found the principle *in the heart* (R7).
- **C, nothing binds:** Holds. No Confucian stake.
- **C, feeling refined:** Holds. No Confucian stake. Mencius would side with the text against the "sensible knave."
- **C, Kant:** Holds.
- **C, contractualism:** Holds.
- **C, utilitarianism:** Holds. Mencius's attack on Mohist impartiality runs parallel.
- **C, secular Aristotelian:** Holds. Placing Xunzi "nearer" here is fair.
- **C, godless realism:** Holds.
- **C, impersonal sacred order:** Holds with repair. Zhu Xi on principle without intention, the moon in ten thousand rivers, Heaven having and not having a mind, Mou's refusal of "impersonal," Sima Qian, and Xunzi's Heaven are all stated accurately. The "(A2)" tag implies that we fail A2, which we do not (R3).
- **C, Buddha's Dhamma:** Holds. No Confucian stake.
- **C, Nietzsche:** Holds.
- **C, God who is Love:** Holds with repair. "Why that line has no exceptions" should not read as something every rival leaves out (R4).
- **Evil ¶1:** Holds with repair. The burden should attach to "willed her," not to "good that she exists" (R6). The Confucian hope (大同) is quoted accurately.
- **Evil ¶2, First:** Holds. Zhu Xi's principle that is wholly good, with unevenness in qi, is near the privation account, as C says.
- **Evil ¶2, Second:** Holds.
- **Evil ¶2, Third:** Holds. It is honestly marked "not a further proof."
- **Conclusion:** Holds. Its final appeal is aimed at the one who gives up A4, not at us. Mencius would say "Yes, she was wronged no less," and the text does not claim that this alone decides between God and Heaven's principle.

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**As written: NONE.**

No Fatal or Serious objection stands. Every place where we part from the text (whether guilt asks the dead for pardon, whether worth needs a will that wants and not only a norm, whether the longing to be kept is a clue or a disorder to be educated, whether the one Source is a Person besides us) is either conceded by the text as a weighable clue or decided against us on explanatory reach without overclaiming.

**Minor items:**
1. A2's shame sentence reads as if all shame were self-regarding. Mencian shame (羞惡) faces the other (R1).
2. W1(1) uses Mozi's barb, which was aimed at Confucians, without the Confucian reply. That is a fairness gap, not a logical one (R2).
3. C, impersonal order: "An order cannot be wronged or forgive (A2)" implies that the Confucian fails A2. We hold A2 fully, and only the order's inability to forgive is in point (R3).
4. A6 and the final C row: the Mencian absolute (*Mencius* 2A2) at every number goes uncredited. The C row's "what the best rivals leave out" should not include it (R4).
5. W2's closing parenthesis omits the heart-as-Source view (Wang, Mou), which accepts W2's conclusion (R5).
6. Evil section: "We say that it is good that she exists, and so we must answer for the child." We say the first clause too and owe no answer. The burden comes from "willed her" (R6).
7. W3: "found a principle" can be sharpened, because the Lu-Wang line found principle in the heart itself (R7).

**Misstatements of my school:** none of substance. The three readings that need care are accurate: Xunzi's view that the dead do not hear, Confucius's 祭如在, and Zhu Xi's 理無情意. The "(A2)" tag (item 3) is the nearest thing to a misstatement.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**
- The second road against the third (E2): whether an unreleasable debt is a wound awaiting healing or the dignity of finite bonds.
- Zhang Zai's peace at death against W3's longing (E8).
- Mou's immanent infinite heart against a Creator: both are first principles. The text's advantage, a forgiver who is not oneself, matters only to a reader who already feels guilt's demand for pardon from another.
- A naturalized Heaven against God as the ground of the inherent level of dignity: both stop. The text's "reach" argument is real but weighable.

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1 (A2).**
Old: "Shame, left to itself, says I am less; guilt says I wronged her; only guilt asks her pardon."
New: "Shame that looks only at itself says I am less; guilt says I wronged her; only guilt asks her pardon."

**R2 (W1(1)).**
Old: "The ancient critic Mozi said that to deny the spirits and still study the rites is like learning the ceremonies for guests when there are no guests."
New: "The ancient critic Mozi said that to deny the spirits and still study the rites is like learning the ceremonies for guests when there are no guests. Xunzi answered that the rites were never for guests: they give grief its due form and finish well what death broke off. That answer is true of the living; it does not reach her."

**R3 (C, impersonal sacred order).**
Old: "An order cannot be wronged or forgive (A2), and it cannot know or love anyone (W3)."
New: "Its teachers know well that a wrong is done to a person, a father, a friend, a stranger (A2); but the order itself cannot forgive, and it cannot know or love anyone (W3)."

**R4a (A6).**
Old: "Fewer hold the line when the numbers grow vast, and many who would never hang the innocent man would bomb a city to end a war."
New: "Fewer hold the line when the numbers grow vast (Mencius did: none of the sages, he said, would kill one innocent man even to win the whole realm, *Mencius* 2A2), and many who would never hang the innocent man would bomb a city to end a war."

**R4b (C, a God who is Love).**
Old: "a ground for the rule that one person may not be spent for others' totals, which many of the best rivals share and rest on a standing they take as given;"
New: "a ground for the rule that one person may not be spent for others' totals, which many of the best rivals share, Mencius at every number, and rest on a standing or a humaneness they take as given;"

**R5 (W2, closing parenthesis).**
Old: "Those who say our moral words voice refined feeling need no meeting of law and mind; for them the question is the one in C.)"
New: "Those who say our moral words voice refined feeling need no meeting of law and mind; for them the question is the one in C. Those who find the one Source in the heart itself, as Wang Yangming and Mou Zongsan did, agree with this witness and part from this book in W1 and W3.)"

**R6 (evil section, ¶1).**
Old: "We say that it is good that she exists, and so we must answer for the child."
New: "We say that it is good that she exists, and that Someone willed her for her own sake, and so we must answer for the child."

**R7 (W3, hiddenness).**
Old: "found a principle rather than a Person."
New: "found a principle, some of them in the heart itself, rather than a Person."

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights:**
- **Mencius 2A2 as a witness for A6.** A non-Christian tradition held the line against killing one innocent, even for the whole realm. This is strong evidence for the claim that reason alone can see this (DI 1, VS 52), and it costs the book nothing.
- **Mencius 6A16–17 for A4's "prior to anyone's recognition."** What another man makes noble, he can make base again; Heaven's nobility no one confers or removes. This is a pre-Christian statement of dignity not conferred by others.
- **Mencius 2A6 for A4 and the Conclusion.** The alarm at the child about to fall into the well arises not to win the parents' favour, not for reputation, and not because the cry is unpleasant. This is the classic witness that regard can track the child and not attraction, which is exactly the book's "worth or only attraction."
- **Confucian two-level dignity** (inherent from Heaven, achieved through cultivation; verify in modern literature, e.g. Joseph Chan, Irene Bloom). It closely parallels *Dignitas Infinita*'s distinction between ontological and moral dignity (DI 7–8, 22), and the book could note the convergence.
- **The *Zhongyong*'s 天命之謂性**, "what Heaven confers is called nature." The Confucian names nature as a *mandate*, not a fact. That is either a Giver or a metaphor, and W1(2)'s "a gift exactly as far as her being is a gift" can take up the question gently.
- ***Classic of Filial Piety* 1.** Body, hair and skin are received from parents and must not be harmed. This is a non-theist "held in trust" intuition that supports CCC 2280's form, and it raises the question of whom the parents received it from.
- **Mencius 7A35 (Shun and his father).** Our hardest case shows justice bending toward kin and away from the victim. It is a usable illustration of why A3 insists that no one but she may release the wrong.
- **Zhang Zai's Western Inscription and 理一分殊.** One origin, love particularized. This is a non-Christian echo of GS 29 ("one origin and one calling"), and the book could set it beside its own "the genius and the newborn are for the same thing."

**Lines worth keeping:**
- "Heaven and Earth give life to things; God wanted *you*."
- "when one river is drained, the moon has lost nothing."
- "Minds under threat reach for a knowing Heaven, ours as well; this shows where the account strains for its own teachers, not that it fails." This is the fairness rule kept in practice.
- "A law or an order gives a nature to every member of a kind; it does not want any one of them."
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."
- "A yes said to whatever happens is said to no one."
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it."

## 8. Score

**8 / 10.** The text states Confucius, Xunzi, Zhu Xi and Mou accurately and concedes our strengths openly. It parts from us only where we really part, over a forgiver besides oneself, a will that wants each one, and the keeping of the dead, and it offers these as weighable clues, not proofs. Against my school it holds; the repairs needed are about credit and wording, not substance.

**Honest ceiling for any theistic case against my school: about 8.5.** We share nearly all of A. So the decision can only rest on whether guilt's request to the absent victim, and love's wish that the beloved be kept, are clues to Someone or desires that the sage learns to set at peace. Reason can weigh that question, but it cannot settle it for every honest mind.
