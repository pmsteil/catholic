# Blind review, Round 8: the Ethics of Care

## 1. School and voice

I speak for the ethics of care: Nel Noddings (*Caring*, 1984), Carol Gilligan (*In a Different Voice*, 1982), Annette Baier (her essays on trust, and her case that justice-centred ethics needs more than justice), Sara Ruddick (*Maternal Thinking*), Joan Tronto (*Moral Boundaries*; *Caring Democracy*), Virginia Held (*The Ethics of Care*, 2006), Eva Feder Kittay (*Love's Labor*, 1999; *Learning from My Daughter*, 2019), with Margaret Urban Walker (*Moral Repair*) close by. We hold that the moral life begins in relations of care, not in abstract duties, rights or laws. Every one of us began wholly dependent, and many of us will end that way. Persons are relational, not first self-sufficient contractors. The defining moral acts are attention to this particular other, response to her needs, and keeping trust. I am a non-theist and argue at full strength. This school has no row in the text's comparison, and the text leans hardest on the newborn and the severely disabled, which is our home ground. So I test two things: whether C silently leaves us out, and whether the law/guilt/debt/Lawgiver framing misreads the moral life we describe. (Paraphrases of named thinkers are mine; verify.)

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** Our starting point is need within dependency. What anyone is owed is the care that her needs call for from those placed to give it. Kittay adds what she calls doulia: those who give care must be cared for in turn, so the care owed to the dependent becomes a claim on the whole society. Held nests justice inside care, and Tronto makes the fair sharing of caring work a political question (verify). **Verdict: holds** for the near and the dependent. **It borrows** when it reaches the distant stranger. Held concedes that care needs justice beside it, and justice for strangers we will never meet is not derived from any actual relation.

**2. A hard dilemma resolved without the book's four terms.** A woman cares for her mother, who has advanced dementia, and also for her own disabled child. Years ago her mother begged never to be "put in a home." Now competent care at home is impossible, and the child is suffering for it. Care ethics does not ask which rule wins. It asks what the promise was *for*: that her mother would never be abandoned. It asks what each person in the web actually needs, including the carer herself, who is not a resource to be used up. And it asks how the care will be received. Resolution: move her mother into residential care and keep up daily presence there, so that the relation the promise protected is kept even though its letter is broken. **Verdict: holds, with overlap.** Attentiveness is close to the book's truth, and responsiveness is close to its mercy, but these are our own concepts and not borrowed. The harder case is Ashley X, a profoundly disabled child whose growth her parents had medically stunted, partly to keep her caring for her at home possible. There loving carers and disability-minded care ethicists split. Kittay's critique (verify, *Hypatia* 2011) leaned on the thought that Ashley may not be shaped for others' convenience, which is close to A4's "never to be used as a mere means." **Where caring love and the cared-for's integrity conflict, care ethics tends to borrow a dignity constraint it does not itself derive.**

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** Kittay's answer is connection-based equality. Each of us is some mother's child (verify): born of someone, kept alive only by others' care, and so owed care whatever we can do. That reaches the newborn and her daughter Sesha without any appeal to capacity, and it beats Kant, Rawls and Singer on exactly the cases the text cares about. **Verdict: holds against capacity views, and strains at the abandoned infant.** If what she is owed rests on an actual relation of care, she is precisely the one whose relation was refused. If it rests on her being *owed* care by all, then that owing is a worth already there, one the relation answers to and does not create. We take that worth as given. That is a stopping point, not a derivation. Noddings's early view, that obligation is bounded by actual or potential relation (verify), does not reach unconditionally, and I do not defend that part of it.

**4. Ending point for flourishing.** What is ultimately good for humans is to live in, receive and sustain good relations of care: to grow as the cared-for, and to become competent, responsive carers, in a society that shares that work justly. Our guiding principles are attentiveness, responsibility, competence and responsiveness (Tronto), with trust as the medium (Baier). **Verdict: coherent, and thin at the grave.** Death ends every relation of care we can give or receive. We grieve that and keep memory, but we promise nothing beyond it. This is the honest limit of the school, not a contradiction within it.

## 3. Debate highlights

**E1. The silent omission.**
*Attack.* C calls itself "an honest comparison" of the best rivals, and the God row claims reach over "what the best rivals leave out." Yet the one major normative ethic built around the dependent newborn and the severely disabled has no row. The nearest row, "Feeling, refined," sorts by metaethics (Hume, Smith, quasi-realism). We are not a metaethics. We are a normative account of what the moral life *is*. An inference to the best explanation that leaves out the rival strongest on its decisive case is weakened by exactly that much.
*Defense.* Care ethics is compatible with several metaethics. Its sentimentalist form (Baier, Slote) falls under "Feeling, refined," its realist form under godless realism, and its eudaimonist form under "the flourishing of our kind." So the argument meets it, row by row.
*Rejoinder.* That covers our *metaethics*, not our *ground*. Kittay's connection-based equality is a distinct, non-capacity ground for A4, and no row states it. The closing appeal then sets up a two-way choice: her worth "followed her strength or her mind," or it "follows what she is." That leaves out the third answer, that worth follows *whom she is bound to*. A reader from my school finds no row and a dichotomy that skips him. **Failed as written. Repairable** (§6, R1–R2).

**E2. The infant no one holds.**
*Attack.* We explain the newborn's equal worth better than the law-framed rivals, and without God. She is some mother's child, owed care by the fact of dependency itself. No Lawgiver is needed, only the relation.
*Defense.* The text's chosen case is the one your account finds hardest: "the infant no one is drawn to hold," left on the hillside. If her worth rests on actual care, she has the least of it. If it rests on her being owed care, the owing is prior to every relation, and you take it as given. Moreover, the text's own ground is relational. She was "willed for itself" (GS 24), and "every child was wanted by the One who did, before anyone saw her" (Jer 1:5). The book offers the one relation that no abandonment can refuse her. That is care's own structure, with a Carer whose care does not wait to be drawn.
*Rejoinder.* That is the strongest reply anyone has made to us, and I grant it parity at least. Our worth-prior-to-relation is a stopping point. Yours is one too, but yours accounts for the very feature we take as given: her being wanted before any human wanted her. I still deny that the Carer exists. But the text cannot be faulted here *on the merits*. It can be faulted only for not *saying* this to us. **Held with repair** (add the reply in C).

**E3. Law, guilt, debt: does the framing misread the moral life?**
*Attack.* Baier's charge against the justice tradition is that it builds ethics for roughly equal adults who choose their bonds, and treats unchosen, unequal dependency as a footnote. The text's vocabulary of law, obligation, debt and Lawgiver repeats that mistake. In this framing the dependent appear as bearers of claims, never as participants in relations, and the carer never appears as a moral agent at all.
*Defense.* Look at what the text actually argues. A2's decisive example is a betrayed trust, which is Baier's own theme. A4 begins from the newborn, the dying and the severely disabled. A6 begins from what real good costs the giver. W3 rests on being known and loved, and even grants "even if we are made of our relationships." W1 sides with Anscombe's suspicion of law-words cut loose from their source. The closing paragraph of W1 defines the Lawgiver not as a commander but as the One who willed her for her own sake, so that the wrong "offends Him who loves her." The law is love's shape. And A5's list of goods includes "the raising of children."
*Rejoinder.* Granted, mostly. The text is far more relational than its title. Two gaps remain. Dependency itself is never named as a fact of the human condition (A5 would be the place). And the carer's work appears only as a channel ("a mother's love before a creed"), never as moral labor that is itself owed support. The Church teaches the latter (CCC 2208–2209), so naming it costs the book nothing. **Held with repair (Minor).**

**E4. The absolute lines (A6).**
*Attack.* Care explains the surgeon case without a Lord of life. The patient trusted him, and spending that patient is a betrayal of the caring relation, not the breaking of a law. Ruddick argued that maternal practice tends toward non-violence, which supports holding the line even against bombing a city.
*Defense.* Then who protects the stranger outside every relation: the man hanged to calm a riot, or the prisoner experimented on? A city's care for its many could weigh against him. Care ethics is characteristically wary of exceptionless rules, and it cannot say why the line is "not even hers to waive."
*Rejoinder.* Held's and Tronto's political care extends trust to every member of a society, so a city that secretly hangs one man betrays everyone's trust. I concede that my school is not absolutist, and many of its members would allow a dying person's request in some cases. The text already marks those claims as "this book's claims... not shared ones." **Held.** Parity at the five, and an honest flag on the rest.

**E5. The debt to the dead (W1(1)).**
*Attack.* Walker's account of moral repair answers the dead friend without a Keeper. Repair after wrongdoing is owed to the victim *and* to the moral community: acknowledgment, truth-telling, vindicating her standing, restoring trust in shared standards. The web of care outlives any one member of it.
*Defense.* That is the text's second road, which it calls "noble" and says the Church "walks much of." What the second road cannot give is *her* pardon, and guilt asks for that. The text does not claim that the asking proves anything. It is "a clue" that "weighs as much as the reader finds the third road... truer."
*Rejoinder.* I grant that the asking is real. Relational selves do want reconciliation with the very one they wronged. I deny that the asking is answered. **Held** as an honest standoff at parity.

**E6. Evil judged by care's own standard.**
*Attack.* The text makes God the Carer of the one no one on earth holds. Then hold Him to a carer's standard: attentiveness, responsiveness, competence. A parent who could prevent his newborn's cancer and permits it "for the order of the whole," or to preserve a world of real causes, fails as a carer. And the more the text says God *wants this one*, the higher that standard rises.
*Defense.* The text concedes this weight in its own words. It does "not answer... the child with cancer," and it is "on this side of the scale... heavy." It does not say permission is a trade. It says God never spends a person as a mere means. It says He suffers with us and will raise her. The analogy also limps: a human carer acts inside a world of causes, while God gives that world its being.
*Rejoinder.* The disanalogy cuts both ways. The Giver of being has *more* competence than any carer, not less. Still, the text has not hidden or understated the cost. It names a love-shaped God and lets that God's love count against Him. **Held** as an honest standoff. My recommendation is that the book name the carer's form of the problem, because its own language invites that form (R5).

**E7. The longing to be received whole (W3).**
*Attack.* Attachment theory explains the longing to be held and known wholly: it is the infant's first experience of care, carried forward.
*Defense.* The text already says that science "can trace the paths this longing travels" and that this "tells us how we long, not whether there is Someone to long for." It claims fit, not proof.
*Rejoinder.* Fair. **Held.**

## 4. Ledger

- **A heading** (offered as truths; denials listed): **Holds.** Care ethicists fall among "many who hold all the rest" and deny the Church's absolutes. No misstatement.
- **A1** (some things really wrong): **Holds.** All three examples are failures of care or trust toward the vulnerable, and we affirm them robustly.
- **A2** (guilt, not mere error; wronging versus worsening): **Holds.** The betrayed secret is a model case of breached trust (Baier), and "guilt faces a person" is relational.
- **A3** (no one on earth can make the gravest wrongs right): **Holds.**
- **A4** (equal dignity): **Holds with repair (Minor).** "Worth or only attraction" leaves out what Noddings calls ethical caring, which is neither. Answered in substance by R2.
- **A5** (is and ought): **Holds with repair (Minor).** Dependency as a universal human condition belongs on the list of what we are (R3).
- **A6** (real good costs; absolute lines): **Holds.** Care ethics explains the five-for-one case at parity, and the book's further claims are flagged as its own.
- **A7** (bound to truth): **Holds.** Attention to the real other presupposes it.
- **W1 frame**: **Holds with repair (Minor).** "The best accounts without God keep them" passes over the accounts that, like ours, speak less of law than of trust and response (R4, optional).
- **W1(1)** (debt still owed; Catholic answer): **Holds.** Walker's moral repair is the second road, fairly treated, and the standoff is honestly stated.
- **W1(2)** (worth no deed confers): **Holds.** "A law or an order... does not want any one of them" is exactly where care ethics agrees with the text against the impersonal rivals. The text's answer to the uncared-for infant is its strongest line against my school.
- **W1 closing** (the Lawgiver is not a commander): **Holds.** This is the passage that rebuts the misreading charge.
- **W2** (law and mind, one Source): **Holds.** This is outside my school's main concern; care ethics takes no settled position on the reach question.
- **W3** (communion; why seekers do not find Him): **Holds.** The relational-self concession is fair, and "a mother's love before a creed" is congenial.
- **C intro**: **Fails as written.** It says "Each is stated as its best defenders state it," but the care rival is not stated at all (S1).
- **C: Nothing really binds**: **Holds.**
- **C: Feeling, refined**: **Holds with repair (Minor).** It is accurate for Hume and Smith. If a reader takes it as covering care ethics, it misreads Kittay, whose equality is relational, not a shared feeling. Also, Hume's "gentleness, not justice" is presented as a demotion; my school rejects that ranking, and the book's own pairing of justice with mercy need not accept it either.

- **C: What we owe each other**: **Holds.**
- **C: Well-being, counted impartially**: **Holds.**
- **C: The flourishing of our kind**: **Holds.**
- **C: Necessary truths**: **Holds.**
- **C: Impersonal sacred order**: **Holds.** "Heaven and Earth give life to things; God wanted *you*" is the right axis, and it is the axis on which care ethics also stands.
- **C: The Buddha's Dhamma**: **Holds.**
- **C: Power and rank**: **Holds.**
- **C: A God who is Love**: **Holds with repair.** Its reach claim is stated against named rivals only. It needs one clause on care (R1b).
- **[Missing row: care between persons]**: **Fails** (S1).
- **Evil, paragraph 1** (the weight): **Holds.** Honest and heavy. The carer's form of the problem is implicit in "we must answer for the child" (R5, optional).
- **Evil, First** (privation; no supreme Evil): **Holds.**
- **Evil, Second** (permission is not a trade): **Holds** as an honest standoff. Care ethics presses hardest here (E6).
- **Evil, Third** (God suffers with): **Holds.** The Confucian hope for the widow, the orphan and the disabled is a fair nod toward care.
- **Conclusion**: **Holds with repair (Serious locus of S1).** The strength/mind versus what-she-is dichotomy omits relational grounding (R2).

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**S1. Serious (repairable): the comparison silently omits the ethics of care, and the closing argument offers a dichotomy that skips its answer.** The text's inference is to the best explanation over "the best rivals." Its decisive case, in A4, in W1(2) and in the conclusion, is the dependent newborn whom instinct passes by and the severely disabled. That is precisely the case on which care ethics, above all Kittay's connection-based equality, offers a distinct non-capacity, non-theist ground. No row states it, and the conclusion's either/or (worth follows "her strength or her mind," or "what she is") leaves out "whom she is bound to." The objection is valid, and its premises (that a major rival exists, that it is strongest on the decisive case, and that it is absent) are plainly true. As written, the text does not answer it. On the merits the text *can* answer it at parity or better, with material it already has: the abandoned infant is the one whose actual relation was refused, "owed care" presupposes a worth prior to the relation, and the book's ground is itself a relation no one can refuse her (GS 24; Jer 1:5). That is why S1 is Serious and not Fatal. R1 and R2 repair it.

**Minor:**
- A5 does not name dependency as part of what we are (R3).
- W1's "the best accounts without God keep them" passes over accounts that set law-words partly aside, as Anscombe proposed (R4, optional).
- A4's "worth or only attraction" does not consider ethical caring, which is neither (covered by R2).
- The "Feeling, refined" row, read as covering care ethics, would misfit it (see ledger). Hume's "gentleness, not justice" is framed as a demotion, a ranking care ethics rejects. No wording change is needed if R1 is added.
- The carer appears only as a channel of God's love, never as one whose labor is itself owed support (R3 covers this with CCC 2208–2209).

**Misstatements of my school:** none. The school is not mentioned, so it is not misstated. The only risk is the implicit filing under "Feeling, refined," noted above.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**
- The debt to the dead: Walker's moral repair versus the third road (E5).
- The absolute lines beyond the five-for-one cases (E4). The text marks these as its own claims.
- Evil judged by care's standard (E6). The text concedes the weight, and neither side can settle it by reason alone.
- The longing to be received whole (E7).

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1. Add a C row** after "What we owe each other." New text:

> - **Care between persons** (the ethics of care: Nel Noddings, Virginia Held, Joan Tronto, Eva Feder Kittay) begins where this book begins, with the newborn and the one who holds her. It sees what accounts built for equals who bargain can miss: that every one of us began, and many will end, wholly dependent on others; that the moral life is first attention and response to *this* person, not rules for anyone; and that a wrong is a betrayal of trust and of care (A2). It can say why the surgeon may not spend the patient who trusted him (A6), and, in Kittay's hands, why worth does not follow ability: each of us is some mother's child, kept alive by others' care and owed care whatever we can do (A4). It is honest that those who give care must be cared for too, as the Church also teaches (CCC 2208–2209). But it stops at the infant no one holds. If what she is owed rests on a relation of care, she is the one whose relation was refused; if it rests on her being owed care by everyone, that owing is a worth already there, which care answers to and does not give, and this account takes it as given. And care ends at the grave: the one we failed is no longer there to be cared for. This book's answer has care's own shape. She is held in a relation no one can refuse her, because the One who made her wanted her before anyone saw her (W1(2)). Where care says love must answer to this one, we agree; we add that there is One whose love does not wait to be drawn.

(Before final, verify Kittay's "some mother's child" usage against *Love's Labor*.)

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **The Catholic account, for the dependent, is the completion of care ethics' own structure.** Our worth-prior-to-relation is a stopping point. The book can say that this worth *is* a relation, being wanted by the One who gives being, and so the one relation no abandonment can refuse. This is the book's best line against my school, and it reads better under the title's second half ("is Love") than under its first ("The Lawgiver").
- **Name Kittay and answer her.** She is the strongest non-theist voice on the newborn and the severely disabled, which are the book's own decisive cases. Honoring her strengthens the book's credibility with every reader who has cared for a disabled child.
- **Baier's ethics of trust supports A2.** The betrayed secret is a pure trust case. The book could note that even the critics of law-centred ethics agree a wrong is done *to her*.
- **Walker's moral repair** is the best secular form of the second road. Citing it would show that the second road is taken at its strongest.
- **The carer must be cared for.** Catholic teaching agrees (CCC 2208–2209, 2276). *Samaritanus Bonus* insists that being incurable never means being beyond care (verify the wording). Saying so heads off the care critique that the ideal of sacrifice burdens carers, chiefly women.
- **The carer's form of the problem of evil** is the form the book's own love language invites. Facing it by name is more honest than leaving it implicit.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "Not every child is wanted by those who made her; every child was wanted by the One who did, before anyone saw her."
- "A law or an order gives a nature to every member of a kind; it does not want any one of them."
- "Guilt faces a person: I did this to *her*."
- "Where instinct fails, for the infant no one is drawn to hold, we learn whether what we meant was worth or only attraction."
- "God says no to what was done to her, and yes to her."
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it." This line states the precise point at which my school and the book part, and states it fairly.

## 8. Score

**7/10 as written; 9/10 with R1–R2.** The text is more relational than its law framing suggests, and it answers my school's strongest case (the infant no one holds) better than my school does. But it never addresses that answer to us: there is no row, and the closing dichotomy skips relational grounding.

**Honest ceiling for any theistic case against the ethics of care: 9.** The carer's form of the problem of evil, together with the debt to the dead, stays a standoff at parity by reason alone.
