# Blind review, Round 8: the Utilitarian school

## 1. School and voice

I speak for the utilitarian tradition at its best: Sidgwick's rational intuitionism (the axioms of justice, prudence and rational benevolence, and "the point of view of the universe"); Smart's frank act-utilitarianism; Hare's two-level universal prescriptivism; Hooker's rule consequentialism; and Singer with de Lazari-Radek, who are now non-naturalist realists and hedonists, and who use evolutionary debunking selectively against partial and deontic intuitions while keeping impartial benevolence. We hold that the good of any one being counts for no more than the like good of any other, that what we ought to do is set by what makes things go best, impartially considered, and that rights, constraints and partial duties are valuable as secondary rules but are not foundational. I argue as a fair opponent. The text is unusually careful about my school. Where it misfires, I say so; where it lands, I say that too.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** Sidgwick's axioms: the formal axiom of justice (like cases alike), and rational benevolence (each one's good counts equally from the point of view of the universe). What anyone is owed is equal consideration of her interests; particular rights are, with Mill, the most stringent class of social utilities, enforced because security matters so much to everyone. → **Holds** on its own terms and borrows nothing. But it is thin exactly where the text presses: "owed to her" becomes "her interests count in the total". Consideration is not claim. That is a cost we own, not a contradiction.

**2. A hard dilemma, resolved.** A pandemic ICU with one ventilator: a 30-year-old with a good prognosis, and an 80-year-old on it whose prognosis has collapsed. We allocate by expected benefit (life-years saved, adjusted for quality), under triage rules set and published in advance so the public can trust them, with full palliative care for the one not ventilated. → **Holds**: we use impartiality, honesty and relief of suffering, but derive all three from well-being, not from the book's definitions. (The Church largely agrees on this case through the proportionality of treatment, CCC 2278.) The honest hard edge is the case where the act is secret and the totals are clear: the sheriff who could hang one innocent to stop a lethal riot. Smart accepted in principle that his theory could require such acts (verify), and Hare answered that a good person's firm intuitions would forbid them and that such cases almost never really arise. → Here the school **breaks in the eyes of most readers**, though not by its own lights. It is consistent and bites the bullet.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** We affirm equal *consideration* of equal interests for every sentient being: the enemy, the dying and the severely disabled count exactly as much as anyone, pain for pain. We deny equal *unconditional worth* grounded in species. Singer holds that a newborn, lacking self-awareness and future-directed preferences, does not have the same interest in continued life as a person (*Practical Ethics*, verify). Hare and Hooker would keep strong equal-protection rules at the level of practice, but only because they serve the good. → **Openly denies; does not borrow.** The cost is the conclusion's newborn, and the text is right to make us pay it.

**4. Ending point of flourishing.** The ultimately good is the well-being of sentient beings: for Sidgwick and for de Lazari-Radek and Singer now, desirable consciousness; for Hare and the earlier Singer, satisfied preference. The principles are impartiality, maximization, prudence, and common-sense rules as secondary guides. → **Holds** as a system. It **strains** in two places. The first is the experience machine, which hedonists answer by appeal to status-quo bias (verify). The second is W3's "it is good that *you* exist": on the total view, what is good is that a happy life exists, and one such life is in principle replaceable by another (Singer's replaceability argument, verify).

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1: the blown letter misfires against preference utilitarians (A2; C, well-being row).**
*Attack.* The C row says utilitarianism "reduces wronging to worsening", and cites the betrayed secret and the letter blown into the street. A preference utilitarian (Hare, the earlier Singer) counts the dead woman's frustrated wish as a real setback in both cases. The difference between the cases is that in one an agent acted, and could have done better, while in the other the wind did it. We already say that: no agent, no wrong act. The letter shows that events are not acts. It does not show that utilitarianism cannot tell them apart.
*Defense.* Against the hedonist (Sidgwick, Smart, de Lazari-Radek and Singer today), A2's first clause lands squarely: "nothing in anyone's experience is worse", so the hedonist must call the telling no wrong, or only an indirect one through character and the practice of promising. Against the preference utilitarian, the text's real point is that the wrong becomes a failure to maximize, owed to the total and not to her.
*Rejoinder.* Granted. But the illustration should show that, and Ross's structure does: had the telling brought slightly more good to someone else, act-utilitarianism says no one was wronged, not even she. The letter does not show it.
**Held with repair** (Repair R1).

**Exchange 2: selective debunking of A6.**
*Attack.* Singer ("Ethics and Intuitions", 2005) and Greene's dual-process work argue that the immediate "no" in the surgeon case is an alarm against up-close personal violence. That trait was useful for primates who fear retaliation, and it is morally irrelevant. The text's own preface says a belief loses its warrant if "what produced the belief had nothing to do with its truth". A6's "Almost everyone sees these at once" is exactly what the debunking predicts.
*Defense.* (a) The riot case and the secret prisoner experiment involve no push and no close contact, yet they draw the same verdict. (b) Berker argued that the normative work in such debunking is done by an armchair premise (that personal force or intention is irrelevant), and that premise is the very point in dispute. (c) Under the fairness rule, impartial benevolence has a genealogy too: Singer's own *The Expanding Circle* traces it to kin altruism and reciprocity, extended by reason. If reason's endorsement redeems benevolence, the same test is open to the constraint, which also binds against interest (for the stranger, the prisoner no one will miss). (d) Empirical work suggests that "utilitarian" verdicts in sacrificial dilemmas do not track greater impartial concern for others (Kahane, Everett, Earp, Farias and Savulescu, 2015, verify). The book should not lean on (d), which comes close to a psychological ad hominem, but it shows the debunking data cut both ways.
*Rejoinder.* De Lazari-Radek and Singer reply that universal benevolence is *not* fitness-enhancing and the constraint is (it protects reputation). Defense: the constraint at every number, against strangers, in secret, is no more fitness-enhancing than universal benevolence. The parity holds. But the text names this, the best-known utilitarian argument against its central datum, nowhere.
**Held with repair** (Repair R4).

**Exchange 3: "a wrong done to him".**
*Attack.* A6 says the best total-counting accounts "cannot say why crossing it would be a wrong done to *him*". The separateness-of-persons objection presupposes a deep, all-or-nothing personal identity. Parfit's reductionism (*Reasons and Persons*) weakens it, and with it the case for treating persons as walls the total may not cross.
*Defense.* The text's claim is exactly what the school concedes. On act-utilitarianism a right act wrongs no one, so if the five's gain outweighs, the one spent has no complaint at all. Reductionism about persons does not restore his complaint; it removes it. And the newborn of A4 is the one who suffers most from a view on which persons are less deeply separate.
*Rejoinder.* Conceded: the text describes us accurately. What remains is whether that directed wrong exists. That is a dispute over the datum, and on it we stand at parity.
**Held.**

**Exchange 4: kind or sentience (W1(2); A4; the conclusion).**
*Attack.* Worth that "follows kind and not ability" is speciesism. Species membership is morally arbitrary, like race. The anencephalic infant then outranks a chimpanzee who knows, plans and grieves. And the conclusion's test proves less than it claims: the weak newborn is "wronged no less than a strong newborn" because both can feel equally, which is our ground, not "what she is".
*Defense.* W1(2) already blocks the sentience ground: "That she can suffer explains why harming her matters; it does not explain this either, for others can always suffer more." And the conclusion's test is conjunctive. It also asks whether she was wronged no less than "a grown man, however gifted". Equal consideration of interests cannot deliver that equality, because the man has more at stake. "Kind" here means a rational nature present but impeded, not a biological label. And the Church does not make animals morally null: cruelty to them is contrary to human dignity (CCC 2416–2418).
*Rejoinder.* The conjunction does its work. But the inference sentence, "her worth never followed her strength or her mind", leaves the sentientist's exit (how much she can feel or want) unnamed, and a careful utilitarian reader will walk out through it.
**Held with repair** (Repair R7).

**Exchange 5: the paradox of deontology.**
*Attack.* If killing an innocent is so terrible, why not kill one to prevent five killings of innocents (Scheffler's paradox)? An absolute that lets more innocents be killed keeps hands clean and leaves bodies on the ground. And the text's grounding cuts the other way: if the line rests on God's dominion over life, then Aquinas allows that at God's command death may be inflicted on anyone, guilty or innocent, without injustice (ST I-II q.94 a.5 ad 2; ST II-II q.64 a.6 ad 1, verify). So the theist's absolute has an exception clause too, as Hooker's code has its disaster rule.
*Defense.* The text's answer to the paradox is its strongest line against my school: "No one may take what is not his to take, even to keep others from taking more." The constraint is about jurisdiction, not about minimizing a bad. That is why it does not turn into a duty to minimize violations, which secular deontologists struggle to show. On the exception: the Church teaches that no one can under any circumstance claim for himself the right directly to destroy an innocent human being (CCC 2258), and that intrinsically evil acts admit no exceptions (VS 80). The text adds the safeguard Aquinas's dictum needs: God wills her "for her own sake... and never spends her as a mere means against her own good" (GS 24), and "He who gives life, and can give it back, may call it home". God's taking of life is not the spending of a person for a total, because it is not her end. And no one may claim a voice from God to kill (CCC 2258, as the text says).
*Rejoinder.* The jurisdiction answer is coherent and better than most secular answers. Its force for the asymmetry between God and us rests on resurrection, which reason does not show. The text is honest that it offers this as an explanation, not as a premise.
**Held.**

**Exchange 6: God as the great consequentialist (the evil section).**
*Attack.* CCC 324 says God would not permit an evil unless He drew good from it. That is a trade. God "wills some things to be naturally corrupted" for the order of the whole, and the text admits that among animals the order "does give one creature's loss to another's life". So God spends sentient beings for the whole, which is exactly what the text forbids us to do to persons. By equal consideration, the fawn's agony counts as much as a child's of equal capacity. And for an omniscient designer who chose the whole package, the line between intending and foreseeing goes thin.
*Defense.* The text separates *why* He permits (a world of real causes, a good not a necessity) from *what* He draws from it (a good offered to the sufferer herself), and says the evil never becomes good (CCC 311, 312). Double effect is not ad hoc for God: the text needs it for us anyway ("Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent"), and Catholic teaching applies the same structure to legitimate defense (CCC 2263–2264). God does not choose a "best possible world" by totals (ST I q.25 a.6), so there is no optimization to accuse. On animals the text concedes the point, plainly: "For the fawn that hope is thinner... this book does not pretend otherwise."
*Rejoinder.* The remaining dispute is the sentience/personhood line and the doing/allowing distinction. Both are contested at the root, and both apply equally to the text's ethics and to its theodicy. That is consistency, not evasion.
**Held** (standoff at parity on the two roots).

**Exchange 7: W2's reach versus utilitarian realism.**
*Attack.* De Lazari-Radek and Singer, following Parfit, hold that reason grasps self-evident normative truths, benevolence above all, much as it grasps logic. No Mind behind them is needed. W2's "one Source" explains any fit whatever and so explains nothing.
*Defense.* The question applies to them more sharply than to anyone, because their whole metaethics rests on reason's power to see truths that survival did not select for, which is the very feature W2 asks about. W2 does not claim proof: "it weighs, it does not settle." The theist is not positing a Mind for each datum. He offers one source for three things the realist must take separately (C, final row).
*Rejoinder.* At parity. W2 also omits a credit due to us: its "engine of correction", reason holding itself to consistency against interest, is Singer's own thesis of the expanding circle.
**Held with repair** (Repair R6, a credit, not a defect).

**Exchange 8: A7 and Sidgwick's secrecy.**
*Attack.* The row ties esotericism to A7. But A7 is about one's own believing; esotericism is about what to teach. De Lazari-Radek and Singer hold that we have objective reasons to believe the truth. And "his life kept A7 better than his theory could" misreads Sidgwick, who argued for the duty of veracity in teachers within his own ethics when he resigned (verify: *The Ethics of Conformity and Subscription*, 1870).
*Defense.* The real point stands: on utilitarianism truthfulness is derivative. Where a comforting falsehood, believed or left in place, does more good, the theory must favor it (Parfit allowed that the theory may be self-effacing). A7 says the norm binds every mind, not only when it pays. The Church draws the contrasting line: she forbids the lie absolutely (CCC 2482–2485), while holding that not every truth must be told to everyone (CCC 2488–2489).
*Rejoinder.* That formulation is fair and I accept it. The sentence about Sidgwick's life is not fair as written.
**Held with repair** (Repair R2).

**Exchange 9: W3 and the love that asks no answer.**
*Attack.* Impartial benevolence is love that does not need to be loved back. Wanting to be "received whole... by someone who will not die" is a self-regarding wish that the point of view of the universe outgrows.
*Defense.* W3 meets the Buddhist version of this, and it applies here: Catholic teaching also says grasping must die, and that desire is to be healed, not killed (*Deus Caritas Est* 5). What is left is "it is good that you exist". The total view can say only that it is good that *a* happy life exists, and that one may in principle be replaced by another. That is the sharpest form of "Heaven and Earth give life to things; God wanted *you*", and it fits my school better than the sacred order it is aimed at.
*Rejoinder.* Fair, and W3 counts it as a clue, not evidence.
**Held** (Insight I3 strengthens it).

## 4. Ledger

- **A3:** Holds. We agree that consent and votes do not make torture right (consent is not what makes anything right for us). We deny the debt to the dead, and W1(1) names that denial fairly as road one.

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**As written: NONE.**

My school's deepest disagreements (directed wrong, kind versus sentience, absolute constraints, a Mind behind reason's reach, the weight of evil) are disputes over the data or the foundations. The text either names them as its own claims or weighs them honestly. None meets the brief's standard for a sound objection.

**Minor items**
1. The C row's letter-in-the-street illustration does not touch preference utilitarianism. It shows that events differ from acts, which we already hold (R1).
2. The "count only totals" description slightly mis-fits Hooker's rule consequentialism (priority-weighted, argued through reflective equilibrium). Optional fix: "the best accounts that count only outcomes".
3. Selective evolutionary debunking of A6's datum is not named. The defense exists at parity but is not on the page (R4).
4. The conclusion's inference leaves "how much she can feel" unnamed (R7).
5. W2's credit to Singer's expanding circle is missing (R6).
6. "What pain is the loss of" is loosely put (R8, optional).

**Misstatements of my school**
- "His life kept A7 better than his theory could" (Sidgwick). He defended a teacher's veracity within his ethics when he resigned (verify). It is unfair as written (R2).
- The A heading lists Singer as denying parts of A2, A4, A6 and A7. He also denies the posthumous debt in A3, and as a hedonist denies A5's list as basic goods (R5).
- W2's bracket files every non-realist under "refined feeling". Hare's universal prescriptivism rests on the logic of "ought", not on feeling (minor; see I5).

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Whether wrongs are directed (owed *to her*) at the foundation, or only through the setback to her interests.
- Rational nature or sentience as the ground of moral status (the newborn, the animals).
- Absolute constraints against thresholds; doing/allowing and double effect, applied consistently to us and to God.
- Whether reason's grasp of necessary normative truths calls for a Mind (W2).
- Whether truthfulness binds non-derivatively (A7).
- The final weighing of evil against the case.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact old → new)

**R1. C, well-being row: make the A2 charge bite both kinds of utilitarian.**
Old: "But it reduces wronging to worsening (A2: the betrayed secret, and the letter blown into the street that frustrates her wish just as much and wrongs no one);"
New: "But it reduces wronging to worsening. Counting experiences, it finds nothing wrong in the secret told to a stranger who will never repeat it, for no one feels it (A2); counting wishes, it can blame the teller, but only because he could have done better overall, so that, had the telling brought a little more good to someone else, no one, she included, would have been wronged at all;"

**R2. C, well-being row: state the A7 charge fairly and correct the line on Sidgwick.**
Old: "and some of its masters have allowed that its truth might be better kept from the public (Sidgwick; de Lazari-Radek and Singer defend that secrecy still), so that the many would live by what their teachers think false (A7). Sidgwick himself gave up his fellowship rather than profess what he could not believe; his life kept A7 better than his theory could."
New: "and it makes truthfulness a servant of the total: where a falsehood, believed or left in place, would do more good, it must favor the falsehood (A7). Some of its masters have allowed that its own truth might be better kept from the public (Sidgwick; de Lazari-Radek and Singer defend that secrecy still), so that the many would live by rules their teachers think are not, at bottom, true. Sidgwick himself gave up his fellowship rather than profess what he could not believe, and held that a teacher's honesty was worth that price; the question is whether, on his theory, it always is."

**R3. C, well-being row: give credits the school has earned, and add replaceability.**
Old: "and is right that consequences matter; the Church weighs them too (CCC 1754)."
New: "and is right that consequences matter; the Church weighs them too (CCC 1754). It has often seen first whom others passed by: Bentham and Mill spoke against slavery and for women, and Singer speaks for the animals and the distant poor, whom the Church too says we may not pass by (CCC 2416–2418, 2443–2449). Yet where it counts lives as goods, it can say that it is good that a happy life exists, not that it is good that *you* do: on its total view, one such life may in principle be replaced by another as happy."
(Verify the Bentham and Mill attributions, and Singer's replaceability argument in *Practical Ethics*, before printing.)

**R4. A6: name the selective debunking and answer it at parity.**
Old: "Almost everyone sees these at once."
New: "Almost everyone sees these at once. Some say that this certainty is only the flinch of a mind built to recoil from violence up close, and so tells us nothing. But no one is pushed in the riot or in the hidden experiment, and the line holds there too; and the regard for the many that would spend the one has a history of its own, in kin and in favors returned. A history settles neither."

**R5. A heading: complete Singer's denials.**
Old: "Singer denies parts of A2, A4, A6 and A7;"
New: "Singer denies parts of A2 through A7;"

**R6. W2: credit the expanding circle.**
Old: "and the engine of that correction is reason holding itself to consistency with goods it already sees: the law of truth again."
New: "and the engine of that correction is reason holding itself to consistency with goods it already sees: the law of truth again. (Peter Singer, no friend of this case, calls this the expanding circle and also credits reason; we ask what reason must be, that it escalates this way.)"

**R7. Conclusion: close the sentientist's exit.**
Old: "If she was wronged no less than either, her worth never followed her strength or her mind;"

Old: "That does not make pain less real; it says what pain is the loss of."

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**New insights**
- **I1. Sidgwick's own Kant moment.** At the end of *The Methods of Ethics*, Sidgwick found that reason gives two counsels, to seek one's own good and to seek the good of all, and that without something like a moral government of the world they cannot be reconciled. He wrote of the "cosmos of duty" falling into chaos (verify the wording and edition), and he could not find such a government proven. That parallels the Kant row's *Religion* 6:98–99 move exactly, and it comes from the founder of modern utilitarian rigor. De Lazari-Radek and Singer answer by debunking egoism. That is worth one sentence in the well-being row.
- **I2. "The point of view of the universe."** This is Sidgwick's phrase for impartiality. The book can answer it directly: there is such a point of view because Someone holds it, and from there no one is a sum. It turns my school's highest image into a witness for W3 without misstating it.
- **I3. Replaceability is the purest form of "Heaven and Earth give life to things; God wanted *you*."** The line is aimed at the sacred order, but it bites the total view literally (R3).
- **I4. The fairness rule cuts in the book's favor against Singer.** Singer treats the sanctity of every human life as a Christian inheritance to be discarded. Mill wrote that the whole spirit of utility is in Jesus' golden rule (*Utilitarianism*, ch. 2; paraphrase, verify). If impartiality and equal worth share an ancestry, the ancestry settles neither, exactly as the Nietzsche row says of the will to truth.
- **I5. Hare's fanatic is Hume's sensible knave.** Hare admitted that the logic of "ought" alone cannot move a fanatic who would sincerely prescribe his own persecution (*Freedom and Reason*, verify). Formal reason, like refined feeling, binds only the one already willing. A clause in W2's bracket or in the well-being row would place Hare honestly.
- **I6. Anscombe coined "consequentialism"** in the same 1958 paper W1 cites, as a criticism. That is a fact worth a clause.
- **I7. Caution.** Do not use Caiaphas (John 11:50, RSV: "it is expedient for you that one man should die for the people") as a picture of utilitarianism. He weighed his nation's advantage, not the impartial good, and the fairness rule forbids the comparison. At most, the verse shows the *shape* of the act A6 forbids, with no motive attributed. Romans 3:8 serves better.

**Lines worth keeping** (the strongest against my school)
- "Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent."
- "That she can suffer explains why harming her matters; it does not explain this either, for others can always suffer more."
- "No one may take what is not his to take, even to keep others from taking more." This is the best answer to the paradox of deontology I have seen in a popular text.
- "He who gives life, and can give it back, may call it home; we, who can do neither, may not take it."
- "where instinct fails, for the infant no one is drawn to hold, we learn whether what we meant was worth or only attraction."
- "The absolute can cost lives, even many. Without it, no innocent is safe from being spent." Its honesty is its strength.
- "For the fawn that hope is thinner than it is for the child, and this book does not pretend otherwise."

## 8. Score

**8 / 10.** The text describes my school accurately where it matters. It makes us pay our real costs (the spent innocent, the newborn, the derivative status of truthfulness, the dead who cannot forgive), and it concedes evil's weight without evasion. It loses points only for one misfiring illustration, one unfair line about Sidgwick, and leaving the selective-debunking argument unnamed.

**Honest ceiling for any theistic case against my school: 9.** A consistent utilitarian who bites every bullet cannot be refuted, only shown the price. With repairs R1–R7, this text would be close to that ceiling.
