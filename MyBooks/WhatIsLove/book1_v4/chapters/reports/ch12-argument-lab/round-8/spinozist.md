# Blind review, Round 8: the Spinozist / naturalist pantheist school

All paraphrases of Spinoza, his modern readers and Einstein are paraphrases, not quotations, and should be checked against a standard edition (Curley's translation of the *Ethics* and *Theological-Political Treatise*): **verify** throughout. The one Latin phrase used, *Deus sive Natura* ("God, or Nature"), is Spinoza's own (*Ethics* IV, Preface). Citation form: E = *Ethics*, part, proposition (P), corollary (cor), scholium (sch), appendix (App); TTP = *Theological-Political Treatise*; TP = *Political Treatise*.

## 1. School and voice

I speak for Spinoza and his modern readers. My claims are these. There is one substance, God or Nature, infinite and necessary. Everything follows from its nature the way the properties of a triangle follow from the triangle (E I P16, P29, P33). It has infinite intellect but no will in the human sense. It has no purposes, and in the passive sense it loves and hates no one (E I P32, App; E V P17 cor). "Good" and "evil" name what helps or hinders the striving of each thing to go on being what it is, its *conatus* (E III P6–9; E IV Def. 1–2). Freedom is acting from the necessity of one's own nature, through adequate ideas. It is not choosing between open options (E I Def. 7; E II P48). Blessedness is the intellectual love of God. That love is virtue itself, not a reward for it (E V P32–36, P42). Curley reads this God as the most general laws of nature. Della Rocca reads Spinoza as the thinker who takes the principle of sufficient reason (PSR) all the way down. Garrett defends the necessitarianism, and Nadler argues that what survives death is no personal self. Einstein said he believed in Spinoza's God, who shows himself in the harmony of what exists and not in concern for human fates (all **verify**). I use the word "God" and mean it. I deny that God is Someone who wants anyone.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**(1) Starting point for justice.** By nature, each thing has as much right as it has power (TTP 16; TP 2). Sin and injustice exist only in a commonwealth, where common law decides what belongs to whom (E IV P37 sch 2). What anyone is *owed* is what that law assigns. Reason recommends the law because nothing is more useful to a human being than other human beings living by reason (E IV P18 sch, P35). It also wants for others the good it wants for itself (E IV P37), and the purpose of the state is freedom (TTP 20). → **Holds** as a coherent system, and it borrows nothing. It **breaks** with the text at "wrong even if every culture approved" (A1), if "wrong" means a violation of what the victim is owed. I can say cruelty is bad and in bondage in every culture. I cannot say it is *unjust* before any law.

**(2) A hard case without borrowing.** Take the text's own case: the surgeon who could secretly kill one healthy patient to save five. My principles are four. Act from adequate ideas, not passions. Pity is a passion, bad and useless in itself for one who lives by reason (E IV P50). Hatred is never good (E IV P45). And the free man never acts in bad faith, *even to save his life*, because if reason counselled bad faith it would counsel it to everyone, and agreement among people would be impossible (E IV P72). So the free surgeon does not do it, even in secret. Reason's dictates are universal, and a profession that spends its patients destroys the trust that makes people useful to each other. → **Holds**, and it borrows truth (good faith) in a thin form but not mercy or self-gift. I openly reject pity and humility as virtues (E IV P50, P53). **Breaks** exactly where the text says the best non-theist accounts break (A6). My ground is the agent's reason and the common good of rational agreement. It is not anything owed to *him*, the one who would be spent. I accept that cost.

**(3) Equal, unconditional dignity.** I deny it as a metaphysical fact. Perfection and reality are the same thing (E II Def. 6; E IV Pref.), and finite things differ in power. An infant whose body can do very little has a mind conscious of almost nothing (E V P39 sch). What I can ground is equal *civil* protection, which reason recommends, and a universal ban on cruelty in the agent, since cruelty always comes from passion. I can also ground generosity toward everyone who shares our nature (E III P59 sch; E IV P35, P37). → **Breaks** with A4, honestly. The newborn who will never think, and the stranger who will never be useful, are protected by my ethics of the agent and by my state. They are not equal in worth, because worth is power.

**(4) The final end of flourishing.** The highest good is knowledge of God. It is understanding every thing as following from the one necessary nature, and the calm joy and intellectual love of God that come with that understanding (E IV P28; E V P27, P32, P36). My guiding principles are conatus, reason, the model of the free man, and freedom as understanding necessity. → **Holds**: it is coherent and borrows nothing. Its cost is that blessedness is "as difficult as it is rare" (E V P42 sch, **verify**). The infant and the profoundly disabled cannot reach it. The text's "seeing given" (Dhamma row; CCC 1250, 1261) has no counterpart in my view.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1. The comparison leaves me out.**
*Attack.* C has rows for error theory, refined feeling, Kant, contractualism, utilitarianism, the Aristotelian, godless realism, the sacred order, the Dhamma and Nietzsche. It has none for the one rival that uses God's name and denies a person. The God row claims "one source for three things [the realist] must take separately: the law, the beings it is about, and the minds that read it." Deus sive Natura is exactly one source for all three. The order and connection of ideas is the same as the order and connection of things (E II P7), and the common notions are adequate in every mind because they are in God's infinite intellect (E II P38–40). The row then says that, against the views that also stop at one, "it is the one that knows and loves us." Mine knows each singular essence eternally (E V P22). In Spinoza's sense it also loves men: the mind's intellectual love of God is part of the love with which God loves himself, and God, so far as he loves himself, loves men (E V P36 and cor).
*Defense.* The text's real distinction against one-source rivals is not mind against matter. It is whether the source *wants* this one for her own sake. The sacred-order row says so: "Heaven and Earth give life to things; God wanted *you*." W1(2) says the person is "the only creature on earth God willed for itself" (GS 24). Spinoza's God wills no one, chooses no one and, strictly speaking, loves no one (E V P17 cor). Every argument the text brings against the sacred order applies to me with more force, because Zhu Xi's principle is at least morally good and mine is not.
*Rejoinder.* Granted, but I hold E V P17 cor and E V P36 cor *together*, and the text meets neither. A reader who knows Spinoza will see the God row's sentence as unproven against him.
**Held with repair**: add a row, and change "knows and loves us" to "wills each of us for her own sake" (§6, R1–R2).

**Exchange 2. Purpose implies lack (E I App).**
*Attack.* "God wanted you." "Willed for itself." If God acts for an end, he wants something he lacks, so the God of purposes is an imperfect God (E I App).
*Defense.* Catholic teaching answers this directly. God does not act to gain anything. He alone is supremely generous, because He acts not for His own profit but only to share His goodness (ST I q.44 a.4 and ad 1). He wills Himself necessarily and creatures freely, as ordered to His goodness, which they cannot increase (ST I q.19 a.2–3). The text already holds this: "nothing harms God (Job 35:6–8)," and He is offended "only because we act against our own good" (SCG III.122).
*Rejoinder.* Then why create at all? A will that gains nothing and is moved by nothing could just as well have produced no world. Its choice has no sufficient reason (Della Rocca, **verify**). The theist's God is non-brute in His essence and brute in His creating.
*Defense, continued.* A sufficient reason need not necessitate. Goodness inclines without compelling (ST I q.19 a.3), and many defenders of the PSR read it that way (**verify**). The Church binds itself on this point: God created "not by any necessity" but "by His will free from all necessity," not as necessarily as He loves Himself (*Dei Filius* ch. 1 and can. 1.5; CCC 295). Spinoza's alternative also has a price. If everything follows necessarily, the torture of every child could not have failed to happen. That is "modal collapse," and it is a bullet, not a premise.
*Rejoinder.* I bite it. Necessity is not a curse when it is understood.
**Held, at parity.** This is the deepest standoff between us. The text does not argue it and need not, but it should say in one clause that God wills creatures "not from need" (§6, R2).

**Exchange 3. "The Lawgiver" is accommodation (TTP 4).**
*Attack.* Calling God a lawgiver is how prophets spoke to the multitude. God's decrees are eternal truths. The command to Adam was a warning that the fruit was poison, in the way a doctor warns, not a decree (TTP 4, **verify**). The chapter's title rests on an anthropomorphism.
*Defense.* The text agrees with most of this, which is to its credit. "This Lawgiver is not a commander who picks rules. A will that invents values can command, but it cannot bind." "He did not choose whether cruelty is evil." "Our share in that law is reason's own light, not a foreign rule" (VS 41). Natural law is the rational creature's share in the eternal law (ST I-II q.91 a.2). The Church and Spinoza both reject the arbitrary legislator. The text's further claim is not that God legislates by fiat. It is that the eternal law is held in a Mind that also wills the persons it is about.
*Rejoinder.* Then "Lawgiver" adds nothing that "Someone" does not already add. The chapter's real case is W1(1), W1(2) and W3, not the law.
**Held.** That is a fair description of the text's own structure: W2 points to one Source and defers "Someone" to W1 and W3.

**Exchange 4. Guilt rests on an inadequate idea (A2, A3, W1(1)).**
*Attack.* Spinoza defines repentance as sorrow with the idea of a deed we believe we did by a free decision of the mind (E III Def. Aff. 27, **verify**). Since no decision is free, the guilt the text describes ("I did this to *her*") is a passion resting on a false idea. Repentance is not a virtue, and one who repents is doubly wretched (E IV P54). The text's first road, "remorse, a wound that will never be healed," leaves out my cure. A passion stops being a passion once we form a clear and distinct idea of it (E V P3). Understanding everything as necessary gives the mind power over the passions (E V P6). The wound heals through understanding, not pardon.
*Defense.* (a) A2's key datum does not need libertarian freedom. It is the difference between telling the secret for a laugh and the letter blown into the street, and Spinoza must recognize that difference too. He does so by locating a vice (ingratitude, bad faith) in the teller (E IV P71–72). But that explains why the *teller* is worse. It does not explain why *she* was wronged in one case and not the other, which is what A2 claims. (b) The text's sentence says the wound "will never be healed *by the one it was done to*." That stays true on Spinoza's account. Spinoza heals the doer; the text asks about the bond. The Buddhist row already names this structure ("what finally frees the doer is his own purification, whether or not she ever pardons him"). (c) Fairness: E I App explains belief in free will and desert as ignorance of causes. That is a genealogy. If I may use it, the text may describe necessitarian calm as a defense against grief. Neither settles anything, so both must be dropped and the dispute fought on the PSR.
*Rejoinder.* Agreed on fairness. The dispute then becomes freedom against necessity, which the text rests on Chapter 1 and does not argue here.
**Held with repair (minor)**: the first road should mention the cure by understanding, so that a Spinozist reader sees himself (§6, R4).

**Exchange 5. Wanting to be loved back (W3).**
*Attack.* "Only a person can love us back." Spinoza argues that whoever loves God cannot want God to love him back, because that would be wanting God to be affected with joy, that is, to pass to a greater perfection, which is wanting God not to be God (E V P19). The longing W3 treats as a clue is a confusion of the imagination. And the text's "no one knows the whole of us" is false on my view. God's infinite intellect contains the idea of the essence of this very body under the form of eternity (E V P22). You are known wholly, and that idea is the eternal part of your mind (E V P23).
*Defense.* E V P19 hits love understood as a passion, and the Catholic God has none. To love is to will another's good (ST I q.20 a.1). God's love does not gain from us; it gives (W1(2): "Our love answers to a worth already there; God's love gives it," ST I q.20 a.2). So the longing is not a wish that God be changed by us. It is a wish that He want us, which He can do without lacking anything. On being known: by Spinoza's own account the eternal part of the mind neither imagines nor remembers (E V P21). What is kept is an essence, not the one who lived "across every role and every year" and who could forgive. The text's hope is resurrection, "body and all" (CCC 999, 1025), and the risen Christ keeping His wounds.
*Rejoinder.* Duration and memory belong to the inadequate, imagining self. You prize what I call bondage.
**Held, at parity.** The disagreement is now clear: is the remembering, durational person real, or a confused idea? The text's resources answer E V P19, but as written they are spread across W1 and are not set against W3 (§6, R5).

**Exchange 6. Worth is power (A4, W1(2), conclusion).**
*Attack.* "A worth no deed confers and no order wants" assumes that worth is something over and above reality. On my view, perfection *is* reality and power. Defining worth as what God wills, and then saying only God explains it, is circular.
*Defense.* The text does not define worth as what God wills. It offers A4 as a datum and tests every account against it, and it says plainly that the case "cannot compel the one who gives up A4." The conclusion asks a question that does not beg: which are you surer of, that the weak newborn on the hillside was wronged no less than the strong one, or any argument that says otherwise? Spinoza must answer that her loss was a lesser loss of reality, because she had less. That is exactly the price C promises to show.
*Rejoinder.* I pay it. The hillside question appeals to pity, which reason does not follow (E IV P50). The free man still never exposes her, because he never acts from hatred or bad faith.
*Defense, continued.* Then the free man protects her, but not *for her sake*. That is the text's point against every account that grounds regard in the agent.
**Held.** The "Someone, not something" argument does not beg the question here: the text names A4 as the contested premise and weighs it. But because C has no row for me, the price I pay is never shown, which is exactly what the A heading promises C will do.

**Exchange 7. Is "Someone, not something" a begged question?**
*Attack.* The text's three signs of a person (guilt asking to be forgiven, forgiveness itself, and communion) count as evidence of Someone only if those experiences are taken as accurate. I take them as passions, so the inference presupposes a personalist reading of its own data.
*Defense.* The A heading gives a general rule: what survives honest reflection is trusted "until something more certain shows it false, or shows that what produced the belief had nothing to do with its truth." The Spinozist trusts his common notions and his reasoning by the same rule. To overturn A2 and A3 he needs something *more certain*. His candidate is the strong PSR with necessitarianism, which is contested among his own friends. Spinoza himself also trusts a moral judgment that cuts against advantage: the free man will not lie to save his life (E IV P72).
*Rejoinder.* E IV P72 is derived, not merely seen. *Defense:* It is derived by a universalizing step that already assumes reason binds every reasoner, which is A7. That is the text's own point.
**Held.** One overclaim remains. W2 says W1 and W3 "show" that the Source is Someone, but W1(1) and W3 call themselves clues. The wording should match (§6, R3).

**Exchange 8. Evil and the "sheer fullness" option.**
*Attack.* My answer to why God did not make everyone rational is that God did not lack the material to make everything, from the highest degree of perfection to the lowest (E I App). This is the text's "god ... who makes and breaks out of sheer fullness." I bear the weight of the fawn and the child with no strain, because no one intended either. The text's own theodicy borrows my plenitude: God "wills some things to be naturally corrupted," and "the lion lives by the slaying of other animals" (ST I q.19 a.9; q.22 a.2 ad 2).
*Defense.* The text concedes that two rivals bear this weight more easily than it does, and it does not pretend otherwise. What it adds against plenitude is that "A person it never gives away" (SCG III.112), and that God "says no to what was done to her, and yes to her." Spinoza's ground says neither no nor yes. "It cannot take her side."
*Rejoinder.* The free man takes her side; the ground does not. I accept that. But the text's phrase "a yes said to whatever happens" fits Nietzsche's *amor fati* and does not fit my *acquiescentia*, which is joy from understanding, not approval.
**Held with repair (minor)**: name Spinoza's version in the evil section and keep the text's reply (§6, R6).

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**As written: NONE.**

I checked two candidates for Serious and neither meets the brief's standard.

First, C's silence about me does not leave the conclusion unsupported against me. Every argument the text uses to separate "Someone" from "something" applies to Deus sive Natura without change. My God forgives no one (W1(1)), wills no one for her own sake (W1(2), the sacred-order row), keeps no one's memory (E V P21), and wants nothing back (W3). The text can answer me at parity with what it already contains.

Second, the PSR objection to free creation, and my denial of free will against Chapter 1 and the evil section, are standoffs at parity, not defects. My premises (a strong PSR, necessitarianism) are at least as contested as the text's.

**Minor items (in priority order):**
1. **The comparison silently leaves the Spinozist out.** The brief asked me to test this, and it does leave me out. It matters more than a typical omission for three reasons. My view is the most common educated form of "God, but not a person" (Einstein's religion). It is the classic critique of God as lawgiver (TTP 4), and Chapter 12 is titled "The Lawgiver Is Love." And it is the one rival that matches the God row's "one source" claim. The A heading promises that C shows each denial's cost, and for my denials it does not (R1, R7).
2. **The God row's distinctive claim is stated too loosely.** "The one that knows and loves us" is contested by E V P22 and E V P36 cor. The text's real difference, that God *wills each one for her own sake, not from need*, is stated elsewhere (sacred-order row, W1(2)) but not in the God row (R2).
3. **E I App (purpose implies lack) is never met in the text,** though Catholic teaching meets it easily (ST I q.44 a.4; ST I q.19 a.2–3; CCC 295). One clause is enough (R2).
4. **E V P19 (a true lover of God cannot want God to love him back) is the sharpest objection to W3,** and the text's answer sits in W1(2) instead of W3 (R5).
5. **W2's "W1 and W3 show"** overclaims against W1(1) and W3, which call themselves clues (R3).
6. **The A5 heading** reads like Hume and is contested by both my school and Thomist natural law; the body is fine (R8).
7. **The W1(1) first road** omits the cure by understanding (R4).
8. **The evil section's "sheer fullness" sentence** fits Nietzsche, not Spinoza (R6).

**Misstatements of my school.** None directly, since the text never names Spinoza. Two sentences fit badly if a reader applies them to me. W3's "if the highest good knows and loves no one" does not fit, because Spinoza's God knows each essence and loves men in an intellectual sense. The evil section's "a yes said to whatever happens" is *amor fati*, not *acquiescentia*.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**
- PSR and necessitarianism against free creation, and modal collapse against brute choice.
- Freedom as understanding necessity against Chapter 1's free will (the guilt of A2 and W1).
- The durational, remembering person who could forgive against the eternal essence that remembers nothing.
- Worth as power against worth as given with being (A4).
- Whether the longing to be loved back is a clue or a passion to be cured (W3).

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact old → new)

**R1. Add a C row** after the "impersonal sacred order" row (before "The Buddha's Dhamma"):

New:
> - **God, or Nature** (Spinoza; and many today who, like Einstein, say they believe in Spinoza's God) is the rival nearest to us that keeps God's name. There is one infinite and necessary being, and everything follows from it as the properties of a triangle follow from what a triangle is; it has infinite understanding but no will, chooses nothing, and intends nothing; good and evil name what helps or hinders each thing's striving to go on being what it is. It explains much. The law holds for every mind because minds and the things they know are one order of one God (W2). The free man, Spinoza held, will not break faith even to save his life, and no one aims at his own death unless something outside him overcomes him (A6). The world's pain needs no excuse, for no one intended it. Its highest good is a seeing too: to understand all things as following from God, and to love God with a love that wants nothing back; and the mind's love of God, Spinoza said, is part of the love with which God loves Himself, and in that love God loves men. But it parts from us wherever persons are. For Spinoza nothing is a wrong except under a people's laws, though cruelty is always bondage (A1). Repentance is a sorrow the wise man outgrows, cured by understanding that the deed had to happen, not by her pardon (A2). What is eternal in us, on his account, remembers nothing, and cannot forgive or be forgiven (W1). Worth is the measure of a thing's power, so the newborn who will never think has less of it than you (A4). And his God knows her eternally but does not want her: He loves no one in particular, and cannot be asked (W3). The Church teaches that the world is not God (CCC 285) and that God made it by His wisdom and love, not by any necessity (CCC 295; *Dei Filius* ch. 1): He wills her not because He lacks anything, but to share His goodness (ST I q.44 a.4). Spinoza paid his price clearly. The question is whether the reader can.

**R2. God row.**

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights:**
- **The real dividing line with the God-word naturalist is will against necessity, not mind against matter.** W2's "one Source" is common ground with Spinoza. What separates the accounts is whether the Source *wants* her. The book can say this in one sentence and gain sharpness throughout: "The question is not whether the world has one Source, but whether that Source wants anyone."
- **Spinoza agrees with the book on more than it expects.** He rejects the arbitrary lawgiver (TTP 4; compare the W1 closing). He holds an absolute against bad faith even at the cost of one's life (E IV P72; compare A6 and A7). He holds that the conatus never aims at its own death (E IV P20 sch; compare the second half of A6, seen from the agent's side). And he holds that blessedness is not virtue's reward but virtue itself (E V P42; compare the Catholic teaching that the reward is God Himself). Naming these would show that the book's absolutes are not Christian-only demands.
- **Modal collapse is the honest price of my view and makes a strong line for the evil section.** Spinoza removes the question "why did God permit it?" only by holding that nothing could have been otherwise, the child's torture included. The theist pays with mystery about why He permits evil; the Spinozist pays by making the evil necessary.
- **E V P19 and E I App are the two best philosophical objections to "a God who loves us back,"** and the Thomist answers (love as willing the good; acting to share, not to gain) are already in the book's sources. Set them where the objections land.
- **Einstein's religion splits across two rows** (refined feeling for ethics, Spinoza for awe). A reader who says "I believe in Einstein's God" should find himself in C.

**Lines worth keeping (unchanged):**
- "Heaven and Earth give life to things; God wanted *you*." This is the strongest line against me.
- "Our love answers to a worth already there; God's love gives it."
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."
- "This Lawgiver is not a commander who picks rules. A will that invents values can command, but it cannot bind."
- "A yes said to whatever happens is said to no one; it must say yes to what was done to her as well as to her, and so it cannot take her side." (Keep it, extended as in R6.)
- "The parasite takes from the child; God does not give her to it."
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it."

## 8. Score

**7 / 10 as written; 8.5 / 10 with R1–R8.** The "Someone, not something" case does not beg the question against me. It names its contested premises (A2–A4) and weighs them, and every argument it brings against the impersonal order lands on Deus sive Natura too. But it never faces by name the one rival that keeps God's name and matches its one-source claim, so a reader of Spinoza or Einstein must do the comparison himself.

**Honest ceiling for any theistic case against my school: about 8.5.** The dispute ends in parity: a strong principle of sufficient reason and necessity on my side, against the trustworthiness of personal moral experience (guilt owed, worth given, the wish to be wanted) on the theist's. No argument from either side can force the other. A case that states that standoff plainly and shows the price on both sides has done all that reason can do here.
