# Blind review, Round 8: the contractualist / second-personal school

## 1. School and voice

I speak for the view that wronging is a failure of justification owed to each person: an act is wrong when principles that no one could reasonably reject would forbid it (Scanlon, *What We Owe to Each Other*). Darwall adds that moral obligation is second-personal. It consists in the standing of persons to make claims on one another and to hold one another to account (*The Second-Person Standpoint*). Wallace develops the same relational structure for directed duties, blame and guilt (*The Moral Nexus*, verify). Rawls supplies the political form: fair terms among free and equal persons. I am a non-theist, but the school's formula is neutral about God, and a theist can accept most of it. My question is therefore not whether the text's morality is right, since we agree about most of it. My question is whether its move from wronging to a Lawgiver adds anything that my account does not already explain at parity.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** What anyone is owed is whatever no principle could deny her without her having reasonable grounds to reject it. Those grounds come from the generic reasons of people in her position: life, bodily security, being told the truth, having a say in what is done to her. Rawls reaches the same point through fair terms of cooperation among free and equal persons. **Holds.** It does rest on a primitive normative idea, "reasonable," which is not derived from anything further (Scanlon accepts this, verify). That is a reliance on a basic notion, not a borrowing from theism.

**2. A hard dilemma.** Case: there is one ventilator and two 40-year-old patients with the same clinical prognosis. One has Down syndrome. A principle that gives priority by perceived quality of life or social value could reasonably be rejected by the person it disfavors. The burden on her is the same, death, and the stated reason is only how others value her. Principles that allocate by medical benefit, with a lottery when benefit is equal, can be justified to both. Scanlon's Transmitter Room case adds the anti-aggregation rule: no number of small benefits to many outweighs one person's grave harm (verify). **Holds** without truth, justice, mercy and self-gift as the book defines them. Mercy has no independent role. **Breaks at the edges**, though. Contractualists divide over killing one to avert a catastrophe that would kill millions, and the tie-breaking argument for numbers is contested.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** Equality is structural on my view: each person is owed justification, and no one's complaint is discounted for who she is. The enemy and the criminal stay inside that scope, so that case **holds** firmly. The ground of the scope is the capacity for judgment-sensitive attitudes (Scanlon), second-personal competence (Darwall), or moral personality as a range property (Rawls, *Theory of Justice* §77, verify). For the newborn, the dying and the severely disabled, the school extends standing in two ways. Trustees speak for them, and Scanlon holds that being born of human parents is itself a strong reason to give a being the same standing (verify). **Borrows / partly breaks.** That extension is an added premise. It is not derived from the contract, and on Darwall's version it strains the most. This is my school's weakest point, and I concede it.

**4. Ending point for flourishing.** There is no single ultimate good. I hold a pluralism of goods, among them friendship, understanding, achievement, self-respect (Rawls's most important primary good) and a life lived on terms of mutual recognition, and justice is the first virtue of institutions. Death ends a life that can still be complete. **Coherent.** It deliberately declines to name an ultimate good. It breaks only if one requires an ultimate good, and that requirement is the very issue between us and W3, so it is a standoff, not a defect.

## 3. Debate highlights

**E1. Scope: "cannot explain."**
*Attack.* The C row says contractualism "cannot explain" why being born one of us puts the newborn among those to whom we must answer. That is false as written. Scanlon addresses the question directly: being born of human parents gives a strong reason to grant a being full standing, and trustees press its claims (WWO ch. 4, verify). Rawls's range property is another answer. The text also undercuts its own charge, since W1(2) says "Being human explains why the newborn belongs." My school uses exactly that.
*Defense.* The charge is not that contractualism says nothing. The point is that the explanation does not come from the contract. The formula covers those who can reject principles, and the kind-based extension is a separate premise whose normative weight is assumed. The theist roots belonging and equality in one source: a rational nature given with her being and an equal call (GS 19, 24; CCC 1700). It does not add an outside relation to a theory built for those with judgment-sensitive attitudes.
*Rejoinder.* That defense is right, and it is not what the text says. The repaired charge is fair. Our extension is a premise we accept, as the theist accepts that God is the Good. Even so, the theist's single source covers the newborn without an add-on, and ours does not. **Held with repair** (wording in §6).

**E2. The grave: does guilt require a forgiver?**
*Attack.* W1(1) assumes that a good account must provide for release. It need not. Moral reality may simply contain unreleasable debts. Wanting release is the kind of comfort A7 forbids us to believe on.
*Defense.* The text does not argue from comfort. It argues from what guilt itself is about: guilt asks her for pardon, and the text calls this only "a clue" that weighs "as much as the reader finds the third road ... truer." It concedes that the second road is noble. It also uses the second-personal analysis: forgiveness belongs to the victim, and no one on earth can give it in her place (A3).
*Rejoinder.* Here my own school sharpens the text instead of blunting it. On Darwall's view forgiveness is a second-personal power belonging to the one wronged, and it cannot be delegated. Trustees can press the claims of the dead, as they press the newborn's, but they cannot forgive for her. So the account that explains wronging best is also the one that sees most clearly that the dead can never forgive on its terms. I can live with that. Remorse without pardon is coherent. But I cannot say it costs nothing. **Held** (a parity standoff, honestly weighted).

**E3. God's forgiveness and the victim's standing.**
*Attack.* If "only God forgives sins" (CCC 1441), and every wrong is "before all else" an offense against Him, a third party is remitting a wrong done to her. That is exactly what Kant's clemency rule forbids, and it subordinates her standing.
*Defense.* The text separates the two explicitly. God forgives the offense against Him, which is His love refused in her. He does not take her place, the debt to her remains, and repair is still owed (CCC 1459). Scripture puts reconciliation with the brother before the altar (Matthew 5:23–24). Her claim is to truth, repair and her own pardon, not to the wrongdoer's punishment, because vengeance belongs to the Lord (Romans 12:19).
*Rejoinder.* That answer is coherent. But the Kant row's sentence "God does what Kant's ruler may not" leaves it unclear on what ground He may. The ground is the text's own claim that the wrong is also against Him, and it should say so in that sentence. **Held with repair** (Minor).

**E4. Killing on request: what makes it a wrong "to her"?**
*Attack.* W1's closing says "The wrong is wrong because of what it does to her." In W1(2), though, the prohibition on killing a man who asks for death rests on a trust from "the One who alone is Lord of life." When she consents, the second-personal wrong to her is gone: consent changes the normative situation. What remains is a wrong to the one who entrusted her life. That brings back the "second injury" W1 disowned, and makes the wrong look like a wrong because forbidden.
*Defense.* A3 already holds that some things cannot be consented into being right, such as enslavement, and my school agrees. The trust is not ownership by a stranger. It is held "for her own sake" (GS 24). Aiming at her death turns against the good of her own life even when she asks for it, and the Church says the same of taking one's own life: it is contrary to love of self as well as to love of God (CCC 2281; Aquinas gives all three reasons, ST II-II q.64 a.5). The text also says plainly that this is "this book's claim," not a shared premise.
*Rejoinder.* Contractualism explains why enslavement cannot be waived without a trustor: people under pressure, and the general standpoint, give reasonable grounds to reject any principle that permits it. Most contractualists would allow a dying man's request under safeguards (verify). So we are at a standoff on which goods cannot be alienated. The text is honest about that, but its wording should show that "what it does to her" covers this case too. **Held with repair** (Minor).

**E5. Evil: is "the order of the whole" aggregation?**
*Attack.* The text says the Stoic Providence "cares for the whole and spends the person, as a part." Its own theodicy rests on God willing an order of the whole in which some things are naturally corrupted (ST I q.19 a.9), with the child's cancer permitted as a side effect of goods that are largely general. That is the Transmitter Room on a cosmic scale.
*Defense.* The text's distinction is the one in A6: side effects may be weighed, but persons may not be spent. More to the point, "within that order He governs each person for her own sake" (ScG III.112), and "the good the Church hopes for is a good offered to her." Contractualism itself accepts ex ante justification of principles that impose risk (Scanlon on risk; Frick, verify). If each person's prospect includes a good offered to her that is incomparably greater (Romans 8:18), the order can be justified to each one, not just to the total.
*Rejoinder.* That is the right answer, and it is the contractualist test applied to God: could it be justified to her? But for the infant who dies in agony, it rests entirely on an eschatological hope the text admits it cannot show, and the fawn falls outside my school's scope. The text already puts all of this on the scale and claims only "a setting, not an explanation." **Held** (optional strengthening in §6).

**E6. "One source for three things."**
*Attack.* The God row says the realist must take three things separately: the law, the beings it is about, and the minds that read it. The beings and minds are contingent and causally explained. Only the normative truths are basic for us. Theism moves the brute fact to "God is the Good," and the Euthyphro question returns for worth: is she willed because worthy, or worthy because willed?
*Defense.* Neither. Her worth follows her nature, and her nature shares in the Good, which is God's nature (Chapter 3, ST I q.20 a.2). God's love gives the worth by giving the being, not by declaring it valuable. The realist still has a contingent world whose existence is brute. A converging case may legitimately count that (CCC 31–32), and the text claims "reach, not count."
*Rejoinder.* In that case the cosmological consideration is doing part of the work, and the moral contribution is the fit of law to mind, which W2 says "weighs, it does not settle." That is a fair claim. **Held.**

**E7. "God wanted you": does being wanted ground worth?**
*Attack.* Being wanted, even by God, cannot be what makes her matter. My school's individualism comes from the separateness of standpoints, and each person must be answered one by one. No wanter is needed.
*Defense.* The text agrees that our love answers to a worth already there. Its claim is that the individual exists as an end because she is willed for her own sake (GS 24), and it aims this at impersonal orders (moon and rivers), not at contractualism.
*Rejoinder.* Agreed. Against my school the only thing left over is scope again: separateness of standpoints extends only as far as there are standpoints, while "willed for itself" extends to every human being. That is E1, not a new defect. **Held.**

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text as written

**As written: NONE.**

No Fatal or Serious argumentative objection stands from my school. The text grants contractualism what it actually explains (the directed wrong, answering to each one, no totals, reaching the abandoned child). It presses only the two real stopping points, scope and the grave, and weights both as clues or as what a rival assumes, not as refutations.

**Misstatements of my school** (most important first):
1. C row: "Why being born one of us puts her among those to whom we must answer, it cannot explain." Scanlon offers an explanation: being born of human parents gives a strong reason for full standing, together with trusteeship (verify). The text's own W1(2) grants that "Being human explains why the newborn belongs." The correct charge is narrower: the explanation does not come from the contract, and its normative weight is assumed. This falls short of Serious only because the repaired claim still stands. Left as written, it is the line a contractualist reader would quote against the whole case, and it breaks the C section's own rule ("Each is stated as its best defenders state it").
2. C row: "a wrong is what could not be justified to the one it is done to." This is close, but wrongness is what principles no one could reasonably reject would disallow. The victim's complaint is usually the decisive one, but not the only one. Minor.
3. C row: "Why answering to each one is what right and wrong are." Scanlon limits contractualism to "what we owe each other," the part of morality concerned with wronging persons, not all of right and wrong. Minor.

**Minor items:**
- Kant row: "God does what Kant's ruler may not" does not state the ground (E3).
- W1(2): the trust reason for refusing to kill on request should be tied explicitly to "what it does to her" (E4), or it reads as a wrong because forbidden.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**
- The grave: unreleasable debt with remorse (my road) against a victim kept to forgive (the text's third road). The text weighs it correctly as a clue.
- Stopping points: the value of mutual recognition and "reasonable" as primitives, against God as the Good. Neither is less brute in principle; the text claims reach, not count.
- Inalienability of life at one's own request, and the number absolute: these are the book's flagged claims, contested by most of my school.
- Theodicy under the "justifiable to her" test: it passes in form (ScG III.112, a good offered to her), and the content rests on a hope the text admits it cannot show.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact old → new)

1. **C row, scope:**
Old: "But it stops at two things. Why being born one of us puts her among those to whom we must answer, it cannot explain."
New: "But it stops at two things. Why being born one of us puts her among those to whom we must answer, its best defenders explain by a relation: she is born of us and of our kind, and others may speak for her as trustees. Why that relation, and not her powers, gives her full and equal standing, it takes as given."

2. **C row, definition:**
Old: "(contractualism: a wrong is what could not be justified to the one it is done to)"
New: "(contractualism: a wrong is what principles no one could reasonably reject would forbid, and so what could not be justified to the one it is done to)"

3. **C row, scope of the theory:**
Old: "Why answering to each one is what right and wrong are,"
New: "Why answering to each one is what we owe each other,"

4. **Kant row:**
Old: "God does what Kant's ruler may not, forgiving the sin whole (CCC 1441), yet not as impunity:"
New: "God does what Kant's ruler may not, because every such wrong is also an offense against Him: He forgives the sin whole (CCC 1441), yet not as impunity:"

5. **W1(2), the trust:**
Old: "what the trust forbids is that her death be the thing aimed at."
New: "what the trust forbids is that her death be the thing aimed at, for that turns against the good of her own life even when she asks it, against love of herself as well as of God, as the Church says of taking one's own life (CCC 2281)."

6. **Evil, "Second"** (optional strengthening). After:
"*What* He brings from the evil is a further thing: for a person who suffers, the good the Church hopes for is a good offered to her; God never spends anyone as a mere means against her own good."
insert:
"The best rivals put the right test to Him: could it be justified to her? An order of the whole cannot answer that by itself, as the Stoics' could not; only what is offered to her can, and that is a hope, not a proof."

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights:**
- **The second-personal account sharpens W1(1).** On Darwall's analysis, forgiveness belongs to the one wronged and cannot be delegated. Trustees can press the claims of the dead, as they press the newborn's, but cannot pardon for her (verify). So the godless account that explains wronging best is also the one that sees most clearly that, on its terms, the dead can never forgive. The book could cite this as a rival's own analysis supporting A3.
- **Anscombe's question has a strong godless answer, and that answer has a telling shape.** Darwall keeps "obligation" by tying it to accountability to a community of free and equal persons. The demand needs someone with standing to make it (verify). That joins Smith's impartial spectator and Kant's moral Ruler as a third witness that obligation seeks an addresser. Darwall himself would refuse the inference to God, so present it as shape, not proof.
- **"Could it be justified to her?" is the right test for the evil section.** The text's ScG III.112 line already meets it in form. Naming the test makes the contrast with Stoic Providence exact instead of rhetorical.
- **Contractualism is largely an ally.** Its formula is neutral about God, and a Catholic can accept most of it. The real disagreement is narrow: scope and the grave. Saying so would make the C row more credible.
- **Rawls's range property** (equality as a threshold capacity) is the honest secular admission the book needs. It shows exactly where the newborn and the severely disabled sit at the edge of every capacity-based account (verify).

**Lines worth keeping:**
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."
- "Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent."
- The betrayed secret and the letter blown into the street (A2): the best short case for directed wrongs I know of.
- "Equal worth is not equal duty."
- "The parasite takes from the child; God does not give her to it."
- "God says no to what was done to her, and yes to her."
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it."

## 8. Score

**8 / 10.** The text states my school fairly in substance, grants what it explains, presses only its two real stopping points (scope and the grave) and weights them honestly. The one flaw is the "cannot explain" clause, which misreports Scanlon; repaired, the text rises to about 9.

**Honest ceiling for any theistic case against my school: 8–9.** The core of contractualism, justifiability to each, is compatible with theism, so no theistic case can refute it. At most a theistic case can show that the scope of equal standing for those without rational capacities, and the possibility of forgiveness from the dead, are points we must assume or concede, and that theism covers them from one source. This text reaches that ceiling once the repairs are made.
