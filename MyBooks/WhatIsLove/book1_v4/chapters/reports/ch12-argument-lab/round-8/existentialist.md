# Blind review, Round 8: the atheist existentialist school

## 1. School and voice

I speak for atheist existentialism: Sartre (existence precedes essence; there is no human nature because there is no God to conceive it; we are condemned to be free; values exist only through the freedom that projects them; seeing values as ready-made is the "spirit of seriousness" and bad faith), Camus (the absurd is the meeting of the human demand for meaning and justice with a world that stays silent; we answer it with lucid revolt, solidarity and measure, without appeal and without hope), and Simone de Beauvoir (the ethics of ambiguity: to will oneself free is to will others free; oppression is the evil). I take the text's starting points as targets as well as its steps: that we have a nature we are *for*, that values are found rather than made, that guilt points to Someone. I argue as a fair opponent. Where my own masters lived as though the text were right, I say so. All paraphrases of Sartre, Camus and Beauvoir below are mine and should be checked (verify).

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** Each human being is a freedom, a transcendence that goes beyond what is given. Justice means the reciprocal recognition of freedoms. What anyone is owed is not to be oppressed: not to have his transcendence cut down into mere "immanence," and to have the material conditions that freedom needs. Beauvoir derives this from the claim that my freedom can reach its ends only through other freedoms, which take up my projects and carry them on (verify). → **Borrows.** The core is coherent. The step from "I will my freedom" to "I must will yours" does not come from my choice; it is a universal norm I find already in force. That is Kant's move (Sartre's own "in choosing myself I choose man," verify), and Sartre later disowned the lecture where he made it.

**2. A hard dilemma.** The case is the captured insurgent in Algeria, tortured to find a bomb. Sartre (preface to Alleg's *La Question*) and Beauvoir (the Djamila Boupacha case) said no. Torture turns a freedom into a thing and makes the torturer a thing as well (verify). Camus's Kaliayev in *The Just Assassins* will not throw the bomb while children are in the carriage, and he pays for the later killing with his own life. → **Holds and breaks.** It holds in Camus, who keeps a limit, but he does so by appeal to a value that revolt *discovers*. It breaks in Sartre and Beauvoir. Sartre's preface to Fanon endorsed counter-violence, and Beauvoir allowed that liberation may require treating some men as things (verify). For both, the line bends when the cause is large enough. Sartre's student, torn between the Free French and his mother, receives only "choose." That is honest, but it gives no guidance.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** Our best answer is that freedom has no degrees. On Sartre's ontology every consciousness is wholly free, so the slow and the gifted, the friend and the enemy, stand equal, and capacity is never graded (verify). This reaches further than Kant's rational capacity does. → **Breaks** for the person who is not, or never will again be, a consciousness: the permanently unconscious, the dying in coma, perhaps the earliest newborn. For them worth is whatever our freedom chooses to give, and equal worth "wins only by being ours," which is the text's own charge in the Feeling row. **Borrows** in Camus, who says revolt shows a human nature common to all (*The Rebel*, verify). That concedes a found nature carrying a found value.

**4. The end of human flourishing.** There is no ultimate good and no end we are for. What remains is authenticity (lucid acceptance of freedom and situation), willing the freedom of others, revolt, solidarity, measure, and happiness in the present: "one must imagine Sisyphus happy" (verify). → **Holds** as a coherent and honest stopping point. It refuses the "ultimately" in the question and pays the price openly: love is answered in the end by no one, and the dead are not kept. That is exactly where the text's W3 says such an account stops. My school accepts that place and calls it the truth.

## 3. Debate highlights

**E1. Found or made? (A preface, A5 "we find")**
*Attack.* The preface's method ("what still seems true after honest reflection") assumes that values come to us as given. That assumption is the spirit of seriousness. Pure reflection shows that a value's demand is held up only by my freedom. Nothing in being, which is simply what it is, contains a value. Value is a lack, and only a for-itself brings lack into the world.
*Defense.* Sartre grants that in unreflective experience values do come as demands. His claim that reflection shows them to be projected is not a further seeming. It is drawn from his ontology, in which value is a lack and the in-itself has none. The book's Chapter 1 holds the rival ontology: being and good are convertible, and evil is a privation. That makes it ontology against ontology, not seeming against seeming. On the seemings themselves, Sartre and Beauvoir condemned the torture of Algerian prisoners whatever France approved. They did not treat it as one choice of values among others. The text's preface lets a belief stand "until something more certain shows it false," and Sartre's dualism is less certain than the wrongness of what was done to Boupacha.
*Rejoinder.* I do not deny the demand. I deny that its ground lies outside freedom.
*Defense.* Then the wrongness is real and binding, and the only question left is its ground. That question belongs to W, which is where the text puts it.
**Held.**

**E2. "There is no human nature" (A5, W1(2), W2)**
*Attack.* Sartre's paper-knife argument: a thing has a nature it is *for* only if a maker conceived it first. There is no God, so there is no human nature-for and no end to answer to. Your W1(2) phrase "made for an end" assumes what it needs.
*Defense.* A5 assumes nothing of the kind. It says that "whether that means we are *for* something, and whether anyone made us for it, is what W must show." Sartre also grants the text's conditional, that a nature we are for goes with a Creator; W1(2) says the same: "here the moral question and the question of creation meet." The two sides agree on the logic and disagree on the antecedent, and the text argues the antecedent from A1 to A4. Sartre also keeps a universal "human condition" and universal structures of consciousness, and Camus goes further and finds a human nature.
*Rejoinder.* A condition is not an end. It is structure without any goal.
*Defense.* Then the existentialist has to explain why cruelty would be wrong "for any freedom," as he himself says, using structure alone. That is a necessary truth about every possible freedom, true before any freedom exists. It is exactly the reach question in W2, and W2 says honestly that the question "weighs, it does not settle."
**Held.**

**E3. "A will that invents values can command, but it cannot bind" (W1 closing)**
*Attack.* This one line is your entire answer to my school, and it is asserted, not argued. Beauvoir reverses Dostoevsky: if God is absent, far from everything being permitted, man is answerable for everything, and his acts are definitive commitments (verify). Your own A3 shows a will binding itself, since a promise is made by the will and still binds.
*Defense.* The sentence is aimed at a voluntarist God, not at human ethics. A promise binds only under a norm that no promiser invented, namely that promises are to be kept, and A3's "a friend can release a promise as a gift" assumes that norm. Beauvoir's demand that freedom will itself and other freedoms is not chosen either. A person who refuses it is in bad faith, and Sartre calls that a "judgment of truth," something one is in before choosing anything (verify). So the binding she describes rests on a norm she found.
*Rejoinder.* It is constitutive of freedom, as logic is constitutive of thought. It is not a value sitting "out there."
*Defense.* A necessary norm that holds for every possible freedom is what the realist and Kant rows describe, and W2 asks where such a law holds.
**Held with repair.** The argument holds, but the sentence as written overclaims and can be read against the text's own A3. See R2.

**E4. Equal dignity without God (A4, W1(2), conclusion)**
*Attack.* Our freedom is all or nothing, so dignity is equal and owes nothing to ability. We have no "stretch to reach the newborn" problem of the kind the text finds in Kant.
*Defense.* This is my opponent's strongest card, and it reaches the slow, the enemy and probably the conscious newborn. It does not reach the permanently unconscious or the dying in coma. More basically, the *ought* to respect another's freedom is something freedom wills, and Sartre and Beauvoir let it bend for liberation. The conclusion's test, the weak newborn left on the hillside, "wronged no less" than the strong one, asks whether worth follows what she is or what she can do or what we choose. Sartre has to answer "what we choose," at least for the never-conscious. Camus's answer is "what she is," and that is the text's answer without the text's ground.
*Rejoinder.* Camus's human nature is found in revolt, and revolt is human. No God is needed.
*Defense.* Then a found nature carries a found value, which is A5's "we find" and A4's "prior to anyone's recognition." What remains is the question W2 asks of any nature: accident or wisdom.
**Held.** My school breaks at Sartre and Beauvoir and borrows at Camus.

**E5. The absurd and the debt to the dead (W1(1), God row in C)**
*Attack.* The debt to the dead is real and cannot be paid. That gap is the absurd. To count guilt's request for her pardon as a clue toward a world in which she can give it is Camus's "philosophical suicide": a leap made because the demand needs it. Grief is apt too, and it asks for the dead to come back. A fitting response is no evidence that what it asks for can be had.
*Defense.* W1(1) does not leap. It says "Reason cannot prove that hope." It calls the request "a clue" whose weight is "as much as the reader finds the third road ... truer than the second to what he owes," and it calls the second road, Camus's own road kept lucidly, "noble." A debt also differs from grief. Grief asks for presence. A debt is a claim of right, and A3, which my opponent accepts, holds that the claim outlives her. A claim that persists is a datum about her, not a wish of mine.
*Rejoinder.* The second road already grants that she can be wronged and owed "as she was," with no one now there to bear the claim. The datum is satisfied without her. And the God row in C turns your careful clue into an explanatory win: "it explains ... forgiveness when no one on earth is left to give it." That is not something in A. It is something guilt asks for.
*Defense.* That is fair. W1(1) is right, and the God row's wording goes further than W1(1) does.
**Held with repair.** See R4.

**E6. The longing to be justified in existing (W3, God row)**
*Attack.* W3 says Smith "does not as easily explain a longing to be told, before we have earned anything, that it is good that we exist." Sartre explains that longing at its root. The for-itself is a lack whose basic project is to be its own foundation, to be in-itself-for-itself, which is to be God. Roquentin's nausea is the felt contingency the longing tries to escape. The account explains the longing's existence, its object (an all-knowing look that would justify me), and why it cannot be met: man is "a useless passion" (verify). Your God row says the rivals "leave out" this longing. I do not leave it out. I explain it better than you do, and I predict its frustration.
*Defense.* W3 never says the longing is unexplained. It says "the longing alone settles nothing" and that it shows only "what answer would fit." It also separates the part that grasps, which must die, from what is left. Sartre's desire to be God is the grasping part, and the Church has named it: man "wanted to 'be like God,'" but "without God, before God, and not in accordance with God" (CCC 398; Genesis 3:5). Sartre diagnosed the shape of sin precisely. What remains is a longing not to be one's own ground but to be *given*: to be told by another that it is good that one exists. Sartre's object is incoherent because it fuses thing and consciousness. The Christian object is neither that fusion nor a rival look. It is pure act (ST I q.3 a.4), the One who gives the creature's freedom its very being (CCC 308). And CCC 398 says man was destined to be divinized *by God*. The destination Sartre longed for is real, and he sought it by the one road that cannot reach it.
*Rejoinder.* To be known wholly by an infinite gaze is still to be fixed as an object. That is the Look made absolute.
*Defense.* Only if knowing is a gaze from outside that competes with my freedom. In *Being and Nothingness* every love is that capture. Beauvoir's later account of authentic love as two freedoms recognizing each other (*The Second Sex*, verify) shows that being known need not mean being objectified even between finite persons. Creation is not competition (CCC 308): the One who knows me is the One who gives my freedom to me.
**Held with repair.** W3 should face Sartre by name, and the God row should not count the longing as something the rivals "leave out." See R3 and R4.

**E7. Rieux's refusal (the evil section)**
*Attack.* Camus's Dr. Rieux, at the deathbed of a child, refuses to love a creation in which children are tortured (*The Plague*, verify). The rebel says no to what was done to her and yes to her, and he needs no God to do it. Your line "a yes said to whatever happens is said to no one" hits Nietzsche's amor fati. It does not touch the rebel.
*Defense.* The line is about sources: an indifferent source, or a god beyond good and evil. It is not about human rebels. In everything that matters the text stands with Rieux against Paneloux's second sermon. God wills no loss for its own sake (ST I q.19 a.9). His permission "never excuses ours," and "the hands that pull a child from the fire" are part of what He made real (CCC 2269). God says no to what was done to her and yes to her. The text also refuses to call the weight light: "It does not answer the fawn ... or the child with cancer."
*Rejoinder.* Then the rebel does all the moral work and God adds nothing.
*Defense.* The rebel takes her side, but he cannot keep her. And the value Camus's revolt defends is one he says revolt *finds*, which is where the case began.
**Held.** On evil itself this is an honest standoff at parity, and the text weighs it as heavily as I would.

**E8. Eternity and the meaning of choice (W3, evil section)**
*Attack.* Beauvoir's *All Men Are Mortal*: Fosca cannot die, and his choices lose their weight because nothing is final. Endless life empties freedom. Sartre adds that a freedom which cannot choose otherwise, your saints "free and cannot sin," is no freedom at all.
*Defense.* The Christian hope is "not more of the same years" (W3). It also keeps Beauvoir's point inside itself: "this life is where the choice is made" (CCC 1021), and a refusal held to the end "becomes final" (CCC 1033). On the Catholic account eternity does not dissolve the stakes. It confirms them. On the saints, the Church teaches that the more one does what is good, the freer one becomes (CCC 1733). Freedom for the good is the fuller freedom, not its loss.
*Rejoinder.* That is your definition of freedom, not mine.
*Defense.* Yes. This is a standoff over what freedom is, and the text's position on it is coherent.
**Held.**

**E9. "Not even hers to waive" (A6, W1(2))**
*Attack.* If freedom is the source of value, forbidding a free woman to choose her own death is seriousness at its worst.
*Defense.* The text labels this as "this book's claims, argued in W1(2), not shared ones." It also has an unexpected ally on half of the question. Camus's *Myth of Sisyphus* argues against suicide: lucid revolt keeps the absurd alive, and suicide settles it falsely (verify). Camus also opposed the death penalty without exception (*Reflections on the Guillotine*), which stands with A4's "not lost ... by the worst of crimes."
**Standoff at parity.** It is honestly labeled.

## 4. Ledger

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**As written: NONE.**

Every attack my school can bring either misses A, because the text has left "for" to W; or ends at an honest standoff the text already names (ontology, contingency, evil, the dead, consent to death); or pushes my school into borrowing a found norm or a found nature. The C table does leave my school out, but the text's own resources answer it at parity or better: A4, the conclusion's newborn test, the Feeling row's "wins only by being ours," and W2's reach question. The omission is a defect of coverage, not of soundness.

**Minor items**
1. **No existentialist row in C** (the most important Minor). The prices charged to the nearest rows do not fit. Error theory's price (torture not really wrong) does not apply to Sartre and Beauvoir. Nietzsche's price (giving up A1–A4) does not apply to Beauvoir. Kant's "reason could unmake it" is accepted by my school. A reader whose creed is "you make your own meaning," the most common secular creed the book's audience will meet, will not find himself in the table. See R6.
2. **W1 closing slogan.** "A will that invents values ... cannot bind" is unargued and is undercut by A3's promise. See R2.
3. **W3 meets Smith but not Sartre** on the longing to be justified in existing. See R3.
4. **The God row overcounts reach.** It turns W1(1)'s clue into "explains forgiveness" and counts as left out a longing that Sartre explains. See R4.
5. **The Power-row dilemma** leaves out the existentialist third option, though that option falls into the first horn. See R5.
6. **The preface's list of deniers** leaves out the existentialists. See R1.

**Misstatements of my school:** None. The text never names it. The one near miss is the W1 closing, which can be read as a one-line dismissal of existentialist ethics.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Value as lack (Sartre) against being and good as convertible (Chapter 1).
- Accident or wisdom (W2).
- Rieux's refusal against the Church's setting for evil.
- The lucid second road against the third road for the dead.
- Freedom to choose one's death against life held in trust (on which Camus is a partial ally).
- What freedom is (the saints' freedom).

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1 (A preface).**
Old: "Singer denies parts of A2, A4, A6 and A7; and many who hold all the rest deny"
New: "Singer denies parts of A2, A4, A6 and A7; Sartre and Beauvoir deny that any of them is found rather than made by human freedom, though both condemned the torture of prisoners in Algeria whatever their country approved; and many who hold all the rest deny"

**R2 (W1 closing).**
Old: "A will that invents values can command, but it cannot bind."
New: "A will that invents values can command; it can even bind itself, as a promise does, but only under a law it did not invent, and it cannot make cruelty good."

**R3 (W3).**
Old: "Smith's account does not as easily explain a longing to be told, before we have earned anything, that it is good that we exist."
New: "Smith's account does not as easily explain a longing to be told, before we have earned anything, that it is good that we exist. Sartre explained even that: we are a lack that longs to be its own ground, which is to long to be God, and so the longing is necessary and can never be met. That is true of the longing that grasps, the wish to be like God without God (Genesis 3:5; CCC 398); it is not true of the longing to be given, and to be told by another that it is good that we exist is to be given, not to be our own ground."

**R4 (God row in C).**
Old: "including what the best rivals leave out or must take as given: forgiveness when no one on earth is left to give it;"
New: "including what the best rivals leave out, leave unanswered, or must take as given: a debt to the dead that she herself can one day receive and forgive, which on their accounts is owed forever and never received;"
Old: "and a longing no one on earth can answer."
New: "and a longing no one on earth can answer, which the best of them can explain but not answer."

**R5 (Power and rank row).**
Old: "but it cannot show that anyone ought to keep that honesty, his own included."
New: "but it cannot show that anyone ought to keep that honesty, his own included. (Sartre and Beauvoir offer a third road: honesty binds every free mind, because to deceive oneself about one's freedom is a lie at the root. But that is the first horn: something then binds every mind before it chooses, and W2 asks where.)"

**R6 (new C row; insert immediately before "- **A God who is Love** is also a stopping point").**
New:
"- **Freedom that makes its values** (atheist existentialism: Sartre and Simone de Beauvoir, with Camus near them) agrees with this case more than it seems. It holds with A5 that nothing we are settles what we ought to do; with A7 that we may not lie to ourselves; and with A2 that we answer for what we do to another. Sartre and Beauvoir condemned the torture of prisoners in Algeria, and Camus the death penalty, whatever the state approved. It also grants the case's hinge: Sartre held that only a Creator could give us a nature we are *for*, and that without God there is none, so values are not found but made by a freedom no one gave us. Its strongest answer to A4 is that freedom has no degrees: the slow and the gifted, the friend and the enemy, are each wholly free. Its price: the one who is not yet, or will never again be, a freedom has the worth that our freedom chooses to give her, and then equal worth wins only by being chosen (A4); its own masters let the line bend when liberation seemed to need it (A6); and what binds a freedom to will others' freedom, rather than flee it, is a law no freedom chose (W2). Camus saw further: revolt, he held, shows a nature common to all and a value the rebel finds and does not make; but then the question of W2 returns. And it stops where every account without Someone stops: the debt to the dead is never received, and the longing to be justified in existing is, on Sartre's account, a passion that must fail. Camus asked us to live that silence lucidly, without appeal. That honesty this book honors (A7). But the rebel can take her side; he cannot keep her."

**R7 (evil section, First; optional).**
Old: "God says no to what was done to her, and yes to her (Wisdom 1:13; CCC 413)."
New: "God says no to what was done to her, and yes to her (Wisdom 1:13; CCC 413). The rebel who refuses such a world says it too, and takes her side; but he cannot keep her."

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **Sartre concedes the case's conditional.** He agrees that a nature we are *for* stands or falls with a Creator, and he denies only the antecedent. That makes the existentialist the rival who most openly shares the book's logic, and the conclusion's newborn test is a modus tollens aimed squarely at him. His and Beauvoir's own judgments on Algeria are the best evidence that they could not live by their antecedent.
- **CCC 398 names Sartre's "useless passion."** Man wanted "to be like God" without God. The Church agrees about the destination (divinization) and differs about the road (gift, not self-grounding). This could be one of the strongest pages in the chapter.
- **Beauvoir's *All Men Are Mortal* sharpens the Catholic teaching on finality.** CCC 1021 and 1033 mean eternity does not empty choice; it confirms it. The worry that endless life drains freedom of meaning has its answer inside the doctrine the evil section already states.
- **Camus is a non-theist ally on two of the book's own claims:** against suicide (lucid revolt refuses a false resolution) and against the death penalty without exception (A4, CCC 2267). Kaliayev, who holds the line at children and pays with his life, is a secular picture of A6's "persons may not be spent" and of sacrifice.
- **Beauvoir's reversal of Dostoevsky is worth granting.** Without God, everything is *not* permitted. What God's absence removes is not obligation but the One who forgives and keeps. That concession makes W1 sharper.
- **Freedom without degrees is a better non-theist ground for equality than rational capacity.** The book should meet it at its strongest and show where it stops: the never-conscious, and an "ought" that freedom only wills.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it." (the most direct answer in the text to Sartre)
- "Our love answers to a worth already there; God's love gives it."
- "Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent."
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."
- "God says no to what was done to her, and yes to her."
- "Heaven and Earth give life to things; God wanted *you*."
- New, for the existentialist row: "The rebel can take her side; he cannot keep her."

## 8. Score

**8 / 10.** The argument already defeats my school at parity or better through A4, the newborn test and the Feeling row's "wins only by being ours," but it does so without naming us. Two claims, the W1 closing line and the God row's reach list, are asserted where Sartre would have to be argued with. With R2 through R6 the text would reach about 9.

**Honest ceiling for any theistic case against my school: 9.** An existentialist who accepts the price, letting worth for the never-conscious be chosen and letting the line bend for liberation, cannot be compelled by moral data. A theistic case can show that he cannot live his own antecedent, as Sartre and Beauvoir did not, but it cannot refute his ontology.
