# Blind review, Round 8: the Kantian school

## 1. School and voice

I speak for Kant and his best modern readers: Christine Korsgaard (constitutivism, the regress argument for the value of humanity), Allen Wood (humanity as the ground, and respect for rational nature in its potential or impaired forms), Barbara Herman (moral judgment and salience), Onora O'Neill (reason's authority as reflexive and self-vindicating), and Thomas Hill (dignity as above price). Kant himself was a theist on grounds of *practical* faith. The school as it stands today is mostly non-theist, and that is the voice I argue in here. Our core claims are four. The moral law is the law of a free rational will as such. It binds by reason's own authority and not by any author's command. Humanity in every person has a dignity that "admits no equivalent" (Kant's phrase, *Groundwork* 4:434; verify translation). And theoretical reason cannot know God, though practical reason may hope. I grade this text as an opponent who agrees with much of what it says and disputes why it is true.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**(1) Starting point for justice.** We start from the one innate right: freedom, meaning independence from another's constraining choice, so far as it can coexist with everyone's freedom under a universal law (*Metaphysics of Morals* [MM] 6:230, 6:237). Innate equality comes with it. What anyone is owed is respect for that freedom (right) and treatment of his humanity always as an end and never merely as a means (ethics, *Groundwork* 4:429). → **Holds.** This is derived from autonomy and borrows nothing. One strain shows up at the edges: right is framed around beings who have choice, so the newborn's claim has to come through Kant's ruling that children are persons from birth (MM 6:280–281).

**(2) A hard dilemma, resolved.** A competent man, dying and in pain that cannot be relieved, asks his physician for a lethal dose. Kant's answer runs through maxims. A maxim of ending one's life to escape ills, out of self-love, cannot be universalized without contradiction (G 4:422). It also uses the humanity in one's own person as a mere means to a bearable condition (G 4:429; MM 6:422–423). The physician may not adopt the maxim of killing. He must adopt the maxim of beneficence, which means making the patient's permissible ends his own (MM 6:388, 6:452), and that means relief, presence and the refusal of futile burdens. Herman's account of moral salience explains why the patient's suffering is the first thing a good agent sees and still does not settle what is permitted. → **Holds without borrowing.** Respect, truthfulness and beneficence are derived from autonomy, not from the book's definitions, though they *resemble* justice, truth and mercy. Honest note: the school is split here. Kant and J. David Velleman ("A Right of Self-Termination?", 1999; verify) hold most of the line. Hill ("Self-Regarding Suicide: A Modified Kantian View"; verify) permits some cases.

**(3) Equal, unconditional dignity.** Dignity belongs to humanity, the rational nature in each person. It is a worth beyond price and admits no equivalent, so it cannot be compared or summed (G 4:434–436). It is grounded in the predisposition to personality that comes with human nature (*Religion* 6:26–28), not in performance. It is not lost through vice: I cannot deny all respect even to the vicious man (MM 6:463, paraphrase). Kant explicitly counts the newborn as a person (MM 6:280). For the severely disabled, Wood's reading and Patrick Kain's species-membership reading (verify) both say we respect rational nature where it is present by kind, even if it is impaired or never exercised. → **Holds, but strains at the margins.** Korsgaard's regress argument directly reaches only beings who confer value by choosing. Reaching the rest takes a step from capacity to *kind*, and Kant's critical philosophy can hold that step only from the practical standpoint, because it denies us theoretical knowledge of the soul (the Paralogisms). That is the same step the text takes ("it follows what she is"), but we take it without the metaphysics that would back it.

**(4) Ultimate flourishing.** The supreme good is a good will, which is worthiness to be happy. The complete good is happiness proportioned to virtue, in a realm of ends where persons treat one another as ends (*Critique of Practical Reason* [CPrR] 5:110–111). Our guiding principles are the formulas of the categorical imperative. → **Breaks, or else borrows, at the top.** Kant himself held that the complete good can be thought achievable only if we postulate God and immortality (CPrR 5:122–132). Secular Kantians reread the highest good as a this-worldly social ideal (Rawls, Andrews Reath; verify). That is coherent but scaled back: it gives up the very proportion Kant said reason demands. On this question our founder walked over to the text's side, and we have not walked back on his terms.

## 3. Debate highlights

**E1. "Where does the law hold?"**
*Attack.* The Kant row asks "where does a law for every possible mind hold." That is a category mistake. The moral law has *validity*, not *existence*. It is the form of a reasoning will, as the law of non-contradiction is the form of thinking. It needs no place to be kept, any more than modus ponens does.
*Defense.* The text already grants the constitutive reading ("the very form of a free will … each finds himself under it"). Its question is not about location but about explanation: why there are beings whose own law this is, and why their a priori reach fits truths that hold for every possible mind. W2 says outright that this "weighs, it does not settle."
*Rejoinder.* On Korsgaard's constitutivism there is no fit to explain. Law and mind are not two things that happen to match; the law is what being a reflective chooser *is*. Only the second question remains, how such choosers arose in nature. Kant said that cannot be explained (G 4:459–463). The theist answers with a cause ("created by God"), but he cannot explain how that cause works either.
*Verdict.* **Held with repair.** Restate the question in its Kantian form (Repair R1). On reach and source, the two sides end in a standoff at parity.

**E2. "If reason made it, reason could unmake it."**
*Attack.* No Kantian holds that reason "made" the law in a way that could be undone. Kant says the rational will must regard itself as the law's author, but authorship here is self-legislation by reason as such, not a choice (G 4:431). Beside the quoted denial of authorship (MM 6:227), the sentence about unmaking is a straw man. The phrase "each finds himself under it" also undersells autonomy: the law is his own, not something he discovers lying over him.
*Defense.* The text corrects itself in the very next sentence by adopting the "best readers'" view, and its target is a crude constructivism that some do hold.
*Rejoinder.* Then that crude view should be named, or the sentence cut. As written, it is attached to Kant.
*Verdict.* **Held with repair** (R1).

**E3. The newborn "stretch" applies to both sides.**
*Attack.* The text says dignity grounded in reason "must stretch to reach the newborn." The Catholic also grounds dignity in rational nature, the individual substance of a rational nature, and the newborn exercises no reason either. The "call to communion" cannot be the *necessary* ground of natural dignity at the level of reason, because the Church teaches the call is gratuitous: God could have made rational creatures without ordering them to the vision of Himself (*Humani Generis* 26 condemns denying this). So at the level of reason, both sides rest equal dignity on rational nature held by kind.
*Defense.* The Catholic step is not a stretch. On a substantial-form metaphysics the newborn *is* a rational animal now, and her rational soul is present, not merely potential. In the actual order, which is the only one she lives in, the call is given with her being. The Church's own wording is that it is the "root reason" for her dignity (GS 19), and it explains why her worth is *equal*: measured against one end that is God Himself, the genius and the infant are for the same thing.
*Rejoinder.* The Kantian can adopt the kind-based reading practically (Wood, Kain). It is no more of a stretch than yours. It simply declines to claim theoretical knowledge of souls. Your real advantage is narrower than the text implies: you can say *why* kind carries worth, where we can only require that it be treated so.
*Verdict.* **Held with repair.** The text should say Kant counted the newborn a person and should locate the strain precisely, in grounding the worth of kind (R2, R5).

**E4. Kant also explains the absolute at every number, and why it cannot be waived.**
*Attack.* The God row lists among the things "the best rivals leave out or must take as given" why the line "has no exceptions and is not even her own to cross." Kant explains both. Dignity "admits no equivalent," so it cannot be traded even for many. Kant holds the line at every number (*fiat iustitia, pereat mundus*, *Perpetual Peace* 8:378–379; verify). He forbids self-killing through the two-aspect doctrine (MM 6:422–423). W1(2) itself concedes the second point ("Kant did"). So against my school, that item of the claimed "reach" is gone.
*Defense.* The row says "many," not all. Kant's non-waivability rests on a two-aspect doctrine (homo noumenon binding homo phaenomenon) that many of his modern heirs drop (Hill), so it is not secure for the school. And the trust account gives a reason that is not just the "untradable value" of a capacity. It says no number of others has any *right* to her life.
*Rejoinder.* Then the advantage is that you secure it for the whole school while ours splits. That is a fair point, but it is not the claim the row makes. The jurisdictional reason also brings its own risk. If what makes the line exceptionless is that her life is not ours *because it is God's*, the wrong becomes a trespass on the Trustor, which pulls against the text's insistence that the wrong is done "to *her*." Our ground lies wholly in her.
*Defense, second round.* The trust is set up *for her sake*: He "willed her for her own sake, gave her life to her keeping." So breaking the trust and wronging her are one act under two descriptions, exactly as W1 says ("His love refused in her," not a second injury). That is participated theonomy, not heteronomy (VS 41).
*Verdict.* **Held with repair.** The W1 frame survives the heteronomy charge. The God row overstates against Kant and needs a clause (R5). The W1(2) parenthetical should also credit Kant's "no equivalent" for the every-number line (R6).

**E5. The clemency analogy turned around.**
*Attack.* The text says "God does what Kant's ruler may not, forgiving the sin whole." By the text's own account, though, every wrong is "also, and before all else, an offense against Him." Kant's ruler *may* remit crimes against himself and may *not* remit what one subject did to another (MM 6:337). So either God's forgiveness covers the wrong done to her, in which case He takes her place, which the text denies (W1(1), A3); or it covers the offense against Himself, in which case He does what Kant's ruler *may* do. The sentence contradicts the text's own structure.
*Defense.* "The sin" in Catholic usage is primarily the offense against God (CCC 1440). What remains toward her, repair and her pardon, is untouched (CCC 1459).
*Rejoinder.* Agreed. Then say so: God keeps *both* halves of Kant's rule. That is stronger than the current wording.
*Verdict.* **Held with repair** (R4). The repair improves the text.

**E6. The dead, forgiveness, and "reason cannot forgive."**
*Attack.* "Reason can condemn us but cannot forgive us" is misleading. Reason never forgives; persons do, and Kantians have an account of that. Kant made forgivingness a duty of virtue (MM 6:460–461). He held that the dead can still be wronged, through their reputation (MM 6:295–296). He even held that the debt of sin is "the most personal" debt, which no one else can take over (*Religion* 6:72, paraphrase; verify). As for the dead victim, the wrongdoer's task is a change of heart, repair to the living, and honor for her memory. The wish for her pardon is legitimate, but inferring from it that she can still give it is exactly what A7 forbids.
*Defense.* The text does not infer from the wish. It calls W1(1) "a clue" that weighs "as much as the reader finds the third road … truer than the second," and it agrees that guilt is owed to *her*. The difference is that on the account without God, what guilt asks for (her pardon) is impossible forever, and the theist's hope is the only account on which guilt's request *fits* what it asks. That is a cost, not a contradiction. Kant himself, finding moral reason's demands outrun nature, postulated God and immortality.
*Rejoinder.* Kant's postulates serve the highest good, not reconciliation with victims, and he forbade turning them into theoretical evidence. Still, the text's modest weighting is fair. We concede the cost: some guilt is forever unanswerable by the one it is owed to.
*Verdict.* **Held at parity.** The "reason cannot forgive" line needs a minor repair (R3).

**E7. Certainty, A7, and the critique of physico-theology.**
*Attack.* The text calls its witnesses clues that "weigh, not settle," and says longing is "not evidence." Yet it says arguments of this kind can bring certainty. Kant's test for conviction (*Überzeugung*) is that the holding-true is valid for every rational being (*Critique of Pure Reason* A820/B848; verify). A case that "an honest reader may weigh … differently" yields at most belief, not certainty. W2's inference from fit to "one Source" also repeats physico-theology, which on Kant's analysis reaches at most an architect and borrows its certainty from the ontological proof (A620–630/B648–658).
*Defense.* The text does not claim *this* case compels anyone. It reports the Church's teaching about what reason *can* reach (*Dei Filius*), and it offers its case "for weighing." Cumulative-case certainty (Newman's convergence) is a respected epistemology, and A7 forbids believing *against* evidence, not believing on convergent evidence. W2 is not a design argument from nature's order. It argues from the reach of moral and logical knowledge. Kant's restriction of theoretical reason is itself a contested doctrine, and the Kantian cannot impose it as a premise more plausible than the Thomist's.
*Rejoinder.* True. The young Kant himself argued that all possibility presupposes a necessary being (*The Only Possible Argument*, 1763), and only the critical Kant withdrew it. This is the deepest standoff between us, not a defect in the text.
*Verdict.* **Held at parity** (a standoff).

**E8. Evil: privation, theodicy, and Job.**
*Attack.* Kant argued that every philosophical theodicy fails ("On the Miscarriage of All Philosophical Trials in Theodicy," 1791). He also treated vice as a real opposing ground, not a mere lack (*Negative Magnitudes*, 2:182; verify), and radical evil as a positive maxim (*Religion* 6:29–32).
*Defense.* The text offers no doctrinal theodicy for natural evil: "a setting, not an explanation"; "this book does not pretend otherwise." That is close to Kant's "authentic theodicy," Job's sincerity. Kant's radical evil is the subordination of the law to self-love, which is a preference for a lesser good over the higher one, and that is Chapter 1's account of sin. Kant also denies that humans can have a diabolical will (*Religion* 6:35), which matches "there can be … no supreme Evil."
*Verdict.* **Held.**

## 4. Ledger

- **A heading.** Holds. Its standard for trust (what survives honest reflection, including reflection on how we came to believe it) parallels Kant's "fact of reason." Its list of who denies what is fair. Kant denies none of A1–A4 or A7.
- **A1.** Holds. Kant is its strongest secular champion, since the law binds every rational being regardless of approval.
- **A2.** Holds. Wronging is not worsening. The betrayed secret is a perfect Kantian case, because what makes it wrong is not anything she experiences.
- **A3.** Holds. Kant rules out a contract of self-enslavement as void (MM 6:330; verify) and says the debt of sin cannot be transferred (*Religion* 6:72).
- **A4.** Holds. Kant agrees, including that the newborn is a person and that the criminal keeps his dignity. He disagrees about the death penalty, which A4 does not depend on.
- **A5.** Holds with a Kantian gloss. The is/ought point is shared. The ordering "goods first, then the law" is reversed for us (CPrR 5:62–64), but A5 defers the teleology to W, so nothing is lost.
- **A6.** Holds. Kant holds the line at every number and against consent. He raised the very case A6 settles, Curtius and deliberate martyrdom (MM 6:423), and left it open as a casuistical question.
- **A7.** Holds. It is the most Kantian item in the text (MM 6:429–430).
- **W1 frame.** Holds with repair. Anscombe is reported accurately. The anti-voluntarism matches G 4:443. The Kantian belongs in the list of rivals who say guilt is owed to the one wronged (R7).
- **W1(1).** Holds as a clue, at parity. The Kantian bears the cost of the dead victim's lost pardon. Kant's own rights of the dead and his postulate of immortality are not mentioned (see §7).
- **W1(2).** Holds with repair. The Kant parenthetical is accurate but should credit "no equivalent" and Kant's counting the newborn a person (R6). "A law … does not want any one of them" does not hit Kant, whose dignity needs no one's wanting. The theist's real gain over Kant is grounding the worth of *kind* and holding the line for the whole school.
- **W1 closing.** Holds. "A will that invents values can command, but it cannot bind" is Kant's own objection to theological voluntarism, and VS 41's participated theonomy is the right answer to the charge of heteronomy. "Whatever voice he says he heard" echoes Kant on Abraham (*Conflict of the Faculties* 7:63n).
- **W2.** Holds with repair. Its claim that the core of the law holds for any rational mind, not only our kind (Darwin's hive bees), is Kant's thesis. But its list of audiences omits constructivists, for whom law and mind are already one (R8). Its conclusion is a fair "weighs, does not settle." Its "one can always refuse to do evil" matches Kant's denial that duties ever collide (MM 6:224).
- **W3.** Holds. It disclaims evidential force and states honestly where the account stops.
- **W3 (hiddenness).** Holds. It is honest about cost. Kant offers a supporting reason the text does not use (§7).
- **C introduction.** Holds. The fairness pledge is kept on genealogy (Nietzsche row: "neither ancestry settles anything").
- **C: Nothing binds.** Holds. It is fair, and Kant would press A7 the same way.
- **C: Feeling, refined.** Holds. Hume's "sensible knave" and the remark that the weak are owed only gentleness under the laws of humanity are reported accurately. Kant agrees about where this view breaks.
- **C: Reason's own law (Kant).** Holds with repair. Religion 6:98–99, MM 6:227 and the clemency rule (6:337) are all cited accurately. Four minor faults: the "unmake" straw man, "where does it hold," the newborn stretch stated without Kant's own ruling, and the clemency sentence inverting the text's own structure (R1–R4).
- **C: What we owe each other.** Holds. It is fair to Scanlon. Its "takes as given" applies here more than it does to Kant.
- **C: Well-being, counted impartially.** Holds. The Sidgwick point would be stronger with Kant's publicity test (§7).
- **C: Flourishing of our kind.** Holds. It is fair, as far as a Kantian can judge.
- **C: Necessary truths that belong to no one.** Holds. It honestly concedes that every account has a first principle.
- **C: Impersonal sacred order.** Holds. The Kantian is not competent to fault the Chinese sources, and the internal logic ("gives life to each thing and intends none") is sound.
- **C: The Buddha's Dhamma.** Holds. "Justice built into a world looks like the work of a mind" is, in structure, Kant's own moral argument, and the text honestly applies the converse against itself.
- **C: Power and rank.** Holds. The A7 dilemma is fair and is the same as O'Neill's point that reason vindicates itself.
- **C: A God who is Love.** Holds with repair. It claims theism alone explains the absolute and non-waivable line, but Kant explains both (R5). "Rest on a standing they take as given" does not describe Kant, who argues for it.
- **Evil, weight paragraph.** Holds. It is honest at the level of Kant's "authentic theodicy." The weighing of hell is candid.
- **Evil, "First" (privation).** Holds. Kant's alternative (real opposition) does not touch the narrow use made of it here (no diabolical will, *Religion* 6:35).
- **Evil, "Second" (permission, not a trade).** Holds. "Not seeing God's reasons is weak evidence" is about as far as Kant's theodicy essay itself allows: we can neither convict nor acquit.
- **Evil, "Third" (God suffers with).** Holds. It is offered as what the God being weighed is, not as proof, which is correct.
- **Conclusion.** Holds. The newborn challenge does not discriminate against Kant (we agree she was wronged no less, because of what she is), and the text does not claim it does. Its "certainty" framing is a doctrinal report, a standoff and not a defect (E7).

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**As written: NONE.**

No Fatal or Serious objection stands. The text represents Kant accurately in substance, claims only "clue" or "weighs, does not settle" force where the two of us truly stand off, and absorbs Kant's anti-voluntarism and his Abraham point.

**Minor items:**
1. **The God row overstates against Kant** (E4). The claim that the best rivals "leave out or must take as given" why the line has no exceptions and cannot be waived is false of Kant, and W1(2) concedes as much. This is the most important repair. It comes close to Serious because it is a comparative claim about a principal rival, but the text's own concession elsewhere and its hedge ("many") keep it Minor.
2. **The clemency sentence** says God does what Kant's ruler "may not," which inverts the text's own structure (E5).
3. **"If reason made it, reason could unmake it"** is a straw man when attached to Kant (E2).
4. **"Where does a law … hold"** is posed in realist terms that a Kantian rejects (E1).
5. **The W2 parenthetical audience list** omits constructivists.
6. **The W1 frame's list** of rivals who say guilt is owed to the victim omits the Kantian.

**Misstatements of my school:**
- "Dignity grounded in the capacity to reason must stretch to reach the newborn": Kant himself counted her a person from birth (MM 6:280). The stretch is a charge against the *grounding*, not a fact about Kant's verdict, and it applies more to Korsgaard's regress argument than to Wood or Kain.
- "Reason can condemn us but cannot forgive us": this omits Kant's duty of forgivingness and his rights of the dead.
- "Kant held that no one … is the author": this is true of authorship by choice (MM 6:227), but the rational will must regard itself as the law's author (G 4:431).

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**
- Whether necessary moral and logical truths need a Mind to hold in. This is Kant's critique of theoretical theology against *Dei Filius*.
- How free rational agents arose in nature. Kant calls it inexplicable; the theist names a cause he cannot explain either.
- Whether guilt toward the dead points beyond the grave. We bear the cost; the text calls its answer a clue.
- Whether certainty can come from converging, individually weak signs (Newman against Kant's test of conviction).
- Whether worth's attachment to *kind* needs a Creator's call. The text has a real but narrow edge here, since we can only hold it practically.

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1 (C, Kant row).**
Old: "Kant held that no one, not even God, is the author of the moral law. If reason made it, reason could unmake it. If it is the very form of a free will, as Kant's best readers say, it still holds for every possible reasoner before any exists: no reasoner set it, and each finds himself under it. Then the question of W2 returns: where does a law for every possible mind hold, and how did minds that live by it arise in a world of nature?"
New: "Kant held that no one, not even God, is the author of the moral law in the sense of choosing it; each rational will must regard itself as giving the law to itself, because the law is the very form of a free will (*Groundwork* 4:431). It holds for every possible reasoner: no reasoner set it by choice, and each finds it his own. Then the question of W2 returns in Kant's form: not where such a law is kept, but why there are beings at all whose own law it is, and how minds that live by it arose in a world of nature?"

**R2 (C, Kant row).**
Old: "Dignity grounded in the capacity to reason must stretch to reach the newborn and the dying."
New: "Kant counted the newborn a person from birth (*Metaphysics of Morals* 6:280), and his best readers ground her dignity in the rational nature she has by kind, not by use; but then they must say why belonging to a rational kind carries that worth before reason is ever used, and Kant, who denied that we can know what the soul is, can hold it only as a demand of practical reason."

**R3 (C, Kant row).**
Old: "And reason can condemn us but cannot forgive us."
New: "And reason can condemn us but cannot forgive us. Kant made forgiving a duty between persons (*Metaphysics of Morals* 6:460–461), and held that the dead can still be wronged (6:295–296); but when the one we wronged is gone, the account without God has no one left who can forgive."

**R4 (C, Kant row).**
Old: "God does what Kant's ruler may not, forgiving the sin whole (CCC 1441), yet not as impunity: the one forgiven must still repair what he can and make satisfaction for his sins (CCC 1459), and God does not take her place."
New: "God keeps both halves of that rule. The sin, which is first an offense against Himself, He forgives whole (CCC 1440–1441), as Kant's ruler may remit a crime against himself; but not as impunity: the one forgiven must still repair what he can and make satisfaction for his sins (CCC 1459), and what was done to her He does not pardon in her place."

**R5 (C, God who is Love).**
Old: "a ground for the rule that one person may not be spent for others' totals, which many of the best rivals share and rest on a standing they take as given; and, as the Church teaches and this book argues, though not every reader grants it, why that line has no exceptions and is not even her own to cross;"
New: "a ground for the rule that one person may not be spent for others' totals, which many of the best rivals share and rest on a standing they take as given, or, with Kant, on a rational nature that must be stretched to reach those who cannot yet or can no longer reason; and, as the Church teaches and this book argues, though not every reader grants it, why that line has no exceptions and is not even her own to cross, which Kant too explains, though many of his heirs let it go;"

**R6 (W1(2)).**
Old: "(Kant did, grounding it in the humanity in her own person, which must stretch to reach the newborn, and, so that she cannot release herself, regarding her in two ways, her free reason binding her as a natural being)"
New: "(Kant did, grounding it in the humanity in her own person, a worth beyond price that admits no equivalent and so cannot be traded even for many, and, so that she cannot release herself, regarding her in two ways, her free reason binding her as a natural being; he counted the newborn a person, though his ground must stretch to reach her)"

**R7 (W1 frame).**
Old: "the realist, the contractualist, the Aristotelian and the Buddhist among them"
New: "the realist, the Kantian, the contractualist, the Aristotelian and the Buddhist among them"

**R8 (W2 parenthetical).**
Old: "Those who say our moral words voice refined feeling need no meeting of law and mind; for them the question is the one in C.)"
New: "Those who say our moral words voice refined feeling need no meeting of law and mind; for them the question is the one in C. Those who say, with Kant, that the law is the very form of a reasoning will need no meeting either, for in them law and mind are one; to them it puts the question why there are such wills at all in a world of nature (C).)"

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights:**
1. **Hiddenness and freedom (for W3, second paragraph).** Kant argued that if God and eternity stood unceasingly before our eyes, we would obey from fear and hope, and the moral worth of our actions would vanish. He saw the measure of hiddenness as fitted to our moral calling (CPrR 5:146–148, paraphrase; verify). This is a reason-level ally of Chapter 4's "a gift forced is no gift," from the philosopher of autonomy. It is a partial answer, not a full one.
2. **Kant's personal debt (A3, W1(1)).** Kant held that the debt of sin is the most personal of debts and cannot be passed to another like a money debt (*Religion* 6:72; verify). This supports "no one else … can give that pardon in her place" from a non-Catholic source.
3. **Kant on Abraham (W1(2), "whatever voice").** Kant said Abraham should have answered that it is certain he ought not kill his innocent son, and uncertain that the voice is God's (*Conflict of the Faculties* 7:63n, paraphrase; verify). The text's line already says this; citing Kant would show that the theist's absolute is not hostage to claimed commands.
4. **The question Kant left open (A6).** Kant listed Curtius's leap and deliberate martyrdom as unresolved questions (MM 6:423). The Church's distinction between laying down one's life and aiming at one's death answers exactly what Kant left open, which is a quiet point in the book's favor.
5. **Publicity (utilitarian row).** Kant's test is that a maxim affecting others' rights is wrong if it cannot survive being made public (*Perpetual Peace* 8:381; verify). It condemns Sidgwick's esoteric morality on grounds that do not depend on the text's own premises.
6. **The highest good (Dhamma row, evil section).** The same mismatch of virtue and happiness that the text counts against a just Mind was, for Kant, the premise of his moral argument: reason demands that the mismatch be righted, so it must hope for an Author who can right it. Use it carefully. Kant held it as practical faith, and most modern Kantians reject it, but it shows that the weight on the evil side is also a demand.
7. **No collision of duties.** Kant denied that duties can truly collide (MM 6:224), an independent confirmation of W2's "one can always refuse to do evil."

**Lines worth keeping:**
- "A will that invents values can command, but it cannot bind."
- "Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent."
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."
- "Our share in that law is reason's own light, not a foreign rule."
- "No one, whatever voice he says he heard in His name, may claim it for himself."
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it."

## 8. Score
