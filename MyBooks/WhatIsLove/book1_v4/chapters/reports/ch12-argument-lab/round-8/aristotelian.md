# Round 8 blind review: Secular Neo-Aristotelian / Virtue Ethics

## 1. School and voice

I speak for the secular heirs of Anscombe's 1958 proposal, the ones who took her advice without taking her God: Philippa Foot (*Natural Goodness*), Rosalind Hursthouse (*On Virtue Ethics*), Michael Thompson (*Life and Action*, and his paper on what it is to wrong someone), John McDowell ("Two Sorts of Naturalism," "Virtue and Reason"), and Martha Nussbaum (the capabilities approach; "Transcending Humanity"; *Anger and Forgiveness*). Our view is that "good" works the same way for oaks, wolves and people. A good human being, like a good root, is measured against the form of life of its kind. The virtues are to human life what deep roots are to an oak. Justice is the virtue that gives another what is owed to *him*, and that relation runs between two parties. The life form explains the norms, and Aristotle held that nature acts for an end without deliberating (*Physics* II.8), so no designer is needed behind the form. I am a non-theist, and I am the rival this text treats most generously. I owe it equal fairness in return.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** Justice is a virtue of the rational social animal. What anyone is owed comes from two things: what a human being needs to live a human life (Foot's "natural necessities"; Nussbaum's central capabilities), and the two-party relations of owing and wronging that belong to the human form itself (Thompson's "bipolar" normativity; paraphrase, verify). **Holds.** It does not borrow from the book. It ends in a stopping point: that our form has justice of *this* shape is a fact about what we are. The text names this stopping point accurately.

**2. A hard dilemma resolved.** The case is the conjoined twins of *Re A* (England, 2000). Separating them would save the stronger twin and kill the weaker one, who depends on her sister's heart. Without separation both die within months. Our resolution: justice gives the weaker twin no claim on her sister's body. The surgery returns to the stronger twin what is hers, and the weaker twin's death comes from her own inability to live alone, so separation is permissible. Charity and practical wisdom allow the parents to refuse, and a virtuous agent who does either acts with grief and is changed by it (Hursthouse on tragic remainder; verify). **Holds, with an honest limit.** Justice, charity and phronesis do this work as human virtues, not as covenant gifts. But the answer is "permissible either way, with remainder," and it never yields the clean verdict the Church's act-analysis tries for.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** Standing in justice follows membership in the human kind, not capacity. The newborn, the dying, the severely disabled and the enemy are bearers of the human form, some of them defective bearers, and a defect is a lack *in* a human, not a change of kind. Standing in justice is all or nothing. **Holds for the core.** It **strains** at three points:
- It is not clear that the human end, a life of reason well lived, is *for* the newborn who will never think. She belongs, but she can never reach it.
- Nussbaum says outright that certain conditions, such as permanent unconsciousness or anencephaly, are not a human life in the relevant sense (verify). For her case, this **breaks**.
- Her principle that "each person is an end," as against Aristotle's own politics, where the citizen is a part of the whole, is admittedly taken in part from Kant. That is a **borrowing**.

We **openly deny** that the line is not even hers to waive (Foot, "Euthanasia," 1977, verify). We do not all hold the line at every number.

**4. Ending point for flourishing.** What is ultimately good for us is *eudaimonia*: a complete human life of activity in accordance with the virtues, lived among friends in a just community. Its goods include knowledge, love, play, bodily health, practical reason and relation to other species and to nature (Nussbaum). Our guiding principles are the virtues, phronesis and a threshold of capabilities for all. This **holds** as a mortal, bounded ideal. Two strains show at the top and at the bottom:
- Aristotle himself, in *NE* X.7, says that contemplation is the most divine activity and that we should "make ourselves immortal" as far as we can (paraphrase, verify), and Nussbaum must argue against him.
- The ending point depends on capacity, so for those who can never reason it names an end they cannot have.

## 3. Debate highlights

**E1. A5's title (nature and ought).**
- *Attack.* A5 is offered as a premise a non-theist must grant: "What we are does not by itself settle what we ought to do." For us it is false. If "what we are" means our life form and not a list of capacities, our nature *does* settle what is good for us. Aquinas agrees: the order of the precepts follows the order of the natural inclinations (ST I-II q.94 a.2), the very article A5 cites. The text's own W1 closing also says God "established what they are for in the very natures He created."
- *Defense.* The body of A5 is about *lists*: "Listing what we can do, or what we want, never by itself tells us which to choose." Foot and Thompson grant that a statistical list is not a form. The last sentence only passes the question to W.
- *Rejoinder.* Then the title says more than the body does. It also contradicts both the Thomist and the Aristotelian sense of "nature" in a premise meant to be shared.
- **Held with repair** (title wording; see R1).

**E2. Does wronging point beyond the two parties?**
- *Attack.* Anscombe's point was that "morally ought," in the sense of law, needs a lawgiver. "Unjust," "wronged" and "owed to her" do not. Thompson shows that two-party wronging is its own logical form, and it belongs to the life of a rational social animal. So "Wronging points to Someone" is false as a heading.
- *Defense.* The text agrees, and says so: "it is owed to her, truly and in her own right, and not to God alone," and "the fact that no one can release us does not decide between the accounts. Nor does the fact that guilt is owed." W1 rests only on two further questions, the grave and the ground of her claim.
- *Rejoinder.* The argument is honest, but the heading claims what the frame paragraph gives up.
- **Held with repair** (heading; R2).

**E3. W1(1): guilt toward the dead, and the parity with grief.**
- *Attack.* Grief longs for the dead to come back, and its longing is fitting *because* the loss is real and permanent (Nussbaum, *Upheavals of Thought*, on grief's judgment of loss; verify). An emotion can be fitting even when its aim cannot be met. If guilt's asking her pardon counts as a clue that she is still someone, grief's longing counts as a clue that she will come back. Both have a likelihood near 1 on naturalism.
- *Defense.* The text already grants that "the speaking alone settles nothing" and calls the point "a clue," weighed by the reader. The Catholic is consistent: he does hope the dead return (CCC 998). The clue does not claim to explain a fact. It asks which road is truer to the debt.
- *Rejoinder.* The stated criterion is mis-built: "truer than the second to what he owes the one he wronged." What he owes is the *same* on both roads: to acknowledge, to repair, to seek pardon. The roads differ only in whether she is there to receive it, and the content of a debt does not decide whether the creditor exists. The C row "A God who is Love" then counts "forgiveness when no one on earth is left to give it" among what theism *explains*. A promised good is not an explained datum. The only datum in A is that guilt *asks*.
- **Held with repair** (R3, R4). These are Minor defects, because the paragraph's own hedging is honest.

**E4. W2: "accident or wisdom" and the reach of the law.**
- *Attack.* The choice between accident and wisdom is false. Aristotle's third option, a form whose ends are its own, needs no deliberating mind (*Physics* II.8, against Empedocles). "If our nature is only an accident, nothing in it had to aim at the good at all" confuses where a form came from with what it aims at. Once a living form exists, it aims at its own good, whatever its origin. The "reach" argument also slips. It starts with what "would be wrong for any creature that can reason and suffer" and moves to what "holds for any rational mind whatever." For a mind that cannot be hurt or betrayed there is no cruelty or betrayal to commit. What remains is a conditional necessity: if there are such minds, cruelty to them is wrong. Conditional truths of that kind need no dwelling.
- *Defense.* W2 does name our view ("mind and law belong to one nature, so that a rational animal simply reads its own form") and grants it is "right as far as it goes." Its question is reach. Darwin's hive bees show the real pressure: on Foot's species-relative account, a rational kind could find its good in what we call fratricide. Yet we judge that gratuitous cruelty would be wrong for any reasoning sufferer. A conditional necessity still has to be *known*. The text's parity with proof fairly asks the McDowellian why the space of reasons reaches modal truth at all. The text claims only that this "weighs, it does not settle."
- *Rejoinder.* I concede that the hive-bee pressure is real for Foot's version. McDowell's answer is that we judge from within the second nature we have, and that is enough. That answer is exactly what the text says to Hume: it can judge "only from within the heart we share." On first principles this is a standoff. The wording slips still need fixing.
- **Held with repair** (R5, R6).

**E5. W1(2): "A law or an order gives a nature to every member of a kind; it does not want any one of them."**
- *Attack.* This is a dilemma. Either her worth rests on *what she is*, and the text says so ("Her worth rests on what she is"), in which case our life form account already has the ground and "wanting" does no work. Or it rests on being *wanted*, in which case a will confers worth, and the text rejects that: "A will that invents values can command, but it cannot bind." The line about wanting sounds strong and decides nothing.
- *Defense.* The text takes the first horn, but with two additions the life form cannot supply.
  - God's wanting is not a separate grant. It is creative love *giving being*: "God's love gives it, by giving her being" (ST I q.20 a.2). So worth stays in the nature, and the being is a gift.
  - What theism adds is *direction*. A species form is about the kind, and for the bee the individual is for the hive. Aristotle's own *Politics* (I.2) makes the citizen a part for the whole. The human person is "willed for itself" (GS 24). And all are called to "one end that is God Himself," so that "the genius and the newborn are for the same thing." Your end, a life of reason well lived, is one the newborn who will never think can never reach.
- *Rejoinder.* Thompson would say that our form includes justice in its two-party shape, so the individual is not for the species. I concede, though, that this is a fact about our form that we take as given, and Nussbaum supplies "each person as end" partly from Kant. The point about one end for all is the text's best move against us. Our answer, that standing does not wait on the end, holds the line on equal standing. It cannot say the newborn is *for* the same thing as the genius.
- **Held.**

**E6. Non-waivability, and "He who gives life ... may call it home."**
- *Attack.* Foot argues that the right to life protects a life *for the sake of the one who lives it*. When that life has stopped being a good to him, he may waive the right, and charity may help him. The trust argument places the wrong in voluntary euthanasia against an owner, God, which is the move A6 faulted consequentialists for: a wrong that is not done to *him*. Also, being able to give life does not confer a right over it. Parents give life, and doctors restore it.
- *Defense.* The text marks these as "this book's claims, not shared ones," which is honest. On the Church's view the trust is *for her sake* (GS 24). Her life does not stop being a good of the person (CCC 2258, 2280). The wrong to her stays first: "what the trust forbids is that her death be the thing aimed at." The authority meant is the Creator's, not a matter of ability.
- *Rejoinder.* This is a fair standoff, flagged as such by the text. The sentence "He who gives life" should say "gives being," or parents qualify (R10).
- **Held** (standoff at parity).

**E7. Evil: parasites that live only in humans, and "A person it never gives away."**
- *Attack.* The text says: "if our nature is a wisdom and not an accident (W2), so is theirs." Some parasites cannot live without human hosts. *Plasmodium falciparum*, pinworm and head lice have life forms whose good *consists* in drawing on human bodies. If those forms are a wisdom, the Creator designed a good that is realized by consuming children. That is the animal pattern the text grants ("the lion lives by the slaying of other animals") applied to persons. Yet the text says: "A person it never gives away ... The parasite takes from the child; God does not give her to it." On the text's own W2, that sentence is false for this class of cases. The Lawgiver of W1(2), who "never spends her as a mere means," appears to do by design what His law forbids.
- *Defense.* The Catholic has two answers, and both are true doctrine.
  1. Body and person must be told apart. Her body is corruptible like any other, and God wills natural corruption only incidentally, in willing the order (ST I q.19 a.9). But she as a person is never made *for* another creature's life. She is governed for her own sake (SCG III.112), and her suffering is ordered to a good offered to her.
  2. In original justice man "would not have to suffer or die" (CCC 376). When that harmony was lost, "visible creation has become alien and hostile to man" (CCC 400; cf. 1008). The parasite's hold on her belongs, as far as she is concerned, to that loss and not to what she was made for.
- *Rejoinder.* The defense works and reaches parity. But neither half is in the text, and the sentence as written draws the line in the wrong place, at "loss given to another's life," which the obligate parasite gives.
- **Held with repair.** It is **Serious as written** (R8).

**E8. W3 and Nussbaum's "Transcending Humanity."**
- *Attack.* The text answers the "wise friends of nature" by saying the hope is not more years but "she will go on being herself, body and all, made whole." Nussbaum's argument is not about length. The human virtues and human love get their form from vulnerability and risk: courage needs danger, generosity needs scarcity, and loving a mortal is a kind of tenderness. A life that cannot be lost is not a longer human life. It is a different kind of life, and wanting it is wanting not to be the one who loves. The book's own definition binds love to *sacrifice*.
- *Defense.* "The risen Christ kept His wounds": what a finite love lived through is kept and healed, not erased. Grace does not destroy nature but perfects it (ST I q.1 a.8 ad 2). Self-gift without loss is the Trinitarian form of what sacrifice is on earth, so love keeps its form when it is no longer under threat.
- *Rejoinder.* Whether a perfected life is still the life *of this kind* is exactly where Aristotle (*NE* X.7) and Nussbaum disagree inside my own school. The text claims only fit ("A longing does not prove its answer").
- **Held** (standoff at parity).

## 4. Ledger

- **Form line:** **Holds.** It cites CCC 31, 35 and 36 accurately. It does not overclaim.
- **A heading** (the epistemic rule; the list of deniers): **Holds.** This is Aristotle's method of *endoxa* in other words, and we accept it. The list of deniers is fair, and our school's denials fall under "many who hold all the rest deny ..."
- **A1:** **Holds.** We grant it in full. Foot's later work withdrew her 1972 view that moral reasons are only hypothetical.
- **A2:** **Holds.** Thompson's two-party wronging is our own doctrine, and the secret and the letter make an excellent pair. On the claim "Shame, left to itself, says I am less," Williams and Aristotle's *aidos* would add that shame can face outward. The hedge "left to itself" covers it.
- **A3:** **Holds.** We grant that no consent makes enslavement or a child's torture right, and that pardon is hers alone to give.
- **A4:** **Holds.** We grant the shared core, and the book's claims are properly parked in the parenthesis. The history sentence is accurate (see section 7 for a sharper version).
- **A5:** **Holds with repair.** The title is false for the life form sense of "nature," which both Aristotle and Aquinas hold. The body is fine (R1).
- **A6:** **Holds.** The cases are Foot's own family of examples. The book's claims are flagged, and the "only totals" charge rightly targets consequentialists, not us. "Without it, no innocent is safe" is true only in principle (Minor, R9).
- **A7:** **Holds.** For us, truthfulness and the intellectual virtues are human goods that do not depend on what anyone happens to want.
- **W1 frame:** **Holds with repair.** It describes Anscombe accurately and concedes honestly that wronging is owed to her. The heading claims more than the frame delivers (R2).
- **W1(1), the three roads:** **Holds.** It describes the second road, the Confucian and Buddhist forms and Mozi fairly, and it concedes that "the speaking alone settles nothing."
- **W1(1), the Catholic answer:** **Holds with repair.** The doctrine is accurate (CCC 1441, 1459, 958; *Spe Salvi* 44, 47–48). The criterion of weight, "truer to what he owes," confuses the content of the debt with whether the creditor exists (R3).
- **W1(2):** **Holds.** The point about one end for all is its best line against us. The wording about "wanting" is rhetorically stronger than it is logically, but the text grounds worth in what she is, which makes it consistent. On "gives life," see R10.
- **W1 closing ("This Lawgiver is not a commander"):** **Holds.** It is anti-voluntarist, and it says that ends are established in natures, which we can endorse except for the Maker.
- **W2:** **Holds with repair.** The choice between accident and wisdom omits intrinsic teleology at the point where it matters (R5). It slips from "reason and suffer" to "any rational mind whatever" (R6). The pressure of the reach argument on Foot is real, and its outcome is honestly stated as "weighs, does not settle."
- **W3, the longing:** **Holds.** It concedes "not evidence." Its reply to Nussbaum ends in a standoff, not a defeat.
- **W3, hiddenness:** **Holds.** It is honest about the cost, and the doctrine is accurate (LG 16, GS 22, CCC 847–848, 1257, 1261).
- **C intro:** **Holds.** The rule of fairness about origins is stated and kept.
- **C, "Nothing really binds":** **Holds.**
- **C, "Feeling, refined":** **Holds.** Hume's sensible knave, Hume on creatures too weak to make their resentment felt, and Smith at III.2.33 are all represented accurately.
- **C, Kant:** **Holds.** *Religion* 6:98–99 and the right of clemency are accurate.
- **C, Contractualism:** **Holds.**
- **C, Utilitarianism:** **Holds.** The points on Sidgwick's esotericism, de Lazari-Radek and Singer, and Sidgwick's resignation are accurate.
- **C, "The flourishing of our kind":** **Holds with repair.** It states McDowell's answer as the only answer of our best defenders and leaves out Foot and Thompson's intrinsic-form answer. It describes Foot's case for euthanasia as ownership when it rests on benefit. It is too generous on "why the weak still belong" and leaves out its own sharpest true point, the one end (R7).
- **C, Godless realism:** **Holds.**
- **C, Impersonal sacred order:** **Holds.** The *Analects* references, Sima Qian and Mou Zongsan are fairly used. This lies outside my school's expertise.
- **C, the Buddha's Dhamma:** **Holds.** The citations are accurate as far as I can judge, and the parting is honestly framed.
- **C, Nietzsche:** **Holds.** The dilemma is fair, and the text concedes that it "does not settle."
- **C, "A God who is Love":** **Holds with repair.** It says theism "explains ... forgiveness when no one on earth is left to give it," but a promised good is not an explained datum (R4). "Against the flourishing view ... which also stop at one" is fair.
- **Evil, paragraph 1 (the weight, hell, the first sin, why not heaven first, the setting):** **Holds.** It is unusually candid. "If our nature is a wisdom ... so is theirs, and that too is on this side of the scale" is exactly right.
- **Evil, First (privation; no supreme Evil; a "yes said to no one"):** **Holds.** Natural goodness also treats defect as a lack relative to the form, so we can grant the privation account.
- **Evil, Second (permission is not a trade):** **Holds with repair.** "A person it never gives away ... God does not give her to it" fails for parasites that live only in humans, on the text's own W2 (R8). This is Serious as written.
- **Evil, Third (God suffers with; hope):** **Holds.** It says "That does not explain why," and gives a fair nod to Buddhist and Confucian hope.
- **Conclusion:** **Holds.** It is presented as the book's judgment, with no presumption of fault. The final test with the exposed newborn is one *we pass*: she was wronged no less, because her standing follows her kind. So the test rightly targets only the reader who gives up A4.

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**Serious (fully repairable)**
1. **Evil, Second: "A person it never gives away ... The parasite takes from the child; God does not give her to it."** The text holds that animal forms are a wisdom ("so is theirs"). Some parasites cannot live without human hosts. So the designed order does give human bodily loss to another creature's life, which is the exact pattern the text grants for lions and denies for persons. As written, the sentence is false on the text's own premise, and it leaves the Lawgiver seeming to do by design what W1(2) says He never does. The text has a reply at parity (the distinction between body and person under SCG III.112, plus CCC 376/400 on the loss of original harmony), but the reply is not in the text. Repair: R8.

**Minor**
- A5's title denies what Aristotle and Aquinas both hold about nature (R1).
- W1's heading claims more than its frame paragraph concedes (R2).
- W1(1)'s criterion of weight confuses what is owed with whether the creditor exists (R3). Grief is the parity case.
- The C row for theism counts forgiveness from the dead as something explained (R4).
- W2's choice between accident and wisdom (R5), and its slip from "reason and suffer" to "any rational mind whatever" (R6).
- A6: "no innocent is safe" should read "in principle" (R9).
- W1(2): "He who gives life" would also cover parents (R10).

**Misstatements of my school**
- C row: our best defenders' answer is given only in McDowell's quietist form. Foot and Thompson hold that a living thing's good is set by its own form, and that evolution explains why the form exists, so no further order needs a mind. Aristotle's teleology without deliberation (*Physics* II.8) is the classic statement.
- C row: Foot did not argue from ownership. She argued that the right to life protects a good *for its possessor* and may be waived when it is no longer that, and that charity seeks his good ("Euthanasia," 1977, verify). The text labels "owned" as the Church's gloss ("the Church would say"), which keeps it from being a straight misstatement. It would be fairer to state her real ground (R7).
- C row: "Its modern heirs have much to say about forgiveness between persons" is true, but the most prominent of them, Nussbaum (*Anger and Forgiveness*, verify), distrusts forgiveness that waits on apology. The sentence should not suggest that we support W1(1)'s model of pardon.
- C row: "why the weak still belong" over-credits us. Foot and Hursthouse fit it. Nussbaum excludes some lives, and Aristotle himself would have had the deformed left unreared (*Politics* VII.16, verify).

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Whether a life form's norms need a Mind behind them: the reach in W2 against McDowell's second nature.
- Non-waivability and euthanasia (Foot against CCC 2277–2282). The text flags it.
- The line held at every number. The text flags it, and our school is divided.
- Whether a perfected, deathless life is still a human life (Nussbaum and *NE* X.7 against the resurrection).
- Guilt toward the dead: the second road against the third.
- Whether the shape of justice, in which the individual is not for the species, needs a Willer or is a brute feature of our form.

## 6. Proposed repairs (old → new)

**R1 (A5).**
- Old: "**A5. What we are does not by itself settle what we ought to do.**"
- New: "**A5. What we can do, and what we want, do not by themselves settle what we ought to do.**"
- Old: "Whether that means we are *for* something, and whether anyone made us for it, is what W must show."
- New: "Whether that means our nature carries its own ends, as Aristotle held, and whether Someone gave it those ends, is what W must weigh."

**R2 (W1 heading).**
- Old: "**W1. Wronging points to Someone.**"
- New: "**W1. Wronging, followed to the grave and to its root, points to Someone.**"

**R3 (W1(1)).**
- Old: "Like the longing in W3, this is a clue, and it weighs as much as the reader finds the third road, on which she is kept as herself, truer than the second to what he owes the one he wronged."
- New: "Like the longing in W3, this is a clue, not a proof. What he owes her is the same on the second road and the third; the roads part over whether she is there to receive it. So it weighs as much as the reader finds that his guilt, asking her pardon, reaches for something that can be real, and does not only ache, as grief aches, for what cannot be."

**R4 (C, "A God who is Love").**
- Old: "forgiveness when no one on earth is left to give it;"
- New: "why guilt asks to be forgiven by the one it wronged, and how that could be answered when no one on earth is left to give it;"

**R5 (W2).**
- Old: "if our nature is only an accident, nothing in it had to aim at the good at all."
- New: "if our nature is only an accident, it still aims at its own good, as every living kind does; but that good is only the good of the kind that happened to arise, and another kind could have found its good in what we call cruelty."

**R6 (W2).**
- Old: "If some of the law holds for any rational mind whatever,"
- New: "If some of the law holds for any mind that can reason and suffer, in any world where such minds could be,"
- Old: "it does not explain how we can see that cruelty would be wrong for any mind in any world."
- New: "it does not explain how we can see that cruelty would be wrong for any such mind in any world."

**R7 (C, "The flourishing of our kind").**
- Old: "But it must say whether our nature, and the good it carries, is an accident or an order it cannot explain; its best defenders answer that the question asks for a place to stand outside the life of reason, and there is none: for a rational animal, seeing what is good is part of being what it is. That is a stopping point."
- New: "But it must say whether our nature, and the good it carries, is an accident or an order it cannot explain. Its best defenders give two answers: that a living thing's good is set by its own form, which, as Aristotle held, acts for an end without deliberating, so that no mind is needed behind it; and that the question asks for a place to stand outside the life of reason, and there is none: for a rational animal, seeing what is good is part of being what it is. Each is a stopping point. And the end it measures us by is a life of reason well lived, which the newborn who will never think can never reach: it can say she belongs and is owed our care, not that she is for the same thing as you (W1(2))."
- Old: "And some of its best defenders allow that a person's life may stop being a good to him and be ended, as though, the Church would say, a person owned his life rather than holding it in trust (CCC 2280; this book's claim, A6)."
- New: "And some of its best defenders allow that when a person's life has stopped being a good to him, he may ask to have it ended and be helped, since justice guards a life for the sake of the one who lives it. The Church answers that his life does not stop being a good to him, and that he holds it in trust rather than owning it (CCC 2258, 2280; this book's claim, A6)."
- Optional: "Its modern heirs have much to say about forgiveness between persons." → "Its modern heirs have much to say about forgiveness between persons, some of it wary of forgiveness that waits on apology."

**R8 (Evil, Second).**
- Old: "A person it never gives away (*Summa contra Gentiles* III.112). The parasite takes from the child; God does not give her to it."
- New: "A person it never gives away (*Summa contra Gentiles* III.112): her body is as corruptible as any, but she is never made for another creature's life, and she is governed for her own sake. The parasite takes from the child; God does not give her to it. The Church teaches that in the harmony God first gave, man would not have had to suffer or die, and that when that harmony was lost, visible creation became alien and hostile to man (CCC 376, 400, 1008). Some creatures can live only on us; their hold on the child belongs, so far as she is concerned, to that loss, not to what she was made for."

**R9 (A6).**
- Old: "Without it, no innocent is safe from being spent."
- New: "Without it, no innocent is safe, in principle, from being spent."

**R10 (W1(2)).**
- Old: "He who gives life, and can give it back, may call it home;"
- New: "He who gives life its being, and can give it back, may call it home;"

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **The one end is the text's best weapon against my school.** It should go into the C row for the flourishing view (R7), not stay buried in W1(2). On our account the human end is something the profoundly disabled can never reach. We can hold equal *standing*, but not that she is *for* the same thing. That is a real difference, and an honest one.
- **Aristotle is an ally on "it is good that you exist."** In *NE* IX.4 the first mark of friendship is wishing the friend to exist and live for his own sake, and in IX.9 perceiving that a friend exists is itself choiceworthy (paraphrase, verify). Parental love (VIII.12) is the unearned form. This helps W3 by showing that the word Pieper names is ancient and human, and it sharpens the real question: can any finite lover say it *wholly* and *for good*?
- **Aristotle's *NE* X.7 is a crack in my own school that the text could use in W3.** The founder of the flourishing view ends his ethics by urging us not to think only human thoughts but to live, as far as we can, by what is divine in us (paraphrase, verify). Nussbaum must argue against him. The longing W3 names is found inside the Aristotelian tradition itself.
- **A4's history can be made exact without using origins against anyone.** Seneca wrote that slaves are men (Ep. 47), and in *De Ira* I.15 he speaks calmly of drowning weak and malformed newborns (verify). Aristotle held both natural slavery (*Politics* I.5) and a law against rearing the deformed (*Politics* VII.16) (verify). This is evidence for CCC 37–38, that reason can see dignity and still lose it, and it implicates my own founder, which makes it fair.
- **Thompson's paper on wronging (verify) supports A2's logic better than any source the text now uses.** It argues that "X wronged Y" is its own form of judgment, not reducible to "X did wrong." That strengthens the secret and letter pair, and it is precisely the ground the text concedes to us in the W1 frame.
- **Grief as the honest test of W1(1).** If guilt's request counts, grief's longing counts too, and the Catholic can welcome that, because he hopes for both (CCC 998). Saying so out loud turns a weakness into consistency.

## 8. Score

**8 / 10.** As written, the text represents my school more generously than any rival does, and every central disagreement ends in a stopping point that is honestly labeled. Its only real exposure to natural-goodness thinking is the one sentence about parasites in the evil section, and two sentences of doctrine repair it. With R1–R10 applied, I would put it at 8.5–9.

**Honest ceiling for any theistic case against my school: 9.** The decisive question is whether the norms of a life form need a Mind behind them, or are simply what it is to be a living thing of a kind. That question ends in rival first principles. Theism can show that it explains things we take as given (the one end for all whatever their capacity, the individual willed for herself, guilt toward the dead), and those are real and converging clues. It cannot force the McDowellian out of the standpoint of second nature, any more than he can force the theist out of his.
