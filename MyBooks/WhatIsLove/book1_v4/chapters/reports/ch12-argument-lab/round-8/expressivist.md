# Blind review, Round 8: the expressivist / quasi-realist school

## 1. School and voice

I speak for the sentimentalist line: Hume and Adam Smith; their emotivist ancestors Ayer and Stevenson; and its mature modern form, the quasi-realism of Simon Blackburn and the norm-expressivism of Allan Gibbard. The thesis is metaethical. Moral judgments express attitudes (sentiments, or acceptances of norms and plans) refined by information, consistency, sympathy and the impartial standpoint. They do not describe a moral realm beyond nature. Quasi-realism adds that, once the attitudes are disciplined, we earn the whole realist-sounding vocabulary in our own voice: "true," "fact," "wrong whatever anyone thinks," "she is owed." What we deny is that any of it needs or has a further metaphysical ground, whether a Mind, a Form or a non-natural property. I am a non-theist. I argue that the case gains nothing against my school from A, and that its real work against us is done by claims about persons (the dead, the longing) and by a contested demand for a "why" beyond first-order verdicts.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** Smith rather than Hume. What anyone is owed is what an impartial, well-informed and sympathetic spectator would resent being withheld from him. For Smith, justice is the negative virtue that rests on resentment of injury, the spectator entering into the resentment of the injured, including those who cannot feel it themselves (*TMS* II.ii.1; verify). Gibbard's version: what we are owed is fixed by the norms for guilt and resentment we can endorse from every standpoint we take up. **Holds**, with a repair to Hume: his justice, in its strict sense, follows the power to retaliate (EPM 3.1), and that is a cost of his theory of property, not of sentiment as such. It **borrows** impartiality and truthfulness, but both are norms we hold in our own voice, not loans from theism.

**2. A hard dilemma.** Take a competent, terminally ill patient in pain that palliative care cannot control, who asks his physician to end his life. Method: (i) get the facts right (prognosis, unmet palliative options, depression, family pressure); (ii) take the impartial spectator's standpoint toward each party, including the vulnerable people a permissive practice would expose; (iii) test the first-order attitudes against attitudes about attitudes (Blackburn's "staircase" of reflection) and against Hume's question of what a general rule does; (iv) settle on the norm one can endorse for every such case. Most of my school would permit it under strict safeguards; some would not, because of what the practice would do to the vulnerable. I do not use truth, justice, mercy and self-gift as the book defines them, that is, as grounded in God. I do use their natural cousins (veracity, justice, humanity, and benevolence in Hume's list). **Holds**: coherent, no borrowing from the book's theology, and this is a real first-order disagreement, which the text correctly fences as its own claim (A6).

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** We hold it as a standing commitment: an attitude of equal respect for every human being that we endorse at every level of reflection and refuse to make conditional on capacity. It is reinforced by what conditional-worth regimes have done (the sentimental education Rorty describes). Its first-order "ground" is simply that each is a human being, someone's child, a life that is hers. **Partly breaks.** Sympathy is graded by nearness, likeness and capacity. The impartial spectator corrects for distance, but not obviously for the newborn who will never think, and my school divides there. Against a dissenter like Singer, I can only press our verdict, and that verdict is not derived from anything he must grant. The text names this cost correctly in substance (see 3.4).

**4. Ultimate flourishing.** There is no single ultimate end. Hume's test is what is useful or agreeable to oneself or others. The goods are friendship and love, conversation, work worth doing, understanding, art, and a character one can survey without shame. Smith's version is to be loved and to be worthy of love (*TMS* III.2.1). The guiding principles are humanity, justice, fidelity and veracity, judged from the impartial standpoint. **Holds** on its own terms. Its cost is the one W3 names: the longing to be received whole, by someone who will not die, is real on our view and goes unanswered, and we call mortality part of a human life (Williams on the Makropulos case; Nussbaum). That is a standoff, not a contradiction.

## 3. Debate highlights

**3.1 Does A discriminate against my school?**
*Attack.* A quasi-realist asserts A1–A7 in full voice. "Wrong even if every culture approved" (A1) is a first-order claim we accept: it is not because we disapprove that cruelty is wrong, it is because of the cruelty (Blackburn, *Ruling Passions*; verify). A2 is Strawson's reactive attitudes plus Gibbard's norms for guilt. A5 is Hume's own is/ought gap. A7 is an epistemic norm we accept for every believer (Gibbard). So A cannot count against us.
*Defense.* The text agrees. The C row says my school "can say nearly everything in A in its own voice," and the deniers named in the A heading are Mackie, Nietzsche and Singer, not the expressivists. A is aimed at error theory, at power-and-rank, and at those who give up A4.
*Rejoinder.* None needed. One note: Ayer, the ancestor, did deny that any of A is true. The heading's list is illustrative and does not have to name him.
**Held.**

**3.2 The "why" beyond the verdict.**
*Attack.* The row says we part over "why that is true." But the text itself says the wrong is wrong "because of what it does to her" and "not wrong merely because it is forbidden" (W1). Then the wrong-making feature is the same for both of us, and the theistic "why" does no first-order work. Blackburn's Euthyphro point: a grounding our moral knowledge never consults is idle.
*Defense.* The theistic "why" is not offered as the wrong-making feature. It answers three other questions: that there is a "her" at all, made for an end (W1 closing); why justice has the shape that forbids spending her (W1(2)); and where truths that hold for every mind first hold (W2). Romans 2:14–15 already grants that the verdict is known without the ground. The text also never claims fewer first principles ("reach, not count," C final row), and it grants that "every account stops somewhere, God's included."
*Rejoinder.* Granted. But then, against my school, the moral argument hands its weight to two other questions: creation ("it is a gift exactly as far as her being is a gift," which the text itself states) and persons (the dead, the longing). My stopping point is a naturalistic account of valuing plus first-order verdicts. Yours is a necessary being whose nature is the Good. Neither side can show the other has stopped too early.
**Held** as an honest standoff at parity. The text frames it correctly. Dreier's "creeping minimalism" problem means the realist/expressivist difference has to sit in the explanation of moral thought, which is exactly where the row places it.

**3.3 "Equal worth wins only by being ours."**
*Attack.* This misstates us. Asked whether the newborn's worth depends on our hearts, the quasi-realist answers no, in first-order voice: she would have it if no heart granted it. The fairness rule cuts too. On the text's own account worth rests partly on her being "wanted" and "called" by God (W1(2): "every child was wanted by the One who did"). If I said "on theism, equal worth wins only by being His," the text would reject the jibe. And dialectically, the theist's equal worth also "wins" against Singer only by the theist's own judgment, since Singer rejects the God who grounds it.
*Defense.* The asymmetry is metaphysical, not dialectical. God's love is not one heart among hearts. It is creative and prior: it "infuses and creates goodness in things" by giving them being (ST I q.20 a.2), and His nature is the Good itself, so His willing cannot part from the truth (Ch. 3). On expressivism, the explanation of why we call worth real runs through human hearts and stops there. "Ours," in that sense, is accurate.
*Rejoinder.* On the metaphysical reading the claim is fair, and I accept that it names the real difference. But the sentence says "wins," which reads as a claim about deciding a dispute, and on that reading it is false as an asymmetry. Each of us judges from where he stands.
**Held with repair** (Repair R1).

**3.4 The newborn who will never think (the defense presses my school).**
*Attack (mine).* The cost the row assigns is not mine alone. Realists, contractualists and Aristotelians also hold equal worth without a theistic ground.
*Defense.* True, and the text presses each of them in its own row. But your school has the narrowest resources. Sympathy is graded. Hume ranks it by nearness and resemblance. The strongest sentimentalist-utilitarian heirs (Singer is the obvious case, and Gibbard leans consequentialist in *Reconciling Our Aims*, verify) let worth follow capacity. Your "commitment" to equal worth is held by some of you and refused by others, and nothing in your metaethics or psychology makes it more than a commitment. The text gives a reason: an equal call that does not wait on ability (GS 19, 24, 29; CCC 1700).
*Rejoinder.* That reason persuades only those who grant the call, so dialectically we are at parity. Metaphysically, you have a ground and I deny one is needed. But honestly, my school strains here. Our equal regard for the newborn who will never think is a reflective commitment, not a deliverance of sympathy. Sympathy, left to itself, does what A4 says instinct does: it favors the healthy and our own. The text's line "where instinct fails... we learn whether what we meant was worth or only attraction" lands on us.
**Held**: the text's charge stands in substance. Only its phrasing (3.3) needs repair.

**3.5 Hume's knave and Hume's weak creatures.**
*Attack.* Both costs belong to Hume's first-order theories (justice as a convention of mutual advantage; a prudential answer to the knave), not to sentimentalism or expressivism. Smith grounds justice in the spectator's resentment on behalf of the injured, and that reaches the infant who cannot resent. Gibbard and Blackburn keep neither Humean doctrine. And a modern quasi-realist will say, in his own voice, that the knave "is turning from a good that is there whether he knows it or not." Yet the text gives that sentence to its own side, as if we could not say it.
*Defense.* The text says "its founders were honest about the cost," which is accurate history: EPM 3.1 says what the text says, and in EPM's Conclusion Hume does concede that if the knave's heart does not rebel, no answer will satisfy him. "On Hume's account" is literally true.
*Rejoinder.* As history, yes. As a comparison, it lets Hume's narrower doctrines stand in for the school, and the knave sentence puts on the theist's side the one thing we can also say. What is left as a real difference is the last clause, "and from Someone." That clause is the honest parting, and it is stronger when it stands alone.
**Held with repair** (R2, R3).

**3.6 The debt to the dead (W1(1)).**
*Attack.* W1(1) reasons from the shape of an attitude (guilt asks the victim's pardon) to the existence of its object. Grief also "asks" for the beloved's return. Smith already described our sense that the dead are wronged as an imaginative sympathy: we lend them the resentment they would feel and the dread we would feel in their place. He called it an illusion of the imagination, and said nature uses it to uphold justice (*TMS* I.i.1.13, II.i.2.5; verify). Reactive attitudes were formed among the living (Strawson), and they outrun their objects at the grave. Moreover, an unpayable debt is not obviously a defect in a view. Tragedy and agent-regret (Williams) are part of what makes guilt serious, and a view on which every debt can at last be received may be less faithful to that.
*Defense.* The text concedes the mechanism ("science can show why minds like ours would speak to their dead whether or not anyone hears, so the speaking alone settles nothing; it cannot show that no one does"). It calls W1(1) a clue and limits its own weight ("it weighs as much as the reader finds the third road... truer"). It keeps the seriousness that Williams prizes, since grace "does not make wrong into right," and the wrongdoer does not sit beside his victim "as though nothing had happened" (*Spe Salvi* 44). It also concedes the second road is noble. And the grief parallel helps the Christian, who hopes for exactly that return (CCC 998).
*Rejoinder.* All fair. But one thing should be said aloud. The clue's weight runs through a verdict about "what he owes." For a realist, a moral truth can bear on who is still there. For my school, no verdict about what is owed can tell you what exists; trying to make it do so is the wishful-thinking problem Dorr pressed against expressivists, run in reverse. So against my school W1(1) weighs close to nothing by construction, and that is a metaethical standoff, not a refutation. The text already admits the parallel point about W2 in its parenthetical. It could admit this one too.
**Held** as a clue. Optional clarity repair R5.

**3.7 The Lord of life (W1(2)).**
*Attack.* Hume's essay "Of Suicide" is the classic sentimentalist reply to "life is held in trust; only He who gives it may call it home." If disposing of one's life encroaches on Providence, then so does preserving it by medicine, or turning a river from its course (paraphrase; verify).
*Defense.* The text already draws the needed line: life is given "to her keeping and not to her disposal," and "what the trust forbids is that her death be the thing aimed at." Medicine serves the purpose of the gift, while killing aims against it. A life is not a river: it is the person's own being, held in trust (ST II-II q.64 a.5; CCC 2280; "none of us lives to himself, and none of us dies to himself," Romans 14:7, RSV). And the text fences the no-exceptions and no-waiver claims as "this book's claims... not shared ones."
*Rejoinder.* The distinction is intelligible. It rests on the trust premise, which only theism supplies, and the text admits that.
**Held** (fenced).

**3.8 Reach (W2) and the longing (W3).**
*Attack.* "Wrong for any mind in any world" needs no faculty of seeing to explain. It is a universalizable attitude, our standard applied to every possible case (Hare; Gibbard's plans for every circumstance). Against W3: the longing to be affirmed before we have earned anything is what infant attachment produces. Smith's "desire to be lovely" covers the earned half, and attachment covers the unearned half.
*Defense.* The text concedes both points in advance. W2 says that those who hold that moral words voice refined feeling "need no meeting of law and mind; for them the question is the one in C." W3 names attachment, and says the longing "settles nothing," that it is "not evidence," and that it shows only "where that account stops."
*Rejoinder.* Agreed. The narrow claim about Smith is true of Smith's spectator taken alone.
**Held.**

## 4. Ledger

- **A heading.** Holds. "Truths, not feelings people happen to share" opposes crude subjectivism, which quasi-realists also reject. The debunking clause ("what produced the belief had nothing to do with its truth") is the standard my school accepts. The list of deniers is accurate and rightly leaves us off it.
- **A1.** Holds. We affirm it, counterfactual included, in first-order voice.
- **A2.** Holds. The two-letters case is excellent: it separates wronging from frustrating a wish, as resentment and indignation do (Strawson).
- **A3.** Holds. Consent cannot make enslavement right. Posthumous wrongs are affirmable (Feinberg, Pitcher).
- **A4.** Holds as a truth we affirm. It is the place where my school's resources are thinnest (3.4). The history is fair.
- **A5.** Holds. This is Hume's gap stated as a Thomist. "On reflection we find certain goods good" is something we can say.
- **A6.** Holds. The surgeon, riot and prisoner verdicts are shared by most of my school. The no-exceptions and no-consent claims are correctly fenced as the book's own.
- **A7.** Holds. Epistemic expressivists (Gibbard) accept it as a norm for every believer.
- **W1 frame.** Holds. The Anscombe paraphrase is accurate. "Wrong of necessity, not up to anyone" can be said by a quasi-realist (as moral necessity, with supervenience). It honestly concedes that release and owed-ness do not decide between the accounts.
- **W1(1).** Holds as a self-limited clue. Against my school it weighs close to nothing by construction (3.6). It does not overclaim.
- **W1(2).** Holds within scope. The anti-spending point stands. The "no order wants" point is aimed at impersonal grounds, not at us. The Lord-of-life claim is fenced (3.7). The Kant (two ways of regarding the person) and Foot (doing and allowing) paraphrases are fair.
- **W1 closing.** Holds. "A will that invents values can command, but it cannot bind" is aimed at voluntarism and Mackie's "inventing," not at sentiment, which does not invent.
- **W2.** Holds. Its parenthetical correctly exempts my school from the reach argument.
- **W3.** Holds. It concedes attachment, and its claim about Smith is narrow and true. The hiddenness paragraph is candid.
- **C: Nothing really binds.** Holds (outside my school). "Cannot say why anyone ought to care" is fair to error theory, though some error theorists would bite that bullet too.
- **C: Feeling, refined.** Holds with repair. The parting "in *why*" is correctly located. Repairs are needed for "wins only by being ours" (R1), for calling sensibility theory "its heirs" (R1), for the knave contrast (R2), and for letting Hume's justice stand for the school (R3). The grave sentence is a cost of naturalism, not of expressivism as such (R4).
- **C: Reason's own law.** Holds (outside my school). The *Religion* and clemency points are apt.
- **C: What we owe each other.** Holds (outside).
- **C: Well-being, counted impartially.** Holds (outside). The two-letters case does real work here.
- **C: Flourishing of our kind.** Holds (outside).
- **C: Godless realism.** Holds (outside).
- **C: Impersonal sacred order.** Holds (outside). Carefully stated.
- **C: The Buddha's Dhamma.** Holds (outside). Carefully stated.
- **C: Power and rank.** Holds. The ancestry symmetry obeys the fairness rule.
- **C: A God who is Love.** Holds. "Reach, not count" is the right claim, and the no-exceptions line is fenced ("though not every reader grants it").
- **Evil, first paragraph.** Holds. It is unusually honest: the pattern of animal pain, wolf and fawn, hell placed on the debit side, and the admission that two rivals bear this weight more easily.
- **Evil, second paragraph: First.** Holds. The privation metaphysics is contested, but it is used only against a "dark god," and it concedes that an indifferent source is answered only by the whole case.
- **Evil, second paragraph: Second.** Holds at parity. "Weak evidence... not no evidence" is a moderate, defensible form of skeptical theism. The text does not use my metaethics to discount the argument from evil, which would have been unfair.
- **Evil, second paragraph: Third.** Holds. It is offered as description, not proof.
- **Conclusion.** Holds. The newborn test is aimed at those who give up A4. My school passes it, and can say the last line ("not the source of her worth... your answer to it") in its own voice. That shows the text has read us correctly: the conclusion does not pretend that the test defeats us.

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**As written: NONE.**

Against my school, the case rests on an honest standoff about the "why" and on self-limited clues about persons. The text names the standoff and does not overclaim.

**Minor items**
- M1. "Equal worth wins only by being ours" (C, Feeling refined) can be read dialectically, and on that reading it breaks the parity the fairness rule requires (3.3). Fair on the metaphysical reading. Repair R1.
- M2. The knave contrast hands the theist a sentence today's quasi-realist also asserts ("a good that is there whether he knows it or not"). The real remaining difference is "and from Someone." Repair R2.
- M3. Hume's weak-creatures cost belongs to his theory of justice as a convention, not to sentimentalism. Smith's resentment-based justice reaches the infant. Repair R3.
- M4. "On the account that keeps only feeling... at the grave no one is left" is a cost of naturalism about persons. Expressivism is a metaethics and is logically compatible with survival. Repair R4.
- M5. W1(1) does not say that its weight against an expressivist reader is close to nothing by construction, as W2's parenthetical does for reach. Optional R5.

**Misstatements of my school**
- "Some of its heirs say worth and feeling arise together, like color and sight." Sensibility theory (McDowell, "Values and Secondary Qualities"; Wiggins, "A Sensible Subjectivism?") was developed against projectivism. McDowell is an explicit opponent, and Wiggins is at most a cousin. They are neighbors, not heirs (verify the attributions).
- "Wins only by being ours" (M1), if read as a quasi-realist's own first-order position.
- The knave contrast (M2), insofar as it implies the school cannot affirm a good independent of the knave's feeling.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Whether moral truth needs a ground beyond first-order verdicts. Both sides stop. The text says so.
- Whether guilt's request to the dead is a clue to survival. It turns on realism versus expressivism.
- Whether the longing for unearned affirmation points beyond attachment.
- No exceptions and no waiver for killing the innocent. Fenced as the book's claim. Hume's "Of Suicide" against the trust premise.
- Equal worth of the newborn who will never think. Both affirm it. The theist claims a ground; my school holds a commitment and partly strains (3.4).
- Evil: real weight, honestly placed.

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1 (C, Feeling refined).**
Old: "Some of its heirs say worth and feeling arise together, like color and sight; then worth is real, but it is worth to a human heart, and when refined hearts part, it can say which one sees truly only from within the heart we share. Two refined hearts can still part over whether the newborn who will never think is worth as much as you, and then equal worth wins only by being ours."
New: "Some near neighbors, who refuse the word 'projection,' say worth and feeling arise together, like color and sight; then worth is real, but it is worth to a human heart. Two refined hearts can still part over whether the newborn who will never think is worth as much as you. Its defenders will say, in their own voice, that the heart which holds she is sees truly, and would see truly if no heart held it. Every one of us judges from where he stands, so the difference is not who must judge. It is what the truth of that judgment comes to: on its account, nothing beyond the judgment and the hearts that reach it; on ours, a truth held first in the Mind that made her."

**R2 (C, Feeling refined).**
Old: "On Hume's account he has lost a feeling and the peace it brings, and if he never misses it, he will never know what he lost; on ours he is turning from a good that is there whether he knows it or not, and from Someone."
New: "On Hume's own answer he has lost a feeling and the peace it brings, and if he never misses it, he will never know what he lost. Today's quasi-realists will go further and say, in their own voice, that he is turning from a good that is there whether he knows it or not. We part at the last step: on ours he is turning also from Someone."

**R3 (C, Feeling refined).**
Old: "Hume granted that creatures too weak to make us feel their resentment would be owed gentleness under the laws of humanity, but not justice, which for him meant the rights of those who can hold us to account."
New: "Hume granted that creatures too weak to make us feel their resentment would be owed gentleness under the laws of humanity, but not justice, which for him meant the rights of those who can hold us to account. That cost belongs to his theory of justice more than to sentiment itself: Smith rested justice on the impartial spectator's resentment on behalf of the injured, which reaches the infant who cannot resent."

**R4 (C, Feeling refined).**
Old: "On the account that keeps only feeling, both judges are no one."
New: "On the account that keeps only feeling, and no one beyond nature, both judges are no one."

**R5 (W1(1), optional; after "...truer than the second to what he owes the one he wronged.").**
Insert: "(For a reader who holds that our word 'owed' voices refined feeling, it weighs little, since on his account no judgment about what is owed can tell him who is still there; for him the question is the one in C.)"

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- Smith himself describes W1(1)'s phenomenon. He says we sympathize even with the dead, lending them the resentment they would feel, and he judges this an illusion of the imagination that nature uses to guard justice (*TMS* I.i.1.13, II.i.2.5; verify). This is the sentimentalist founder standing exactly at the fork between the second and third roads. Citing him there would show the text meeting its strongest rival on his own ground: he saw the debt to the dead, and could call it only an illusion that serves the living.
- The sharpest one-sentence statement of the parting with my school: on first-order verdicts, the quasi-realist and the Catholic can speak the same sentences, even "her worth does not depend on us." They part over whether those sentences are said, in the end, in anyone's voice but ours. The text's Smith line ("both judges are no one") already says this. R1 makes the rest of the row consistent with it.
- Smith's impartial spectator is my school's best standard, and it is no one. The book can fairly say that the ideal judge every sentimentalist appeals to is a standard looking for a Person. It should say this as a clue, not a proof, which is how the text already treats Smith's appeal to the all-seeing Judge.
- Hume's "Of Suicide" is the classical sentimentalist challenge to the trust premise. The text's "keeping, not disposal" answers it in one sentence. Naming Hume there would make the answer visible.
- The text already avoids the two unfair moves available against my school: using expressivism to discount the argument from evil, and using a genealogy of secular sentiment. Keep it that way.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "Had the same stranger found it in a letter blown into the street, her wish that it stay hidden would be just as frustrated, and no one would have wronged her."
- "Where instinct fails, for the infant no one is drawn to hold, we learn whether what we meant was worth or only attraction."
- "No argument compels such a man, ours included; the difference is what he is missing."
- "Where it parts from us is in *why* that is true."
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it." (A quasi-realist can say this too, which is exactly why it is well placed: it does not claim a victory it has not won.)

## 8. Score

**8 / 10.** The text represents my school accurately in substance, concedes that A and W2 do not discriminate against us, places the real parting where Dreier's problem says it must be (the explanation of moral thought, and persons), and claims only clues. What keeps it from 9 is a few phrasings in one C row that let Hume's narrower doctrines, and a dialectical reading of "ours," stand in for the school.

**Honest ceiling** for any theistic case against expressivism/quasi-realism: about **8.5**. Once quasi-realism earns first-order objectivity, the dispute is over whether moral truth needs a ground beyond the verdicts, and no case can compel on that question. The best a theist can show is greater explanatory unity, plus clues from persons that weigh only for a realist. With repairs R1 to R4, this text reaches that ceiling.
