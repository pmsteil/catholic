# Blind review, Round 8: the Buddhist school

## 1. School and voice

I speak for the Buddhist school in its two strongest philosophical forms: Theravāda (the Pāli Canon, the Vinaya, Buddhaghosa's commentaries and *Visuddhimagga*) and Madhyamaka (Nāgārjuna, Candrakīrti, Śāntideva), read with modern scholars such as Peter Harvey, Damien Keown, Jay Garfield and Mark Siderits. My stance is non-theist. There is no Creator. Saṃsāra has no first beginning. The deed is the intention. Persons are real by convention and empty of any self that exists by its own nature. The final good is the ending of craving, nibbāna, or for the Mahāyāna, buddhahood for the sake of all beings. I accept almost all of A as true, but I explain it without a Keeper. My job is to find where the text describes my school wrongly and where its comparison overreaches against us. Where my school strains, I record that just as plainly.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**Q1. Starting point for justice.** Non-harm, grounded in a reflexive equality: every being fears the rod and holds life dear, so I put myself in the other's place and neither kill nor cause killing (Dhp 129–130; SN 55.7; Ud 5.1, verify). Moral weight sits in the intention (AN 6.63). Beyond non-harm, what people are owed comes from the duties of each relationship (DN 31) and from a ruler's duty to protect everyone and to give to the poor so that theft and violence do not arise (DN 26). Desert is carried by kamma, not by courts. **Verdict: holds** for non-harm and for duties. It **borrows** when it speaks in the modern language of "rights owed"; Keown's derivation of rights from the precepts is a modern construction (verify).

**Q2. A hard dilemma resolved.** Case: a dying man in great pain asks his doctor, who is a lay Buddhist, to end his life. My principles are four:
- the first precept;
- the Abhidhamma teaching that any intention to cut off a life is rooted in aversion;
- the Vinaya's third pārājika, under which a monk is defeated for killing a human, for supplying the means, or for praising death, including in the case stories where it was done "out of compassion" (verify);
- the value of a calm, wholesome mind at death.

Resolution: do not kill him and do not urge death on him. Relieve his pain. Stop treatment that is futile or too burdensome. Help him die mindful. This uses non-harm, compassion and wisdom, not the book's covenant-defined virtues. **Verdict: holds.** It **breaks into internal disagreement** on the many-versus-one case. Theravāda holds the line. Some Mahāyāna sources do not: the Upāyakauśalya Sūtra's captain kills one robber to save five hundred, and Asaṅga's *Bodhisattvabhūmi* has a similar case (verify). In both, the killer knowingly accepts the karmic cost.

**Q3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** We ground equal regard, not "dignity" as a property of a self, in four things:
- every being shares the fear of pain and the wish to be well (Dhp 129–130);
- across beginningless lives, every being has been one's mother (SN 15.14–19);
- a human birth is precious and rare;
- in the Mahāyāna, every being has buddha-nature.

Mettā is meant to break down the barriers between self, dear one, neutral person and enemy until all are held alike (Vism IX, "breaking the barriers," verify). It reaches the enemy even under the saw (MN 21).

**Verdict: holds** for equal and impartial regard, including the newborn, the dying and the enemy. **It strains on the severely disabled.** AN 8.29 counts a birth "dull and unable to understand" as an inopportune moment for the path, and MN 135 links ugliness, dullness and poverty to past deeds. Modern teachers rightly refuse to blame anyone for these, but the texts are there. **It breaks** where the commentaries grade how grave a killing is by the victim's virtue (and, among animals, by size) (Buddhaghosa, verify). The worst abuse of that grading is the *Mahāvaṃsa*'s consolation that slain enemies counted as barely human (verify). That is non-canonical and condemned today, but it is ours. In fairness, the grading measures the weight of the deed, on the idea that the virtuous are a "field of merit," not what the victim is worth. That is comparable to CCC 2268 treating parricide as especially grave. **We deny equal worth as a metaphysical property of a self (anattā). We affirm unconditional, impartial regard.**

**Q4. Ultimate flourishing.** Nibbāna: the end of greed, hatred and delusion, the unconditioned, peace. In the Mahāyāna it is buddhahood that does not rest in a private peace but works for every being. The guide is the Eightfold Path (virtue, concentration, wisdom) and the four boundless states of mind. Rebirth gives the infant who dies and the severely disabled person a future chance at the path. **Verdict: coherent and borrows nothing.** The cost, honestly: the person as a lasting "she" is not what is finally kept. What is kept is freedom.

## 3. Debate highlights

**E1. Karma as "justice built in."** (C, the Buddha's Dhamma row)
- *Attack.* The text infers that "justice built into a world looks like the work of a mind." This is the oldest inference we refuse. A world shaped by kamma is the work of minds: the doers' own, since the deed is the intention. The variety of the world is born of action (Abhidharmakośa IV.1, verify). The order of kamma is a natural regularity, like the order by which seeds give their own kind (the commentaries' five orders of nature, verify). One designing Mind would also make that Mind answerable for every evil (Bhūridatta Jātaka, verify).
- *Defense.* The text only says "looks like." It immediately turns the same measure against itself: a world where pain does not follow justice weighs against a just Mind. The Fifth Way (ST I q.2 a.3) argues that order toward an end needs intellect.
- *Rejoinder.* The hedge is honest. But the text never names our answer, that the mind at work is the doers' own, and the Fifth Way is not in the text.
- **Held with repair** (name the Buddhist answer).

**E2. "What remains is a ripening."** (W1(1); C, the Buddha's Dhamma row)
- *Attack.* This misdescribes us. What remains after her death is not only the doer's ripening. Her own stream of life also goes on, and conventional truth is truth: without the conventional, the ultimate cannot be taught (MMK XXIV.10).
  - The text itself relies on identity across lives when it praises karmic justice "across lives." If the later being is enough the same to deserve the fruit, as in the Milindapañha's mango thief (verify), she is enough the same to pardon.
  - Our tradition tells exactly such a story. Two women killed each other's children through many lives; the Buddha brought them face to face, showed them what had been done, and their enmity ended: "hatred is never appeased by hatred" (Dhammapada Commentary on v. 5, verify).
- *Defense.* "Neither the same nor another" is the Buddhist's own formula. At the ultimate level there is no "her," and guilt faces a her. The meeting depends on chance and on a Buddha appearing to bring it about. No one keeps her for it.
- *Rejoinder.* On identity the Catholic is no better placed. Between death and resurrection there is a separated soul, and Aquinas says the soul is not "I" (Super I Cor. 15, lect. 2, verify). The sameness of the risen body rests on a posit, just as ours does. What truly survives your defense is the Keeper: on our road the reconciliation is possible but not assured, and no one guarantees that she is kept. That is a real difference. The text's sentences claim more than that.
- **Failed as written; Held with repair.**

**E3. "Whether or not she ever pardons him."** (W1(1), last clause about the Buddhist)
- *Attack.* The text says that on our path, what frees the doer is his own purification, whether or not she ever pardons him. The same is true of the Church. God absolves whole (CCC 1441) whether or not she pardons, and absolution does not wait on her. By the fairness rule, the text cannot hold this feature against us.
  - We also have the structure the text praises in CCC 1459. King Ajātasattu confessed killing his father, a wrong against a man already dead. The Buddha accepted the confession and said the deed's fruit still stood (DN 2, verify).
  - Aṅgulimāla, after awakening, still bore stones and blows as the fruit of his killings (MN 86).
  - So we have a receiver of confession who does not take the victim's place, and a remainder that pardon does not cancel.
- *Defense.* On the Catholic account the One who forgives is the One whose love made her precious, so her claim is kept inside the forgiving. And the wrongdoer does not sit beside her "as though nothing had happened" (*Spe Salvi* 44).
- *Rejoinder.* Agreed: the real difference is an offended, loving One. That difference is real and the text states it elsewhere. This sentence draws the contrast in the wrong place.
- **Held with repair.**

**E4. "The Buddhist, who grounds regard in suffering."** (W1(2); C, God row)
- *Attack.* That describes a sentientist, not us. Theravāda ethics is not aggregative (Keown's reading as an ethics of virtue, verify). Its line against killing a human holds at every number. It also holds against the person's own request: the third pārājika arose from monks who were killed at their own wish, and it forbids praising death. Our reason is not a sum of suffering. The will to end a human life is always rooted in aversion, and it takes from her the rare birth in which she could be freed. A wish for death is itself craving for non-existence. The text says "few say why the line is not even hers to waive" and names only Kant. We are among the few. So the God row's claim that the best rivals "leave out" a ground for why the line "has no exceptions and is not even her own to cross" is false against Theravāda.
- *Defense.* (a) Dhp 129–130 *is* a suffering-based ground. (b) Śāntideva makes suffering ownerless and therefore removable wherever it is found (BCA VIII.101–103, verify), which opens the door to summing. (c) The Mahāyāna captain kills one to save five hundred. (d) The Buddha called Channa's suicide blameless (SN 35.87), so even the consent line has an exception for arahants. (e) Most important: your ground is about the doer's heart and her future chances, not a worth she was given, and your commentaries grade killings by the victim's virtue. You secure an absolute line but not *equal* worth.
- *Rejoinder.* (b) and (c) are Mahāyāna and not Theravāda; Christian moral history is divided in the same way. (d) concerns self-killing by one already freed, a case the Catholic does not face. I concede (e): that is the true remaining difference, and the text should rest its comparison there.
- **Held with repair.**

**E5. "When one river is drained, the moon has lost nothing."** (C, sacred order row; echoed in the Buddha's Dhamma row)
- *Attack.* By the text's own account, nothing harms God (Job 35:6–8), and on Thomist doctrine God has no real relation to creatures (ST I q.13 a.7). If the drained river shows that the moon is indifferent, the same image tells against your God.
- *Defense.* God's impassibility is fullness, not indifference. He knows each singular (ST I q.14 a.11), and His love causes the good in each one (ST I q.20 a.2). The image is about intending, not about loss.
- *Rejoinder.* Granted. But then the wording points at the wrong difference.
- **Held with repair** (Minor).

**E6. Madhyamaka on the God who "needs nothing outside Himself."** (C, God row)
- *Attack.* Nothing exists by its own nature. A being that did could not change, cause or relate (MMK XV, XX). Yet the text's God "wanted *you*," "lets Himself be asked," and loves us back.
- *Defense.* *Ipsum esse subsistens* is not one entity with its own nature among others. It is the act of being itself, so Nāgārjuna's target, the Abhidharma's self-standing elements, misses it. And the Buddhist stops somewhere too: emptiness is itself empty, and the Dhamma stands "whether Tathāgatas arise or not" (SN 12.20).
- *Rejoinder.* The doctrine of mixed relations protects aseity at the price of real reciprocity, which puts strain on W3's "love us back." The text, though, claims only "one mystery, and a mystery that explains."
- **Held. Standoff at parity.**

**E7. The longing to be received whole.** (W3)
- *Attack.* The longing "to be received whole, across every role and every year, by someone who will not die" is craving for continued existence, the very craving that turns the wheel.
- *Defense.* The text already concedes that grasping must die, says that longing is not evidence (A7), and claims only fit. Mettā itself wishes this being well; it does not wish her to cease.
- *Rejoinder.* Mettā wishes her *well*. It does not say it is good that she *exists*. The text states that parting accurately.
- **Held. Honest standoff.**

**E8. The weight of evil.** (evil section)
- *Attack.* A Creator answers for the fawn and the parasite (Bhūridatta Jātaka, verify). Our hells end; even Devadatta will become a paccekabuddha (Dhp commentary, verify). Yours is eternal.
- *Defense.* The text concedes all of this openly. It places hell on the other side of the scale, gives a setting rather than an explanation, and does not overclaim.
- *Rejoinder.* That concession is honest and accurate about us.
- **Held.**

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**S1. Serious (repairable).** W1(1) and the C row on the Buddha's Dhamma misdescribe what the Buddhist says remains after death, and so overstate the comparison.
- "What remains is a ripening" is false on our account. Her conventional person continues.
- The text accepts identity across lives enough for justice ("across lives") but denies it enough for pardon.
- It passes over the tradition's own stories of victim and wrongdoer reconciling across lives (Dhp-a on v. 5, verify).
- "Whether or not she ever pardons him" is equally true of Catholic absolution, so under the fairness rule it cannot count against us.

What does survive is real and sufficient for the book: there is no Keeper, the meeting is not assured, and the wish to be forgiven by *her, herself* is something we treat as clinging, while the Catholic treats it as truthful.

**S2. Serious (repairable).** W1(2) says "the Buddhist, who grounds regard in suffering" and then dismisses suffering-based grounds because "others can always suffer more." That casts Theravāda as aggregative, which it is not. Theravāda holds the line against killing at every number and against the victim's own request (third pārājika), with a reason that is not a count. So the God row's claim to give, where the best rivals do not, a ground for why the line "has no exceptions and is not even her own to cross" is false against Theravāda as written.

What does survive: our ground looks to the doer's heart and her chance of liberation, not to a worth she was given, and our commentaries grade the gravity of killing by the victim's virtue. The text can rest on that.

**Minor.**
- **M1.** "Guilt is owed" (W1 frame; the C Buddhist row). We speak of asking pardon and making amends (*khamāpana*, *paṭikamma*). Remorse that persists after amends is *kukkucca*, a hindrance to be let go.
- **M2.** "Justice built into a world looks like the work of a mind." Our answer, minds and not one Mind, is not named.
- **M3.** "The moon has lost nothing." By the text's own Job 35:6–8, the same is true of God; the real difference is knowing and willing *this one*.
- **M4.** W3: "what is left is boundless kindness …" leaves out wisdom. On our account the boundless states alone lead to a Brahmā heaven, not to liberation (MN 97, verify). What is left is wisdom joined with them.

**Misstatements of my school:** S1 ("what remains is a ripening"), S2 ("grounds regard in suffering"), M1 ("guilt is owed"). Everything else said of us is accurate and often generous.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects):**
- One personal Source, or dependent arising with no Source.
- Is the wish to be forgiven by her, herself, truthful, or is it clinging?
- "It is good that you exist," or "may you be well and free."
- Identity across death: a soul, or a continuum.
- Grace for the infant, or rebirth for the infant.
- Aseity, or emptiness.

On evil, my school bears the weight more easily, and the text says so.

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1 (W1(1)).**

Old:
> But on his own account the one born again is neither the same as she was nor another, will not, as a rule, know what was done, and is not, at the ground of things, a self at all; and on his path what finally frees the doer is his own purification, whether or not she ever pardons him.

New:
> His tradition even tells of a woman and the one who wronged her meeting again across lives, and of a Buddha who showed them what had been done so that the hatred between them could end (Dhammapada Commentary on v. 5; verify). But on his own account the one born again is neither the same as she was nor another, and is not, at the ground of things, a self at all; whether the two ever meet again depends on the currents of their deeds, and no one keeps her for the meeting; and the wish to be forgiven by her, herself, is on his path a form of the clinging the path dissolves.

**R2 (C, Buddha's Dhamma row).**

Old:
> When the one we wronged is gone, the Buddhist says she is born again; but the one born again will not, as a rule, know what was done, and on his own account is neither the same as she was nor another (W1). What remains is a ripening, and a ripening is owed to no one and cannot forgive.

> When the one we wronged is gone, the Buddhist says she is born again, neither the same as she was nor another, and she may one day meet the one who wronged her and let the old hatred go (W1). ## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**

- **DN 2 is the closest non-Christian parallel to CCC 1459.** The Buddha accepts the confession of a man who killed his father, a wrong against the dead, and says the fruit of the deed remains (verify). Paired with Aṅgulimāla (MN 86), this shows my school has a receiver of confession who does not take the victim's place. The book can then say exactly what the Catholic adds: not a receiver of confession, but a Keeper of the victim.
- **The Dhammapada Commentary's Kāḷī story (on v. 5) is the nearest analogue to *Spe Salvi* 44–47.** A Buddha shows victim and wrongdoer what was done so that hatred can end. Naming it makes W1(1) fairer and sharpens the contrast: on our road the meeting is possible but not assured; on yours it is promised.
- **The third pārājika is a witness that reason without revelation can reach the line against killing even at the person's own request.** That fits DI 1 ("recognizable by reason alone"). It means the book should claim that only theism gives the line its ground in *her* worth, not that only theism draws the line.
- **SN 15.14–19 ("every being has been your mother") is our strongest answer to the enemy case in A4,** and the book should know it is there. Our real strain is AN 8.29 together with MN 135 on disability, and the commentarial grading of a killing's gravity. The book should not press MN 135 uncharitably, since modern Buddhists reject blaming the disabled.
- **Against the Buddhist, the God row's advantage is not unity.** Dependent arising also unifies law, beings and minds. The advantage is personhood. The text already rests there ("for whom love is not the ground of things"), and should keep it there.

**Lines worth keeping**

- "Heaven and Earth give life to things; God wanted *you*."
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."
- "For the Buddhist, compassion is the seed and the fruit of the path; for the Catholic, love is also the root of the world."
- "Both say grasping must die, and they part over what is left, and over whether it is good that we exist at all."
- "The Buddhist never said that being born is good … so a world full of suffering is what he expects. We say that it is good that she exists, and so we must answer for the child."
- "The parasite takes from the child; God does not give her to it."
- "God says no to what was done to her, and yes to her."

## 8. Score

**7 / 10 against my school as written; 8 after repairs R1–R8.**

The text is unusually accurate and generous about the Dhamma, and it honestly concedes that we carry the weight of evil more lightly. Two Serious misdescriptions remain: what remains after death, and the ground of our absolute against killing. Both can be repaired without loss, because the true differences (a Keeper, and a worth given with being) survive the repair.

**Honest ceiling for any theistic case against my school: 8.** Reason can bring the Catholic case to parity and show what it explains that we decline to ask. It cannot compel us, because we read its key evidence, the wish to be kept and forgiven by *her* and the sense that "it is good that you exist," as the very craving our path ends. That disagreement is settled, if at all, in practice and not by argument.
