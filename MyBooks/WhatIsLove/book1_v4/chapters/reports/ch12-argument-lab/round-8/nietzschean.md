# Blind review, Round 8: the Nietzschean school

## 1. School and voice

I speak for Nietzsche as his best recent readers present him: Leiter's naturalist (no stance-independent moral facts; critique of "morality in the pejorative sense" because it harms the highest human types), Clark's mature Nietzsche (who no longer calls truths falsifications and accepts science and logic), Reginster's philosopher of affirmation (will to power is the will to overcome resistance, so suffering is a condition of value and not an objection to life), and Richardson's power ontology of the drives. My questions are not only "is it true?" but "what is the value of these values, and where did they come from?" I am bound by the same fairness rule as the text: any genealogy I use against it, I let it use against me.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** Justice begins among parties of roughly equal power as exchange and settlement: each thing has its price, and everything can be paid off (HH 92; GM II 8). Its highest form is the strong one's freedom from resentment (GM II 11), and at its peak it overcomes itself and calls itself mercy, the privilege of one strong enough to let a debtor go (GM II 10). What anyone is owed is what he can claim within a relation of rough equality; beyond that, nothing is *owed*, though much may be *given*. **Holds** as a coherent account and borrows nothing. **Breaks** only by the book's measure: like Hume, it owes the newborn and the severely disabled gentleness at most, never justice.

**2. A hard dilemma.** A young composer of real gift has a father dying slowly, with no one else; years of nursing would cost him the work only he can do. My principles: (a) rank, meaning what serves the highest in *him*; (b) against pity, meaning he asks whether staying would be overflowing gift (Z I, "On the Gift-Giving Virtue") or a self-diminishing pity that harms both (GS 338); (c) the sovereign individual, meaning that if he gave his word, he keeps it even against fate (GM II 2); (d) amor fati, meaning he chooses so that he could will the choice again forever. Verdict: if he promised, he stays, because a man who breaks his word is small. If he did not promise, he may go, provided he goes from his task and not from cowardice. **Borrows the forms, not the grounds.** Truthfulness (honesty with himself), fidelity and gift all appear, but as expressions of rank, not as anything owed. It **breaks** where it cannot tell the father he was wronged, and has nothing to say to a son who is simply base.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** I deny it. Men are not equal (Z II, "On the Tarantulas"); every enhancement of the type "man" has come through a pathos of distance and order of rank (BGE 257–258). The regard I do keep comes from the high soul's reverence for itself and its generosity downward. Late in life Nietzsche even called it the *duty* of the exceptional to treat the mediocre more tenderly than his peers (A 57, verify). **Holds** as an open denial. **Breaks** at the text's final test. Faced with the infant on the hillside, I feel what the reader feels, and on my own account I must call that feeling the pious residue in us (GS 344). That is a bullet, not an answer. A 57 also strains against BGE 260, which leaves the noble free to treat those below him as he pleases.

**4. Ending point for flourishing.** The enhancement of man in his highest specimens (UM II 9, verify): types who overcome themselves, create values, and affirm life so wholly that they would will its eternal return (GS 341; Z IV, "The Drunken Song"). My guiding principles are rank, self-overcoming (Reginster: power needs resistance, so great suffering is the discipline of every greatness, BGE 225), honesty, health, and affirmation. **Holds** as a perfectionism for the few. **Borrows** in one place: in practice it treats honesty as unconditional while denying that honesty has unconditional value, a tension Nietzsche named himself (GM III 24–27) and never resolved. It offers the many no ultimate good, and it does not pretend to.

## 3. Debate highlights

**Exchange 1. Guilt is a debt-institution (A2, W1(1)).**
*Attack.* GM II derives guilt (*Schuld*) from debt (*Schulden*): creditor and debtor first, then the tribe's debt to its founders, which grows with the tribe's power until the ancestors become gods (GM II 19–20). That process predicts exactly the datum W1(1) builds on, that guilt asks the dead for pardon and seeks a creditor beyond the grave. The datum is equally expected whether or not anyone hears, so it cannot be a witness.
*Defense.* The text already grants that "the speaking alone settles nothing" and weighs W1(1) only "as much as the reader finds the third road ... truer than the second". Its argument does not run from the *feeling* of guilt. It runs from the *normative* claim of A3, that we still owe her, together with "only someone can forgive". Your genealogy explains why we would feel the claim. It does not show the claim false (fairness rule: the text lets you trace its beliefs to Christian training, and you let it trace yours). And your genealogy confirms A2's phenomenology: guilt began as something owed *to a person*.
*Rejoinder.* Granted. Then W1(1) has force only for a reader who already holds A3, and none against me, since I deny A3. The text says as much in A's preamble.
**Held.** W1(1) never claimed force against a reader who denies A3. (Repair 6 makes the text name the genealogy itself.)

**Exchange 2. Resentment as the defeater of A4, under the text's own rule.**
*Attack.* A's preamble withdraws trust from a belief when "what produced the belief had nothing to do with its truth". GM I says equal worth was produced by the slave revolt: resentment, the priest's will to power, and the weak's need to call their weakness merit (GM I 7, 10, 13–14). Resentment is motivated reasoning, a paradigm of a process that is blind to truth. By the text's own rule, A4 loses its standing. The C row answers only with a tu quoque, "neither ancestry settles anything". I never said ancestry settles *truth*. I say it removes the belief's *evidential* force.
*Defense.* The tu quoque is not the text's only resource.
(i) Equal regard arose where, by your own account, there was no resentment. Nietzsche praised the Buddha as a physiologist who was free of resentment and taught against it (A 20; EH "Wise" 6, verify), and the Buddha taught a boundless heart toward every being. Seneca told his friend that slaves are men (Ep. 47), and A4 itself records that the ancient world "taught that slaves are men". A single resentment story cannot explain several independent origins.
(ii) W2: conscience "corrects itself, against interest and toward those who can never repay". Resentment does not extend worth to the infant who cannot repay, or to the enemy.
(iii) The historical premises of GM I are speculative psychology, and Leiter concedes the work is a hypothesis-laden history (verify). They are less plausible than A4 itself, so the objection fails the brief's test for soundness.
*Rejoinder.* "Love your enemies" is resentment's masterpiece: revenge deferred to the Last Judgment. Aquinas wrote that the blessed will see the punishments of the damned so that their bliss may please them more (GM I 15, citing ST Suppl. q.94 a.1).
*Defense.* The same question goes on to say that the blessed rejoice not in the pains as such, only in the order of divine justice (Suppl. q.94 a.3, verify). The text's own hope is healing, not revenge: the fire that "heals whoever is open to Him, the guilty too" (*Spe Salvi* 47). A4 holds that dignity is "not lost ... even by the worst of crimes". Nothing in the text uses hell as a satisfaction. It puts hell on the *other* side of the scale.
**Held with repair.** As written, the C row leans on the tu quoque, which misses my actual move (evidence, not truth). The text holds the better answers but does not deploy them where I attack (Repair 1).

**Exchange 3. The truthfulness dilemma (A7; C, "Power and rank").**
*Attack.* The dilemma's second horn is my position, not a reductio. Truthfulness binds those who hold it; I never claimed anyone universally "ought" to keep it. And the first horn does not follow: if truthfulness binds every reasoning mind, that need not mean "something binds beyond power". A constitutivist reader (Katsafanas, verify) grounds norms that bind every agent *in* the will to power as the constitutive aim of agency.
*Defense.* The text says outright that the dilemma "does not settle" the question. And you condemn: the priest "lies", and lying is contemptible (A 38, 44, paraphrase). If you hold honesty against those who do not share your taste, you treat it as binding beyond taste. A constitutivist ground for universal truthfulness is still a norm that binds every mind by what a mind is. That returns us to W2's question of where such a norm holds and how minds came to read it.
*Rejoinder.* My condemnation is contempt, a judgment of rank (good and bad), not blame (good and evil, GM I 10–11). As for constitutivism, the realist faces the same W2 question, and W2 admits that it "weighs, it does not settle".
**Held with repair.** This is a standoff at parity, and the text claims no more than that. The words "beyond power" should be replaced (Repair 2).

**Exchange 4. Agent-centered rank against patient-centered wronging (A2, the secret and the letter).**
*Attack.* The betrayed secret and the letter in the street differ only in the agent. In the first case a man broke his word and debased himself; in the second, no one did anything. The sovereign individual's ethic of the kept word (GM II 2) explains the asymmetry without saying that *she* was wronged. The case beats the utilitarian, not me.
*Defense.* Then whose pardon does the betrayer ask? On your account, no one's: he has fallen in rank and owes only himself. But the case's datum is that guilt faces *her* and that repair is owed to *her* and those she loved. Your account also makes the wrong depend on the betrayer's station: a man who has no rank to lose has done nothing.
*Rejoinder.* Correct, and I pay that openly.
**Held.** The text already lists A2 among my denials. The fair addition is that I can still despise the act (Repair 1).

**Exchange 5. The infant on the hillside (A4, Conclusion).**
*Attack.* "Which are you surer of?" measures psychological certainty, and two thousand years of training predict exactly that certainty (BGE 202; GS 344). The strength of a conviction is not evidence of its truth (A 54).
*Defense.* The preamble asks for what survives honest reflection *including* reflection on its origin, and the Conclusion offers the skeptical option ("or that you cannot know it"). You trust your own felt certainty that your genealogy is sound. The Moorean comparison is the same tool every philosopher uses at bedrock. Nietzsche himself could not keep the rank ethic free of obligation (A 57). Most of all, the text claims only that "it cannot compel the one who gives up A4".
*Rejoinder.* I feel what you feel and call it a residue. Neither of us can move the other here.
**Held.** This is an honest standoff at parity, and the text represents it as one.

**Exchange 6. Amor fati and "God says no to what was done to her, and yes to her" (evil section, First).**
*Attack.* CCC 324 says God would not permit an evil unless He drew a good from it. That is amor fati with a guarantor: your God also says yes to the order in which she was harmed.
*Defense.* Permitting is not willing (CCC 311), and the evil "never becomes good" (CCC 312). Amor fati wills the evil *again*, forever (GS 341). Christian hope wills its defeat (Revelation 21:4).
*Rejoinder.* That hope is the spirit of revenge. Zarathustra calls it the will's ill will against time and its "it was" (Z II, "On Redemption"). The healthy will says "thus I willed it" and does not ask another to undo the past.
*Defense.* The Christian hope does not undo "it was". W3: "The risen Christ kept His wounds ... what a finite love lived through is not erased but kept, and healed." The past is kept, not abolished. What differs is *who* completes its meaning: in Nietzsche, my will; in the Church, One who loves her.
**Held.** This is the strongest line the text has against my school, and it already contains it. The remaining difference (self-redemption against redemption by Another) is a standoff.

**Exchange 7. Destruction from overfullness (evil section, First).**
*Attack.* "A will that lacks nothing has nothing to grasp at": but destruction can come from overflowing energy, not lack (GS 370, the Dionysian).
*Defense.* The text concedes this in the next sentence: privation "does not by itself rule out ... a god beyond good and evil who makes and breaks out of sheer fullness", and it answers that only "by the whole case".
**Held.**

**Exchange 8. Original sin as the priest's trick (W1 closing).**
*Attack.* "The wound of sin" is the priest redirecting the sufferer's resentment inward: you yourself are to blame (GM III 15, 20).
*Defense.* Original sin "does not have the character of a personal fault in any of Adam's descendants" (CCC 405). The text never makes the suffering child guilty, and its evil section refuses to explain the child's cancer by anyone's sin, offering "a setting, not an explanation".
**Held.**

**Exchange 9. The infinite creditor (W1 frame).**
*Attack.* Making every wrong "before all else" an offense against God turns God into an infinite creditor. The debt can never be paid, and the conscience tortures itself (GM II 21–22).
*Defense.* The text closes this off deliberately: "not a second injury beside hers, for nothing harms God (Job 35:6–8) ... He is offended by us ... only because we act against our own good" (SCG III.122). God loses nothing and is no creditor. The debt Nietzsche found unpayable is the one he himself describes the Christian God paying out of love (GM II 21). Scrupulosity is a disorder the Church treats, not the shape of her teaching.
**Held.**

**Exchange 10. The longing for eternity (W3).**
*Attack.* I share the longing ("all joy wants eternity", Z IV) and answer it without a Person: say yes to everything so wholly that you would will it again. W3 answers the Buddhist and the friends of nature, but never the most direct rival to Pieper's word.
*Defense.* That yes is said *by* me, not *to* me, and it cannot be "it is good that *you* exist" spoken first by another. It must also be said to what was done to her.
*Rejoinder.* The need to be told is weakness; the noble soul reveres itself (BGE 287).
**Held with repair.** This is a standoff about which longing is the healthy one, but W3 should name my answer (Repair 5).

## 4. Ledger

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**As written: NONE.**

No Fatal or Serious objection stands. Every place where my school strikes hardest (the genealogy of guilt and of equal worth, the truthfulness dilemma, amor fati, Dionysian fullness, the creditor-God, the priest's use of sin) either is already conceded where it should be or can be answered from materials already in the text. My denials of A1–A4 are named openly and weighed, not refuted, which is correct.

**Minor items.**
1. The C row's tu quoque ("neither ancestry settles anything") answers a claim I do not make. My genealogy targets A4's evidential force, not its truth. The text's better answers (independent origins, correction against interest) sit elsewhere and should be brought here.
2. "Something binds beyond power" overreaches. A constitutivist Nietzschean can ground universal norms *in* will to power as the aim of agency.
3. The evil section's appeal to free will (Chapter 1) does not reach me, and the text does not say so: I deny any doer behind the deed free to have done otherwise (GM I 13; TI "Four Great Errors" 7–8; BGE 21).
4. The evil section lists two rivals who bear the weight of suffering more easily, but omits Nietzsche, who does not merely expect suffering but prizes it (BGE 225).
5. W3 never names the boldest non-personal answer to the longing for eternity (eternal recurrence).

**Misstatements of my school (all small).**
- "He hoped that truthfulness would at last turn on Christianity itself": he held that it already had turned on Christian belief in God and must next turn on Christian morality (GM III 27; GS 357).
- The list of my denials omits free will and the good/evil framing of A5's first principle.
- "Why equal worth spread where Christianity spread (the Church agrees ... CCC 37–38)": CCC 37–38 concern how hard moral and religious truths are to know without revelation. They support "slow to be seen", not "spread with the Gospel". Cite *Dignitas Infinita*'s historical section for the latter (verify the paragraph).
- "Power and rank" is acceptable shorthand, though Leiter and Clark would say my standard is the flourishing of the highest types, of which power is the measure, not power as such.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects).**
- Moral realism against my anti-realism: the Moorean certainty of the hillside against the genealogy of that certainty.
- Whether epistemic norms bind categorically (A7) or only those who will truth.
- Truth against value. I ask what morality is *worth* for life; the text asks whether it is *true*. The book's "truth and life have one Source" is its answer, offered as faith, not proof, and the text says so.
- Self-redemption ("thus I willed it") against redemption by Another (the wounds kept and healed).
- Whether the longing to be told "it is good that you exist" is health or weakness.

## 6. Proposed repairs (exact old → new)

**Repair 1 (C, Power and rank: answer the actual genealogical move and steelman the rival).**
Old: "His own account gives the will to truth the same Christian ancestry he gives equal worth, so neither ancestry settles anything."
New: "His own account gives the will to truth the same Christian ancestry he gives equal worth, so neither ancestry settles anything. Nor does resentment explain regard for every human being: it grew too where he found no resentment at all, for he praised the Buddha as free of it, and the Stoics taught that slaves are men; and it reaches the infant and the enemy, who can repay no one (W2). Nor need he praise cruelty: he can despise the man who tortures a child as base. What he cannot say is that the child was wronged."

**Repair 2 (C, Power and rank: the dilemma's first horn).**
Old: "Either truthfulness binds every reasoning mind, and something binds beyond power (A7);"
New: "Either truthfulness binds every reasoning mind, and then something binds every mind by what a mind is, which is the question of W2 (A7);"

**Repair 3 (C, Power and rank: accuracy on GM III 27, and the free-will omission).**
Old: "He hoped that truthfulness would at last turn on Christianity itself;"
New: "He held that Christian truthfulness had already turned on Christian belief in God and must next turn on Christian morality;"
And at the end of the row, after "which is why he said we need art to bear the truth.", add: "He also denies the free will with which Chapter 1 answers the evil we do: for him there is no doer behind the deed who could have done otherwise, so that answer does not reach him, and Chapter 1, not this case, must meet him there."

**Repair 4 (Evil, paragraph 1: name the rival who bears the weight most easily).**
Old: "And two rivals bear this weight more easily than we do."
New: "And three rivals bear this weight more easily than we do."
Old: "so a world full of suffering is what he expects."
New: "so a world full of suffering is what he expects. Nietzsche bears it most easily of all: he did not merely expect great suffering but prized it as the discipline behind every human greatness, and asked us to will it all again. What he pays for that is the yes answered below."

**Repair 5 (W3: name the Nietzschean answer to the longing).**
Old: "Love does not say that for a while only."
New: "Love does not say that for a while only. Nietzsche heard that wish for always too, and gave the boldest answer without a Person: say yes to your life, and to everything, so wholly that you would will it all again forever. But that yes is said by me, not to me; and it must be said to what was done to her as well."

**Repair 6 (W1(1): name the historical genealogy alongside the scientific one).**
Old: "Science can show why minds like ours would speak to their dead whether or not anyone hears, so the speaking alone settles nothing; it cannot show that no one does."
New: "Science can show why minds like ours would speak to their dead whether or not anyone hears, and Nietzsche traced how a people's debt to its founders grew into a debt to its gods; so the speaking alone settles nothing. Neither can show that no one does."

**Repair 7 (C, Power and rank: citation).**
Old: "(the Church agrees that equal worth was slow to be seen and spread with the Gospel, CCC 37–38)"
New: "(the Church agrees that such truths are slow to be seen without revelation, CCC 37–38, and that the dignity of every person came to be seen more fully in the light of the Gospel, *Dignitas Infinita* [verify paragraph])"

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights.**
- **Rank in holiness, not in worth.** Leiter's Nietzsche attacks morality chiefly for levelling the highest types. A4's "Nor is it equal holiness: the saints are higher than I am in how well they love, never in what they are worth" concedes his truth (there is an order of rank) while denying his inference (rank in worth). The Council of Florence's teaching that the blessed see God more or less perfectly according to their merits (verify DS 1305) shows the Church has never been egalitarian about excellence. Chapter 12 or Chapter 13 could say this plainly.
- **The saint as the highest type.** Nietzsche granted that the most powerful men have bowed before the saint as the riddle of self-overcoming (BGE 51, paraphrase, verify). The book can claim the saint as the answer to his search for the higher type: self-overcoming that ends in self-gift, not in self-reverence.
- **Mercy as justice's crown.** Nietzsche saw that justice overcomes itself in mercy (GM II 10), but grounded mercy in a creditor rich enough to afford the loss. The book's mercy is that of One who loses nothing (Job 35:6–8) and pays anyway. Nietzsche himself described, with astonishment, a creditor who pays for his debtor out of love (GM II 21). This bridges Chapters 7–8.
- **Independent origins.** By Nietzsche's own account the Buddha was free of resentment, yet he taught universal kindness. That is the cleanest answer to the slave-revolt debunking of equal worth, and it uses the opponent's own judgment.
- **Wounds kept, not erased,** is the precise answer to Zarathustra's charge that redemption is revenge against "it was". Worth one sentence in the evil section linking back to W3.

**Lines worth keeping.**
- "A will that invents values can command, but it cannot bind."
- "A yes said to whatever happens is said to no one; it must say yes to what was done to her as well as to her, and so it cannot take her side. God says no to what was done to her, and yes to her."
- "The risen Christ kept His wounds: what a finite love lived through is not erased but kept, and healed."
- "Not a second injury beside hers, for nothing harms God ... but His love refused in her."
- "Equal worth is not equal duty ... Nor is it equal holiness."
- "His own account gives the will to truth the same Christian ancestry he gives equal worth" (keep, with Repair 1).
- "Heaven and Earth give life to things; God wanted *you*."

## 8. Score

**8/10.** The text names every denial my school makes, concedes the Dionysian possibility and the non-settling of the truthfulness dilemma, anticipates the creditor-God and "it was" critiques with its own best lines, and claims against me only what a Moorean standoff allows; what remains is a set of small precision and steelmanning gaps.

**Honest ceiling for any theistic case against my school: 9/10.** A consistent Nietzschean who accepts the second horn and calls his own revulsion at the hillside a residue cannot be compelled by any argument, and the text rightly says so. With the repairs above, this case would sit at that ceiling.
