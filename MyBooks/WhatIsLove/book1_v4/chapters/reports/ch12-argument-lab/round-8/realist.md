# Round 8 blind review: the non-naturalist secular moral realist

## 1. School and voice

I write for robust, non-naturalist moral realism without God: Parfit (moral truths are necessary and need no ontological dwelling), Enoch (normative facts are irreducible, and a "third factor" explains why our beliefs track them), Shafer-Landau (sui generis moral facts that supervene necessarily on natural ones), Wielenberg (brute ethical facts that God's existence does not help to explain), and Huemer (intuitionism: what seems true on reflection is evidence until defeated). For essence and grounding I draw on Fine; for directed duties on Darwall (the second-personal standpoint), Wallace (moral nexus) and Thompson (bipolar normativity); for constraints on Kamm (inviolability as a status) and Nagel (agent-relative reasons); for infants on McMahan (time-relative interests), whose view splits my school on A4. I agree with almost all of A, which makes this a test of whether the W and C sections show that God explains what I must leave brute. All paraphrases of these thinkers below are mine and should be checked ("verify").

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

**1. Starting point for justice.** Justice is giving each what she is owed. What she is owed comes from necessary truths about persons. Each person has claims that run to her and not to "the world" (Thompson, Wallace), and she has the standing to demand that they be met (Darwall). Those claims are limited by the separateness of persons and by constraints that express her inviolability (Kamm). Scanlon's test of what could be justified to each person is the working method; I hold it as a truth about reasons, not as a contract. **Holds.** It borrows nothing from the book's definition. It ends in a first principle, "persons have this standing," which I hold as self-evident on reflection.

**2. A hard dilemma.** Take the conjoined twins case (Re A, England, 2000; verify details). Mary lived only by Jodie's heart and lungs. Separation would kill Mary; leaving them joined would kill both. My principles are three. Jodie has a claim not to have her body used to keep another alive. Removing an imposition is not killing someone as a means (Thomson's violinist; Kamm on removing support one provides). Mary's death is foreseen, not aimed at. So separation is permitted, and it is not a case of spending one twin for the other. **Holds.** It uses doing and allowing, intending and foreseeing, and bodily claims. These are my own principles, not the book's "mercy" or "self-gift." I note that Catholic moralists were divided on this case (the Archbishop of Westminster opposed separation; verify), so my principles neither simply borrow the Church's view nor depend on it.

**3. Equal, unconditional dignity.** My school is divided, and the text should know this. (a) The equality wing holds, as a necessary truth known by reflection, that every individual who has a rational nature has the same basic moral status, whether or not she can use that nature, and that she keeps it through infancy, impairment, dying and crime. That covers the newborn, the dying, the disabled and the enemy. (b) McMahan's wing denies strict equality. It puts infants and some of the profoundly impaired under a "morality of interests" rather than a "morality of respect," so killing them is wrong, but less gravely (verify). **Holds, but brute and divided.** Wing (a) borrows the Aristotelians' metaphysics of natures, though not their God, and takes the bridge "a nature you have but cannot use grounds equal status" as given. Wing (b) is consistent but gives up A4. This is where my school is under the most strain.

**4. Ending point for flourishing.** No single ultimate good, but a plurality of intrinsic goods: knowledge, achievement, love and friendship, virtue, appreciation of beauty, happiness. A good life is rich in these and governed by duty (Parfit's objective-list sympathies; Hurka; verify). **Coherent; it borrows nothing; it stops at finitude.** The greatest goods we know, the loves, end at death, and on my view the dead are not repaid. I count this as tragic, not inconsistent (compare Scheffler on the collective afterlife; verify). The text's line in W3, "where that account stops," describes my position accurately.

## 3. Debate highlights

**E1. The epistemic rule of A.** *Attack:* A's rule, that what still seems true after honest reflection may be trusted until defeated, is my own (Huemer's phenomenal conservatism). By that same rule, my reflective sense that "cruelty is wrong" needs no further ground counts as evidence too. *Defense:* The rule is used in A to secure the moral truths themselves, which we share. Whether they need a ground is not a seeming of the same kind. It is an explanatory question, and W2 asks it about the *fit* between those truths and contingent minds, not about whether they need a dwelling. *Rejoinder:* Fair. The rule is applied evenly, and A openly names its deniers. **Held.**

**E2. Whom wronging points to (Darwall against the W1 heading).** *Attack:* Darwall answers Anscombe directly. Obligation does need an authority who can make demands, as the theological voluntarists saw, but every person has that authority (verify). So wronging points to *her*, not to God. *Defense:* The text grants this in so many words: "guilt is owed to the one we wronged, and this book agrees," and "the fact that no one can release us does not decide between the accounts. Nor does the fact that guilt is owed." W1 rests only on what remains: the grave, and what gives her the claim. *Rejoinder:* Then the heading "Wronging points to Someone" claims more than the paragraph under it, which concedes that wronging as such points to her. **Held with repair** (R1).

**E3. The debt to the dead.** *Attack:* A debt that can never be released is a tragic fact, not a defect in an account. Guilt "asking" for the dead woman's pardon is no evidence that she can give it. That is a wish, and A7 forbids believing because a wish is comforted. *Defense:* The text does not say otherwise. It calls this a clue that "weighs as much as the reader finds the third road ... truer than the second." It bases the clue on A2's phenomenology, already granted: guilt is directed, and only the one wronged can heal the bond. On the second road the request is addressed to no one who can answer it. That is a real difference in fit, not an inconsistency. *Rejoinder:* Accepted as a clue. But the Catholic road does not guarantee the pardon either. If the victim has refused God forever, she cannot give it, and the Church does not say how her claim is answered. So "this hope promises it: to be forgiven by her" claims more than the text's own "the Church does not spell out." The theistic advantage is that the pardon stays possible, which on my view it is not. That is still an advantage, but a smaller one. **Held with repair** (R2).

**E4. Equal worth: does the "call" add anything I lack?** *Attack:* "Her worth rests on what she is." If so, I have the same ground: worth goes with nature. God *causing* the bearer does not *ground* the value, just as God creating triangles does not ground their angle sum. Saying "A law ... does not want any one of them" assumes that worth needs to be wanted. It does not, and if it did, worth would be conferred, which the text denies. *Defense:* The text grounds worth twice: in the image (what she is) and in the call (GS 19, "the root reason"). The call does explanatory work my school has no counterpart for. Worth is measured by the end a being is made for. One end, God Himself, is the same for the genius and the newborn ("measured against one end that is God Himself, the genius and the newborn are for the same thing"). Measured against an infinite end, finite differences of power and mind disappear. This is not voluntarism. The call is given with her being, not added later, and "the gifts and the call of God are irrevocable" (Rom 11:29). *Rejoinder:* Two points. (i) The theist also needs a bridge principle, "worth is measured by the end one is called to," and must take it as given; the C row concedes that every account has a first principle. (ii) The supernatural call is gratuitous: Pius XII (*Humani Generis* 26) rejects the claim that God could not create intellectual beings without ordering them to the beatific vision. So the floor of dignity must rest on the image, that is, on nature, and there my wing (a) holds the same ground without God. I concede the remaining point. Explaining *equality* by an equal, infinite end is a real explanatory gain, and my wing (a) has only a bare kind principle to set against it. **Held**: honest parity on the count of first principles, with theism ahead on explaining why worth is equal.

**E5. The line at every number, and the paradox of deontology.** *Attack:* "No one may take what is not his to take, even to keep others from taking more" is just an agent-relative constraint, and grounding it in God's dominion does not dissolve Scheffler's paradox. Why not *minimize* violations of God's dominion? Kamm's inviolability already explains why no number may override a person's status. And "He who gives life, and can give it back, may call it home" reads as power generating a right. *Defense:* The text claims only that a reader who holds the line at every number "has here a reason for it." It explicitly lists "a person has the standing of one who may not be violated" as the rival answer, and it labels the absolute as the book's claim. The theistic addition is that *we* have no right to take innocent life at all, because that right belongs to the Creator alone. The giving-back clause makes a different point: God's taking does her no wrong, because He can restore. It does not claim that power makes right. A further point the text does not make: on theism the agent does not manage the world's totals; providence does, so refusing to do evil is never finally a betrayal of the many (Geach argued this; verify). *Rejoinder:* The providence point is good and the text should use it (§7). The paradox is answered by agent-relativity on both views, so theism's real advantage lies at "not even hers to waive," not at "every number." The giving-back clause needs rewording so it cannot be read as might makes right. **Held with repair** (R6).

**E6. Reach (W2): the realist's best reply.** *Attack:* Reach is not a separate thing to explain. It is conceptually true that the moral supervenes on the natural, so to understand *wrong* at all is to know that whatever makes a deed wrong would make it wrong in any world and for any agent. Survival fixed our attention on the wrong-making features (Enoch's third factor; Wielenberg). Our concepts then supply the modal reach. No Mind is needed. *Defense:* Grasping the concept tells you *that* some natural features make a deed wrong necessarily. It does not tell you *which*. Darwin's hive bees, raised to think killing their brothers a sacred duty, show that a mind shaped only for survival could fix on the wrong features and still feel the same "reach." The third factor needs survival-relevant features to coincide with the truly wrong-making ones in every case, including the case of "for any mind in any world." That coincidence is the fit, and one Source explains it. The text says "it weighs, it does not settle." *Rejoinder:* Fair. Theism explains the fit by design, I explain it by a correlation I take as given, and the text calls the result a matter of weighing. But the text should state my supervenience reply in my voice, since C promises "each is stated as its best defenders state it." **Held with repair** (R8).

**E7. Euthyphro and the idle wheel.** *Attack (Wielenberg):* The text says cruelty is wrong "because of what it does to her," and God "did not choose whether cruelty is evil." So the deed and its victim are what make it wrong. Locating the necessary connection "in God's wisdom" adds nothing. Either God knows it because it is true, which is realism, or it is true because He knows it, which drifts toward voluntarism. *Defense:* Neither. The text places the wrong-making in the deed and her. The necessity of the connection rests in God's knowledge of His own essence as imitable, "where every possible nature is first known" (ST I q.15). That is the Augustinian placement of eternal truths: not willed, not standing outside Him. Its payoff is not a better account of why cruelty is wrong. It is the account of why minds and natures fit the law (W2). *Rejoinder:* Then God's nature is my "brute necessary truth" with a personal face. Whether a person can be the Good, rather than merely have goodness, is the question at parity (Ch. 3 handles it). The text does not overclaim here. **Held**, an honest standoff at parity.

**E8. "Three things he must take separately."** *Attack:* The C row says the realist must take three things separately: the law, the beings it is about, and the minds that read it. That counts too many. Moral truths are conditional ("if there are persons, then ...") and hold whether or not there are persons. The beings I explain as physics and biology do, not by ethics. Only the fit between law and mind is an extra debt, and that is W2's question of reach. *Defense:* Theism also claims a teleological fit: natures *made for* what the law asks (W2, "an accident or a wisdom"). *Rejoinder:* That is a cosmological and teleological consideration, not a moral one. On realism, any rational nature has the norms that fit it, so there is no coincidence between beings and law to explain. The row should count one debt, not three. **Held with repair** (R5).

**E9. Evil and Wielenberg's divine-lies objection.** *Attack:* The evil section holds that "not seeing God's reasons is weak evidence that He has none." Wielenberg argues that the same reasoning lets God have unseen reasons to deceive, which would undercut the hope of resurrection the section relies on (verify). *Defense:* The text's own A6 absolute binds God too. God never does evil; He permits it (CCC 311). Lying is doing, not permitting, and God "never lies" (Titus 1:2); "it is impossible that God should prove false" (Heb 6:18); He is "Truth itself, whose words cannot deceive" (CCC 215). Unseen reasons can explain a *permission*. No reason can license an evil *act* by God. So the skeptical move goes only as far as the doing/permitting distinction allows, and the text holds that distinction absolutely. *Rejoinder:* For a first cause who "gives the act its being," the doing/permitting line is strained. The Thomist answer (God causes the being, not the defect) is coherent, so this is a standoff. The text is also modest here ("It is not no evidence"). **Held.**

**E10. The newborn test in the conclusion (McMahan).** *Attack:* McMahan's time-relative interest account (verify) predicts that a careful reader, asked whether the newborn was wronged "no less than ... a grown man," will answer: wronged, gravely, but the grown man lost more, so the wrong to him was greater. The test pits the book's strongest intuition against the one place where many thoughtful readers' intuitions really do waver, on the size of the *loss*. *Defense:* The test asks about *wronging*, not loss. The Catholic view can grant that one death takes more years and plans than another while holding that the violation is the same: one innocent deliberately killed. *Rejoinder:* Then say so. Without that distinction my school's readers will merge "wronged" with "lost," and the test will fail for the wrong reason. **Held with repair** (R7).

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

**As written: NONE.**

No Fatal or Serious objection from my school stands. At its base, every point where I press (equal worth, reach, the debt to the dead, Euthyphro) ends in a standoff at parity that the text itself acknowledges, or in a clue the text weighs no higher than a clue.

**Minor**
1. The W1 heading claims more than the W1 body concedes (E2; R1).
2. "This hope promises it" overclaims. The Church does not teach that every victim will pardon, and a victim who refused God forever cannot (E3; R2).
3. The C "A God who is Love" row says the realist must take three things separately. Only the fit between law and mind is an extra debt (E8; R5).
4. "He who gives life ... may call it home" can be read as power generating a right (E5; R6).
5. The conclusion's newborn test does not separate wronging from loss, so readers of McMahan's school will answer "lesser" for a reason the book does not hold (E10; R7).
6. W2 does not state the realist's best reply on reach (supervenience is conceptual) before answering it (E6; R8).
7. A5 reports Aquinas's "the truth about God included" inside the shared premises. It is reported, not demanded, so it is acceptable; a non-theist can grant "the truth about whether and what God is."

**Misstatements of my school**
- By omission: the text presents A4's denial as Singer's (a utilitarian's), and the godless-realism row implies that realists all take kind-based worth as given. In fact, leading non-consequentialist realists (McMahan; in part Kagan's hierarchy of moral status; verify) deny strict equality for infants and the profoundly impaired. Naming this makes the book's case stronger (R3, R4).
- "The debt to the dead, which on this account can never be answered": slightly misleading. On my view the debt can be honored and partly repaid to the living, but never released by her (R4).
- Otherwise accurate: Parfit's "no dwelling," the essence-based grounding, the third-factor answer in W2, the "few deep principles," and "Every account has a first principle, ours included" are all fair.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Brute necessary truths versus God's necessary nature as the stopping point (E7).
- Whether reach is explained by conceptual competence plus a third factor, or by one Source (E6).
- Whether a debt that can never be released is a tragic truth or a clue to Someone (E3).
- Whether the floor of equal dignity is better grounded by a bare kind principle or by the image plus an equal infinite end. Theism leads on *explaining* equality, but its bridge principle is also given (E4).
- The doing/permitting distinction for a first cause (E9).

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**New insights**
1. **Providence and the absolute.** The book's absolute ("The absolute can cost lives, even many") is easiest to bear on theism because the agent does not manage the world's totals. Refusing to do evil is never finally a betrayal of the many, since outcomes rest with a provident God (Geach made this argument; verify). My school has no counterpart: Kamm and Nagel can say *why* the line exists, but not why holding it at catastrophic cost is not finally reckless. This belongs in W1(2) or the conclusion, labeled as the book's claim.
2. **Where theism's real advantage on constraints lies.** Against Kamm's inviolability, theism's distinctive gain is "not even hers to waive," not "every number." The text could say so plainly and spare itself a fight with the paradox of deontology, which both sides answer with agent-relativity.
3. **Equal worth by an equal end.** The strongest point against my school is that an equal, infinite end explains *equality*, while a bare kind principle only asserts it. State the principle (R9) and keep the image as the floor. This guards against reading the gratuitous supernatural call (*Humani Generis* 26) as making dignity depend on a free choice that is separate from her nature.
4. **Name Darwall.** In the W1 frame, "The best accounts without God keep them" could name Darwall's second-personal answer to Anscombe (verify). That is the best version of the view the text is conceding to, and naming it shows the concession is not a straw man.
5. **Name McMahan.** It shows that A4 is denied by careful non-consequentialists, not only by Singer, and it makes the conclusion's newborn test sharper.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."
- "Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent."
- The secret told to a stranger versus the letter blown into the street: the cleanest illustration of a directed duty I have seen in a popular text.
- "Equal worth is not equal duty."
- "A will that invents values can command, but it cannot bind."
- "Heaven and Earth give life to things; God wanted *you*."
- "What rises in you then is not the source of her worth. It is your answer to it."
- "Every account has a first principle, ours included." This honesty is why my school cannot land a Serious blow.

## 8. Score

**8/10.** Against non-naturalist realism the text concedes exactly what it should (shared moral truths, directed duties, parity of first principles). It then puts its weight where my school is genuinely thin: why worth is *equal*, the debt to the dead, and the fit between law and mind. Each of these it calls a clue, not a proof, and the repairs above are refinements of wording.

**Honest ceiling for any theistic case against my school: about 8.5.** Because my view holds the moral truths themselves and lets them be brute, a theistic case can at best show better unification and fit, never a contradiction. This text is close to that limit.
