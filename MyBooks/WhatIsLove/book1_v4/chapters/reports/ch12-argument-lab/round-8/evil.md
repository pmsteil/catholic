# Blind review, Round 8: "A good God would never allow this"

## 1. School and voice

I argue as the atheist who presses the problem of evil and divine hiddenness at full strength: Mackie's logical problem (an omnipotent God could have made free creatures who always choose the good), Rowe's and Draper's evidential arguments (pointless-seeming horrors; pain and pleasure distributed as an indifferent nature predicts), Schellenberg's hiddenness argument (perfect love would leave no nonresistant person unable to believe), Maitzen's demographics of belief and his "ordinary morality" argument, Stephen Law's evil-god symmetry, Marilyn Adams and Thomas Talbott on hell, Morriston and Wielenberg on the divine-command killings, and Ivan Karamazov's returned ticket. For my positive ethics I speak as a godless moral realist in Wielenberg's manner (verify), because the argument from evil is strongest in the mouth of someone who thinks the child's suffering is *really* bad, not merely disliked. I grant what is true in the text and press where it gives way.

## 2. Answers to the four standing challenges

1. **Starting point for justice.** What anyone is owed comes from what he is: a subject whose life can go well or badly, with interests and rational agency of his own. Basic ethical truths (that gratuitous cruelty is wrong; that persons are owed fair treatment and may not be used up for others) are necessary and need nothing behind them (Wielenberg, verify). **Holds.** It is coherent; it stops at brute necessary truths, as the text's "godless realism" row says fairly.
2. **A hard dilemma.** Take a competent patient dying in pain that cannot be controlled, who asks her doctor for a lethal dose. My principles: respect for her rational agency (what could be justified to *her*); the badness of suffering; a strong presumption against killing, grounded in the loss of a future worth having; and the difference between doing and allowing. Where the future, on her own considered judgment, holds nothing but agony, the presumption is defeated, with strict safeguards. **Holds, borrows vocabulary only.** I use honesty, compassion and her claim, but not as the book defines truth, mercy or self-gift; I do without "self-gift". I part openly from A6, as the text says many will.
3. **Equal, unconditional dignity.** I ground it in being a subject of a life who belongs to a rational kind, so that the newborn, the dying and the severely disabled share the standing of their kind (on the lines of S. Matthew Liao's genetic-basis view, verify). **Borrows and strains at the edge.** "Worth follows nature, not capacity" is the text's own move. For a child who will never be conscious at all I cannot say she is owed *everything* a grown person is, and I will not pretend to. I note against my own side that Ivan's protest needs worth of A4's strength; Rowe's argument needs only that intense suffering is bad.
4. **Ultimate good and guiding principles.** There is no ultimate good beyond finite lives: love, knowledge, meaningful work, freedom from horrors, justice among us. I am guided by reducing horrors and honoring persons. **Coherent; I pay the price the text names.** The longing in W3 is, on my view, answered in the end by no one, and the debt to the dead (W1(1)) is kept on the "second road" only. I own both.

## 3. Debate highlights

**E1. Mackie: God could have made free creatures who never sin.**
*Attack.* The text concedes my premise twice. God kept Mary free of every sin by grace, her whole life, without destroying her freedom (CCC 491–493), and the saints in heaven "are free and cannot sin." So Plantinga's defense, that God perhaps *could not* bring about a world of free creatures who always do right, is closed to a Catholic. Why not a world of Marys?
*Defense.* The text does not rely on free will to explain *why* God permitted the first sin. It says free will answers *whose* the evil is ("ours, not His"), and that to the question why He did not keep Adam as He kept Mary, the Church answers "only with what He brought from it" (CCC 412). It also offers the good of creatures who give love back "in a life where it could be refused," calls that "a good, not a necessity," and puts its cost on the scale.
*Rejoinder.* That is honest, and it holds for moral evil in this life: it claims attribution, not justification. But the same concession reaches somewhere the text has not carried it (E2).
**Held.**

**E2. Adams and Talbott: hell, made sharper by Mary.**
*Attack.* Talbott's three propositions cannot all be true: God wills to save all (1 Timothy 2:4, cited); God can bring about what He wills; some are lost forever (CCC 1035). The text supplies the second in its strongest form. Mary shows that God can bring a free creature to her yes by grace *without forcing it*. So the text's rationale for hell, "God does not force His love on anyone able to refuse it," does not reach the case: no one is asking Him to force anyone. Add Adams: a God good *to each person* must make each participant's life a great good to her on the whole. The text commits itself to her premise: God "governs each person for her own sake" (SCG III.112), "never spends anyone as a mere means," and love's first word, "it is good that you exist," is not said "for a while only." Yet Scripture says of one man: "It would have been better for that man if he had not been born" (Matthew 26:24, RSV).
*Defense.* Catholic teaching allows two accounts of grace (the *De auxiliis* dispute, which Paul V closed in 1607 without settling it: verify). On the Molinist account, Mary's yes came through grace together with how she would freely respond, and some creatures would refuse under any grace. On the Thomist account, God gives sufficient grace to all, and the refusal is wholly the creature's own (CCC 1037: He predestines no one to hell). Even the lost keep a being that is good, and God holds it in existence (ST I q.20 a.2). The refusal is final because it was made with full knowledge and consent, like the angels' choice (CCC 393).
*Rejoinder.* Each account strains the text's own principle. The Thomist must finally say, with Aquinas, that God manifests His goodness by mercy in some and by justice in others (ST I q.23 a.5 ad 3, verify). That is a person's eternal loss serving to display a perfection, which is what SCG III.112 and GS 24 forbid. The Molinist must say God created a person He foresaw would be lost forever, for whom it would have been better not to exist; if He created her for the sake of the world's balance, she was spent. The text currently presents freedom as the reason and adds "through no defect in His mercy," while its own Mary sentence, a few lines later, removes freedom as a sufficient reason.
*Defense, last.* The Church does not claim to reconcile these; the text says death's fixing of the will is "not something this case shows," and that "hell stays on this side of the scale."
**Failed as written; Held with repair** (see R1). The repair concedes, not answers. Hell then stays where the text puts it, heavier than the paragraph now admits.

**E3. Rowe and Draper: the evidential problem.**
*Attack.* The fawn burned in the forest fire, the child with cancer, and pain distributed across 500 million years "where survival would put it, not where justice would" are far likelier on the hypothesis that nature is indifferent than on theism (Draper, verify). The noseeum defense, "we can't see God's reasons, so they may be there," either proves too much (moral skepticism about whether we should stop *any* evil) or proves nothing.
*Defense.* The text grants nearly all of this in so many words: "A world with no one behind it would put pain where survival puts it, which is where we find it." It then states its claim narrowly. "We know enough of goodness to know what a good God would value, which is why this weight is real"; what we would not expect to see, *if* the good is finished only in eternal life and in a creation set free (Romans 8:21), is how each particular evil is answered. So not seeing His reasons is "weak evidence that He has none. It is not no evidence."
*Rejoinder.* That avoids both horns of the skeptical-theism dilemma: it keeps moral knowledge and names a specific reason why the answer would be out of sight. I have nothing that defeats it. What remains is a likelihood that favors my side by exactly the amount the text concedes, set against the moral witnesses. That is a judgment, and the conclusion presents it as one.
**Held (standoff at parity).** One overclaim: the heading "Three things keep that weight from overturning the case." On the text's own account only the second lessens the weight as evidence (the first answers a *dark* god; the third "is not a further proof"). See R3.

**E4. Double effect, applied to God.**
*Attack.* A6 says "side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent." The evil section applies this to God: He wills the order, never the loss for its own sake (ST I q.19 a.9). But double effect has a proportionality condition: there must be no less harmful way to the good. For an omnipotent agent there almost always is. An order of real causes does not need *Plasmodium falciparum*, a parasite fitted to human hosts that kills mostly small children. The text says "if our nature is a wisdom ... so is theirs." Then the parasite's nature, whose good is to feed on the child, is a wisdom too, and "God does not give her to it" is a distinction we cannot see.
*Defense.* There is no best possible world: whatever God makes, He could make better (ST I q.25 a.6). So "He could have done it with less harm" is true of every world He could create, and cannot by itself be a defect, or creating anything would be wrong. God's goodness is not measured by the world, which He creates freely and owes nothing to. On the parasite, the Church teaches that man was not made to stand exposed to such harms: he lost by sin the harmony that shielded him, and "visible creation has become alien and hostile to man" (CCC 376, 400). The parasite's nature is ordered to a host, not to *her* as such; her exposure belongs to the human fall, not to the parasite's design.
*Rejoinder.* Rowe argued that a no-best-world sets a perfectly good creator a dilemma (verify). The Catholic answer, that God's goodness is not a maximizing goodness, is coherent and is not ad hoc. The CCC 400 reply is real, though it is a teaching, not something seen. And it is absent from the text, which leaves the fawn-and-child distinction resting on an intention no one can observe.
**Held at parity; the parasite sentence needs repair** (R5).

**E5. Stephen Law: the evil god.**
*Attack.* Law's challenge is symmetry, not a claim that the world's darkness proves a dark god. Every theodicy has a mirror: a maximally evil god gives us love so that loss will hurt, freedom so that we will betray, and beauty so that its fading will grieve us. Turn it on the witnesses. A debt that outlives every creditor on earth is perpetual, unpayable guilt, a torment; a longing to hear "it is good that you exist" that no one on earth can answer is the cruelest gift a tormentor could plant. The text says neither an indifferent source nor a god beyond good and evil "explains" these. The evil god explains them very well.
*Defense.* The text breaks the symmetry metaphysically, not by counting goods against evils. Evil has nothing of its own; it is a good harmed. There can be a supreme Good but not a supreme Evil (ST I q.49 a.3). A will that lacks nothing has nothing to grasp at. And the ground of being, "whose very being is to be" (Exodus 3:14; ST I q.3 a.4), lacks nothing. So a maximally evil *ultimate* being is incoherent, not just improbable. In addition, the text's moral facts are necessary (W1): an evil god could not make cruelty good, and could not be the Good in whose wisdom those truths hold (W2).
*Rejoinder.* That is a real asymmetry, and the privation account is at least as defensible as its denial among metaphysicians. But the asymmetry rests wholly on that account. The earthly data in W1(1) and W3 do not by themselves tell a good God from an evil one; only the promised answer does, and the text rightly says the promise is not evidence. The text should say on what the asymmetry rests.
**Held with repair** (R4).

**E6. Schellenberg and Maitzen: hiddenness and the demographics of belief.**
*Attack.* A perfectly loving God would always make conscious, reciprocal relationship available to every capable creature who does not resist it. The text's own definition makes this premise stronger: love "binds ... into life-giving communion," and W3 says "only a person can love us back." Yet the most disciplined, nonresistant contemplatives in history found "a peace beyond the self rather than a Person." The mediation answer (God makes Himself known through persons, CCC 306, 848) explains the route, not the failure: it makes a seeker's conscious relation to God hostage to whether other people were faithful. And God bypasses mediation when He chooses (Paul on the Damascus road), so the dignity of secondary causes does not explain why He does not do so for the honest seeker. Maitzen adds that who comes to know Him by name tracks where and to whom one was born far more than how honestly one sought. By the text's own fairness rule, that is as weighty as "pain falls where survival would put it, not where justice would."
*Defense.* The relationship can be begun before it is named. God withholds nothing needed for salvation (LG 16); the Spirit offers every person a share in the Paschal Mystery "in a manner known only to God" (GS 22); "a child is loved long before he can say who loves him." Conscious knowing is promised in full only at the end (1 Corinthians 13:12). And the text does not pretend: "not a full answer to why Love would choose this way, and it has a real cost," and honest seekers who do not find Him sit on the scale.
*Rejoinder.* The text does not use the epistemic-distance reply (that evidence of God would coerce the will), which is wise, because "even the demons believe—and shudder" (James 2:19, RSV): belief does not coerce. Implicit relationship is a genuine Catholic answer to the *salvation* question, but Schellenberg's question is about relationship as a good of love in itself. The text concedes that remainder, so this is a standoff, not a defeat. But "a real answer" claims slightly more than "part of an answer," and the text does not notice that its own definition of love sharpens the problem, or weigh the demographic point.
**Held with repair** (R6).

**E7. Morriston and Wielenberg: the divine-command killings, and the conclusion turned.**
*Attack.* W1(2) grounds the absolute line in this: "He who gives life, and can give it back, may call it home; we, who can do neither, may not take it." That is Aquinas's premise, and Aquinas drew the conclusion from it: since God is Lord of life and death, killing the innocent at His command is not murder (ST I-II q.94 a.5 ad 2; q.100 a.8 ad 3, verify). Scripture reports such a command: "kill both man and woman, infant and suckling" (1 Samuel 15:3, RSV). Now turn the text's own closing appeal. Which am I surer of: that the Amalekite infant was wronged when she was killed, or any argument (inspiration read literally, plus the Lord-of-life premise) that says she was not? If the text says she was not wronged, its unconditional worth (A4) bends to a command; if she was, the text owes the reader a word on 1 Samuel 15.
*Defense.* The text already says no one, "whatever voice he says he heard in His name," may claim that right (CCC 2258), so no human hand today may act on such a command. The Church reads the Old Testament in the light of Christ: its books contain things "incomplete and temporary" (*Dei Verbum* 15), and Benedict XVI acknowledged that revelation was suited to the moral level of distant times and recounts violence and massacre without always condemning it (*Verbum Domini* 42; paraphrase, verify). A Catholic is therefore free to hold that the Amalekite infant was wronged by those who killed her, and to read the herem as Israel's understanding of God at that stage, not as His approval of killing children. And the text's own last line in W1 supplies the measure: "the Judge lets Himself be asked (Genesis 18:25)," Abraham's very plea that the innocent not die with the guilty ("Wilt thou indeed destroy the righteous with the wicked? ... Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?" Genesis 18:23, 25, RSV).
*Rejoinder.* That defense is strong and I accept that it reaches parity. But none of it is in the text. As written, the text states the Lord-of-life premise that Morriston attacks, without the reading of Scripture that keeps it from licensing what Aquinas allowed. That leaves its distinctive absolute line, and its final appeal, open to the most damaging objection my school brings against the Christian God in particular.
**Failed as written; Held with repair** (R2).

**E8. Ivan Karamazov: the returned ticket.**
*Attack.* No future harmony can justify one child's tortured tears; the mother has no right to forgive the torturer in her child's place; I return my ticket.
*Defense.* The text agrees with Ivan's two premises and refuses the bargain he rejects. "No one else on earth can give that pardon in her place" (A3); "God does not take her place in giving the pardon that is hers to give" (W1(1)); God's permission "is not a trade," and the evil "never earns that good and never becomes good itself." It does not claim the harmony justifies the tears. It claims God suffers with her and "will not leave it in the ground," and it says plainly: "That does not explain why."
*Rejoinder.* Then the creator's choice is unexplained, and the text says so ("whether it is worth this world's cost is part of the weighing"). Ivan's protest stands as weight, and the text weighs it honestly. The theist may note that Ivan's protest rests on A1–A4, the very witnesses the case rests on. That is fair against Ivan; it does not touch Rowe, whose argument needs only that suffering is bad.
**Held (standoff at parity).**

**E9. The bystander God (the conclusion's Moorean appeal, turned again).**
*Attack.* The conclusion asks which I am surer of: that the exposed infant was wronged, or any argument that she was not. Then I ask which I am surer of: that a being who could save her at no cost to Himself and let her die is not perfectly loving, or any theodicy that says He is.
*Defense.* The two certainties differ in kind. The first is about what she is. The second is a judgment about what an omniscient being's reasons could be, and the text has argued (E3) that our not seeing them is weak evidence. A bystander's duty to rescue presupposes that he has no reason we would accept if we saw it; and God is not a bystander, but the One who holds the whole order of causes in being, our rescuing hands included ("His permission never excuses ours").
*Rejoinder.* That asymmetry is real but not large. The text should let the reader see that the turn exists rather than leave it implicit.
**Held at parity; optional repair** (R7).

## 5. Remaining sound objections against the text AS WRITTEN

1. **Serious: Hell, freedom and Mary (Talbott, Adams).** The text gives "God does not force His love on anyone able to refuse it" as the reason a final refusal is possible, and says the refusal is final "through no defect in His mercy." But its own sentence on Mary concedes that God can bring a free creature to her yes by grace without forcing her. So freedom is not a sufficient reason why any are lost. And every Catholic account of why efficacious grace is not given to all strains the text's own principles: God governs each person for her own sake, never spends anyone as a mere means, and His word "it is good that you exist" is not for a while only. Matthew 26:24 sharpens it. The text cannot answer this at parity as written, because it presents a rationale its own premises undercut. Repairable by honest concession (R1). After the repair it is a weight, not a defect, and the heaviest one on the scale.
2. **Serious: The divine-command killings (Morriston, Wielenberg).** W1(2) grounds the absolute line in God's sole right to "call [life] home," which is the premise from which Aquinas concluded that killing the innocent at God's command is not murder. Scripture reports such a command against infants (1 Samuel 15:3). The text says nothing about it, so its distinctive absolute line and its closing Moorean appeal ("was she wronged?") can be turned against it. Catholic teaching allows a strong answer (*Dei Verbum* 15, *Verbum Domini* 42, Genesis 18:25), but the text does not give it. Repairable (R2).

**Minor**
- The heading "Three things keep that weight from overturning the case" overclaims: by the text's own account only the second is evidence that lessens the weight (R3).
- "So the world's darkness is not evidence of a dark god" rests entirely on the privation account and should say so; on earthly data alone W1(1) and W3 are symmetric between a good and an evil god (R4).
- "The parasite takes from the child; God does not give her to it" makes a distinction no one can observe, and omits the Church's own support for it (CCC 376, 400) (R5).
- "That is a real answer to why many do not find Him" should be "part of an answer." The book's definition of love as communion sharpens Schellenberg's premise and should be named. Maitzen's demographic point is not weighed, though the fairness rule asks for it, as it does for pain falling where survival puts it (R6).
- The conclusion does not show the reader that its Moorean appeal can be turned against a God who permits the exposure (R7, optional).

**Misstatements of my school**
- Law: "not evidence of a dark god" answers a claim Law does not make. His challenge is symmetry (that the good-god hypothesis should be as hard to believe as the evil-god hypothesis), not that darkness supports a dark god. This is mild, since Law is not named.
- Schellenberg: "Honest seekers look for God and do not find Him" narrows his argument. It concerns every nonresistant nonbeliever, including those who never had occasion to seek, and its claim is that conscious relationship should be *available*, not that searches fail. This is mild.
- Draper, Rowe, Mackie and Ivan are stated fairly, often better than their critics state them.

**Honest standoffs at parity (not defects)**
- Rowe/Draper against moderate skeptical theism: a likelihood favoring naturalism, conceded and weighed against the moral witnesses (E3).
- The proportionality of double effect for an omnipotent agent, against the no-best-world reply (E4).
- Schellenberg's relationship-as-intrinsic-good against implicit relationship begun by grace (E6).
- Ivan's returned ticket (E8).
- The final weighing itself: on the text's own concessions it is a judgment, and it is offered as one.

## 6. Proposed repairs

**R1 (hell; Evil ¶1).**
Old: "That death fixes the will is the Church's teaching (CCC 393, 1021), not something this case shows, and so hell stays on this side of the scale."
New: "Freedom does not finish the answer. God kept Mary from every sin by grace without forcing her yes (CCC 491–493), so He can bring a free creature to say yes without overriding her; why His grace prevails in one and is refused to the end by another, the Church does not explain, and she lets her theologians differ over it. That death fixes the will is her teaching too (CCC 393, 1021), not something this case shows. So hell stays on this side of the scale, and for a book that says God's first word to each of us is that it is good that we exist, it is the heaviest thing there."
(If wanted: footnote the 1607 close of the *De auxiliis* dispute under Paul V, verify.)

**R2 (divine-command killings; W1(2)).**
Old: "That right is never ours, and no one, whatever voice he says he heard in His name, may claim it for himself (CCC 2258)."
New: "That right is never ours, and no one, whatever voice he says he heard in His name, may claim it for himself (CCC 2258). Scripture tells of wars in which Israel believed God had commanded it to destroy whole peoples, children too (1 Samuel 15:3). The Church reads those pages in the light of Christ, as part of a revelation given to a people at the moral level of its time, which recounts violence and massacre without always condemning it (*Dei Verbum* 15; *Verbum Domini* 42); this book reads them so, and measures them by Abraham's question, asked so that the innocent would not die with the guilty: 'Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?' (Genesis 18:25)."

**R3 (heading of Evil ¶2).**
Old: "Three things keep that weight from overturning the case, though they do not make it light."
New: "Three things bear on that weight, though none makes it light; only the second lessens it as evidence."

**R4 (privation; First).**
Old: "So the world's darkness is not evidence of a dark god."
New: "So the world's darkness is not evidence of a dark god. That rests on what evil is; a reader who holds that malice could be as full and real as love must weigh a dark god against the whole case, as he weighs an indifferent source."

**R5 (parasite; Second).**
Old: "The parasite takes from the child; God does not give her to it."
New: "The parasite takes from the child; God does not give her to it. What we see is the same as with the fawn; the difference is in what He wills. The Church adds that man was not made to stand exposed to such harms: the harmony he lost by sin was his shield, and creation has become hostile to him (CCC 376, 400). That is a teaching, not something we see, and the parasite stays on this side of the scale."

**R6 (hiddenness; W3 and Evil ¶1).**
(a) Old: "That is a real answer to why many do not find Him, though not a full answer to why Love would choose this way, and it has a real cost:"
New: "That is part of an answer to why many do not find Him, though not a full answer to why Love would choose this way; and this book's own definition makes the question harder, for a love that binds into communion wants to be known back, not only to be at work unseen. It has a real cost:"
(b) Old: "Honest seekers look for God and do not find Him."
New: "Honest seekers look for God and do not find Him, and who comes to know Him by name follows where and to whom he was born far more than how honestly he sought."

**R7 (conclusion; optional).**
Old: "An honest reader may weigh it differently, and should still weigh it;"
New: "An honest reader may weigh it differently, and may turn the question on us: which is he surer of, that she was wronged, or that a good God could have reasons to let her die there? The section on evil says how far this book can answer that. He should still weigh it;"

## 7. New insights for the book; lines worth keeping

**Insights**
- **Genesis 18:25 is Ivan's question inside Scripture.** The text ends W1 with "the Judge lets Himself be asked," but does not use it. Abraham's plea, Job, Psalm 44 and Habakkuk 1:13 show that the protest my school makes is one faith licenses, and the same verse is the measure the book needs for 1 Samuel 15. Developed, this would turn my school's strongest weapon into one of the book's own witnesses: God is asked to be just *by His own standard*, which is why He is not a commander who picks rules.
- **The evil section is A6 applied to God.** "Side effects may be weighed; persons may not be spent" is exactly the logic of "God's permission is not a trade" and "a person it never gives away." Saying this explicitly would unify the chapter. It also obliges the book to face proportionality for an omnipotent agent, where the no-best-world teaching (ST I q.25 a.6) is the honest reply.
- **The strongest form of the argument from evil is itself a moral argument.** Ivan's version ("the child was wronged; no one may forgive in her place") needs A1–A4 at full strength. The book can fairly say that whoever presses evil hardest is pressing with the very witnesses the case rests on, while granting that Rowe's version needs only that pain is bad. Overstated, this becomes a cheap answer to an internal critique; stated with that limit, it is fair.
- **The Mary concession is a strength if owned.** It closes Plantinga's route, but owning it makes the book more honest than most Christian theodicies, and it fits the book's thesis. If God can win a free yes without force, then freedom is not His limit but His gift, and the remaining mystery is a mystery of love's choosing, not of His power.
- **The text's epistemic rule cuts both ways, and it already lives by that.** It could say so once: the same rule that gives A1 its standing gives Rowe's seeming its standing, which is why the weight is real.

**Lines worth keeping**
- "A yes said to whatever happens is said to no one ... God says no to what was done to her, and yes to her."
- "The hands that pull a child from the fire are part of what He made real, so His permission never excuses ours."
- "If our nature is a wisdom and not an accident (W2), so is theirs, and that too is on this side of the scale."
- "For the fawn that hope is thinner than it is for the child, and this book does not pretend otherwise."
- "What Christ gave was given, not taken, and it was not lost."
- "The question is not whether a distant designer fits this world, but whether a God who suffers with it and will not leave it in the ground does."
- "Not seeing God's reasons is weak evidence that He has none. It is not no evidence."
- "We say that it is good that she exists, and so we must answer for the child."
- "A truth can be true; only a person can forgive."

## 8. Score

**Robustness against my school: 7/10 as written (8/10 with R1 and R2).** It concedes almost everything my side can rightly demand and holds a moderate skeptical theism without falling into moral skepticism; but its freedom rationale for hell is undercut by its own Mary sentence, and its Lord-of-life ground for the absolute line leaves 1 Samuel 15 unanswered. **Honest ceiling for any theistic case against my school: 8/10.** The problem of evil cannot be removed by argument, only weighed with full honesty against the positive case, and a text that did everything right would still leave a heavy weight on the scale, as this one rightly says it does.
