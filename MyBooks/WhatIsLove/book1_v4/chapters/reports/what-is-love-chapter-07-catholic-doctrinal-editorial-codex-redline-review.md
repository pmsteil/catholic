## Chapter 7 · Justice: God Is Justice

### Chapter assessment

Chapter 7 has a strong governing insight: justice is the stable will to render God and neighbor what is due, and Catholic social teaching carries that obligation from personal conduct into institutions and the common good. The edited chapter is particularly effective when it joins the virtue of religion, the imago Dei, care for the vulnerable, duties as well as rights, and the social mission announced by Christ in Luke 4.

Its principal doctrinal problem is the repeated claim that justice is not fully justice until it restores a person or relationship. Catholic punishment also includes proportionate redress of disorder, protection, deterrence and medicinal aims; restitution restores an equality of justice but does not automatically reconcile. The chapter also treats *dulia* as generic mutual honor, blurs moral right with civil immunity, groups abortion, euthanasia and capital punishment too tightly, makes grace identical with God’s Perfect Love, weakens the enduring marriage bond after betrayal, and gives the Sacrament of Penance the wrong internal structure. Several cultural applications are stated through broad labels rather than precise acts or policies. These revisions preserve the chapter’s urgency while making its justice genuinely Catholic rather than definitionally restorative or politically elastic.

**Severity:** Major / anthropology and reasoning · **Control:** DC-27, DC-29 · **Location:** `chapter_07.md:31–43`

**Issue.** Natural law gives every person a basic orientation to good and justice, but particular judgments can be obscured by ignorance, formation, passion and sin. “Immediately, instinctively, universally” exceeds what the examples prove.

**Controlling sources:** CCC 1954–1960; Romans 2:14–16.

**Source passage with proposed inline redline:**

Every child ~~knows~~ ++can recognize++ it. Every human heart ~~feels~~ ++is ordered toward++ it. The ~~instinctive~~ recognition that some things are objectively wrong—not just unpleasant or inconvenient, but actually, truly unjust.

The bully taking lunch money. The boss stealing credit. The judge taking bribes. We know—~~immediately, instinctively, universally~~ ++often with striking immediacy++—that these things are wrong. Not just socially disapproved. *Wrong.*

This universal cry reveals something profound: justice is written on every human heart — part of the natural law built into creation by the God whose image we bear, the God who IS just. ++That law is real even when our perception or application of it is clouded and needs formation.++

**Editorial consequence.** The opening retains its experiential force without claiming infallible moral intuition.

### CH7-06 · Qualify the Hebrew gloss and do not make all biblical justice restorative

**Severity:** Stop-ship / Scripture and justice · **Control:** DC-13, DC-27, DC-28 · **Location:** `chapter_07.md:129–131`

**Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:**

### CH7-07 · Correct Aquinas on restitution, punishment and the “parts” of justice

**Severity:** Stop-ship / moral theology · **Control:** DC-13, DC-27, DC-28 · **Location:** `chapter_07.md:133–137`

**Issue.** Restitution restores equality by returning what was unjustly taken or repairing harm; it does not necessarily restore the relationship. Reconciliation is not simply one of Aquinas’s named virtues annexed to justice. Punishment can be just through redress, defense, deterrence and correction even when reconciliation does not occur.

**Controlling sources:** CCC 2266; CCC 2411–2412; *Summa Theologiae* II–II, qq.58–62, 80–120.

**Source passage with proposed inline redline:**

**Commutative justice** is fairness between individuals—keeping contracts, paying debts, honoring agreements. And crucially, when this justice is violated, Aquinas insists it is not satisfied by mere apology or punishment: it demands *restitution*—the active restoring of what was taken, whether goods~~, reputation, or the relationship itself~~ ++or reputation, insofar as repair is possible++ (*Summa Theologiae* II-II, q.62); justice between persons is ~~inherently restorative~~ ++ordered to restoring the equality violated by the unjust act, not automatically to relational reconciliation++. **Distributive justice** is the fair distribution of common goods according to need and contribution—which ~~necessarily includes lifting up those who have been deprived~~ ++can require correcting deprivation and ensuring participation++, not merely preventing future deprivation.

Aquinas also treats **~~restitution, truth-telling, gratitude, honor, and reconciliation~~ ++religion, piety, observance, gratitude, vindication, truthfulness, friendliness and liberality++** — acts or virtues belonging to justice's own household — as essential to understanding what the virtue requires (*ST* II-II, qq.58–122). Justice, rightly understood, is never content with ~~bare equilibrium; it presses toward the restored communion~~ ++an abstract equality detached from persons and the common good; charity and mercy press beyond strict due toward the healed communion++ that *tsedaqah* names. ~~Punishment without restoration is not yet justice; it is only its first half.~~ ++Proportionate punishment can itself be just when it redresses disorder, protects the community, deters wrongdoing and, when possible, fosters correction; restoration is not always achievable and reconciliation requires more than justice alone.++

**Editorial consequence.** The account becomes recognizably Thomistic and leaves mercy real as a gift beyond strict due.

### CH7-08 · Add the moral limits to obedience under Romans 13

**Severity:** Stop-ship / political authority · **Control:** DC-14, DC-25 · **Location:** `chapter_07.md:139–141`

**Issue.** Governing authority is owed obedience only within legitimate competence and the moral order. Unjust laws do not bind conscience; sometimes resistance is required.

**Controlling sources:** CCC 1897–1904; CCC 2238–2243; Acts 5:29.

**Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:**

Legal justice—what we owe the common good—has a concrete shape in Scripture. St. Paul spells it out directly: "Let every person be subject to the governing authorities. For there is no authority except from God, and those that exist have been instituted by God... Pay all of them their dues, taxes to whom taxes are due, revenue to whom revenue is due, respect to whom respect is due, honor to whom honor is due" (Romans 13:1-7). This is legal justice in one breath: authority is owed obedience, and the state is owed its due, because governing authority itself borrows its standing from God, not from itself. ++That debt is morally bounded: laws contrary to right reason, divine law or human dignity do not bind conscience, and no ruler may demand what belongs to God alone.++

**Editorial consequence.** The passage cannot be misused to sanctify tyranny or silence conscientious resistance.
