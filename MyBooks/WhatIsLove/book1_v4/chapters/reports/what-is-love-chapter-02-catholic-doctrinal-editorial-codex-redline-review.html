    Chapter 2 · The Answer Is Love

    Chapter assessment

    Chapter 2 performs an indispensable task well: it moves from the wound named in Chapter 1 to the book’s positive governing vision, gives the canonical definition its first sustained exposition, and introduces natural evil with unusual pastoral honesty. Its strongest passages are the opening Catechism quotation, the insistence that love must be concrete, and the closing promise of a Love strong enough to be trusted within suffering. The chapter’s central doctrinal repair is to distinguish four related realities that the present prose sometimes collapses: God is the objective supreme good; charity is the greatest theological virtue that unites us to God; natural human love is real but requires healing and supernatural elevation; and the author’s four-facet definition is a subordinate pastoral synthesis rather than the Church’s exhaustive answer to evil. The chapter also needs a clearer reason–revelation boundary, a guarded account of divine simplicity and sacrifice, a non-circular use of its five criteria, and safer language about redemptive suffering. These changes preserve the chapter’s thesis while locating it more securely in Christ, grace, the Trinity, and the whole Catholic faith.

    CH2-01 · Make the Chapter 1 recap match its corrected moral and social account

    Severity: Major / continuity and pastoral precision · Control: DC-10, DC-15, DC-24, DC-25, DC-26 · Location: chapter_02.md:19

    Issue. The recap repeats Chapter 1’s most inflammatory labels and its claim that evil simply “erupts from human hearts,” then says human solutions fail because they address symptoms rather than the heart. That preserves the false choice between personal conversion and institutional reform that Chapter 1’s redlines correct.

    Why it matters. Chapter 2 should inherit the corrected account, not reintroduce the defect on the next page. It can name moral evil plainly while preserving agency, the interaction of hearts and structures, and the indispensable role of grace.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1735; CCC 1849–1851; CCC 1886–1889.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    In Chapter 1, we stared into the abyss of evil—abortion as contraception, children mutilated by gender ideology, media lies destroying truth, families shattered by fatherlessness. We saw how evil erupts from human hearts that choose selfishness over good. We discovered that human solutions fail because they address symptoms, not the source—the human heart itself. In Chapter 1, we examined moral evil in its personal, relational and social forms. We saw that abuses of freedom, disordered desires, broken relationships and unjust structures all wound communion. We also saw that personal conversion and institutional reform cannot be set against one another: hearts and structures both require healing, and neither is healed without God’s grace.

    Editorial consequence. The transition remains forceful while no longer relying on graphic shorthand or a single-cause theory of evil.

    CH2-02 · Present the three spheres and five criteria as a diagnostic, not an exhaustive proof

    Severity: Major / scope and reasoning · Control: DC-05, DC-15, DC-19, DC-26 · Location: chapter_02.md:27–43

    Issue. “All three spheres” makes an authorial taxonomy sound exhaustive, and the five requirements are selected by the chapter itself. The section then asks which one reality meets them, preparing a circular conclusion. “Spiritual death, despair, meaninglessness, societal collapse” also combines distinct theological, psychological, and political outcomes as though each followed uniformly from every evil.

    Why it matters. These categories are useful if they are honestly identified as a pastoral diagnostic. They cannot, by themselves, prove that one proposed framework is the complete answer to evil.

    Controlling sources: CCC 309–314; CCC 1849–1851; CCC 1886–1889.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    ## What Would an Adequate Solution Require? What Would an Adequate Christian Response Require?

    Any solution to evil must address all three spheres from Chapter 1: Chapter 1 used three overlapping spheres to help us see moral evil’s reach:

    Personal evil: The individual human heart choosing selfishness over good—pride, greed, lust, envy, gluttony, wrath, sloth. Personal sin: free human acts and omissions that offend God, deform the person, and fail in genuine love.

    Relational evil: Broken bonds between persons—betrayal, abandonment, abuse, exploitation, broken promises, shattered trust. Relational harm: betrayal, abandonment, abuse, exploitation, broken promises and shattered trust between persons.

    Social evil: Systemic injustice in communities and civilizations—oppression, exploitation, dehumanization, corruption, violence. Social evil: unjust customs, institutions and structures that are created or sustained by human choices and that, in turn, make further wrongdoing easier.

    Any adequate solution must address all three levels—a fix for individual hearts that leaves relationships broken is incomplete; a reformed social structure without transformed hearts will collapse with the next generation. These spheres are neither sealed off nor reducible to one cause. Personal conversion should bear fruit in repaired relationships and just institutions; institutional reform can restrain harm and help form persons toward the common good.

    But there's a deeper problem: if a solution can't overcome the destruction evil leads to—spiritual death, despair, meaninglessness, societal collapse—it only postpones defeat. A Christian response must also face the deepest consequences of sin and suffering: alienation from God, wounds that human effort cannot fully heal, death itself, and the temptation to despair.

    An adequate solution must transform the human heart from selfishness, heal broken relationships, create "just" social structures, overcome the despair and meaninglessness that evil leads to, and be grounded in objective truth. The solution must be concrete. The response we seek must therefore include conversion by grace, reconciliation, just social action, hope stronger than death, and truth about the human person’s origin and end. It must become concrete in the way we live. These are tests for recognizing partial remedies, not premises from which a private formula can be proved.

    What reality meets all five criteria? What reality gathers these goods together without reducing one to another?

    Editorial consequence. The section keeps its clear architecture while becoming logically modest and doctrinally proportionate.

    CH2-03 · Identify God—not an abstraction called love—as the supreme good

    Severity: Stop-ship / doctrine and philosophical argument · Control: DC-01, DC-07, DC-19, DC-26 · Location: chapter_02.md:45–55

    Issue. The text says every culture and every person gives the same answer—“Love”—to the question of the highest good, then says the Church calls “this” the summum bonum. Aquinas does not conclude in Summa Theologiae I–II, q.2 that love is the last created candidate standing; he argues that no created good constitutes final happiness. The objective supreme good and the final end of the person is God, consummated in the beatific vision. Charity is the greatest theological virtue because it unites us to God.

    Why it matters. “God is love” does not permit the argument to reverse the terms and substitute an ambiguous human abstraction for God. The chapter’s thesis becomes stronger when the ultimate good, the virtue of charity, and ordinary experiences of love are related without being confused.

    Controlling sources: CCC 27; CCC 1023–1029; CCC 1720–1722; CCC 1822–1829; Summa Theologiae I–II, q.2 and q.3, a.8.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    ## The Universal Human Answer The Universal Human Longing and Its Final End

    Ask anyone across cultures and eras: what makes life worth living? What is the highest human good? The answer is always the same: Love. Across cultures and eras, human beings seek happiness through relationships, truth, beauty, virtue, achievement, pleasure and many other goods. Love of family, friends and neighbor often gives life its deepest created meaning, but neither human testimony nor philosophical history yields one uniform answer to the question of our final end.

    Not wealth. Not honor. Not fame. Not power. Not health or beauty. Not pleasure. Not even the goods of our own soul—nor any created good at all. These are the eight limited goods St. Thomas Aquinas examined one by one, asking whether each could bear the weight of human happiness, and found every one of them wanting (Summa Theologiae I-II, q.2). When pressed, every human being points to love: love of family, love of friends, love of beauty, love of truth, love of something or someone beyond ourselves. From Aristotle's philia to the Stoics' universal benevolence to Confucius's ren (benevolence) and the Buddha's metta (loving-kindness)—even traditions whose creeds name another summit could not describe a life worth living without love. We all know, deep in our bones, that love is what we're made for. St. Thomas Aquinas tests wealth, honor, fame, power, bodily goods, pleasure, goods of the soul and finally every created good, asking whether any can bear the full weight of human happiness. None can (Summa Theologiae I–II, q.2). Created loves and goods can be real and profound, but they cannot satisfy the whole person or constitute the beatific end for which we were made.

    The Church calls this the summum bonum—the "highest good" that all human beings seek, whether they know it or not. St. Thomas Aquinas demonstrated what reason discovers: all human action aims at some good, and no created good can satisfy completely—only union with God. And revelation confirms who that God is: Love itself. As St. Augustine expressed: our hearts are restless until they rest in God. The Church identifies God himself as the supreme good and last end of the human person. St. Thomas shows that every deliberate action seeks some perceived good and that final happiness cannot consist in any creature; it is fulfilled only in the vision and enjoyment of God. Revelation then discloses the mystery reason could never penetrate by itself: the one God is Father, Son and Holy Spirit, and “God is love.” Charity is therefore not a rival supreme good beside God. It is the infused theological virtue by which we love God above all things and our neighbor for God’s sake, drawing us into communion with him. As St. Augustine confessed, our hearts remain restless until they rest in God.

    The Catechism affirms this: "The desire for God is written in the human heart, because man is created by God and for God; and God never ceases to draw man to himself. Only in God will he find the truth and happiness he never stops searching for" (CCC 27).

    This is why love—specifically, love that leads to communion with God—is the ultimate good, and therefore the complete answer to evil. This is why the Triune God is our supreme good, why charity is the greatest of the virtues, and why communion with God is the fulfillment of the human longing to love and be loved.

    Editorial consequence. The chapter’s most important philosophical claim now says what the Church and Aquinas actually teach.

    CH2-04 · Use privation theory without reducing every missing good immediately to love

    Severity: Major / metaphysical precision · Control: DC-01, DC-05, DC-19, DC-26 · Location: chapter_02.md:57

    Issue. Privation is the absence or corruption of a good that ought to exist in a subject. The due good may be life, health, sight, truth, justice, fidelity, order, or another perfection. It does not follow directly that the absent proximate good in every instance is “love,” even though rightly ordered love seeks the good and the loss of communion is a central consequence of sin.

    Why it matters. The current syllogism appears to build the answer into its premise. A more exact account can preserve love’s integrating and restorative role without making privation theory say more than it does.

    Controlling sources: CCC 385; CCC 309–314; St. Augustine, Confessions VII.12.18; St. Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I, q.48, a.1.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    And if evil is, at its core, the absence of a good that ought to be there, then the cure for evil is the restoration of the very good that is missing. Chapter 1 showed what is missing in every sphere of evil—personal, relational, social: each one is a communion broken. The absent good is love. This follows from the very logic of privation: where love is lacking, evil takes root; where love is given, evil is undone. Evil is the privation or corruption of a good that ought to be present: truth in speech, justice in a relationship, health in a body, fidelity in a promise, or right order in a human act. Not every proximate privation can simply be renamed “an absence of love.” Yet love rightly understood wills the genuine good, and charity animates the Christian’s work of restoration. Personal sin, relational harm and social injustice all wound communion; God’s reconciling love in Christ heals that communion at its deepest source and sends us to seek the particular goods that have been denied.

    Editorial consequence. The Augustinian argument remains central without becoming circular.

    CH2-05 · Convert the five answers from an elimination proof into a Christian convergence

    Severity: Major / logic and pastoral scope · Control: DC-01, DC-05, DC-06, DC-13, DC-15, DC-19, DC-26 · Location: chapter_02.md:59–73

    Issue. Each question is answered “Yes” by defining authentic love so that it includes the criterion being tested. The chapter then declares love “the only reality” that passes and likens the result to a jury verdict on which a life can be staked. This is not an independent demonstration, and several answers require grace, justice, institutions, prudence, safety, and eschatological hope—not the word “love” by itself.

    Why it matters. The section can show a meaningful convergence without claiming philosophical certainty its premises do not supply. That honesty will make the later revealed and Christological case more credible.

    Controlling sources: CCC 309; CCC 1810–1811; CCC 1822–1829; CCC 1886–1889.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    But does love meet our five criteria for an adequate solution? Read as a diagnostic rather than a proof, does Christian charity illuminate each of these needs?

    1. Transform hearts? Yes—authentic love requires choosing another's good over our preferences, training the heart from selfishness toward self-gift. 1. Conversion of heart: Charity is God’s gift before it becomes our response. Grace heals and elevates freedom, enabling us to turn from sin and will the genuine good of God and neighbor.

    2. Heal relationships? Yes—love creates and restores relationship. Forgiveness, reconciliation, rebuilt trust are all love in action. 2. Healing relationships: Love seeks forgiveness, reconciliation and the rebuilding of trust. Forgiveness does not erase truth, safety, restitution, or the fact that some relationships cannot immediately—or ever—resume their former form.

    3. Create "just" structures? Yes—a society honoring each person's dignity, pursuing justice, tempering judgment with mercy is a just society. Every unjust society has replaced love with exploitation. 3. Reforming structures: Charity requires justice and the common good. Just laws and institutions can restrain harm and protect dignity, while converted persons remain obliged to reform structures that perpetuate injustice.

    4. Overcome darkness? Yes—love addresses isolation, despair, and meaninglessness by offering connection, purpose, and unshakeable hope. 4. Answering despair: Human affection can console, but Christian hope rests finally in Christ crucified and risen, whose victory over sin and death exceeds every earthly remedy.

    5. Grounded in truth? Yes—we cannot truly love while deceiving. Love directly addresses evil's root cause: the heart choosing self over what is truly good. 5. Living in truth: Charity “rejoices in the right” and wills the true good rather than a comforting counterfeit. It does not replace truth, justice, prudence, or grace; it gives Christian moral action its living form and final orientation.

    Love is the only reality in human experience that meets all five criteria. These converging lines explain why charity belongs at the center of the Christian response to evil.

    This is why we can say with philosophical confidence, not just religious faith: The answer to evil is love. Of course we haven't yet defined what love is. We will do that shortly. But notice what we have just done: we lined up the answers the world proposes—politics, education, knowledge, power, pleasure—and tested each against what a solution to evil must actually do. Love is the last one standing. That is not a theorem; it is a verdict—the kind of certainty a jury reaches, and the kind a life can be staked on. Reason can recognize that no merely political, educational, technological, or therapeutic program reaches the whole mystery of evil. It can also recognize the integrating power of love rightly ordered to the good. But the Christian answer does not arise from eliminating competitors on an author-designed test. It comes to fullness in God’s revelation of Jesus Christ and in the whole faith of the Church. The rest of this chapter now asks how the book’s definition can serve that revealed answer.

    Editorial consequence. The chapter retains its persuasive cumulative case while relinquishing a circular proof and an overstated claim of certainty.

    CH2-06 · Distinguish what natural reason can know from the revealed mystery that God is Love

    Severity: Stop-ship / revelation and Trinity · Control: DC-02, DC-19, DC-20, DC-26 · Location: chapter_02.md:75–77, 85–87

    Issue. “Scripture confirms what reason discovers” implies that natural reason independently reaches the revealed proposition “God is love.” The Church teaches that reason can know God as first principle and last end and can know genuine attributes from creation, while God’s inmost Trinitarian life is inaccessible to unaided reason and is freely revealed in the Son and Holy Spirit.

    Why it matters. The phrase “God is love” means more than a philosophical conclusion about benevolence. In its Christian fullness it names the one God whose eternal life is Father, Son and Holy Spirit.

    Controlling sources: CCC 32–38; CCC 50; CCC 221; CCC 237; Dei Verbum 2–6.

    Source passages with proposed inline redline:

    ## Scripture Confirms What Reason Discovers Revelation Brings Reason’s Search to Fulfillment

    The conclusion we've reached through reason aligns perfectly with Scripture. The Bible reveals that "God is love" (1 John 4:8)—not merely something God does, but who God is. Since love is the ultimate good and God is Love, God's Love is the ultimate good and therefore the answer to evil. Natural reason can know from creation that God exists as the first principle and last end of all things, that he is good, and that the moral law is not our invention. Yet reason cannot enter God’s inmost life by its own power. Scripture reveals the mystery: “God is love” (1 John 4:8), and the Son and Holy Spirit disclose that this one God is eternally Father, Son and Holy Spirit. The Christian answer to evil therefore rests not on an abstract property called love, but on the God who reveals and gives himself in Christ.

    What reason discovers, Scripture confirms: God is love. Love is the path to eternal life and the answer to evil. Reason can recognize our orientation toward the good and our need for God; revelation brings that search to fulfillment by disclosing that God is Love and by drawing us through charity into the life of the Trinity.

    Editorial consequence. The chapter honors both natural theology and revelation without collapsing one into the other.

    CH2-07 · Present love as the fulfillment of law and worship, not their opposite

    Severity: Major / Scripture and Old Covenant · Control: DC-16, DC-19, DC-20, DC-27 · Location: chapter_02.md:79–83

    Issue. The Shema and Luke 10 are strong anchors, but the conclusion opposes love to “rules,” “ritual,” and “knowledge.” Scripture instead presents love as the fulfillment and living form of God’s commandments, and Catholic worship as a gift through which covenant communion is enacted. The problem is not law or ritual, but their corruption or separation from faith and charity.

    Why it matters. A contrast between love and ritual unintentionally diminishes Old Covenant worship and the Catholic sacramental life the book later celebrates.

    Controlling sources: Deuteronomy 6:4–5; Luke 10:25–28; Romans 13:8–10; CCC 1965–1974; Dei Verbum 14–16.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    Scripture's consistent message is clear: Love is the apex of biblical teaching. Not rules for their own sake. Not ritual divorced from relationship. Not knowledge without transformation. But love—authentic, complete, perfect love—as the path to eternal life and the answer to every form of evil. Scripture’s consistent message is clear: love of God and neighbor is the living fulfillment of the law. Christ does not oppose love to God’s commandments, covenant worship, or revealed truth; he restores them to their proper center and brings them to fulfillment. Rules severed from the good they serve, ritual emptied of faith, and knowledge without conversion are distortions. Authentic charity keeps God’s commandments and leads us into communion with him and with our neighbor.

    Editorial consequence. The Shema remains the chapter’s scriptural hinge while honoring both Israel’s worship and Catholic sacramental life.

    CH2-08 · Locate the crisis in confused usage, not in the Church losing love’s definition

    Severity: Major / ecclesial and rhetorical precision · Control: DC-01, DC-19, DC-20, DC-26 · Location: chapter_02.md:89–95

    Issue. “We’ve lost the definition” is rhetorically effective but literally suggests that the Church failed to preserve a coherent doctrine of charity. The paragraph then treats “our culture” as a single speaker and says an imprecise definition means we will “never truly know God.”

    Why it matters. The book exists because contemporary usage is confused, not because the deposit of faith lacks an account of love. The author’s definition should clarify and synthesize what the Church teaches, not fill an ecclesial void.

    Controlling sources: CCC 25; CCC 1822–1829; Deus Caritas Est 1–18.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    ## The Crisis: We've Lost the Definition The Crisis: We Use One Word for Different Things

    But here's the crisis: If love is the answer, why is the world still drowning in evil? But here is the crisis: if love is central to the Christian life, why do our appeals to love so often justify opposing choices?

    Because we've lost clarity about what love actually is, using the same word for sexual attraction, ice cream preferences, God's nature, and our favorite TV shows. Our culture has reduced love to feelings, to tolerance, to whatever makes us comfortable. We've been sold a thousand counterfeits, and we can't tell the real thing from the fakes anymore. The Church has not lost the meaning of charity, but ordinary speech uses the same word for sexual attraction, affection, preference, God’s nature and commitment to another’s good. Powerful currents in contemporary culture reduce love to feeling, approval, tolerance, or comfort. Christians can absorb those reductions too. When the word carries incompatible meanings, counterfeits become difficult to recognize.

    This is why we desperately need a precise definition. Not just to win arguments or satisfy intellectual curiosity, but because societies depend on it. Families depend on it. Children depend on it. We need it. If we get this wrong, we'll keep fighting evil with counterfeits that only make things worse. And we will never truly "know" God. This is why we need a precise working account of love. Not to replace the Church’s doctrine or merely to win arguments, but to help us recognize and live what she has received. Societies, families and children are affected by what we call love. If we mistake a counterfeit for the real thing, we obscure our witness to the God whose love we profess.

    Editorial consequence. The section preserves the “dictionary problem” premise while making the Church’s prior teaching explicit.

    CH2-09 · Correct the four-Greek-words simplification and distinguish love from infused charity

    Severity: Major / linguistic and theological sources · Control: DC-07, DC-09, DC-26, DC-28 · Location: chapter_02.md:97–103

    Issue. Greek does not divide love neatly into exactly four non-overlapping words, and the familiar eros/philia/storge/agape scheme is not an exhaustive linguistic taxonomy. The paragraph also says “love gives form to all other virtues,” whereas Aquinas’s precise claim concerns the infused theological virtue of charity.

    Why it matters. A popular teaching device can illustrate distinctions, but it should not become linguistic proof. The charity distinction is essential to the chapter’s natural/supernatural boundary.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1822–1829; St. Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I–II, q.26, a.4; II–II, q.23, a.8; DC-27.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    ## Why Love is Is So Hard to Define

    Throughout history, philosophers and saints have offered profound insights—the Greek language's four distinct words for love—eros, philia, storge, and agape—each naming a different facet; Augustine's teaching that love is the ordering principle of all virtue; Aquinas's definition that "to love is to will the good of another" and that love gives "form" to all other virtues. Each captured essential truths about love's nature. Yet defining love comprehensively has remained challenging. Why? Because love has many different facets—as even the Catechism acknowledges when it says "all the works of perfect Christian virtue spring from love" (CCC 25). Philosophers, saints and theologians have illuminated different dimensions of love. Greek offers several overlapping terms often translated as love—including eros, philia, storge and agape—but the familiar four-word scheme is a useful teaching aid, not an exhaustive map of the language. St. Augustine explored how rightly ordered love shapes the moral life. St. Thomas Aquinas described love as willing good to another and taught more precisely that charity, the infused love of God, gives living form to the Christian virtues. These insights do not leave the Church without a doctrine of love; they show why no brief human formula exhausts the mystery named by the Catechism’s teaching that “all the works of perfect Christian virtue spring from love” (CCC 25).

    Editorial consequence. The paragraph keeps its accessible intellectual history without depending on a popular linguistic myth or misattributing charity’s role to every kind of love.

    CH2-10 · Label the four-facet pattern as the author’s pastoral synthesis

    Severity: Stop-ship / framework status · Control: DC-01, DC-07, DC-09, DC-19, DC-20, DC-21 · Location: chapter_02.md:105

    Issue. “All the virtues integrate and converge into four essential facets” and “support everything else” present the framework as a comprehensive discovery. The present Chapter 13 matrix itself does not say that every particular virtue contains justice and mercy in the same way. The paragraph also implies that prior thinkers saw pieces while this book finally sees the integrated whole.

    Why it matters. A private synthesis can be fruitful and original without functioning as new revelation, a replacement for the Church’s categories, or the hidden completion of Augustine and Aquinas.

    Controlling sources: CCC 65–67; CCC 1804–1829; Dei Verbum 10.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    Through years of prayer, study, and living out love as husband, father, and teacher, I have noticed a pattern: all the virtues integrate and converge into four essential facets of love: truth, justice, mercy and sacrifice. These four are the pillars that seem to support everything else. This explains the centuries-long challenge—each thinker grasped important truths, but seeing how all the virtues simultaneously integrate into this multi-faceted "crazy little thing called love" requires understanding how these four facets work together. Through years of prayer, study, teaching and family life, I have found it pastorally fruitful to contemplate love through four interrelated facets: truth, justice, mercy and sacrifice. I do not offer these as an exhaustive taxonomy, as four parts assembled inside God, or as a claim that every particular virtue contains each facet in the same way. They are a subordinate lens for seeing how authentic love wills the true good, gives what is due, responds to misery, and gives itself for another. Their value must be judged under Scripture, Sacred Tradition and the Magisterium—not the other way around.

    Editorial consequence. The author can claim and use his synthesis confidently without implying inspiration or doctrinal finality.

    CH2-11 · Introduce the canonical definition beneath Christ and the Church’s rule of faith

    Severity: Stop-ship / revelation and framework hierarchy · Control: DC-01, DC-19, DC-20, DC-21 · Location: chapter_02.md:107–113

    Issue. The introduction says that “we can see” perfect love must integrate “all the essential elements” revealed by Scripture and the Church, immediately before presenting the author’s sentence. This makes the definition sound deduced and ecclesially established rather than proposed and submitted.

    Why it matters. Christ is the fullness of revelation, and the Church’s deposit of faith exceeds every pastoral synthesis. The canonical wording can remain exact while its authority is described correctly.

    Controlling sources: CCC 65–67; CCC 84–95; CCC 309; Dei Verbum 10.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    Through careful study of Scripture and the Church's teaching on love, we can see that perfect love cannot be reduced to a single dimension. It must integrate all the essential elements that Scripture and the Church reveal about love's nature. Here is the definition that will guide our entire journey: Scripture and the Church’s teaching show that charity cannot be reduced to sentiment, approval, or any single moral impulse. The sentence below is my proposed pastoral synthesis of that teaching. It does not replace the Gospel, define the divine essence exhaustively, or bind the Church; it will serve as the book’s working lens and remain accountable to the faith the Church has received:

    God's Perfect Love is the sacred gift of covenant which binds truth, justice, mercy and sacrifice into life-giving communion.

    Editorial consequence. The canonical definition remains word-for-word while its subordinate status becomes unmistakable before the reader adopts it.

    CH2-12 · Guard divine simplicity, the Trinity, and the economic meaning of sacrifice

    Severity: Stop-ship / doctrine of God and Trinity · Control: DC-02, DC-03, DC-04, DC-27 · Location: chapter_02.md:115
