    Why the doctrinal edits

    The Buddhist/atheist paragraph (line 121). The old wording said "God's perfect justice gives each person what they are due… (CCC 1257, 1260; Lumen Gentium 16)," but none of those texts says that. The new wording uses the Church's own sentences: salvation always comes from Christ through His Body (CCC 846); God "is not bound by his sacraments" (CCC 1257); those who through no fault of their own do not know the Gospel "may achieve eternal salvation" (CCC 847, from LG 16). It keeps the Council's warning and the duty to evangelize (LG 16; CCC 848), so it cannot be read as "the Church is optional."

    The natural law and the New Law (line 145). The chapter called the law written on every heart "the Law of the Gospel." The Church distinguishes the two. The natural law is written on every heart by creation (CCC 1954–1956). The New Law is "the grace of the Holy Spirit given to the faithful through faith in Christ" (CCC 1966), and it is "the perfection" of the natural law (CCC 1965). The edit keeps your whole argument and simply says Christ brings the first law to its fullness.

    Euthyphro (line 85). Chapter 3 already gives the simplicity answer (God does not conform to the good or pick it; He is it). The one sentence added is the Catholic link the chapter was missing: the natural law is our share in God's eternal law (Summa I-II q.91 a.2). That is how the good in us is grounded in God's nature and not in an arbitrary command.

    Unbelief (line 131). "Not because the evidence is insufficient, but because 'they suppress the truth'" made every unbeliever culpable, and that contradicts the "no fault of their own" eight paragraphs earlier. Now it says both.

    Line 125. "In God's perfect love, they integrate perfectly" spoke of God as if He had parts in balance (todo 61). Now: in Him they are one.

    The crucial question: If these facets are built into us—if even atheists cannot deny their pull—how did they get there? We did not invent them. We discovered them—the way we discovered the laws that hold the stars together and the mathematics our minds are uncannily fitted to read: already there, already binding, waiting to be found.     Euthyphro · accurate source; chapter subject; the Catholic answer through natural law

    Section: The Divine Command Theory Problem · line 85

    There's a famous objection to this argument, going back to adapted from a question Socrates asks in Plato's Euthyphro dialogue: is something good because God commands it, or does God command it because it's good? Chapter 3, on love's sacred source, already answered it: goodness is neither a standard above God nor an invention of His will. It is His own unchanging nature. God's commands do not create the good; they reveal the Good that He eternally is. And the moral law in our hearts is a share in that same Good: St. Thomas Aquinas calls the natural law "the rational creature's participation of the eternal law" (Summa Theologiae I-II, q.91, a.2), that is, of God's own wisdom ordering all things, imprinted on the minds He made.

    And notice: we don't recognize this authority by argument, the way we might infer some distant lawgiver to explain a law. We live inside it. Chapter 1, on the problem of evil, already named the pattern — even : sin is "choosing a perceived good for ourselves at the expense of others lesser good over the greatest Good," never a choice of no good at all.     Why only God · Aquinas quoted as he wrote it

    Section: Why Only God Can Ground Love · line 99

    And here St. Thomas Aquinas hands us one more treasure, in a single sentence: our love for anything "is not the cause of the goodness of things... whereas its goodness," whereas "the love of God infuses and creates goodness" (Summa Theologiae I, q.20, a.2). Read that again. Your love responds to goodness—you love your child because she is precious. God's love creates goodness—your child is precious because His love called her into being and holds her in being right now. His love is not an opinion about her that could have been otherwise; it is the act by which there is a her at all. Creatures confer value on what they find; the Creator's love is why there is anything to find. Her dignity is truly her own, woven into what she is: rational, free, made for communion with Him. And it is truly a gift, because what she is, is His gift. This is why the Church can teach that every person's dignity "is not conferred subsequently... it is prior to any recognition, and it cannot be lost" (Dignitas Infinita, 15). No one bestowed it on her—not her parents, not the state, not even her own achievements—and so no one can revoke it. The Love that grounds her worth is not anyone's opinion of her. It is the creative Love that is the reason there is a her at all.

    Natural law · the Buddhist/atheist question in the Church's own words (CCC 846–848, 1257; LG 16)

    Section: Natural Law: God's Moral Order Written on Hearts · line 121

    This raises a question worth facing directly: if the law of love is written on every heart, can someone who follows that light — a Buddhist, an atheist, a person who never heard the Gospel — be saved? The Church's answer is careful and honest. The four pillars of love All salvation comes from Christ: "the one Christ is the mediator and the way of salvation; he is present to us in his body which is the Church" (CCC 846). Truth, justice, mercy and sacrifice are not a series of tasks that save by completion; they are the shape of love as God designed it. And the fullest, most precisely articulated teaching on what love requires is found in the Catholic Church — including the doctrinal clarity that tells a soul exactly when love has been broken and exactly how it is restored. But God's grace is not imprisoned by human ignorance. This follows the same logic as the sacraments: God binds us to the sacraments as the ordinary means of grace — but God is not Himself bound by them. He can pour grace through any channel He chooses. Those who follow the light they have been given — the natural law, the grace active in conscience, the partial truth written on their hearts — "God has bound salvation to the sacrament of Baptism, but he himself is not bound by his sacraments" (CCC 1257). And so the Church teaches that "those who, through no fault of their own, do not know the Gospel of Christ or his Church, but who nevertheless seek God with a sincere heart, and, moved by grace, try in their actions to do his will as they know it through the dictates of their conscience — those too may achieve eternal salvation" (CCC 847, quoting Lumen Gentium 16). Such people are, in a real sense, doing the will of the Father, even without yet knowing the Father's name. God's perfect justice gives each person what they are due, accounting for what was offered to them and not what was withheld through no fault of their own (CCC 1257, 1260; , and whatever grace reaches them is Christ's grace. The Council is just as honest that "often men, deceived by the Evil One, have become vain in their reasonings" (Lumen Gentium 16). But none , and the Church "still has the obligation and also the sacred right to evangelize all men" (CCC 848). None of this cancels the necessity of the Church — it only deepens the urgency of bringing the fullness to those who are already responding to its shadow.
