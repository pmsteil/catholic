All four “unity without” paragraphs now lead with everyday family examples, both for the pillar missing and for the pillar pushed alone. The over-critical father illustrates justice without mercy, and the mother who covers for her son illustrates mercy without justice. A new short paragraph after the four names the father/mother pattern and grounds it in CCC 2333 on the complementarity of man and woman (vatican.va, cached). One caution, said honestly: the Church teaches complementarity, but she does not assign justice to fathers and mercy to mothers. So the text says “leans” and “part of how God made us,” not that this is the design itself, and it ends with God the Father, in whom justice and mercy are one. The cartel, the insiders and the propaganda nation are gone.

Unity without truth

Section: Unity Requires Submission to the Authority of His Perfect Love · line 294

A unity without truth is a lie agreed upon. Consider the family that keeps the peace by never naming the father's drinking, the nation "unified" by propaganda husband and wife who stopped arguing only because they stopped telling each other what they really think, the parish that papers over a grave error to avoid an awkward conversation. On the surface it looks like harmony. In reality it is only the shared suppression of what everyone knows—and suppressed reality sooner or later resurfaces, usually with interest. A communion built on a comfortable lie is a house built over a sinkhole. Yet notice that truth cannot stand alone either: truth . Truth without mercy hardens into cold , accusing orthodoxy that accusation: the parent who is always right and never kind, until the children stop confiding at all; the parish whose orthodoxy drives wounded people out the door. …

Unity without justice

Section: Unity Requires Submission to the Authority of His Perfect Love · line 296

… Where some members are treated unfairly—denied the fair pay, honest dealing, respect or care that justice says is theirs—what looks like communion is really some people using others: the "unity" of a cartel, of insiders who cover for one another, of a mother who cooks, cleans, drives and remembers every birthday while the family treats it as simply her job; the one sibling who cares for an aging parent alone while the others come for holidays; the child whose gifts are barely noticed beside a favored brother's; the marriage in which one spouse's needs are always sacrificed to the other's comfort. Such arrangements feel stable precisely because the cost is being silently absorbed by the powerless. But a body cannot be one while quietly devouring its own weaker members; the moment they can leave, they often do. And justice, too, fails on its own: pressed without mercy, it curdles into relentless score-keeping that no real relationship survives. Think of the controlling, over-critical father whose rules are never wrong and whose approval is never given: his children obey while they must, and leave as soon as they can.

Unity without mercy

Section: Unity Requires Submission to the Authority of His Perfect Love · line 298

… Every actual communion is made of sinners who wound one another—so without forgiveness, every offense becomes a permanent crack, and the cracks only multiply. The family that will not forgive hardens into estrangement; the : the brothers who have not spoken since their father's funeral, the daughter still punished for a mistake she made at sixteen. The community that cannot absolve becomes a courtroom where old grievances are re-tried forever. Mercy is what allows imperfect people to remain one across years of friction. But mercy untethered from truth and justice degenerates into mere enabling— : the mother who pays her grown son's debts again and again, covers for him with his father, and never lets him face what he has done. That is a false peace that pronounces the wound healed while it festers, and so betrays the very people it claims to love.

Unity without sacrifice

Section: Unity Requires Submission to the Authority of His Perfect Love · line 300

… It holds only as long as belonging costs nothing. Friendships that evaporate when the price rises, marriages that dissolve the moment "my needs aren't being met," parents who give their children everything except their time, congregations that scatter under the first real pressure—these reveal that comfort was the only glue. Communion is held together instead by people willing to bear one another's burdens (Galatians 6:2), to absorb a cost rather than pass it downstream. Sacrifice is the love that shows the bond is real precisely when keeping it hurts. But sacrifice without truth and justice is squandered—poured out for a lie, or extracted from the weak by the strong, which is not love at all. The mother who gives and gives while no one in the house is ever asked to give anything back is not building communion; she is wearing herself out, and the family learns to take.¶Many families know the pattern behind these examples: a father who leans toward justice, a mother who leans toward mercy. This is part of how God made us. The Church teaches that the "difference and complementarity" of man and woman "are oriented toward the goods of marriage and the flourishing of family life" (CCC 2333). Each needs the other. His justice without her mercy crushes a child; her mercy without his justice spoils one. Held together, they show a child what God's love looks like, for in our Father in heaven, justice and mercy are one.
