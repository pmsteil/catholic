The Hebrew Scriptures use two main words for justice: Mishpat and Tsedeqah Tsedaqah. Mishpat appears over 400 times in the Old Testament, meaning right judgment, legal decision, and vindication of the oppressed—it's the justice of the courtroom, ensuring fair treatment. Tsedeqah Tsedaqah means righteousness, right relationships, and covenant faithfulness—it's the justice of restored communion. When the prophets cry "Let justice (mishpat) roll down like waters, and righteousness (tsedeqah) like an ever-flowing stream" (Amos 5:24), they're calling for both: fair treatment AND restored relationships. Together, they reveal that biblical justice always aims at healing and restoration, not mere avoidance of punishment merely at the punishment of wrong.

Gratitude: why worship is owed (todo 40)

line 117

… To render God worship, gratitude, and obedience is not a preference piled on top of justice — it is the highest act of justice a creature can make. ¶Why is worship owed at all? Not first because it is commanded, but because of what we have received. We saw in Chapter 6 that we did not have to exist, and that we are alive, and the rock is not. Everything we are was given before we could ask for it: "For who sees anything different in you? What have you that you did not receive? If then you received it, why do you boast as if it were not a gift?" (1 Corinthians 4:7). And the gift goes deeper than we first notice. To give thanks at all, we must be able to know a gift as a gift and to answer the Giver freely. A rock cannot be grateful, and no one could command it to be. So even our power to give thanks was itself given to us. This is why justice toward God can never be settled. St. Thomas observes that "whatever man renders to God is due, yet it cannot be equal, as though man rendered to God as much as he owes Him" (ST II-II, q.80, a.1). What can never be repaid in equal measure can only be answered, and gratitude answers it by becoming worship. The Catechism describes adoration in exactly these terms: to acknowledge "the 'nothingness of the creature' who would not exist but for God," and to praise Him, "confessing with gratitude that he has done great things and holy is his name" (CCC 2097).
