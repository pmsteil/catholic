To say " Many Christians sum up the gospel in one line: Jesus died for our sins " is true, but to stop there so that we can be forgiven and go to heaven. Every word of that is true, and it is apostolic. St. Paul handed it on as the heart of the gospel: "For I delivered to you as of first importance what I also received, that Christ died for our sins in accordance with the scriptures, that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day in accordance with the scriptures," (1 Corinthians 15:3–4). But the Church has never shrunk the gospel to one line. The Catechism opens with the whole story: God "freely created man to make him share in his own blessed life. For this reason, at every time and in every place, God draws close to man" (CCC 1). The Church calls four whole books the Gospels, not four sentences, because "Christ's whole life is a mystery of redemption. Redemption comes to us above all through the blood of his cross, but this mystery is at work throughout Christ's entire life" (CCC 517). His teaching, His healings, the Kingdom He proclaimed, the Church He founded: all of it is the good news. To stop at the one line is to grab one dimension of the Cross and let the rest fall to the floor. The Cross is total consecration and atonement and reparation and priestly mediation and substitution and liberation and communion and consummation: all in one integrated act of perfect love. "When Jesus had received the vinegar, he said, 'It is finished'; and he bowed his head and gave up his spirit" (John 19:30). The Church's Latin Bible renders His words Consummatum est: love given all the way to its end. It is the Bridegroom's total gift of Himself to His Bride (cf. Ephesians 5:25). A marriage images that gift when husband and wife become one flesh in the marital act, which the Church calls the marriage's consummation (cf. Code of Canon Law, canon 1061). The priest, acting in the person of Christ, makes it present at the altar (cf. CCC 1548). And in the Eucharist, the wedding feast of Christ and His Church (cf. CCC 1617), we receive it whole. God spent the whole history of His covenants, from Adam to Noah, to Abraham, to Moses, to David, forming His people through covenant after covenant and the offerings and sacrifices that went with them, precisely so that, when His Son hung upon the tree, His people would have eyes to see all of what was happening. Even the names of the Bible's two halves say so: testament is the old word for covenant, so the Old and New Testaments are the story of the Old Covenant and the New (see Chapter 6). The Catholic faith has always held that full breadth, and the Mass is where that breadth becomes present to us, every day, in every parish on earth. …
