    Chapter 4 · Gift of Covenant: Love's Willing Submission

    Chapter assessment

    Chapter 4 has a sound Catholic center: God freely binds his people to himself through covenant; the New Covenant is fulfilled in Christ; valid consummated sacramental marriage is indissoluble; marital fidelity images Christ’s fidelity to the Church; and freedom reaches maturity in willing the good. Its strongest material is the scriptural sequence from Abraham through the Eucharistic words of institution, the recognition that nullity is not Catholic divorce, and the effort to include consecrated and single vocations. The chapter’s governing contrast, however, is too absolute. Contracts are portrayed as temporary, selfish and disposable, while covenants are portrayed as unconditional, without duties or consequences, and extended to parenthood, friendship and nearly every relationship. Biblical covenants contain commands, blessings, sanctions and the real possibility of human breach; contracts can be just and socially necessary. The same binary distorts authority: legitimate human authority participates in God’s authority and can bind even when resisted, while divine authority commands and judges as well as invites. The marriage section then turns fidelity into “no escape clauses” without safety language, calls marriage a sacred necessity, makes it the fullest fulfillment of every baptismal vocation, assigns rigid sex roles, and applies the book’s canonical definition directly to marriage as though marriage were God’s Perfect Love. The following redlines retain the chapter’s covenantal vision while restoring doctrine on grace, authority, vocation, marriage, separation, nullity, human dignity and historical evidence.

    CH4-01 · Carry Chapter 3’s corrected Trinitarian language into the opening

    Severity: Stop-ship / continuity and Trinity · Control: DC-02, DC-03, DC-07 · Location: chapter_04.md:25

    Issue. “The eternal exchange of gift between Father, Son, and Spirit” imports Chapter 3’s social-exchange language after that chapter’s redline has distinguished the one divine essence and operation from relations among three separate agents. It also makes “gift” sound like an object passed among the Persons.

    Why it matters. A corrected Chapter 4 cannot reopen the Trinitarian defect in its first sentence. Covenant belongs to God’s free economy with creatures, not to the relations that constitute the immanent Trinity.

    Controlling sources: CCC 232–260; CCC 257–260; DC-02; DC-03.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    In Chapter 3, we saw that love originates in God—the eternal exchange of gift between Father, Son, and Spirit the one God who is eternally Father, Son and Holy Spirit, perfect communion without division of essence, will or operation. But how does that eternal pattern translate to our temporal, fragile human relationships? And how does God freely reveal his fidelity to creatures through covenant? How can finite beings make permanent promises when everything around us changes?

    Editorial consequence. The opening remains continuous with Chapter 3’s thesis and its needed doctrinal guardrails.

    CH4-02 · Replace the culture-war binary with a precise account of freedom and commitment

    Severity: Major / rhetoric, contracts and universality · Control: DC-12, DC-16, DC-26 · Location: chapter_04.md:27–39

    Issue. “We want love but worship personal freedom” assigns one interior motive to modern people, and the callout makes contracts conditional and temporary by definition and covenants unconditional by definition. Contracts may be long-term, just and other-serving; covenants contain duties and can be violated by their human parties. “I’m yours, no matter what” also lacks an immediate safety qualification.

    Why it matters. The chapter needs a distinction between forms of commitment, not a contrast between bad contracts and idealized covenants. Freedom is not the enemy of covenant; free consent makes marriage, while objective duties govern it thereafter.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1730–1748; CCC 1601–1666; CCC 1950–1953; DC-12; DC-16.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    This is the crisis of modern love: We want love but worship personal freedom. We crave commitment but demand escape clauses. We long for covenant but settle for contracts. One crisis of modern love is the tendency to treat freedom as the absence of durable obligation. Yet freedom and commitment are not opposites: a covenant can begin in free consent and then establish real duties that do not fluctuate with feeling. Contracts also serve legitimate goods; the problem begins when a transactional measure is treated as the whole meaning of a personal bond.

    Love requires covenant, not contract. Contracts are conditional and temporary—"I'll stay as long as you meet my needs." Covenants are unconditional and permanent—"I'm yours, no matter what." Without covenant, love has no foundation to endure. Certain forms of love are covenantal, especially God’s covenant with his people and marriage. A covenant establishes a faithful bond with enduring promises, duties and consequences; it is not simply an unconditional feeling. A contract orders an exchange through justice and may be temporary or enduring. Love is distorted when persons are reduced to contractual benefits, but covenant and contract are not moral opposites.

    Editorial consequence. The chapter’s central distinction becomes defensible and no longer prejudges every later application.

    CH4-03 · Describe children and divorce without universalizing trauma or erasing justified protection

    Severity: Stop-ship / pastoral safety and evidence · Control: DC-23, DC-24, DC-25, DC-26 · Location: chapter_04.md:41–53

    Issue. The passage says every child assumes parental permanence, every divorce breaks the covenant in the same way, every child grieves, the child’s world crumbles, and every future relationship is shaped. It does not distinguish divorce following marital abandonment or abuse, civil divorce sought for legal protection, physical separation while the bond endures, nullity, or the wide range of children’s responses.

    Why it matters. Children can be deeply wounded by family rupture, but deterministic language burdens divorced and separated families and can pressure victims to remain in danger. The Church permits separation and, when necessary, civil divorce to secure rights or protection.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1649; CCC 2382–2386; Amoris Laetitia 241–246; DC-25; DC-26.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    "Mommy and Daddy are getting divorced."

    No words need to follow. The action itself speaks louder than anything that could be said. The covenant is being broken. For many children, those words announce a painful rupture in family life. They do not, by themselves, tell the whole moral story: one spouse may have abandoned the marriage, separation may be needed for safety, civil divorce may secure legal protection, or a tribunal may later determine that no valid bond arose.

    Consider what a child assumes about family before anyone teaches them anything. A child doesn't hope their parents will stay together—they assume it. Mother and father together isn't a wish or a preference—it's the default, the given, the foundation upon which their entire world is built. It would never occur to a child that parents could separate—unless society has already normalized it, unless they've seen it happen to friends, unless the unthinkable has become thinkable. And notice what normalization actually changes: the child's belief, never the child's wound. A child can be taught to expect divorce; no child has ever been taught to stop grieving one. The constancy of the wound—in every culture, under every arrangement—is the witness. Children commonly depend upon the stability of their family and may reasonably expect their parents to remain faithful. Separation or divorce can bring grief, confusion, relief, divided loyalties or a mixture of responses, especially where violence or chronic conflict preceded it. Social normalization does not remove the need for faithful parenting, truthful explanation and attentive care, but neither does every child experience the same wound in the same way.

    This is remarkable: children instinctively assume that their parents' union is permanent. No one teaches them this. They don't learn it from books or conversations. It is the same witness we met in Chapter 3—not a proof, but a testimony—and what it testifies to is covenant permanence written on the human heart. They know it because their parents—united in one flesh—are meant to image God's own covenant love. They know it because it is intertwined with the most basic truth that their existence originated through Mommy and Daddy. Breaking the marriage rips out this most fundamental ground and origin of their existence. This common expectation is testimony, not proof. It points toward the real goods of marital fidelity and stable care. A family rupture can shake a child’s felt security, but it cannot erase the truth of the child’s origin, dignity, or continuing relationship to both parents; adults must never make the child carry blame for the rupture.

    And when that covenant breaks? When the unthinkable becomes real? The child's world doesn't just change—it crumbles. The foundation they stood on gives way beneath their feet. When family life changes, a child needs assurance, continuity and protection. Some wounds are grave; some children also experience safety after danger. Each deserves care appropriate to the actual circumstances.

    This wound runs deep precisely because children know what love should be—they knew it in their souls before anyone taught them. And now they've learned something far more devastating: that covenants can be broken, that "forever" has conditions, that even the most fundamental promise—the union of mother and father—can be revoked when love gets hard. This shapes how they approach every future promise, every relationship, every vow. If the most basic covenant of their existence could be broken, why would any promise be different? Marital failure can affect a child’s later trust, but it does not determine the child’s future. With grace, stable relationships and competent help, children can heal and learn that another person’s failure does not make fidelity impossible.

    Editorial consequence. The section keeps its advocacy for children while becoming factually modest and safe for families affected by abuse, separation, divorce or nullity.

    CH4-04 · Define covenant and contract without caricature

    Severity: Stop-ship / covenant theology and moral reasoning · Control: DC-16, DC-26, DC-30 · Location: chapter_04.md:55–67

    Issue. Contracts are defined as selfish, temporary and relationshipless; covenants as total, unconditional, permanent and unbreakable. A just contract is an agreement ordered by commutative justice and can serve persons and relationships. Biblical covenants establish mutual relations with promises, commands, signs, blessings and sanctions; human beings can violate them even when God remains faithful.

    Why it matters. The false binary makes later reasoning circular and creates pastoral danger when “unconditional” is heard as tolerating abuse or removing all consequences.

    Controlling sources: Genesis 17:1–14; Exodus 19–24; Deuteronomy 28–30; Jeremiah 31:31–34; CCC 153–165; CCC 1601–1666.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    Our culture has forgotten the difference between covenant and contract. This confusion destroys families, fractures communities, and leaves a generation terrified of commitment. Covenant and contract are distinct forms of obligation, and confusing or moralizing the distinction can obscure both.

    Contracts are based on mutual benefit and conditions. They say, "I'll do this if you do that." They are temporary and self-interested, asking "what's in it for me?" And they are breakable—if you violate the terms, I'm out. They create business arrangements but not relationships. usually specify an exchange, service, office or shared undertaking through enforceable terms. They may be temporary or enduring, and a just contract can protect persons, relationships and the common good. Breach does not automatically release every party from every duty; law and justice determine the remedy.

    Covenants are based on mutual gift. They say, "I give myself to you completely." They are unconditional ("no matter what happens") and permanent ("until death"). They are other-centered ("what can I give you?") and unbreakable ("even if you fail, I remain faithful"). They create family bonds. establish a relationship through solemn promises and obligations. In Scripture they include divine initiative, human response, commands, signs, blessings and consequences. God’s fidelity is indefectible, but human parties can violate covenant. Marriage is a covenant ordered by its nature to permanence; Baptism establishes an indelible bond with Christ and the Church. Not every covenant has identical terms or duration.

    When we treat marriage as contract a mere transaction, we constantly evaluate: "Are you meeting my needs? Holding up your end? Is this still worth it?" The moment cost-benefit analysis tips negative, we're out.

    But covenant says: "I'm yours. Period. Not because you're perfect, make me happy, or it's easy. Because I gave myself to you, and that gift is irrevocable. I gave myself to you in a bond that does not disappear when feeling changes. Fidelity still includes truth, justice, repentance, protection of the vulnerable and the consequences of grave wrongdoing."

    This isn't oppression—it's freedom. The freedom of knowing someone is committed regardless of your performance, appearance, success, or failures. It is never permission to coerce, abuse or conceal danger.

    Editorial consequence. The distinction now illuminates marriage without falsifying contract law, biblical covenant or pastoral safety.

    CH4-05 · Define freedom as power for the good, not only total self-gift

    Severity: Major / moral theology and quotation control · Control: DC-12, DC-28 · Location: chapter_04.md:69–73

    Issue. The paragraph says true freedom is “the capacity to give yourself totally to another,” which is one exalted use of freedom but not its exhaustive definition. It also places a period after a quotation mark in American style incorrectly and presents “freedom exists for the sake of love” as a direct quotation without a page or edition.

    Why it matters. Freedom is rooted in reason and will and perfected by choosing the true good. Total personal self-gift belongs especially to particular vocations; ordinary duties, contemplation and legitimate self-care also exercise freedom.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1730–1748; Gaudium et Spes 17, 24; DC-12; DC-28.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    Karol Wojtyła—the future Pope Saint John Paul II—taught that "freedom exists for the sake of love" freedom is ordered toward love (Love and Responsibility), not for autonomy. Our culture says freedom means no commitments, no obligations. "I can do whatever I want".. But that's isolation, not freedom. Culture says freedom begins and ends with me. "I" can do whatever "I" want. What is important is me.

    True freedom is the capacity to give yourself totally to another. True freedom is the rational capacity to choose and act for the good; grace heals and perfects it. One of its highest expressions is sincere self-gift according to one’s vocation. As the Second Vatican Council taught—in a line John Paul II never tired of quoting—man "cannot fully find himself except through a sincere gift of himself" (Gaudium et Spes 24). Covenant doesn't restrict abolish freedom—it fulfills can give freedom an enduring form ordered to the good.

    Editorial consequence. The section remains personalist without converting one vocational expression into a complete definition of freedom.

    CH4-06 · Qualify the biblical covenant sequence and its typology

    Severity: Major / Scripture and source accuracy · Control: DC-16, DC-19, DC-20, DC-28 · Location: chapter_04.md:75–95

    Issue. The Adamic covenant is a legitimate theological reading but Genesis 1–3 does not explicitly use the term. The Noahic promise concerns destruction of all flesh by flood, not every destruction of the earth. The Genesis 15 theophany is made to mean God assumes Abraham’s obligation “even if Abraham fails,” and Davidic endurance is left without its fulfillment in Christ. “Each covenant builds” also needs the Catholic language of preparation, continuity, fulfillment and transformation rather than a simple linear ladder.

    Why it matters. The typology is useful, but a bishop-facing text must distinguish the biblical text from later theological synthesis and avoid suggesting that covenant has no human requirements.

    Controlling sources: Genesis 6:18; 9:8–17; 15; 17; Exodus 19–24; 2 Samuel 7; Luke 1:32–33; CCC 54–64; Dei Verbum 14–16.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    God doesn't relate to us through contracts a merely negotiated exchange. He relates through covenant. And every covenant in Scripture reveals something essential about love's nature God’s saving plan.

    As St. Augustine taught: "The New Testament is concealed in the Old, and the Old Testament is revealed in the New."[^1] Each covenant builds on the previous one, progressively revealing God's plan of love: Read in the unity of Scripture, the covenants prepare for and find their fulfillment in Christ, while each has its own commands, signs and historical setting:

    Adamic Covenant (Genesis 1-3): God creates humanity in His image, calling us to communion and stewardship. Even after the Fall, He promises redemption. Although Genesis 1–3 does not explicitly use the word “covenant,” Catholic and patristic theology can read creation, command, communion and the promise after the Fall as the primordial covenantal setting. Label this as theological synthesis rather than an explicit biblical title.

    Noahic Covenant (Genesis 9): God covenants with all creation, promising never to destroy the earth again destroy all flesh by the waters of a flood. The rainbow is the sign of His faithful love.

    Abrahamic Covenant (Genesis 15, 17): God promises to be Abraham's God and make his descendants a great nation. God walks through the sacrifice alone (Genesis 15:17)—taking on Himself the obligation even if Abraham fails. In the theophany of the smoking fire pot and flaming torch, God confirms his promise through the covenant rite; the scene emphasizes divine initiative without eliminating Abraham’s later covenant obligations.

    Mosaic Covenant (Exodus 19-24): God gives the Law as blueprint for covenant relationship: "I will be your God, and you shall be my people." The Law shows covenant love in practice. Israel can violate the covenant, yet God’s saving purpose and fidelity endure.

    Davidic Covenant (2 Samuel 7): God promises David an eternal kingdom. Even when David's descendants fail spectacularly, God remains faithful. The covenant endures because it's grounded in God's faithfulness, not human performance. The promise survives human infidelity and is fulfilled definitively in Jesus Christ, the Son of David.

    New Covenant (Jeremiah 31, Luke 22): God writes His law on our hearts, forgives our sins, becomes our God. Sealed not with animal blood but Christ's blood—the ultimate sacrifice making permanent relationship possible the unique sacrifice of the New Covenant by which Christ reconciles us to the Father and communicates the Spirit.

    See the pattern? God initiates. God commits. God remains faithful even when we fail. This is what covenant love looks like—not "I'll love you if you perform," but "I'll love you, period." grace that precedes our response, commands that order us to life, and mercy that remains faithful even when human beings violate the covenant.

    Editorial consequence. The sequence remains accessible while the text, typology, human response and Christological fulfillment remain distinct.

    CH4-07 · Say that parents procreate while God creates each spiritual soul

    Severity: Stop-ship / creation and family · Control: DC-04, DC-16, DC-23, DC-24 · Location: chapter_04.md:97–99

    Issue. Parents are said to create children, author their lives and thereby enter a family covenant. Catholic teaching says parents cooperate with God through procreation; every spiritual soul is immediately created by God. The parent–child relationship carries natural, moral and legal duties but is not simply the same kind of covenant as marriage or Baptism. “Without family, there is no existence” is literally false for orphaned, abandoned and technologically conceived persons.

    Why it matters. Creaturely cooperation must not be confused with divine creation, and no person’s dignity may seem contingent on having been welcomed into an intact family.

    Controlling sources: CCC 355–366; CCC 2201–2209; CCC 2366–2379; Dignitas Personae 4–10.

    Source paragraphs with proposed inline redline:

    Why is covenant so central to love? Because covenant is directly tied to the truth of our very existence and our need for love to sustain our souls. God is our Creator—the same way our parents create us and create the biological reality of the family. This obviously mirrors God's covenant with us because He created us. God could have caused us to be born in an egg, totally isolated in the desert or any myriad of ways that don't involve being born into the loving arms of a mother, father, extended family, etc. We ought not take this for granted. God’s free initiative reveals that covenant is first a gift. Human generation offers an important but analogical image: a child receives life through a mother and father who cooperate with God, while God alone immediately creates the child’s spiritual soul. The child’s dependence gives parents grave duties of care; it does not make parenthood identical to God’s covenant with his people.

    Instead He created us to be cared for by our family. The family covenant reflects the divine covenant because both flow from the act of creation itself. When parents bring a child into existence, they enter a covenant relationship grounded in the very fact that they (most immediately, but not on their own) authored that child's life. And as that child grows up, he learns how to love his family and eventually how to love his own family. This is why covenant love is inseparable from the truth of our existence. Without family, there is no existence. Without our existence there is no family. God ordinarily entrusts children to the care of a family. By procreating, parents assume primordial and enduring responsibilities toward a person whose dignity comes from God and is never lost through abandonment, adoption, infertility or an irregular circumstance of conception. The Christian family can image covenant fidelity, but “covenant” should not replace the distinct natural duties proper to every parent–child bond.

    Editorial consequence. The family analogy remains strong without confusing procreation, creation, dignity or kinds of bond.

    CH4-08 · Present the New Law as grace without opposing interior life to law or contract

    Severity: Major / grace, law and sacramental theology · Control: DC-06, DC-16, DC-19 · Location: chapter_04.md:101–107

    Issue. “Not external legislation—interior transformation” sets the New Covenant against external law too sharply. The New Law is principally the grace of the Holy Spirit, but it also includes evangelical teaching and ecclesial precepts. The next paragraph makes contracts fragile because they require enforcement and capitalizes “Grace” as though it were simply the book’s Perfect Love.

    Why it matters. Interior grace and objective norm are complementary. Grace precedes and enables free cooperation; external law and just enforcement remain necessary in a wounded social world.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1965–1974; CCC 1987–2005; CCC 2008–2011.

    Source paragraphs with proposed inline redline:

    The New Covenant's difference: "I will put my law within them, and I will write it upon their hearts" (Jeremiah 31:33). Not external legislation—interior transformation. The New Law is principally the grace of the Holy Spirit given through faith in Christ; it does not abolish objective teaching but fulfills and interiorizes it. God doesn't just tell us what to do; He changes who we are.

    This is why covenant creates permanent relationship while contracts remain fragile. Contracts depend on external enforcement. But Covenant permanence does not arise merely from interior feeling, and just contracts are not defective because law can enforce them. In the New Covenant, when God's law is written on your heart, you follow it because you recognize it as true and freely cooperate with His perfect love (Grace) grace. The Holy Spirit transforms you from within, enabling you to freely embrace what God freely offers. As the Catechism teaches, through the New Covenant we are no longer merely servants but "friends" of God, adopted as "sons" and made "heirs" of His kingdom (CCC 1972).

    Editorial consequence. The section preserves its powerful interiority without setting grace against law, institution or justice.

    CH4-09 · Ground legitimate authority in participation, office and the common good—not an author pun

    Severity: Stop-ship / authority · Control: DC-14, DC-19, DC-27 · Location: chapter_04.md:109–121

    Issue. “True authority is inherent only to the author of the laws” denies the real authority of parents, bishops, teachers and civil rulers. The authority/author wordplay is not an etymological or theological proof. The callout then says authority becomes active only when the subject freely submits, which is false: a legitimate command can bind in conscience even when resisted.

    Why it matters. Catholic teaching calls created authority a participation in God’s providential order. Its legitimacy depends on office, competence, right reason, the common good and morally licit means—not on authorship or the subject’s acceptance.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1884; CCC 1897–1904; CCC 2197–2257; Romans 13:1–7; DC-14; DC-27.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    True authority is inherent only to the "author" of the laws. All legitimate authority has its ultimate source in God, but God communicates real, participated authority to persons and institutions for the common good.

    For example, if you decide to write a novel, as the author, you have the authority to establish the laws of your fictional universe. You decide if there are even concepts like liberty or freedom or any other laws of your universe. You get to define reality for the characters and you become the authority. An author’s control over a fictional world can illustrate creative causality, but it does not define moral or political authority. Human rulers do not create truth or persons; they exercise limited offices within an objective moral order.

    God's "authority" comes from being the "author" of all Creation and the laws of love specifically—He designed how love works. His "laws" aren't arbitrary commands but the blueprint for authentic love. Authority is only truly active when WE FREELY SUBMIT our will to His laws since he is the Author. God’s authority is intrinsic to the Creator and supreme Good; his law is the work of divine Wisdom, not arbitrary will. Created authorities participate in that order within defined limits. A legitimate law can truly bind even when a person refuses it, though an unjust command contrary to the moral order does not bind in conscience. Free obedience perfects the subject’s response; it does not create the authority being obeyed.

    Editorial consequence. The chapter now teaches the Catholic doctrine of authority rather than an attractive but misleading pun.

    CH4-10 · Do not claim that “gift” settles the grace–freedom controversy or limits divine freedom

    Severity: Stop-ship / grace and divine freedom · Control: DC-04, DC-06, DC-12, DC-19 · Location: chapter_04.md:123

    Issue. The paragraph says a gift cannot be forced, claims this word settles a long Christian controversy over grace and free will, and concludes God “could not have made us any other way without contradicting His own nature.” Grace is prevenient and can move, heal and enable the will without coercion; Catholic schools legitimately debate how divine motion and created freedom cohere. Creation is free, not necessary, and speculative limits should not be imposed on what God could create.

    Why it matters. A private definition cannot settle disputes the Church allows or make this created order necessary to God’s essence.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1730–1748; CCC 1996–2002; Council of Trent, Decree on Justification, chapters 5–6; DC-04; DC-06.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    This is where the word gift in our definition is doing real philosophical theological and pastoral work, not just poetic work. A gift, by its very nature, cannot be forced on someone and remain a gift—the moment it's coerced, it becomes something else: a tax, a seizure, an imposition. If God's Perfect Love truly is a gift, as we established in Chapter 2, then it must be capable of being freely received or freely refused. This settles a real and long-running argument among Christians over whether we actually have free will, or whether grace simply overrides the will. The very structure of gift answers it: love that could not be refused would not be a gift at all, and a God who is love could not have made us any other way without contradicting His own nature. is freely given; a coerced human response would not be an act of love. Yet divine grace is not an external object waiting for an unaided will. God’s prevenient grace awakens, heals and enables the very response it invites without destroying freedom. The word “gift” does not by itself settle every Catholic account of grace and free will, nor does it prove that God was metaphysically compelled to create this world or these creatures.

    Editorial consequence. Grace remains gift and the response remains free without semi-Pelagian imagery or speculative necessity.

    CH4-11 · Reject the false choice between human law and divine authority

    Severity: Stop-ship / authority, natural law and political theology · Control: DC-14, DC-15, DC-19, DC-22, DC-26 · Location: chapter_04.md:125–135

    Issue. The text says human-authored morality must be imposed by force, God’s authority is always only an invitation, and law inevitably escalates from coercion to eliminating dissenters. Human positive law can derive valid conclusions from natural law, make prudent determinations for the common good, and use proportionate coercion legitimately. God also commands, judges and sanctions; invitation describes faith’s free assent but not the whole of authority.

    Why it matters. The binary undermines civil and ecclesial authority and accidentally makes all enforcement intrinsically authoritarian. Catholic social teaching instead distinguishes legitimate authority from abuse by its end, competence and means.

    Controlling sources: CCC 160; CCC 1897–1904; CCC 1950–1960; CCC 2238–2243; Dignitatis Humanae 1–8.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    This is the key to understanding covenant love: When humans try to author morality without God, they must impose it through force. But God's authority is always an invitation that human hearts freely recognize and accept.
