    Chapter 13 · Faith Works Through Love

    Chapter assessment

    Chapter 13 rightly defines human virtue as a stable disposition, honors charity’s primacy, insists that living faith acts through love, rejects merely external compliance, affirms interior renewal in justification, encourages concrete practice and sacramental life, and offers the saints as companions in formation.

    Its central matrix, however, is circular and often doctrinally inaccurate. The chapter defines each virtue in language selected to produce checkmarks, assumes truth and sacrifice belong to every virtue by definition, and then treats the completed pattern as confirmation that the private definition captures the essence of all Christian virtue. Charity as the “form of the virtues” does not mean every acquired or natural virtue is made out of charity. The theological virtues are infused, not trained into existence; stable virtue can act promptly and joyfully without a felt cost. Hope resides in the will and is confident desire and expectation, not simply the mind’s assent. Dulia is technical veneration, penance is not payment of an unpaid divine bill, zeal can be disordered, and the seven-item “capital virtues” list is not a fixed Magisterial taxonomy. Humility is neither purely vertical nor prior to faith nor a definition of Catholic identity. Initial justification is a real grace-filled transition, ordinarily in Baptism, in which the theological virtues are infused together—not a lifetime of God forming one virtue at a time. Dead faith remains faith, and “state of grace” is not the name of a fully ordered lifestyle.

    CH13-01 · Distinguish acquired human virtues from infused theological and moral virtues

    Severity: Stop-ship / virtue and grace · Control: DC-06, DC-08 · Location: chapter_13.md:29–35

    Issue. Repeated practice acquires and strengthens human moral habits; it does not create faith, hope or charity, which God infuses with sanctifying grace. Infused moral virtues and gifts also cannot be reduced to spiritual muscle built by training.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1803–1811, 1812–1829, 1996–2005; Council of Trent, Decree on Justification, chapter 7.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    A virtue is a habitual and firm disposition to do good. It's ... having trained yourself through repeated practice Human virtues can be acquired and strengthened through repeated good acts. The theological virtues—faith, hope and charity—are infused by God; infused moral virtues and the gifts of the Spirit also come with grace, although our cooperation exercises and deepens their operation. The muscle analogy applies only with that distinction.

    Editorial consequence. The chapter does not teach that supernatural virtues are acquired by moral exercise.

    CH13-02 · Explain “charity is the form of the virtues” accurately

    Severity: Stop-ship / charity and virtue · Control: DC-07 · Location: chapter_13.md:33–41

    Issue. Charity gives Christian virtues their supernatural orientation and meritorious living form; it does not mean every natural or acquired virtue is materially “made of” charity. Nonbelievers can possess real acquired virtue without infused charity.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1812–1829; Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II, q.23, a.8.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    Love itself is not one virtue standing beside the rest—it is what every other virtue is made of. As ... charity “is the form of the virtues”: this is how we do love the theological virtue of charity, greatest of the three. To call charity “the form of the virtues” means that it orders and animates the Christian’s virtues toward the supernatural end in God; it does not make every natural habit a piece of charity or deny real acquired virtue outside the state of grace.

    Editorial consequence. A technical Thomistic phrase is no longer made to carry a false material meaning.

    CH13-03 · Do not make four-facet compliance a necessary test of every virtue

    Severity: Stop-ship / method and circularity · Control: DC-01, DC-26 · Location: chapter_13.md:35–43

    Issue. The hypothesis says every virtue “should” map to the selected pillars and then constructs definitions to make that happen. A useful devotional comparison cannot serve as validation that the definition is exhaustive or correct.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    If virtues align us with the good, every virtue should uphold and flow from the four pillars we may ask whether the four terms illuminate aspects of their operation. A virtue need not display every term in each act, and failure to fit a box may reveal a limit of the framework rather than a defect in the virtue.

    The matrix shows which facets ... each row first states the virtue's defining act—the definition its marks are read from offers tentative pastoral associations. Its brief descriptions are not complete scholastic definitions, and the marks should not be read as doctrinal classifications or proof of the framework.

    Editorial consequence. The matrix becomes an aid that can be corrected rather than a circular demonstration.

    CH13-04 · Remove “capital virtues” as though it were a fixed Catholic taxonomy

    Severity: Major / terminology and source control · Control: DC-28 · Location: chapter_13.md:41, 60–67

    Issue. The Church has a stable list of seven capital sins, but lists of corresponding remedial virtues vary. Humility, generosity, chastity, patience, diligence, kindness and contentment are not defined by the Catechism as an official class called “the capital virtues.”

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    The Tradition has named theological virtues, cardinal virtues and many related virtues. The capital virtues The following seven are a common devotional list of virtues opposed to the capital sins, not a fixed Magisterial taxonomy.

    ¶029 · table
    Capital Virtues A traditional remedial list

    Editorial consequence. Readers are not taught that one popular list carries a formal status it does not possess.

Hope resides in the will as confident desire and expectation of beatitude and the grace to attain it, not simply “truth” or mental assent.     CH13-06 · Correct the definitions of the cardinal virtues

    Severity: Major / virtue definitions · Control: DC-01, DC-28 · Location: chapter_13.md:55–60

    Issue. Prudence discerns the true good and right means, not “what love must do” as defined by the manuscript. Justice, fortitude and temperance have received definitions not dependent on assigning cost, covenant or bodily trusteeship.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1805–1809.

    Source table rows with proposed inline redline:

    ¶041 · table
    Prudence
    		Right judgment of what love must do here and now Practical reason discerning the true good in circumstances and choosing right means
    Justice
    		The constant will to render God and neighbor their due The constant and firm will to give God and neighbor their due
    Fortitude
    		Firmness in rendering what is owed when it costs—even life Firmness in difficulties and constancy in pursuit of the good, moderating fear and daring
    Temperance
    		Governing appetite so the body God bought is kept in trust Moderating attraction to pleasures and balancing use of created goods

    Any four-facet marks should follow these received definitions and remain interpretive, not be placed into the definitions themselves.

    Editorial consequence. The cardinal virtues regain their actual Catholic content.

    CH13-07 · Correct penance, dulia, zeal and contentment

    Severity: Stop-ship / definitions · Control: DC-10, DC-18, DC-28 · Location: chapter_13.md:67–88

    Issue. Penance is conversion, contrition and satisfaction united to Christ, not paying an unpaid divine debt. Dulia is technical veneration of saints, not generic honor of excellence. Zeal is ardor that can be good or disordered and cannot be defined as winning back “every soul.” Contentment must not prohibit legitimate aspiration or redress of injustice.

    Source table rows with proposed inline redline:

    ¶047 · table
    Contentment
    		Rendering God due gratitude for His allotment, desiring nothing beyond it Peaceful gratitude for God’s gifts and providence without envy or grasping, compatible with just aspiration and action against wrong
    Penance
    		Rendering to God the debt sin left unpaid Interior conversion expressed in contrition, confession, satisfaction and amended life; satisfaction does not purchase forgiveness
    Dulia
    		Rendering honor due to another’s excellence ... apart from parents or country The veneration given to angels and saints, distinct from the adoration owed to God and from the special veneration of Mary
    Zeal
    		Ardent labor ... going out to win back every soul in ruin Ardor for God’s glory and the true good; zeal is virtuous only when governed by charity and prudence and can otherwise become bitter or disordered

    Editorial consequence. Four concrete definitional errors are removed from the table.

    CH13-08 · Do not define wisdom as seeing everything as God sees it

    Severity: Major / epistemic humility · Control: DC-26 · Location: chapter_13.md:71, 100

    Issue. Created wisdom judges divine and human things in right order and, as a gift, gives connaturality with divine things; no creature in this life sees “all things as God sees them.” The inflation then justifies giving wisdom every checkmark.

    Source table row with proposed inline redline:

    ¶052 · table
    Wisdom
    		Seeing all things as God sees them and ordering love accordingly Judging created and divine realities in right order under their highest causes; as a gift of the Spirit, a connatural judgment of divine things through charity

    Three virtues carry all four marks because their definitions take in the whole of love ... wisdom sees what love is doing in all things have been assigned broad marks by the author, but those marks should not be inferred from an omniscient definition no creature possesses.

    Editorial consequence. Wisdom is elevated without being confused with divine omniscience.

    CH13-09 · Remove the claim that truth and sacrifice belong to every virtue by definition

    Severity: Stop-ship / matrix logic · Control: DC-07, DC-26 · Location: chapter_13.md:90–98

    Issue. Virtue necessarily concerns a true good as judged by right reason, but “truth” need not be the virtue’s proper object. Stable virtue can act with ease, promptness and joy; it need not give something away or feel costly. Eutrapelia itself shows virtuous rest and play.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    Two pillars are built into the definition of virtue itself ... every virtue gives something away ... A virtue that cost nothing and gave nothing would be a preference ... Truth and Sacrifice are marked in every row. Every genuine virtue is ordered to a real good and right reason, but that does not make “truth” the defining object of each virtue. Nor is sacrifice definitional: mature virtue often acts promptly, joyfully and without felt strain, and some acts receive, contemplate, rest or play. Remove the automatic Truth and Sacrifice checkmarks and assess only specific, defensible relations.

    Editorial consequence. The matrix no longer manufactures universal fit by definition.

    CH13-10 · Correct the psychology and theological account of hope

    Severity: Stop-ship / hope · Control: DC-09 · Location: chapter_13.md:96–100

    Issue. Hope is not “the mind’s assent,” does not hold its object with the same certainty as possessed knowledge, and is not contrasted with charity as mere reception versus outward spending. It resides in the will and includes confident expectation amid possible fear.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    To hope is to hold as certain what has not yet arrived ... an act of truth ... Hope leads with Truth alongside Faith: both are the mind's assent ... Faith and Hope receive the gift; Charity gives it away to desire and confidently expect beatitude and the grace to attain it, relying on God’s fidelity rather than our strength. Faith perfects the intellect; hope and charity perfect the will in distinct ways. Hope can coexist with fear and remains oriented toward one’s own and others’ salvation; it is not merely passive reception, nor is charity reducible to spending what hope receives.

    Editorial consequence. Faith, hope and charity remain distinct theological virtues ordered to living unity.

    CH13-11 · Correct the account of humility

    Severity: Stop-ship / humility and Catholic identity · Control: DC-07, DC-09 · Location: chapter_13.md:102–104

    Issue. Humility is a created virtue like other human virtues; it is not uniquely “created,” purely vertical, devoid of mercy, prior to faith, the cause that makes assent possible, or the definition of being Catholic. It moderates self-estimation under truth and affects how one treats neighbors directly.

    Controlling sources: CCC 2540, 2559, 2631; Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II, q.161.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    A note on humility. Alone among the created virtues ... every pillar faces God ... it never turns outward ... arrives with faith—perhaps even before it ... precisely what it means to be Catholic ... the door they all walk through Humility keeps self-estimation truthful under God, restrains disordered ambition and disposes us to receive grace. It has immediate neighbor-facing effects: listening, repentance, service and refusal to dominate. Grace can dispose a person toward faith through many movements; humility is not chronologically or causally prior to the infused gift as a rule. Catholic identity is baptismal and ecclesial, not a synonym for one virtue. Humility is foundational, but not the sole door through which every virtue exists.

    Editorial consequence. Humility remains indispensable without being inflated beyond the tradition.

    CH13-12 · Do not use divine simplicity to prove every human virtue contains four facets

    Severity: Stop-ship / analogy and divine simplicity · Control: DC-01, DC-02, DC-27 · Location: chapter_13.md:104

    Issue. God’s attributes are identical with His simple essence; created habits are finite accidents with distinct formal objects. Divine simplicity does not entail that every human virtue “deeply” contains every selected facet.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    In the deepest sense, every virtue touches all four pillars—since every virtue is an expression of love, and God, who is Love, is perfectly simple and undivided. God is perfectly simple, but created virtues are genuinely distinct habits with distinct objects. Charity orders Christian virtues toward God; it does not follow that divine simplicity places every one of the author’s four categories inside every human habit. The marks remain analogical observations only.

    Editorial consequence. A metaphysical doctrine is no longer used to validate a chart about created psychology.

    CH13-13 · Remove the four “families” as a diagnostic taxonomy

    Severity: Major / unsupported classification · Control: DC-01, DC-26 · Location: chapter_13.md:104–122

    Issue. The four families are generated by the same disputed marks, duplicate virtues across columns and then diagnose open and closed “doors” in the reader. No evidence shows that ease with one virtue identifies how grace enters or that difficulty identifies a single blocked facet.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    Read the stars by column: the virtues sort into four families. The following four columns are one provisional thematic index, not families recognized by the Church; duplicated entries demonstrate that the categories overlap.

    The column where you are strong is the door through which God's love already enters may suggest an area of relative strength, though self-assessment is fallible. The column you avoid is the door still closed—and now you know exactly where to knock may suggest questions for prayer or counsel, but cannot diagnose grace, vice or vocation by itself.

    Editorial consequence. The table remains usable for reflection without masquerading as spiritual diagnosis.

    CH13-14 · Distinguish real natural virtue from the supernatural “fullness” of infused virtue

    Severity: Major / grace and nonbelievers · Control: DC-06, DC-07 · Location: chapter_13.md:124–130

    Issue. The chapter rightly acknowledges virtue outside churchgoing, then says grace gives merely the highest form of the same list and power to live it. Infused theological virtues have God Himself as object; sanctifying grace elevates acts to supernatural merit rather than simply making natural virtue stronger.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    Every person may practice real acquired virtues through reason, formation and actual grace—the honest neighbor who never enters a church is not pretending. What sanctifying grace gives the Christian is the fullness of the same list: the highest form of each virtue ... with the power to live it a supernatural participation in divine life, the infused theological virtues whose direct object is God, and infused moral virtues ordering acts to beatitude. Grace heals and elevates natural virtue rather than merely turning the same habits up to a higher setting.

    Editorial consequence. Natural goodness and supernatural charity are both honored without being collapsed.

    CH13-15 · Do not force every act into virtue-or-vice or every virtue into a vow

    Severity: Major / moral action · Control: DC-10, DC-16 · Location: chapter_13.md:130–134

    Issue. Virtue and vice are stable habits, not the only two labels for every isolated act. Some acts are morally indifferent in object before intention and circumstances specify them. Not every virtue is a covenant vow, and virtuous action does not guarantee visible communion.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    Every act sets the same free choice: virtue or vice. Each virtue is a small vow kept ... each vice that vow broken. Repeated good or bad acts can form virtues or vices; a particular act must be judged by object, intention and circumstances and is not automatically an expression of a stable habit. Covenant language applies especially to actual covenant duties, not metaphorically to every virtue. Choose virtue and communion follows is served, though earthly relationships and outcomes remain affected by others’ freedom and circumstances.

    Editorial consequence. Moral acts, habits, covenants and outcomes are no longer treated as interchangeable.

    CH13-16 · Remove the claim that the matrix confirms the definition

    Severity: Stop-ship / circular validation · Control: DC-01, DC-26 · Location: chapter_13.md:132–138

    Issue. The table’s descriptions were written to contain the framework’s words, so the resulting checkmarks cannot confirm the framework’s essence or completeness. Salvation is also not definable as human nature reaching integrated virtue.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    The pattern reveals ... This confirms that our definition captures the essence of authentic Christian virtue. The exercise may show that the four terms can prompt questions about many virtues. Because the terms and row descriptions were selected by the author, the resulting pattern cannot independently confirm that the definition captures their essence.

    This is what it means to be saved: healed ... until love is our nature fulfilled one aspect of sanctification. Salvation is Christ’s gratuitous work through His Paschal mystery and grace, including justification, adoption, sacramental life, perseverance, resurrection and glory; virtuous integration is a fruit of grace, not the whole definition of being saved.

    Editorial consequence. The chapter stops using a self-generated matrix as proof and a virtue program as a complete soteriology.

    CH13-17 · Say dead faith remains faith

    Severity: Stop-ship / faith and charity · Control: DC-09 · Location: chapter_13.md:142–145

    Issue. Faith without charity is dead or unformed but remains faith unless faith itself is lost. “Requires sacrifice to be authentic” makes sacrifice the criterion of whether faith exists and repeats Chapter 9’s error.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1814–1816; Benedict XVI, General Audience, November 19, 2008.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    St. James says faith without works is dead. Even faith requires sacrifice to be authentic—faith without love is dead. Catholic theology calls faith without charity dead or unformed: it remains genuine assent, but without charity it does not fully unite the believer to Christ and cannot save. Living faith acts through love and may require sacrifice when fidelity is costly; sacrifice is not what makes assent count as faith.

    Editorial consequence. The chapter’s title is explained in the Church’s exact distinction.

    CH13-18 · Distinguish initial justification from lifelong sanctification

    Severity: Stop-ship / justification · Control: DC-08 · Location: chapter_13.md:146–154

    Issue. Initial justification is not God gradually forming one virtue at a time. It remits sins, sanctifies and renews, gives sanctifying grace and infuses faith, hope and charity together; ordinarily this real transition occurs in Baptism. Growth follows throughout life.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1213, 1262–1274, 1987–2029; Council of Trent, Decree on Justification, chapters 7 and 10.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    Justification is real interior renewal. God does not merely declare us righteous; He forms us into righteousness, one virtue at a time. None of this is instantaneous. Working it out is the labor of a lifetime in initial justification He forgives sins, gives sanctifying grace, adoption and the infused theological virtues—ordinarily through Baptism. The justified then grow throughout life in sanctification and virtue, can fall through mortal sin, and can be restored through Penance.

    Editorial consequence. Catholic interior renewal is kept without denying the initial gift.

    CH13-19 · Remove the invented sequence from fear to prudence to wisdom

    Severity: Major / virtue development · Control: DC-06, DC-09 · Location: chapter_13.md:150–152

    Issue. Humility, religion, obedience, temperance, penance, fortitude and perseverance do not unfold in the stated linear order; nor does fear mature into prudence and finally wisdom as a universal sequence. Virtues and gifts are distinct and can grow unevenly under grace.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    Made concrete, filial fear can accompany humility, religion, obedience, temperance, penance, fortitude and perseverance. Working it out ... growing from this fear into Prudence ... and at last into Wisdom These virtues and gifts are not steps in a fixed ladder. God infuses and increases gifts according to His grace, while acquired habits grow through practice; prudence, wisdom, charity and filial fear can operate together and mature unevenly.

    Editorial consequence. The spiritual life is not forced into an author-created developmental sequence.

    CH13-20 · Correct teleios and the definition of state of grace

    Severity: Stop-ship / holiness and sacramental life · Control: DC-08, DC-10, DC-27 · Location: chapter_13.md:154–158

    Issue. Matthew 5:48 calls disciples to perfection in charity; “not flawless or sinless” is too categorical and word-study driven. A state of grace means possessing sanctifying grace and not being conscious of unrepented mortal sin, not having one’s whole lifestyle ordered around every virtue.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    “Perfect” (teleios) can mean complete, mature or perfect according to context; here Christ calls us to not flawless or sinless, but whole ... a heart that keeps no corner back the perfection of charity patterned on the Father, genuinely begun by grace and consummated in glory.

    This is what it means to live in a state of grace: to order our whole life around charity and the virtues ... until we are free to be fully loved to possess sanctifying grace, ordinarily received in Baptism and restored after mortal sin through sacramental reconciliation. Growth in an ordered life is its fruit, not its definition; God already loves the sinner before that growth is complete.

    Editorial consequence. Holiness and sacramental status are no longer conflated with moral achievement.

    CH13-21 · Label the heart–soul–mind–strength map as devotional

    Severity: Major / exegesis · Control: DC-20, DC-27 · Location: chapter_13.md:160–166

    Issue. Scripture’s terms express total love of God with overlapping anthropological language. They do not assign heart to four facets, soul to communion, mind to truth and strength to sacrifice as a lexical anatomy.

    Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:

    The command gathers the whole person into love of God and neighbor. Heart is to desire truth, justice, mercy and sacrifice; soul is communion; mind is truth; strength is sacrifice As a devotional application, heart can prompt examination of desire, soul of one’s whole life before God, mind of revealed truth and strength of embodied service. The biblical terms overlap and should not be treated as four technical compartments or proof of the framework.

    Editorial consequence. A practical meditation remains without being presented as Christ’s hidden taxonomy.

    CH13-22 · Avoid a blanket Pharisee caricature

    Severity: Major / Jewish readers and exegesis · Control: DC-20, DC-22 · Location: chapter_13.md:168

    Issue. Jesus denounces hypocrisy among particular scribes and Pharisees; the Gospels do not define “the Pharisees” as cost-paying rule-keepers devoid of truth, justice and mercy. Paul himself was a Pharisee, and Jewish observance should not function as Christianity’s loveless foil.

    Source sentence with proposed inline redline:

    Jesus confronted the Pharisees [who] paid every cost religion asked while truth, justice, and mercy bled out ... rule-keepers Jesus called whitewashed tombs particular scribes and Pharisees for hypocrisy—meticulous external observance joined in some cases to neglect of justice, mercy and faithfulness. His warning addresses every religious community, including Catholics; it is not a description of all Pharisees or Judaism.

    Editorial consequence. The moral warning is universalized without anti-Jewish shorthand.

    CH13-23 · Do not treat emotional discomfort as a probable divine signal

    Severity: Stop-ship / conscience and scrupulosity · Control: DC-11, DC-25 · Location: chapter_13.md:174–180

    Issue. Discomfort can arise from true conviction, scrupulosity, trauma, anxiety, shame or manipulation. Conscience is a judgment of reason formed by objective norms, not an affective sting. “Follow the conviction” can harm scrupulous readers.

    Controlling sources: CCC 1776–1794.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    Wherever those questions stung most, follow the conviction—that discomfort often signals where God is calling you to grow do not assume the feeling itself is God’s verdict. Test it against Scripture, commandments, Church teaching, facts, freedom and prudent counsel. Scrupulous, traumatized or anxious readers should follow a stable confessor or qualified clinician rather than repeatedly interrogating feelings. Choose a concrete virtue only after the moral issue is identified accurately.

    Editorial consequence. The exercise forms conscience without feeding scrupulosity.

    CH13-24 · Correct the Augustine biography and inflated historical claims

    Severity: Major / history and pastoral tone · Control: DC-28 · Location: chapter_13.md:182–190

    Issue. Augustine spent about nine years as a Manichaean hearer, then passed through skepticism before Platonism and conversion; “most of two decades” building known lies overstates his self-awareness. His long relationship, though objectively outside marriage, should not be flattened into intellectual dishonesty. The garden voice may have been a boy or girl. Calling him the architect of Church doctrine and assigning exact twenty/forty-year eras is inflated.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    For much of his youth and early adulthood Augustine sought truth through ambition, a long nonmarital relationship, Manichaeism, skepticism and philosophy. For most of two decades he spent that mind building elaborate justifications for a life he half-suspected was a lie He later interpreted these wanderings with severe honesty, but we should not claim continuous conscious fraud or reduce his relationship and intellectual search to one motive. In a Milan garden he heard a childlike voice—a child's voice perhaps a boy or girl—chanting tolle, lege. After Baptism in 387 he spent more than four decades serving and writing. He became the architect of her doctrine one of the Church’s most influential Latin Fathers on grace, sin and the interior life, not the sole architect of doctrines received from Scripture and Tradition.

    Editorial consequence. Augustine’s conversion remains powerful without biography being shaped to fit four doors.

    CH13-25 · Make sacramental practice exact and avoid implying confession dispenses mercy on demand

    Severity: Major / Penance and Eucharist · Control: DC-17, DC-18 · Location: chapter_13.md:192–194

    Issue. Confession truly effects forgiveness through absolution, but “as often as grace is needed” is imprecise because grace is always needed and the Church’s requirements concern grave sin and annual confession, with frequent confession recommended. Communion requires more than a self-assessed “state of grace”: ecclesial communion, Eucharistic faith, fast and no conscious unconfessed grave sin.

    Source passage with proposed inline redline:

    Bring sins to Confession as often as the grace is needed ... mercy is dispensed there promptly when conscious of grave sin and regularly as a fruitful practice also for venial sin; Christ grants forgiveness through priestly absolution to the properly disposed penitent. Receive Communion in a state of grace when baptized and in ecclesial communion, believing, properly disposed, observing the fast and not conscious of grave sin without prior sacramental confession except in the Church’s narrowly stated exceptional case. The Eucharist is food for the journey, not a reward for moral perfection.

    Editorial consequence. Good sacramental counsel becomes canonically and pastorally safe.
