1 · Opening. Exodus 34:6 is now the whole verse. Isaiah 30:18 comes back as the second quotation, the one Ch. 7 released because its weight falls on mercy. It also holds justice and mercy in one sentence about one God, so the chapter's simplicity point is made before a word of prose. Four quotations, OT then NT. (RSV prints v. 6 ending in a comma because the proclamation runs on into v. 7; the period is only the close of our quotation.)

2 · Scripture. Every partial verse is now whole, or its quotation marks come off and it takes "cf." The one real correction: RSV Matthew 18:34 reads "jailers," not "torturers." Basanistais is the Greek behind it, so the text now says both. Luke 23:34 is quoted whole once (line 147), and line 167 refers back to it without quoting it a second time. That also removes the repeated "not strangers in the abstract."

3 · Callout. "Pay what you owe" is the unforgiving servant's own line (Matt 18:28, RSV). Putting it in the mouth of justice pits justice against mercy. It also sits badly beside Ch. 7's debt God never asks us to repay. "The wrong must be put right" keeps the contrast without either problem.

4 · Simplicity (todo 61). Mercy in God is "not as an affection of passion" but His goodness driving out misery (ST I q.21 a.3). We name His perfections one at a time because that is how creatures know Him (I q.13 a.4). "All three work together" made them three agents, so it is gone. Faustina's "greatest attribute" is now reported as her words. The book's own voice no longer ranks the attributes, and the Aquinas line no longer claims he did.

5 · Satisfaction. "Pay the debt" and "paid it" become "make satisfaction," the Church's word (CCC 615). CCC 616 says it is love that gives the sacrifice its value, which keeps the Cross from reading as a transaction. "Taking what we deserved" leans toward the Father punishing the Son, while CCC 615 speaks of an offering and of obedience. "Expiation/oblation" was not a mapping the Church uses.

6 · Marriage. Per entry 66, never state separation without the bond. Line 106 now matches Ch. 7 (CCC 1649, can. 1152). Can. 1152 is apt here because it earnestly recommends that the innocent spouse forgive, which is exactly what the paragraph describes. "Justice would demand termination" implied that a broken covenant may be ended, and that is the one thing the book denies of marriage.

7 · Doctrine. (a) "Mercy without repentance is not mercy at all" contradicted the chapter's own teaching that we must forgive the unrepentant, and Christ's prayer on the cross. Repentance conditions reconciliation, not forgiveness. (b) "Every sin separates us from God": venial sin wounds charity, and only mortal sin destroys it (CCC 1855). (c) "Before a soul encounters God's Perfect Love, it truly does not know what it is doing" would excuse every sin before conversion (against CCC 1860 and Rom 1:20). The new line keeps the blindness in sin without the excuse. (d) "It must flow through us to reach us" put our giving before God's; the king forgave first. (e) Children lost to abortion: the Church entrusts them to God's mercy (CCC 1261), so "gathers" becomes "holds." (f) The Sacrament: restoration is a fruit, not a "part." The parts are now named from CCC 1448, and the §7 four-facet mapping is kept. (g) "Moved by His grace" so that repentance is not the soul's own initiative.

4 · Divine simplicity: mercy in God is not a feeling, and not a part

Section: What Mercy Actually Means · line 72

… hesed (steadfast love, covenant faithfulness) and rachamim (from the Hebrew word for "womb"—the tender, instinctive compassion a mother has for her child). Together, they reveal mercy as faithful love that doesn't abandon despite betrayal, compassionate love that feels the beloved's pain, forgiving love that releases the debt, and restoring love that heals what is broken for the good of the covenant relationship. When Scripture speaks of God's mercy in the language of a mother's womb, it does not give Him a changing feeling; in God, mercy is His own goodness driving out our misery (cf. St. Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I, q.21, a.3).

Section: Mercy vs. Justice: Not Opposition but Completion · line 100

Both must always be present for love to be authentic—to pit them against each other is a false dichotomy. God is simple and undivided—justice and mercy are not separate parts of God but belong wholly to . His justice and His mercy are not two parts of Him, nor two powers balancing each other; each is His one divine nature. , and we name them one at a time because that is how creatures come to know Him (cf. CCC 271; St. Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I, q.13, a.4). In all His works, His truth reveals what is right, His justice upholds it, and His mercy restores it. All three work together in perfect unity.
