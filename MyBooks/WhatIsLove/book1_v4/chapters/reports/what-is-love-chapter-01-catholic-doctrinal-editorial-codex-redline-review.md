## Chapter 1 · The Problem of Evil

### Chapter assessment

Chapter 1 has a strong governing intuition: evil is parasitic upon the good, sin wounds communion, and neither personal conversion nor social repair can be detached from God. Its Augustinian privation account, the long quotation from CCC 309, and the insistence that the Church faces evil without flinching give the chapter a sound Catholic center. The largest doctrinal defect is the claim that freedom requires an equal capacity to choose evil. Catholic teaching says instead that the possibility of sin belongs to freedom that has not yet adhered definitively to God, while freedom becomes more perfect as it chooses the good. A second cluster reduces sin to taking a perceived good for oneself at another’s expense, which excludes interior sins, omissions, Godward sins, and sins against oneself. A third cluster weakens the argument through speculative motives, racialized single-cause analysis, deterministic family outcomes, and false binaries involving atheists, law, education, and science. The chapter can retain its urgency, pro-life conviction, Catholic anthropology, and call to conversion while becoming more doctrinally complete, evidentially disciplined, and pastorally credible.

### CH1-01 · Distinguish moral evil from physical evil at the chapter’s outset

**Severity:** Major / scope · **Control:** DC-05, DC-19, DC-26 · **Location:** `chapter_01.md:17–19`

**Issue.** “When love is absent” is too narrow to introduce the entire problem of evil. Catholic teaching distinguishes moral evil caused by the abuse of created freedom from physical evil within a creation still “in a state of journeying.” The chapter later defers natural evil to Chapter 2, but the reader needs that scope before the opening moral examples.

**Why it matters.** The title and opening quotations promise the full Christian problem of evil. Naming the chapter’s principal focus prevents the reader from mistaking an account of moral evil for an exhaustive theodicy.

**Controlling sources:** CCC 309–314, 324.

**Source passage with proposed inline redline:**

`## Why we need to understand love`

~~Before we can understand love, we must confront what happens when love is absent.~~ ++Before we can understand love, we must confront what evil does to communion. Moral evil arises from the abuse of creaturely freedom; physical or natural evil includes suffering, decay and death within a creation still journeying toward its perfection. This chapter begins with moral evil, while Chapter 2 returns explicitly to natural suffering.++

**Editorial consequence.** The chapter states honestly what it will and will not establish before presenting contested moral cases.

### CH1-10 · Preserve ignorance, wounded freedom, and degrees of culpability in the account of sin

**Why it matters.** Objective evil and subjective culpability are distinct. Failing to say so can form consciences badly and contradict the Catechism.

**Controlling sources:** CCC 1735; CCC 1790–1794; CCC 1854–1864.

**Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:**

~~**Yet here is the tragedy:** We have deep intuitions about what's good and true, yet we still choose evil. **Editorial consequence.** The chapter can condemn moral evil without presuming full knowledge and consent in every person it describes.

### CH1-13 · Correct the claim that freedom requires the power to choose evil

**Severity:** Stop-ship / doctrine · **Control:** DC-06, DC-12, DC-26 · **Location:** `chapter_01.md:71–73`

**Issue.** “There is no freedom to choose good without the freedom to choose evil” is false as a universal Catholic account. God, Christ, and the blessed are perfectly free without being able to sin. The account of heaven then implies that glory cannot be given until earned by a daily path.

**Why it matters.** This is the chapter’s central doctrinal error and a likely episcopal objection. It affects freedom, grace, heaven, merit, and the logic of the whole book.

**Controlling sources:** CCC 1731–1733; CCC 1996–2001; CCC 1023–1029.

**Source paragraphs with proposed inline redline:**

~~**And here is the logic at the heart of freedom:** There is no freedom to choose good without the freedom to choose evil. You cannot have one without the other. But the ability to sin is not freedom’s perfection; it is its possible abuse. “The more one does what is good, the freer one becomes,” because true freedom reaches its perfection in God and in what is good and just (CCC 1731–1733). ~~And if you are already thinking of heaven—where the saints are perfectly free and sin no more—you have seen the point exactly. **Editorial consequence.** The book’s foundational freedom principle becomes Catholic rather than libertarian and remains fully compatible with real consent.

**Source paragraph with proposed inline redline:**

~~**The very fact that you recognize evil bears witness to the existence of good.** Evil has no substance of its own—it is the *absence* of good, the corruption of what should be. ### CH1-17 · Make room for biblical lament and admit the limits of the free-will defense
