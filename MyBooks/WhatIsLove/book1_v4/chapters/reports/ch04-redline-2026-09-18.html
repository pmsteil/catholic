    Group A — The safety passage · line 173

    Answers Row 3. Line 173 stays exactly as it is; a new paragraph follows it. This is the one place the chapter turns from the ideal to the hard case, it already sits downstream of indissolubility at 171, and it already has the plain informational register.

    The five constraints hold: permission first and unhedged, no severity adjective the reader has to measure herself against, children named as CCC 2383 names them, separation framed as protecting what covenant is for, bond stated after the permission rather than before it.

    > Note: The Church's annulment process doesn't "dissolve" marriages—it recognizes that a valid sacramental bond never existed in the first place due to lack of consent, capacity, or form.

    > Indissoluble has never meant that you must stay in the house. If you or your children are in danger, you may leave, and leaving is not a sin against your vows. The Church permits spouses to separate and live apart—for danger, and for other reasons that make living together practically impossible (CCC 1649). Where a civil divorce is the only way to secure legal protection, the care of the children, or what your family is owed, the Catechism says it "can be tolerated and does not constitute a moral offense" (CCC 2383). A spouse who has sincerely tried to be faithful and is unjustly abandoned "has not contravened the moral law" (CCC 2386). The bond itself endures—none of this dissolves a valid marriage, and everything said above about indissolubility stands. What does not endure is any obligation to remain in harm's way. Covenant exists for the protection of the persons inside it, and getting them to safety is not the breaking of covenant but the honoring of what it was always for.

    Group B — Parents and the soul · lines 97 and 99

    Answers Row 5. The one hard doctrinal error in the chapter. Authored survives — the whole authority argument needs it — but what parents author is now stated exactly.

    Line 97:

    > Why is covenant so central to love? Because covenant is directly tied to the truth of our very existence and our need for love to sustain our souls. God is our Creator—the same way our parents create us and create the biological reality of the family. God is our Creator, and parents share in that work really—but not on the same level. They give the body and the family; each soul is created directly by God Himself and is not "produced" by the parents (CCC 366). This obviously mirrors God's covenant with us because He created us. God could have caused us to be born in an egg, totally isolated in the desert or any myriad of ways that don't involve being born into the loving arms of a mother, father, extended family, etc. We ought not take this for granted.

    Line 99:

    > Instead He created us to be cared for by our family. The family covenant reflects the divine covenant because both flow from the act of creation itself. When parents bring a child into existence, they enter a covenant relationship grounded in the very fact that they (most immediately, but not on their own) authored that child's lifeauthored that child's life in the world—truly, though never as the author of his soul. And as that child grows up, he learns how to love his family and eventually how to love his own family. This is why covenant love is inseparable from the truth of our existence. Without family, there is no existence. Without our existence there is no family.

    Line 175:

    > What About Those Called to Celibacy? Marriage isn't the only form of covenant self-gift. Those called to religious life or consecrated celibacy make a covenant gift of themselves directly to God and His Church. Their vows of poverty, chastity, and obedience are covenant commitments that image Christ's own spousal love for the Church. And the Church does not rank this second. Marriage belongs to this life: "For in the resurrection they neither marry nor are given in marriage, but are like angels in heaven" (Matthew 22:30 · RSV), and the Catechism calls marriage "a reality of this present age which is passing away" (CCC 1619). Virginity for the kingdom is the sign of what does not pass—"the supremacy of the bond with Christ" (CCC 1619)—which is the bond every other bond exists for. Single persons, too, live covenant love through Baptism, deep friendships, and service to the Body of Christ. Widowhood, a marriage that never came, a marriage that could not bear children: none of these shuts anyone out of the love this book is about, because the covenant that finally matters is the one made at Baptism and kept by God. Marriage is the paradigmatic human covenant—the clearest image of how covenant works—but it's not the only path to covenant love.
