line 41

line 47

Satan is called "the father of lies" (John 8:44) because every sin traces back to some falsehood believed or spoken. Lying is not merely one sin among many — it is the foundational sin that makes every other sin possible , and the first sin came in through his lie: "But the serpent said to the woman, 'You will not die.'" (Genesis 3:4). The root of that sin was not the lie itself but what it won: man "let his trust in his Creator die in his heart and, abusing his freedom, disobeyed God's command" (CCC 397). Lying is one particular sin, not the source of all the others. But every sin carries a falsehood inside it somewhere — a lie believed about what is good — and that is why lying strikes so close to the heart of love.

line 164

… The Eighth Commandment prohibits not only direct lying but "every kind of act contrary to justice and truth" (CCC 2476). Lying is the foundational destroyer of love And the Church names what a deliberate lie does to the person deceived: "The deliberate intention of leading a neighbor into error by saying things contrary to the truth constitutes a failure in justice and charity" (CCC 2485). A lie is not only false; it withholds what the other is owed, and it fails to love them. Lying strikes at the foundation of love, because love cannot exist without truth.

But a careful distinction matters: the Word of God is not first a book — He is a Person. "The Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14). From the very first Easter, the Church's gravitational center has been Christ Himself — proclaimed in Scripture, made present at every Mass (older than the Gospels celebrated before any Gospel was written; cf. Now look at those dates next to the dates of Scripture itself. The earliest Gospel (Mark) was not composed until c. A.D. 65; the last (John) not until c. A.D. 95; and the record of the Old and New Testament canon was not formally defined until the late 4th , Mark, is usually dated to the 60s; the last, John, to the 90s; and the full list of the books of both Testaments was not settled by councils until the end of the fourth century. The Church was preaching, baptizing, ordaining bishops, celebrating the Eucharist, and planting communities on three continents for more than thirty years before a single Gospel existed — and for over three centuries before the canon was defined roughly a generation before the first Gospel was written — and for more than three centuries before her councils settled the canon. The Church is not the product of the Bible. The Bible is the inspired testimony of the Church — written Nor is the Bible the product of the Church: its books "have God as their author" (Dei Verbum 11). But they were written within the Church, and received, gathered, and authenticated handed on by the same apostolic authority that founded these Churches local churches.¶Consider what those three centuries held. The books of the New Testament were written and read aloud in the churches long before anyone drew up a list of them. Around the year 155, St. Justin Martyr described a Sunday on which "the memoirs of the apostles and the writings of the prophets are read, as much as time permits," and then the Eucharist was offered (CCC 1345). But the Scriptures were read inside a life far larger than any list of books. Through those centuries the Church baptized and taught, celebrated the Eucharist every Lord's Day, ordained bishops in unbroken lines, and buried her martyrs through wave after wave of persecution. By the year 300, historians estimate, roughly one person in ten in the Roman Empire was a Christian, some six million souls. In 314 three bishops came from Britain to a council at Arles. In 325 more than two hundred and fifty bishops gathered at Nicaea from Europe, Africa and Asia, and at least one from Persia, beyond the empire's edge. By the middle of the fourth century the kings of Armenia, Georgia and Ethiopia had become Christians. The Creed we still recite was confessed at Nicaea and completed at Constantinople in 381, sixteen years before Carthage. All of this was the apostolic deposit being handed on: not a book waiting to be compiled, but the Church, "in her doctrine, life and worship," transmitting "to every generation all that she herself is, all that she believes" (CCC 78). Scripture is the Word of God set down in writing, and the Church reveres it above every other text; but she "does not derive her certainty about all revealed truths from the holy Scriptures alone" (CCC 82).¶Even the name we give those books began at a table. At the Last Supper, St. Luke records: "And likewise the cup after supper, saying, 'This cup which is poured out for you is the new covenant in my blood.'" (Luke 22:20). New covenant — in Greek kaine diatheke, in Latin novum testamentum — was first the name of what Christ gave that night, and only later the name of the books that record it. As Scott Hahn has put it, the New Testament was a sacrament before it was a document. The covenant was Christ Himself, given in the Eucharist and lived out by a people drawn into His love; the inspired books are the written witness to that covenant, not its beginning. Before there was ever a New Testament on a shelf, there was a Church bringing people into the love of Christ.
