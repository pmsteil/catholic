The Peace Offering paragraph.

The Peace Offering: peace is communion restored, and Christ is our peace (Ephesians 2:14)

Your instinct is right that the paragraph never connected peace to the meal, but the two belong together. In Hebrew, shalom is not just the absence of conflict; it is wholeness, a right relationship made whole again. At the peace offering, the worshiper sat at God's table and ate, and that meal was the peace, lived out. Paul's “participation” (communion) is the same idea. So the fix is to say what shalom means and name Christ as our peace (Ephesians 2:14). It also adds the thanksgiving offering, one kind of peace offering (Leviticus 7:11–15); “Eucharist” means thanksgiving (CCC 1328, vatican.va). The last line drops “transaction”, which is on the banned list.

The Peace Offering (shelamim, pronounced sheh-lah-MEEM) — the name comes from shalom, peace: not merely the end of hostility, but a friendship made whole. The shelamim ended in a meal shared by the worshiper himself: God's portion consumed by altar fire, the priest's portion, and the worshiper's portion, all eaten together at the sanctuary. One kind was offered in thanksgiving (cf. Leviticus 7:11–15). The sacrifice did not merely end sin; it began fellowship. Christ's sacrifice ends in the Eucharistic banquet . Peace with God was shared at His table. Christ is that peace: "For he is our peace, who has made us both one, and has broken down the dividing wall of hostility" (Ephesians 2:14). His sacrifice, too, ends in a meal of thanksgiving (the word Eucharist means thanksgiving; cf. CCC 1328), where we eat the Lamb who was slain: "The cup of blessing which we bless, is it not a participation in the blood of Christ? The bread which we break, is it not a participation in the body of Christ?" (1 Corinthians 10:16). The Cross is not a transaction does not end at a distance; it ends in a meal — at a table, and we are invited.
