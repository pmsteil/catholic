5. The figure for the year 300 is an estimate, not a count. Keith Hopkins, "Christian Number and Its Implications," Journal of Early Christian Studies 6 (1998): 185–226, takes "10 per cent of the population of the Roman empire, i.e. roughly 6 million people" as the benchmark on which historians broadly converge; Rodney Stark, The Rise of Christianity (Princeton University Press, 1996), Table 1.1, projects about 6.3 million, or 10.5 percent. The signatures of the Council of Arles name three bishops from Britain: Eborius of York, Restitutus of London, and Adelfius (A. W. Haddan and W. Stubbs, Councils and Ecclesiastical Documents Relating to Great Britain and Ireland, vol. 1, 1869; F. Haverfield, "Early British Christianity," English Historical Review 11, 1896). Eusebius, who was at Nicaea, writes that the bishops came "from all the churches which abounded in Europe, Lybia, and Asia," that "a Persian bishop too was present," and that "the number of bishops exceeded two hundred and fifty" (Life of Constantine 3.7–8); later tradition fixed the number at 318. Armenia: Sozomen, Ecclesiastical History 2.8, on King Tiridates; the traditional date is 301, and many scholars prefer 314. Georgia (ancient Iberia): Rufinus, Ecclesiastical History 10.11, and Sozomen 2.7, on King Mirian, most often dated about 326. Ethiopia (the kingdom of Aksum): Rufinus 10.9–10, on Frumentius, whom St. Athanasius made its first bishop; the letter of the Emperor Constantius to King Ezana, preserved in St. Athanasius, Apology to Constantius 31; and Ezana's own coins, on which the cross replaces the crescent and disc. 6. …
